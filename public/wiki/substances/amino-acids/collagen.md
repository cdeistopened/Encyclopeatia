# Collagen

## Overview

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the human body, comprising roughly 30% of total protein mass. It forms the structural framework for skin, bones, tendons, ligaments, blood vessels, and connective tissue throughout the body. Yet despite this ubiquity, modern nutrition largely ignores collagen as a dietary component, focusing instead on muscle meat proteins that provide entirely different amino acid profiles.

Ray Peat's attention to collagen and its hydrolyzed form [[gelatin]] represented a return to traditional dietary wisdom. For most of human history, people consumed the whole animal—not just muscle meat but also skin, bones, cartilage, and tendons rich in collagen. This provided a natural balance between the anti-metabolic amino acids concentrated in muscle ([[methionine]], [[tryptophan]], [[cysteine]]) and the protective amino acids that dominate collagen ([[glycine]] and proline).

The modern shift toward boneless, skinless cuts of meat and away from collagen-rich preparations may have inadvertently created amino acid imbalances that contribute to accelerated aging and chronic disease. Understanding collagen's role in both body structure and amino acid nutrition provides practical insights for optimizing [[protein]] intake.

## Structure and Composition

Collagen's unique structure determines both its physical properties and its nutritional characteristics. The molecule consists of three polypeptide chains wound together in a characteristic triple helix. This structure gives collagen tremendous tensile strength—it can withstand pulling forces while remaining flexible.

The amino acid composition of collagen differs dramatically from muscle proteins. Roughly one-third of collagen's amino acids are glycine, making it the richest dietary source of this conditionally essential amino acid. Another 15-20% consists of proline and hydroxyproline (a modified form of proline found almost exclusively in collagen). The remaining amino acids include various others, but notably, collagen contains essentially no tryptophan, minimal cysteine, and relatively low methionine.

This amino acid profile makes collagen nearly the mirror opposite of muscle meat. Where muscle proteins deliver high levels of the anti-metabolic amino acids Peat warned about, collagen provides abundant glycine and proline while lacking the problematic amino acids. This complementary relationship suggests traditional diets that included both muscle and collagen achieved amino acid balance through food combinations rather than through any single optimal protein source.

The hydroxyproline in collagen requires special attention. The body converts some proline to hydroxyproline after incorporating it into collagen molecules—a process requiring vitamin C. Scurvy, the vitamin C deficiency disease, results primarily from inability to properly hydroxylate proline, leading to defective collagen that cannot maintain structural integrity. This makes collagen synthesis one of the body's most vitamin C-dependent processes.

## Collagen Versus Gelatin

The terms collagen and gelatin are often used interchangeably in nutrition discussions, but they technically describe different forms of the same protein. Understanding the distinction helps clarify their respective roles and uses.

**Collagen** refers to the intact structural protein as it exists in animal tissues. The triple helix structure remains intact, giving collagen its characteristic toughness. Raw collagen is essentially indigestible—the human digestive system cannot break down the tightly wound helices without extensive cooking.

**Gelatin** is collagen that has been partially hydrolyzed (broken down) through long, slow cooking in water. This process—used to make bone broth, traditional stocks, and commercial gelatin—breaks the collagen triple helix into shorter chains that remain too large to dissolve in cold water but dissolve readily in hot water. When cooled, gelatin solution forms a gel as these chains reassociate.

**Collagen peptides** (often marketed as "hydrolyzed collagen") undergo more extensive breakdown, creating very short peptide chains that dissolve even in cold water and don't form gels. This increased digestibility makes collagen peptides convenient for supplementation but may sacrifice some of the functional properties of intact gelatin.

From a nutritional standpoint, these forms are largely equivalent once digested—all provide the same amino acid profile rich in glycine and proline. But Peat expressed preference for gelatin over collagen peptides, possibly because traditional gelatin preparation through long cooking preserves the gel-forming properties that may have functional benefits in the digestive tract.

The practical implication: homemade bone broth and commercial gelatin powder provide similar nutritional benefits and are preferable to highly processed collagen peptide powders, though all forms supply the beneficial amino acid profile.

## Metabolic Functions Beyond Structure

While collagen's structural role dominates most discussions, the amino acids it provides serve numerous metabolic functions beyond simply building connective tissue. Glycine in particular participates in multiple essential pathways.

**Glycine as inhibitory neurotransmitter:** Glycine functions as one of the brain's primary inhibitory neurotransmitters, counterbalancing excitatory signals from [[glutamate]] and other excitatory amino acids. Adequate glycine helps prevent excitotoxicity and supports calm, stable neurological function.

**Glycine in heme synthesis:** Every heme molecule (the iron-containing component of hemoglobin and numerous enzymes) requires glycine for synthesis. Insufficient glycine could theoretically limit heme production and contribute to anemia or impaired oxygen transport.

**Glycine in glutathione synthesis:** While [[cysteine]] is the rate-limiting amino acid for glutathione production, glycine is also required. Glycine deficiency could impair this critical antioxidant pathway.

**Glycine in detoxification:** The liver uses glycine to conjugate various toxins for elimination. Glycine conjugation represents one of the major phase II detoxification pathways, particularly for benzoate and other aromatic compounds.

**Glycine in blood sugar regulation:** Glycine helps stabilize blood sugar through multiple mechanisms, including supporting insulin secretion and improving glucose tolerance. Some research suggests glycine supplementation can improve glycemic control in diabetic individuals.

**Proline in collagen synthesis:** Beyond serving as a collagen building block, proline availability determines the rate of new collagen production for wound healing, tissue repair, and maintenance of existing structures.

These diverse metabolic roles suggest glycine becomes conditionally essential during stress, aging, or modern dietary patterns that emphasize muscle meat over collagen sources. The body can synthesize glycine from serine, but synthesis capacity may not meet demand when glycine is continuously used for neurotransmission, detoxification, and other functions while dietary intake remains low.

## The Glycine-to-Methionine Ratio

One of collagen's most important nutritional properties emerges from considering it alongside other protein sources rather than in isolation. The ratio between glycine and methionine in total dietary protein appears to influence longevity and disease risk more than the absolute amount of either amino acid.

Methionine restriction extends lifespan 30-40% in animal studies, but extreme methionine restriction isn't practical for most humans. Glycine appears to moderate methionine's pro-aging effects, allowing adequate methionine intake for essential functions while preventing the accelerated aging associated with excess methionine.

The mechanism involves multiple pathways. Glycine supports the conversion of homocysteine (the toxic methionine metabolite) back to methionine or forward to cysteine, preventing homocysteine accumulation. Glycine also appears to activate different metabolic pathways than methionine, creating beneficial metabolic competition that reduces reliance on methionine-dependent processes.

Traditional diets that used whole animals naturally provided favorable glycine-to-methionine ratios. A meal of chicken with bone broth delivered muscle meat (high methionine) balanced by the collagen-rich broth (high glycine). Modern meals of boneless chicken breast without broth or stock provide methionine without the glycine balance.

Research by various longevity investigators suggests optimal glycine-to-methionine ratios somewhere around 2:1 or 3:1—substantially higher than what modern muscle-meat-focused diets provide. Achieving this ratio requires either dramatic reduction in muscle meat consumption or substantial addition of collagen/gelatin to balance the muscle meat.

The practical strategy Peat implicitly recommended involves both: moderate muscle meat intake (rather than the excessive amounts promoted by high-protein diets) combined with daily gelatin consumption to ensure adequate glycine. This provides the amino acid balance that traditional diets achieved naturally.

## Collagen in Skin, Joints, and Gut Health

Beyond its amino acid contribution to overall nutrition, collagen serves specific functions in tissues where its structural properties matter enormously.

**Skin:** Collagen provides the tensile strength and elasticity that keep skin firm and youthful. Age-related collagen degradation—from oxidative stress, excess estrogen, UV exposure, and inadequate collagen synthesis—causes the wrinkles, sagging, and thinning associated with aging skin. While topical collagen cannot penetrate deeply enough to rebuild dermal structure, ensuring adequate collagen synthesis through nutrition and metabolic optimization can support skin maintenance.

**Joints:** Cartilage consists primarily of collagen, providing the cushioning that protects joint surfaces during movement. Collagen degradation in cartilage leads to osteoarthritis—loss of joint space, pain, and eventually bone-on-bone contact. Dietary collagen/gelatin provides the amino acids needed to maintain cartilage, though whether supplementation can reverse existing degradation remains debated.

**Bones:** While bones are famous for their calcium content, the structural matrix that holds calcium consists of collagen. Bone strength depends on both adequate mineralization and healthy collagen matrix. Age-related bone loss involves both calcium deficiency and collagen degradation. Adequate dietary collagen alongside calcium, vitamin D, and other bone nutrients supports bone health more effectively than mineral supplementation alone.

**Gut lining:** The intestinal mucosa contains substantial collagen in its basement membrane and connective tissue layers. Maintaining gut barrier integrity requires continuous collagen turnover. Some practitioners attribute gut healing properties to gelatin/bone broth consumption, theoretically providing the building blocks needed for intestinal tissue repair. While controlled studies are limited, the biological plausibility is strong.

**Blood vessels:** Arterial walls contain substantial collagen providing tensile strength to withstand blood pressure. Collagen degradation in vessel walls contributes to aneurysm formation and arterial stiffness. The vitamin C-collagen connection becomes critical here—scurvy's cardiovascular manifestations result from defective collagen in blood vessel walls.

These tissue-specific roles suggest collagen adequacy matters for multiple age-related conditions beyond just amino acid balance. Supporting collagen synthesis through adequate glycine and proline intake, sufficient vitamin C, and metabolic optimization may provide broad protective effects.

## Sources and Supplementation

Obtaining adequate collagen/gelatin requires either consuming collagen-rich foods or using supplemental forms. Traditional diets achieved high collagen intake naturally; modern diets typically don't.

**Bone broth:** Homemade broth from bones, cartilage, and connective tissue simmered for many hours provides gelatin alongside minerals and other compounds extracted from bones. The long cooking time breaks down collagen into digestible gelatin. Chicken feet, beef knuckles, and pork trotters make particularly gelatinous broths.

**Commercial gelatin powder:** Derived from pork or beef collagen through controlled hydrolysis, gelatin powder provides pure protein without the minerals and other compounds in bone broth. It dissolves in hot liquids and can be mixed into tea, coffee, or juice. Typical serving: 10-20 grams daily.

**Collagen peptides:** More extensively hydrolyzed than traditional gelatin, collagen peptides dissolve in cold liquids and don't form gels. They're convenient but more processed and may lack some functional properties of gelatin. Peat's preference appeared to be for traditional gelatin over collagen peptides.

**Skin-on poultry and fish:** Chicken skin, duck skin, and fish skin contain substantial collagen. Eating these rather than discarding them increases collagen intake, though the amounts remain far less than bone broth or supplemental gelatin provides.

**Tendon and cartilage:** In cultures that consume whole animals, tendon, cartilage, and other collagen-rich tissues contribute to intake. These are generally unpalatable to modern Western eaters without extensive cooking.

The practical approach for most people involves either daily bone broth consumption or supplementing with gelatin powder. A simple protocol: 10-20 grams of gelatin powder dissolved in juice or coffee daily, alongside a diet emphasizing dairy and eggs over excessive muscle meat. This provides the amino acid balance traditional diets achieved naturally.

## Collagen Production and Degradation Balance

The collagen in tissues undergoes continuous turnover—old collagen gets broken down while new collagen is synthesized. The balance between synthesis and degradation determines net collagen status. Age-related collagen loss reflects degradation outpacing synthesis.

**Factors supporting collagen synthesis:**
- Adequate glycine and proline (from dietary collagen/gelatin)
- Sufficient vitamin C for proline hydroxylation
- Adequate protein intake overall
- Good thyroid function providing energy for synthesis
- Adequate copper for lysyl oxidase (collagen cross-linking enzyme)
- Vitamin A for collagen gene expression

**Factors accelerating collagen degradation:**
- Oxidative stress from PUFA, inflammation, or metabolic dysfunction
- Excess estrogen activating collagenase enzymes
- UV radiation in skin
- Chronic inflammation driving tissue breakdown
- Cortisol elevation from stress
- Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) from high blood sugar

The practical implication: consuming collagen/gelatin provides necessary building blocks but doesn't guarantee collagen maintenance if degradation is accelerated. Optimal collagen status requires both adequate synthesis (proper nutrition) and minimized degradation (metabolic optimization).

This connects to Peat's broader framework—supporting thyroid function, reducing estrogen, minimizing PUFA intake, and controlling inflammation all protect collagen throughout the body. Combined with adequate dietary collagen/gelatin, these interventions support the synthesis-degradation balance that maintains youthful tissue structure.

## The Traditional Wisdom of Collagen Consumption

The modern neglect of collagen as a nutritional component represents a departure from traditional dietary patterns across virtually all cultures. Before industrial food production made boneless cuts standard, whole-animal consumption necessarily included substantial collagen.

French stock and demi-glace, Chinese chicken feet and pork hock, Jewish chicken soup, Vietnamese pho with tendon and tripe, Latin American menudo with tripe and feet—traditional cuisines worldwide featured preparations rich in gelatin. These weren't luxury items but practical use of the whole animal. Wasting collagen-rich parts was economically inefficient and, as it turns out, nutritionally unwise.

The French and other European traditions of sauce-making relied on stock reduced to concentrate gelatin, creating the rich, mouth-coating texture prized in classic cuisine. This wasn't just about flavor—it provided concentrated collagen that balanced the meat proteins in the same meal.

The Jewish tradition of chicken soup as medicine for illness wasn't superstition. The gelatin in properly made chicken soup provides easily digested protein rich in glycine and proline, supporting tissue repair and immune function during illness. The modern processed "chicken broth" sold in stores, made from meat without bones and thickened with starch, provides none of these benefits.

Peat's recommendations to consume gelatin daily simply formalized what traditional diets achieved naturally. The challenge for modern eaters involves either reviving traditional preparations like bone broth or using supplemental gelatin powder to restore the amino acid balance that whole-animal consumption once provided.
