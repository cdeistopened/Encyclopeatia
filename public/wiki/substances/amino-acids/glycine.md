# Glycine

If [[gelatin]] is the food that makes Ray Peat's protein framework work, glycine is the molecule that makes gelatin work. It is the simplest amino acid a cell can manufacture, carrying only a single hydrogen atom where the other amino acids carry elaborate side chains, and yet Peat spent decades arguing that this biochemical modesty conceals a pharmacological range that no synthetic drug can match. Glycine is the body's native inhibitory transmitter, its chloride-channel stabilizer, its anti-serotonin, its anti-tryptophan, its anti-estrogen, its anti-fibrotic, and its antidote to many of the processes that constitute aging. "The simplest, nonessential, amino acid, glycine, has been found to protect against carcinogenesis, inflammation, fibrosis, neurological damage, shock, asthma, and hypertension," Peat wrote in his newsletter [[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|"Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging"]]. That catalog, more than any other single sentence in his work, captures why he considered a small, humble amino acid worth writing newsletters about.

## Structure and the Inhibitory Transmitter

Glycine's formula, NH2-CH2-COOH, makes it the only amino acid with no chiral center. Where every other amino acid carries a bulky side chain, glycine is symmetric and small enough to occupy cellular spaces that exclude a larger molecule. Peat argued that part of glycine's protective effect is physical rather than metabolic. In [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity" (2009)]], he wrote that "there is evidence that glycine and alanine act protectively without being metabolized, simply by their physical properties." The amino acid does not need to be burned for energy or converted into another substance to exert its effect. Its presence is its function.

Most popular accounts of neurotransmission stop at GABA when listing the brain's inhibitory signals. Glycine is the other one, and in the spinal cord and brainstem it is the dominant one. Peat treated the two as functional siblings. "The other well-studied inhibitory neurotransmitter is GABA," he wrote in the same newsletter, "so it's significant that GABA (gamma amino butyric acid) is a close analog of glycine (alpha amino acetic acid). The similarity between the structures and actions of glycine and GABA suggest that their 'receptors' are similar, if not identical."

The mechanism is subtractive rather than additive. Glycine does not tell cells what to do. It raises the electrical threshold required before cells can do anything at all. Peat described the effect as a "chloride current": when glycine binds its receptor, a chloride channel opens, the membrane hyperpolarizes, and the cell becomes harder to fire. The net result is that noise is filtered out. Marginal stimuli fail to trigger nerve firing. Strong, relevant stimuli still get through.

Peat believed this chloride-channel effect was not confined to neurons. "Skin cells and nerve cells and many other cells are 'electrically' stabilized by glycine," he wrote. In every tissue where cells can be damaged by excessive excitation, glycine reduces the probability of that damage. The brain calls it inhibitory transmission. The liver, the skin, and the gut are running the same protection on a lower volume.

## The Anti-Serotonin

Glycine and [[serotonin]] form one of the cleanest opposing pairs in Peat's framework. Where serotonin generates [[tumor-necrosis-factor|tumor necrosis factor (TNF)]], glycine inhibits it. Where serotonin promotes inflammation, glycine resolves it. Where serotonin impairs learning, glycine improves it. The mechanism operates at several layers at once, and Peat laid it out in [[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|"Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging"]]: "The varied antiinflammatory and protective effects of glycine can be thought of as an antiserotonin action. For example, serotonin increases the formation of TNF (tumor necrosis factor, also called cachectin), glycine inhibits it. In some situations, glycine is known to suppress the formation of serotonin. Antagonists of serotonin can potentiate glycine's effects."

The practical consequence is that a diet high in [[tryptophan]], the amino acid precursor of serotonin, without a counterweighting source of glycine produces exactly the biochemistry Peat spent his life warning about. "People who ate traditional diets, besides getting a lower concentration of tryptophan, were getting a large amount of glycine in their gelatin-rich diet," he noted. The two levers work together. Cutting tryptophan reduces the substrate. Adding glycine opposes the downstream effects.

## The Krebs Cycle Connection

One of the more elegant features of glycine biosynthesis is the way it cleans up the very molecule that otherwise causes excitotoxic damage. The body makes glycine from glucose, with the addition of ammonia removed from glutamic acid. Glutamic acid, the brain's primary excitatory transmitter, becomes ketoglutaric acid once stripped of its amine, and ketoglutarate is a component of the [[Krebs-cycle|Krebs cycle]]. Forming glycine therefore achieves three things at once. It removes an excitotoxin from circulation, produces a protective inhibitory amino acid, and replenishes the oxidative metabolism that Peat considered the foundation of cellular health.

"In effect, the formation of glycine helps to eliminate an excitotoxin while replenishing the pathway of oxidative metabolism," Peat wrote in his newsletter [[fibrosis|"Fibrosis"]]. Glycine synthesis is not a side reaction. It is a detoxification reaction, a calming reaction, and a metabolic replenishment reaction folded into a single molecule.

## The Anti-Fibrotic

Glycine's ability to prevent and even reverse fibrosis cuts against conventional reasoning, because glycine is itself the major amino acid in [[collagen]], the protein that fibrotic scar tissue deposits in excess. Peat's answer was that fibrosis is a stress response, and glycine is an anti-stress molecule. "Inflammation produces fibrosis," he wrote in the same newsletter, "because stress, hypoxia, and inadequate supply of glucose stimulate the fibroblasts to produce increased amounts of collagen. In lungs, kidneys, liver, and other tissues, glycine protects against fibrosis, the opposite of what the traditional view would suggest." Providing glycine does not add to the collagen the fibroblasts are depositing. It tells the fibroblasts to stop depositing.

The liver offered the clearest experimental evidence. "In specific fibrotic conditions, such as cirrhosis of the liver, it is known that glycine and saturated fats can reverse the fibrosis." Saturated fats replace the [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] that drive the inflammatory cascade upstream, glycine quiets the downstream effects, and together they dismantle the fibrotic program rather than merely slowing it. Peat noted in [[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|"Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer"]] that "both proline and glycine (which are major amino acids in gelatin) are very protective for the liver, increasing albumin, and stopping oxidative damage."

## The Anti-Estrogen

The connection between glycine and [[estrogen]] is subtler than the connection to serotonin, but no less real. Peat built the argument on two observations. First, [[progesterone]] and its metabolites act on the glycine receptor, amplifying its inhibitory effect. Second, the phytoestrogen genistein, which Peat considered a particularly toxic compound in [[soy]], antagonizes that same receptor. If progesterone reinforces glycine's action and a representative estrogen blocks it, then glycine belongs, by functional definition, to the antiestrogenic side of the physiological ledger.

"In the context of the excitatory actions of estrogen, and the inhibitory action of glycine," Peat wrote, "it would be reasonable to think of glycine as one of the antiestrogenic substances. Glycine's inhibitory effects appear to oppose estrogen's actions generally, in sensory and motor nerves, in regulating angiogenesis, and in modulating the cytokines and 'chemokines' that are involved in so many inflammatory and degenerative diseases, especially tumor necrosis factor (TNF), nitric oxide (NO), and prostaglandins."

That list matches the list of things estrogen promotes. Estrogen increases angiogenesis; glycine blocks it. Estrogen increases TNF; glycine inhibits it. Estrogen increases nitric oxide; glycine opposes it. Every major mechanism by which estrogen does damage in Peat's framework has a corresponding glycine mechanism that undoes it. "Since GABA opposes estrogen and inhibits the growth of breast cancer," Peat observed, "it wouldn't be surprising if glycine, alanine, etc., did the same."

## Sleep, Stroke, and the Nervous System

Because glycine is an inhibitory transmitter, its effects on sleep and neurological injury are direct rather than metaphorical. "A small dose of glycine taken shortly after suffering a stroke was found to accelerate recovery," Peat wrote, "preventing the spreading of injury through its inhibitory and antiinflammatory actions. Its nerve-stabilizing action, increasing the amount of stimulation required to activate nerves, is protective in epilepsy, too. This effect is important in the regulation of sleep, breathing, and heart rhythm."

[[Sleep]] is where most people who follow Peat's recommendations encounter glycine first. The classical vehicle is [[gelatin]] dissolved in fruit juice at bedtime. In his newsletter [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|"Solving Some of the Problems of Aging"]], he put the case plainly: "The amino acids taurine and glycine are also considered to be inhibitory transmitters in the brain. Gelatin, which is extremely rich in glycine, is often added to fruit juice to take at bedtime as a sleep-supporting drink." The juice supplies [[sugar]] that prevents the nocturnal drop in blood glucose that otherwise triggers [[cortisol]] and adrenaline. The glycine quiets nerve excitation directly. The two interventions address wakefulness from opposite directions.

In the list of inhibitory defenses Peat catalogued in [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|"Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation"]], glycine appeared alongside the body's core adaptive hormones: "When the most powerful adaptive mechanisms, such as the timely synthesis of progesterone, pregnenolone, DHEA, T3, and the inhibitory transmitters, GABA and glycine, fail, then some of the primitive defense mechanisms will become chronically activated, and even sleep may fail to restore normal cellular water and metabolism."

## Mitochondrial Protection and the Antitoxic Action

The full enumeration of glycine's protective actions in [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]] is one of Peat's most concentrated sentences: "Fibrosis, free radical damage, inflammation, cell death from ATP depletion or calcium overload, mitochondrial damage, diabetes, etc., can be prevented or alleviated by glycine." Each item corresponds to a distinct injury pathway, and a single molecule addresses all of them. By stabilizing cell membranes and buffering [[calcium-overload|calcium overload]], glycine prevents the conditions under which [[mitochondria]] rupture. The chloride current is the upstream defense against the calcium current that drives the final common pathway of cell death.

The gut, with its constant exposure to [[endotoxin]] and dietary irritants, is one of the body's most glycine-responsive tissues. "A recent publication shows that glycine alleviates colitis; but the use of gelatin, especially in the form of a concentrated gelatinous beef broth, for colitis, dysentery, ulcers, celiac disease, and other diseases of the digestive system, goes far back in medical history." The chloride-current effect quiets the smooth muscle spasm of inflammatory bowel conditions, and the direct antagonism of TNF addresses the ulcerative damage on the immune side.

In his newsletter [[rosacea-inflammation-and-aging-the-inefficiency-of-stress|"Rosacea, Inflammation, and Aging"]], Peat listed glycine among the core factors that protect against inappropriate cellular excitation: "Carbon dioxide, glycine, GABA, saturated fatty acids, vitamin K, coenzyme Q10, niacinamide, magnesium, red light, thyroid hormone, progesterone, testosterone, and pregnenolone." The list spans hormones, vitamins, a gas, an amino acid, a class of fats, and the inhibitory transmitters. Glycine sits among them as a member in full standing.

## Practical Dosing

Peat's recommendations on glycine dosing tended toward the generous. "Since 30 g of glycine was commonly used for treating muscular dystrophy and myasthenia gravis, a daily intake of 100 g of gelatin wouldn't seem unreasonable, and some people find that quantities in that range help to decrease fatigue." The range most people hear about, two to ten grams, is the low end of what Peat actually discussed. Therapeutic doses for specific conditions reached thirty grams or more.

His warning about pure glycine supplementation was characteristic: "Although pure glycine has its place as a useful and remarkably safe drug, it shouldn't be thought of as a food, because manufactured products are always likely to contain peculiar contaminants." The preference was always for whole [[gelatin]], not isolated glycine, because gelatin arrives with its native companions, proline and alanine, which "have some antiexcitatory properties similar to glycine." Pure glycine remained useful in specific cases. It is slightly sweet, water-soluble, and safe at doses that would be toxic for almost any other supplement. "Glycine is non-toxic," Peat wrote, "if the kidneys are working, since any amino acid will contribute to the production of ammonia." A person with significant renal impairment should not take large doses of any amino acid, and glycine is no exception.

One correspondent asked Peat, in [[supplements|an email on supplements]], whether [[taurine]] could substitute for glycine if a person reacted badly to gelatin. Peat's reply was unsentimental: "I don't think it will have a similar effect, but it might provide its own unique benefit." The two amino acids live in adjacent territories of the same protective constellation, but they are not interchangeable.

## The Small Molecule Principle

Glycine exemplifies a pattern that runs through Peat's thinking about biology. The molecules that matter most are often the smallest. [[Carbon-dioxide|Carbon dioxide]] is a waste gas that turns out to be a master regulator of cellular metabolism. Progesterone is a small, lipid-soluble steroid that organizes cellular water. [[Thyroid]] hormone is a modified amino acid that controls the rate of every oxidative reaction in the body. Glycine fits the pattern. It is the smallest amino acid, the structurally simplest, and the one whose presence most reliably tips the physiological balance away from excitation and toward quiet oxidative work. That balance is what the modern diet, built around muscle meat and depleted of gelatinous tissues, has silently dismantled, and it is what any adult who feels the effects of [[hypothyroidism]], poor sleep, or chronic inflammation can restore with a cup of broth at the end of the day.
