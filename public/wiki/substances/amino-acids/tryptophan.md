# Tryptophan

Tryptophan occupies a singular position in Ray Peat's framework: it is the only amino acid he identified as carcinogenic, and the primary dietary precursor to [[serotonin]], a substance he spent decades arguing was one of the body's most destructive stress mediators. While mainstream nutrition treats tryptophan as a benign or even beneficial essential amino acid, and popular culture celebrates it as the sleep-inducing component of turkey dinners, Peat built a case that excess tryptophan after the growth period is a driver of [[inflammation]], [[cancer]], thyroid suppression, and accelerated [[aging]]. "Although several amino acids can be acutely or chronically toxic, even lethal, when too much is eaten, tryptophan is the only amino acid that is also carcinogenic," he wrote in his [[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|"Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging" (2006)]] newsletter. The practical conclusion he drew from this was not that tryptophan should be eliminated entirely, but that it should be minimized in the adult diet through the strategic use of [[gelatin]], which contains none of it, and that the modern habit of eating only muscle meats without the collagen-rich parts of the animal was a metabolic catastrophe hiding in plain sight.

## The Electronic Property: Why Tryptophan Matters

Peat's critique of excess tryptophan was not a dismissal of its biological importance. In a [[2019-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-tryptophan|2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on tryptophan]], he traced its significance to the deepest level of biological organization. "Albert Szent-Gyorgyi demonstrated the electronic nature of life," Peat explained, and tryptophan is "the amino acid that gives proteins that basic property which, according to Szent-Gyorgyi, is the essential mechanism of life that lets it sense and move." The fluorescence, luminescence, and electronic conductivity of living systems depend on tryptophan's presence in proteins. Cell division, sensation, movement, secretion, and consciousness all rely on this excitability.

In muscle [[protein]], Peat identified a specific tryptophan residue in myosin that interacts with [[cysteine]], an oxidizable sulfur-containing amino acid, to form what he called "the electric motor of life processes." During growth, this system is essential. The problem arises when growth stops. "If you keep the same ratio coming in via your diet after you stop growing, they are no longer being consumed for growth," Peat told listeners. "They become free actors which can spread excitability and oxidizability randomly in the organism." What was once a precisely regulated component of growth becomes, in the adult consuming standard amounts of muscle meat, a source of random excitatory and inflammatory signals.

## Growth Versus Maintenance: The Declining Requirement

The foundation of Peat's argument against tryptophan excess rests on a distinction the mainstream nutrition establishment has largely ignored: the difference between what a growing organism needs and what a fully grown adult needs. "Tryptophan is an essential amino acid for reproduction and growth of the young animal," he wrote in his 2006 newsletter. "Most research on the nutritional requirements for amino acids has been done on farm animals, because of the economic incentive to find the fastest growth. Farmers aren't interested in the nutritional factors that would produce the longest-lived pigs."

The research that does exist on post-growth requirements tells a striking story. In his [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity" (2009)]] newsletter, Peat summarized the state of the evidence: "The amino acid and protein requirements during aging have hardly been studied, except in rats, whose short life-span makes such studies fairly easy. The few studies that have been done indicate that the requirements for tryptophan and cysteine become very low in adulthood." The word "very" is doing significant work in that sentence. Peat was not arguing for a modest reduction but for a fundamental rethinking of how much tryptophan adults actually need.

In a [[2009-09-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-ten-most-toxic-things-in-our-food|2009 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on the ten most toxic things in food]], he pointed to historical evidence that confirmed the low adult requirement: "40 or 50 years ago, nutritionists wondered how 'little old ladies' could be so healthy just eating gelatin and toast because they thought of tryptophan and cysteine as essential amino acids. But old people can essentially live on it with just a trace of the others in things like bread and fruit." The bewilderment of midcentury nutritionists was, in Peat's view, a clue they failed to follow.

When Danny Roddy asked on [[55-bioenergetic-nutrition-basics-the-ray-peat-diet-appetite-and-metabolism-with-ray-peat|*Generative Energy* episode 55]] whether there was any way to gauge if someone was becoming too deficient in tryptophan, Peat's answer was characteristically precise: "Digestion is usually the first thing to go wrong. They'll start getting gas or some digestive symptom, which could be that they're not replacing the lining of the intestine fast enough." The intestinal lining, skin, hair, and nails are the primary tissues that require ongoing tryptophan for renewal. The quantities needed for this maintenance are far smaller than what most people consume.

## Longevity: The Restriction Experiments

The most dramatic evidence for Peat's position came from animal experiments on tryptophan restriction. "When rats were fed a diet completely lacking tryptophan for a short period, or a diet containing only one fourth of the 'normal' amount for a more prolonged period, the results were surprising," he wrote in the 2006 newsletter. "They kept the ability to reproduce up to the age of 36 months (versus 17 months for the rats on the usual diet), and both their average longevity and their maximum longevity increased significantly. They looked and acted like younger rats."

The magnitude of these effects rivaled or exceeded the most celebrated longevity intervention in gerontology. "Restricting only tryptophan, or only cysteine, produces a greater extension of the life span than achieved in most of the studies of caloric restriction," he wrote in the 2009 gelatin newsletter. This is a remarkable claim because caloric restriction has been the dominant paradigm in longevity research for decades. Peat was arguing that the benefits attributed to eating less food might be largely explained by consuming less of specific amino acids, and that the same benefits could be achieved without hunger or caloric deficit simply by shifting the amino acid profile of the diet.

In the [[2010-12-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-radiation|2010 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on radiation]], he extended the point to [[methionine]]: "Experiments with animals show they live 30% to 40% longer when they are almost completely lacking methionine. Similar effects are seen with tryptophan. These are the anti-metabolic amino acids." On *Generative Energy* episode 55, Georgi Dinkov asked whether there was any role physiologically needed for tryptophan aside from producing [[niacin]]. Peat answered: "A little bit for tissue replacement, making hair, skin, and gut cells that have to be shed. When you reduce any of those three amino acids to a minimum, life expectancy in animal experiments goes up tremendously."

## Tryptophan, Serotonin, and the Stress Cascade

The mechanism by which excess tryptophan causes harm runs primarily through its conversion to serotonin. In his [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|"Serotonin, Depression, and Aggression"]] newsletter, Peat described serotonin and its derivative [[melatonin]] as substances "involved in the biology of torpor and hibernation." Serotonin inhibits [[mitochondria|mitochondrial]] respiration, lowers temperature by decreasing the metabolic rate, and produces what Peat called the "excitotoxic death of nerve cells" through the combination of limited energy production and increased cellular activation.

The stress cascade that links tryptophan to serotonin is self-reinforcing. As Peat explained in the 2006 newsletter: "The fatigue produced by 'over-training' is probably produced by a tryptophan and serotonin overload, resulting from catabolism of muscle proteins and stress-induced increases in serotonin. Muscle catabolism also releases a large amount of cysteine, and cysteine, methionine, and tryptophan suppress [[thyroid]] function. Stress also liberates free fatty acids from storage, and these fatty acids increase the uptake of tryptophan into the brain, increasing the formation of serotonin. Since serotonin increases [[ACTH]] and [[cortisol]] secretion, the catabolic state tends to be self-perpetuating."

This self-perpetuating quality is what makes the tryptophan-serotonin system so dangerous in Peat's framework. Once initiated by stress, it drives further stress hormone production, further muscle breakdown, further tryptophan release, and further serotonin formation. The process does not correct itself. It accelerates.

## Estrogen and PUFA: The Amplifiers

Two substances in Peat's framework dramatically amplify the tryptophan-to-serotonin conversion: [[estrogen]] and [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]].

In a [[2011-08-25-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-serotonin-and-endotoxin|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on serotonin and endotoxin]], Peat laid out estrogen's double action with unusual precision: "Estrogen activates the enzyme that turns tryptophan into serotonin. It is inhibited by [[progesterone]]. The route out of serotonin, monoamine oxidase type A, is inhibited by estrogen, so it doesn't break down serotonin. So, the 'in' route is increased by estrogen and the 'out' route is decreased by estrogen." This two-pronged mechanism means that estrogen does not merely increase serotonin production; it simultaneously prevents the body from clearing the serotonin it produces. The result is accumulation.

The PUFA connection operates through a different mechanism but toward the same end. "The polyunsaturated fatty acids come in too," Peat continued in the same episode. "They are specifically effective at releasing tryptophan to enter the brain or other cells to produce more serotonin." In the 2006 newsletter, he described the molecular mechanism: PUFA "liberate tryptophan from serum albumin, increasing its uptake and the formation of serotonin in the brain. Saturated fats don't liberate serotonin." The serum albumin that normally binds tryptophan and prevents its excessive brain uptake releases its cargo when polyunsaturated fats are present in the bloodstream, creating a pathway from dietary fat composition to brain serotonin levels that bypasses any conscious intake of tryptophan itself.

The interaction between PUFA and serotonin forms a vicious cycle: "Serotonin liberates free fatty acids from the tissues, especially the polyunsaturated fats, and these in turn liberate serotonin from cells such as the platelets, and liberate tryptophan from serum albumin, increasing its uptake and the formation of serotonin in the brain." Stress, exercise, and darkness all increase free fatty acid release and therefore promote this cycle. "Increased serum [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] is specifically associated with serotonin-dependent disorders such as migraine," he noted.

## The Toxic Metabolites

Tryptophan's dangers extend beyond its conversion to serotonin. In a [[2011-01-18-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-inflammation|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on inflammation]], Peat described how omega-3 fatty acids divert tryptophan metabolism in a particularly harmful direction: "One of the events caused by the excess of n-3 in particular is that the metabolism of tryptophan, which should go largely to form niacin and melatonin, is diverted. This type of unsaturated fat causes tryptophan to be metabolized in a toxic direction, producing less niacin and more of the excitotoxic quinolinic acid and carcinogenic indole derivatives."

This distinction between the healthy and toxic metabolic fates of tryptophan runs throughout Peat's work. In a healthy organism with adequate [[vitamin-b6|vitamin B6]], tryptophan is converted primarily to niacin (vitamin B3). As Peat noted in his [[serotonin|email exchanges on serotonin]]: "B6 helps for turning tryptophan into niacin rather than serotonin." But estrogen, PUFA, and stress all push tryptophan metabolism away from niacin and toward serotonin and its toxic derivatives. The same molecule can follow radically different metabolic paths depending on the hormonal and nutritional environment.

In his [[protein|email exchanges on protein]], Peat offered a practical timeline for normalization: "I think it just takes a few hours, or a day, to normalize the tryptophan. Vitamin B6 helps to guide the metabolism of tryptophan away from excessive serotonin."

## Thyroid Suppression

Tryptophan's suppression of thyroid function was one of Peat's most consistent claims across decades of interviews. In a [[2008-09-11-politics-and-science-politics-science-thyroid-and-regeneration|2008 *Politics and Science* episode on thyroid and regeneration]], he listed the major antithyroid substances: "The goitrogens that you find in a lot of vegetables, grains, and nuts are antithyroid. The polyunsaturated fats are antithyroid. Tryptophan and cysteine, which you find in muscle meats at a very high concentration, are also antithyroid."

The thyroid-suppressive effect connects to the stress cascade. Cortisol, which rises during stress, "directly signals the liver," Peat explained. "In vitro experiments indicate that cortisol blocks the enzymes that convert thyroxine into the active T3 hormone. So it's doing many related things: increasing the circulating cysteine and tryptophan to suppress the gland itself and changing several levels of physiology." The tryptophan and cysteine released by cortisol-driven muscle breakdown suppress the thyroid, which lowers the metabolic rate, which increases vulnerability to further stress.

Peat offered a vivid practical illustration on *Generative Energy* episode 55: "Broda Barnes experimented eating a lot more meat in his diet. He found he had to double his thyroid dose. It's very easy to overdo the protein, displacing carbohydrate and liberating the thyroid-suppressing tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine." Barnes, the physician who spent a career documenting the epidemic of undiagnosed [[hypothyroidism]], discovered through self-experimentation that the amino acid composition of his diet directly affected how much thyroid hormone he required.

## Muscle Meat as a Refined Food

One of Peat's most provocative reframings was his characterization of lean muscle meat, the centerpiece of modern high-protein diets, as a nutritionally unbalanced food. "When only the muscle meats are eaten, the amino acid balance entering our blood stream is the same as that produced by extreme stress, when cortisol excess causes our muscles to be broken down to provide energy and material for repair," he wrote in the 2009 gelatin newsletter. The modern steak dinner, in other words, mimics the biochemical signature of a cortisol crisis.

In his email exchanges on protein, Peat contrasted this with traditional practice: "I've always been very sedentary, but I have usually had close to 150 grams daily. The traditional meat eaters didn't waste anything, ate all the skin, ears, tails, snouts, feet, tendons, lungs, intestines, marrow, blood, brains, gonads and other glands, picked the ligaments off the bones, so they had a much better balance of amino acids. Muscle meats are essentially a refined food."

The 2006 newsletter put numbers on the imbalance: "In the traditional diet, rather than just eating muscle meats, all the animal parts were used. Since collagen makes up about 50% of the protein in an animal, and is free of tryptophan, this means that people were getting about half as much tryptophan in proportion to other amino acids when they used foods such as 'head cheese,' ox-tails, and chicken feet." The shift from nose-to-tail eating to the exclusive consumption of tender steaks and chicken breasts roughly doubled the tryptophan load in the modern diet relative to the ancestral one.

## Gelatin: The Antidote

Gelatin occupies the complementary position to tryptophan in Peat's framework. Where tryptophan promotes inflammation, serotonin production, and thyroid suppression, gelatin counteracts all three. "It happens that gelatin is a protein which contains no tryptophan, and only small amounts of cysteine, methionine, and histidine," he wrote in the 2009 newsletter. "Using gelatin as a major dietary protein is an easy way to restrict the amino acids that are associated with many of the problems of aging."

The protective substance in gelatin is primarily [[glycine]], which makes up about 32% of gelatin's amino acid units. "The simplest, nonessential, amino acid, glycine, has been found to protect against carcinogenesis, inflammation, fibrosis, neurological damage, shock, asthma, and hypertension," Peat wrote in the 2006 newsletter. The symmetry with serotonin's effects is nearly perfect: "Serotonin excess produces a broad range of harmful effects: cancer, inflammation, fibrosis, neurological damage, shock, bronchoconstriction, and hypertension. The varied antiinflammatory and protective effects of glycine can be thought of as an antiserotonin action."

In the 2009 newsletter, Peat expanded on this therapeutic range: "A generous supply of glycine/gelatin, against a balanced background of amino acids, has a great variety of antistress actions. Glycine is recognized as an 'inhibitory' neurotransmitter, and promotes natural sleep." He connected this to specific disease states: "Since excess tryptophan is known to produce muscle pain, myositis, even muscular dystrophy, gelatin is an appropriate food for helping to correct those problems, simply because of its lack of tryptophan. All of the conditions involving excess [[prolactin]], serotonin, and cortisol (autism, postpartum and premenstrual problems, Cushing's disease, 'diabetes,' impotence, etc.) should benefit from reduced consumption of tryptophan."

## Practical Recommendations

Peat's dosage guidance for gelatin with meat was consistent across sources, though it evolved slightly. In the 2006 newsletter: "If a half-pound of steak is eaten, it would probably be reasonable to have about 20 g of gelatin at approximately the same time. Even a higher ratio of gelatin to muscle meat might be preferable." In the 2009 newsletter, he offered a somewhat lower figure: "If a person eats a large serving of meat, it's probably helpful to have 5 or 10 g of gelatin at approximately the same time, so that the amino acids enter the blood stream in balance."

The timing matters because the goal is to change the amino acid ratio entering the bloodstream from the intestine. If gelatin is consumed hours after the meat, the tryptophan from the meat will have already been absorbed without the glycine buffer.

Beyond gelatin, Peat identified several protective factors against excess tryptophan and serotonin: "[[Carbon-dioxide|Carbon dioxide]], high altitude, thyroid, progesterone, [[caffeine]], [[aspirin]], and decreased tryptophan consumption protect against excessive serotonin release. When sodium intake is restricted, there is a sharp increase in serotonin secretion." He also noted that [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]]'s medium-chain saturated fatty acids have antiserotonin actions, contrasting them with PUFA's serotonin-liberating effects.

## Protein Powders and Supplemental Tryptophan

Peat was unequivocal about the danger of supplemental tryptophan and processed protein powders. "There are some supplements that are worse than others," he said on the [[2011-01-18-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-inflammation|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on inflammation]]. "Probably the two most dangerous are the carotenes, because they are polyunsaturated, and tryptophan. Tryptophan and 5-HTP (hydroxy-tryptophan) activate the inflammation process very directly."

Regarding protein powders, he told Patrick Timpone on a [[2019-07-18-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-milk|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on milk]]: "Powdered proteins contain two or three amino acids that are very unstable. When they break down, they can produce inflammatory toxins. Cysteine and tryptophan are the worst for breaking down during processing. The only protein that doesn't have these unstable amino acids is gelatin." In his [[protein-powders|email exchanges on protein powders]], he elaborated: "Powdered foods that contain tryptophan are extremely susceptible to harmful oxidation, and the best things are removed, for example calcium, lactose, and casein, with its anti-stress properties."

The whey protein industry came in for particular criticism. From the 2006 newsletter: "Whey has been promoted as a protein supplement, but it contains a slightly higher proportion of tryptophan than [[milk]] does. Cheese (milk with the whey removed) contains less tryptophan." Whey isolate, marketed as the premium protein, is in Peat's framework the worst milk fraction. He also noted that "some people have been encouraged to eat only the whites of eggs, 'to avoid cholesterol,' but the egg albumin is rich in tryptophan." Both cases where mainstream advice inadvertently increases tryptophan exposure.

## The Aging Dimension

Peat placed tryptophan at the center of his theory of aging. "In old age, the amount of serotonin in the brain increases," he wrote in the 2006 newsletter. "This undoubtedly is closely related to the relative inability to turn off cortisol production that is characteristic of old age. Hypothyroidism increases the formation of serotonin, as does cortisol." The aging body produces more serotonin, which drives more cortisol, which drives more serotonin, in the same self-perpetuating cycle that acute stress initiates.

He pointed to an unusual biomarker: "In white hair, the amount of tryptophan is higher than in hair of any other color. Although serotonin and tryptophan are very important during rapid growth, their presence in senile tissues is probably closely associated with the processes of decline." Hair whitening, in this reading, is not merely a cosmetic change but a marker of the tryptophan accumulation that characterizes the aging process.

The cumulative argument linked tryptophan to the other central villains in Peat's framework: "I think these facts imply that excessive tryptophan, estrogen, and polyunsaturated fats contribute significantly, maybe decisively, to the degenerative changes that occur in aging. Experiments have separately shown that reducing dietary tryptophan or unsaturated fats can extend the healthy lifespan, and several antiestrogenic interventions can slow age-related changes and delay degenerative diseases. Since these factors interact, each tending to promote the others, it would be reasonable to expect greater results when several of the problems are corrected at the same time."

## Serotonin's Downstream Destruction

The 2006 newsletter cataloged serotonin's effects on nearly every organ system. "Serotonin's contribution to high blood pressure is well established," Peat wrote. "It activates the adrenal cortex both directly and through activation of the pituitary. It stimulates the production of both cortisol and [[aldosterone]]." On cell growth: "Serotonin stimulates cell division and tends to increase the formation of connective tissue, so its formation should be closely regulated once full growth is achieved. It contributes to the age- or stress-related thickening of blood vessels, and other fibrotic processes that impair organ function."

He cited Hans Selye's 1965 demonstration that injecting serotonin caused muscular dystrophy, and noted that "subsequent studies suggest that serotonin excess is involved in both muscular and nervous dystrophy or degeneration." People who advocate tryptophan supplementation for [[fibromyalgia]] are, in Peat's view, administering the very substance responsible for the condition: "The evidence increasingly suggests that excessive serotonin, interfering with muscle mitochondria, is a major factor in the development of that syndrome."

When Patrick Timpone stated on a [[2019-10-15-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-a-plethora-of-wide-randing-questions|2019 *One Radio Network* episode]], "I thought we needed tryptophan to go to sleep," Peat's response was blunt: "No, not really. Like histamine, too much serotonin keeps you awake." Glycine from gelatin, not serotonin from tryptophan, was what he recommended for promoting natural sleep.

## The Broader Framework

Peat's position on tryptophan cannot be understood in isolation. It forms one point in a triangle with estrogen and PUFA, the three substances he considered most responsible for degenerative aging. Each promotes the others: estrogen increases serotonin production from tryptophan; PUFA liberates tryptophan from binding proteins; serotonin promotes estrogen production through its effects on the pituitary and adrenals. As Peat wrote in the 2006 newsletter: "Since these factors interact, each tending to promote the others, and also interact with exogenous toxins, excess iron accumulation, and other stressors, it would be reasonable to expect greater results when several of the problems are corrected at the same time."
