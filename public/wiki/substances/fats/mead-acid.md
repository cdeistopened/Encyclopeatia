# Mead Acid

Mead acid is the fat the body makes when the polyunsaturated fats are gone. Named after the biochemist James Mead, this omega-9 fatty acid (5,8,11-eicosatrienoic acid, abbreviated ETrA) is synthesized endogenously from [[oleic-acid|oleic acid]] whenever dietary [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] and other so-called essential fatty acids are absent or low. In mainstream biochemistry, its presence in the blood is treated as a clinical alarm: a marker of "essential fatty acid deficiency." In Ray Peat's framework, its presence is the opposite. It is a sign that the body has restored one of its most important protective systems. "If we didn't eat linoleic acid and the other so-called 'essential fatty acids,' we would produce large amounts of the 'Mead acid,' n-9 eicosatrienoic acid, and its derivatives," Peat wrote in [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|"Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer" (2006)]]. "This acid in itself is antiinflammatory, and its derivatives have a variety of antistress actions." Wherever conventional nutrition sees a deficiency to correct, Peat saw a defense to cultivate.

## The Inversion of Deficiency

The standard medical interpretation of Mead acid rests on a simple syllogism: PUFA are essential; when PUFA are absent, the body makes Mead acid instead; therefore Mead acid signals a dangerous nutritional deficiency. Peat spent decades dismantling every step of this argument, and nowhere more pointedly than in his [[membranes-plasma-membranes-and-surfaces|"Membranes, Plasma Membranes, and Surfaces" (2009)]] newsletter:

> Now, instead of demonstrating harm from a dietary lack of the "essential" fats, the presence of the Mead acid or omega-9 fatty acids is taken as evidence of a deficiency. Our cells (and animal cells) produce these unsaturated fats when their special desaturase enzymes are not suppressed by the presence of exogenous linoleic or linolenic acids. Normally, the inactivation of an enzyme system and the suppression of a natural biological process might be taken as evidence of toxicity of the vegetable oils, but here, the occurrence of the natural process is taken as evidence of a deficiency. To me, this seems very much like the "disease" of having tonsils, an appendix, or a foreskin--if it is there, you have a problem, according to the aggressive surgical mentality.

The analogy with unnecessary surgery was not ornamental. Peat was identifying a persistent pattern in institutional medicine: treating endogenous, adaptive processes as pathology, then intervening with substances (in this case [[seed-oils]] and [[fish-oil|fish oil]]) that suppress the natural process. The cure for the "disease" of Mead acid production is feeding the patient the very PUFA that Peat considered toxic.

In [[suitable-fats-unsuitable-fats-issues-in-nutrition|"Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats" (2007)]], he turned the logic inside out: "Ordinarily, in biochemistry and physiology the inhibition of an enzyme is taken as a suggestion of toxicity, but when the point of reference is the idea of the goodness of PUFA, the activity of an intrinsic enzyme is taken to be evidence of harm, and its inhibition (by PUFA) is taken to be the proper, healthful situation." The delta-5 and delta-6 desaturases that convert oleic acid into the Mead series are normal biosynthetic machinery. Their suppression by linoleic acid is the real deficiency state, not their activity. If Peat is right, the entire "essential fatty acid" paradigm is inverted: the condition labeled deficiency is health, and the condition labeled sufficiency is chronic suppression of a protective system.

## Biosynthesis: From Sugar to Shield

The pathway that produces Mead acid begins, remarkably, with sugar. Peat described the sequence in a [[2010-11-18-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-science-behind-the-dangers-of-polyunsaturated-fats|2010 *EastWest Healing* episode on polyunsaturated fats]]: "When we synthesize the highly unsaturated fats ourselves from eating either sugar or protein, the longer unsaturated fats are called Mead acid. Sugars are able to produce energy, making it unnecessary to convert protein to energy." And on a [[2019-01-24-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fats-and-questions|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on fats]], he traced the chain more precisely: "If we eat only sugar for calories, we synthesize monounsaturated fat--oleic acid, the main fat of olive oil. From oleic acid, we can make Mead acid."

The human body, given glucose and no PUFA, constructs its own antiinflammatory fatty acid system from scratch. No dietary fat is required. The entire chain is endogenous.

In a [[2012-05-12-source-nutritional-show-source-nutritional-show-brain-and-tissue-l|2012 *Source Nutritional Show* episode on brain and tissue]], Peat described the suppression dynamic: "Those fats happen to inhibit the enzymes which make our natural n-9 polyunsaturated fats, such as the Mead acid. It's called an 'essential fatty acid deficiency' when you can find the Mead acid (n-9) in the blood. But it happens that that is a protective, anti-inflammatory fat that gives great resistance to the animals that are so-called 'deficient' in the essential fatty acids."

The threshold for suppression is startlingly low. In his newsletter [[sugar-issues|"Sugar Issues" (2012)]], Peat cited early data: "Although 0.6% of calories as polyunsaturated fat prevents the appearance of the Mead acid (which is considered to indicate a deficiency of essential fats) the 'high fructose' diets consistently add 10% or more corn oil or other highly unsaturated fat to the diet." A trace amount of PUFA shuts down the entire Mead acid system. The modern diet does not merely inhibit it; it buries it under a hundred-fold excess of the suppressing agent.

## The Fetus and the Newborn

Peat's most vivid evidence for Mead acid's protective role came from the beginning of life. Newborn mammals, he argued, represent the closest thing in nature to a Mead acid-dominant state. In [[regeneration-and-degeneration-types-of-inflammation-change-with-aging|"Regeneration and Degeneration" (2012)]], he wrote: "Since the fetus is able to synthesize fat from glucose, the newborn animal usually contains a high proportion of saturated fats and their derivatives, such as stearic acid, oleic acid, and Mead acid, which can be synthesized from glucose or amino acids." Even in human newborns, whose fatty acid profiles reflect their mothers' diets more closely than those of ruminant calves, "Mead acid is still present in human newborns."

The placenta plays a critical filtering role. In a [[2022-08-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-lipofuscin|2022 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on lipofuscin]], Peat explained: "Even when the mother is eating fish, grains, and unsaturated fats, babies demonstrate at birth that they are making some of the anti-inflammatory, natural Mead acid. This shows they were assimilating very little of the PUFA from the environment." The placenta, in Peat's reading, functions as a PUFA barrier, protecting the developing brain from the mother's dietary errors and allowing the fetus to build its tissues from the endogenous omega-9 series rather than the environmentally derived omega-3 and omega-6 fats.

In the same newsletter, Peat cited a study of prenatal habituation: "the experimenters found that the relative absence of the supposedly essential fatty acids improved the short term and long term memory of the fetus. The size of the baby was found to be negatively associated with the highly unsaturated fatty acids DHA and AA, showing a general growth-retarding effect of these environmentally derived fats."

This evidence struck at the heart of the fish oil marketing aimed at pregnant women. In a [[2019-02-19-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-thyroid-pufas-oj-and-sugar|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on thyroid and PUFAs]], Peat confronted the claim directly: "One of the selling points for fish oil was that PUFAs are essential for making membranes. They say our brains are 60% fish oil. But if you look at a newborn baby, their brains are defined as 'essential fatty acid deficient' because they contain Mead acids. If you want a newborn, energetic, able-to-learn brain, you don't want it loaded with fish oil."

In a [[2019-07-22-butter-living-podcast-butter-living-podcast-a-casual-conversation-with-ray-peat|2019 *Butter Living Podcast* conversation]], he extended this into a critique of infant formula: "Doctors have been taught to define this as a deficiency of soy/safflower oil fatty acids. When they recognize an ideal situation, they call it a serious nutritional deficiency and start advocating formulas with added algae oil or fish oil." The medical profession, confronted with a newborn brain in its most energetic state, diagnoses a deficiency and prescribes the fats that will degrade it over the next fifty years.

## Antiinflammatory Properties

Peat described Mead acid's antiinflammatory action as qualitatively different from the supposed antiinflammatory effects of omega-3 fatty acids. Fish oil suppresses [[inflammation]] by suppressing the immune system. Mead acid limits inflammation by modulating it, allowing the repair process to proceed without amplification into chronic tissue damage.

The distinction was clearest in his discussion of wound healing. In a [[2016-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidant-theory-and-the-continued-war-on-cancer|2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on the antioxidant theory]], Peat drew the contrast: "The derivatives of linoleic and [[arachidonic-acid|arachidonic acid]] are amplifiers of the little bit of inflammation that should trigger replication and repair. If you had only the Mead acid derivatives, they would take care of limiting the inflammation response. As it is with our present environmental foods, the [[prostaglandins]] amplify the inflammation, derange the healing process, and make scar tissue, keloids, or cancers."

This is a pivotal passage. It means that inflammation in the presence of PUFA-derived prostaglandins is not normal inflammation. It is distorted inflammation, magnified beyond its purpose, driven past repair into fibrosis and malignancy. Mead acid derivatives, by contrast, keep the signal proportional to the injury. They are the thermostat for a system that PUFA-derived eicosanoids turn into a runaway furnace.

The stability of the omega-9 series compounds the advantage. On the 2019 One Radio Network episode about fats, Peat noted that "an Australian researcher found that Omega-9 fats are powerfully anti-inflammatory and don't break down or oxidize easily. They concentrate in the brain and provide good stability." The same molecular features that make PUFA dangerous, their susceptibility to oxidation and free radical chain reactions, are absent from the Mead acid series. It provides its antiinflammatory protection without generating the [[lipid-peroxidation|lipid peroxidation]] products that accompany every use of fish oil or seed oil.

Beyond inflammation, Mead acid derivatives act on the nervous system directly. In the "Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer" newsletter, Peat noted that the fetus "can produce the Mead acid, ETrA, and its derivatives, which are antiinflammatory, and some of which act on the 'bliss receptor,' or the cannabinoid receptor." This places Mead acid derivatives in the same signaling family as the endocannabinoids, the body's internal analgesic and mood-regulating molecules. If dietary PUFA suppresses Mead acid production, it also suppresses a natural source of endocannabinoid-like signaling. Peat positioned the Mead acid family as serving not just antiinflammatory but broadly "antistress" functions.

## Cartilage: A Protected Reservoir

One of the most striking details in Peat's case involves cartilage. As he wrote in the "Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer" newsletter: "In the adult, tissues such as cartilage, which are protected by their structure or composition from the entry of exogenous fats, contain the Mead acid despite the presence of linoleic acid in the blood." Cartilage is avascular. PUFA circulating in the blood cannot reach it efficiently, so it retains its endogenous fatty acid profile even in adults eating a standard diet.

In [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity" (2009)]], Peat connected cartilage's Mead acid content to the broader benefits of gelatin consumption: "Cartilage contains 'Mead acid,' which is itself antiinflammatory." The presence of Mead acid in a tissue renowned for its resilience is not incidental. It is a demonstration of what the body's fatty acid profile would look like everywhere if dietary PUFA were removed.

## Aspirin as Surrogate

Peat's most provocative claim about Mead acid may be that [[aspirin]] and related compounds act as chemical stand-ins for it. In the "Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer" newsletter, he proposed:

> If salicylic acid and the structurally similar antiinflammatories, local anesthetics, muscle relaxants, expectorants, and antihistamines, act as surrogates for the absent Mead acid family, and thereby act as defenses against all the toxic effects of the unstable fats, it would explain the breadth and apparent coherence of their usefulness.

The hypothesis is structural. Aspirin and salicylic acid compete with PUFA for binding sites on [[COX-2|COX enzymes]] and other proteins. "The competition between aspirin and salicylic acid, and other antiinflammatories, for the active site on the COX enzyme, shows that the structural features of these molecules are in some ways analogous to those of the polyunsaturated fatty acids," Peat wrote. Aspirin fits into the same molecular slots that Mead acid would occupy if it were being produced in sufficient quantities.

This reframes aspirin's pharmacology entirely. It is not merely blocking an enzyme or two. It is substituting for an entire lipid signaling system that the modern diet has silenced. The breadth of aspirin's benefits, spanning cancer, heart disease, neurodegeneration, and fibrosis, ceases to be mysterious once you understand it as replacing a compound whose own protective range was equally broad. And it explains why aspirin outperforms the targeted COX-2 inhibitors: those drugs occupy a single site on a single enzyme, while aspirin, like the Mead acid family it replaces, occupies binding sites "in mitochondria, chromosomes, cytoskeleton, collagen networks--essentially everywhere in and around the cell."

## The n-9 Horizon

Peat regarded the Mead acid system as largely unexplored territory. In [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids" (2008)]], he wrote: "Real elimination of the degenerative diseases will require an exploration of the effects of the entire series of lipid signalling substances derived from the saturated and omega minus 9 fatty acids." And in "Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats," he identified the reason this exploration has not happened: "The Mead acid happens to have some valuable anti-inflammatory effects, and is associated with many biological advantages, but research in that direction is prevented by the lack of funding."

The funding problem is structural. The seed oil, fish oil, and pharmaceutical industries have no incentive to fund research demonstrating that the body's own fats, produced from sugar and protein, provide better protection than their products. The third-party research that does exist supports Peat's reading. Leslie Cleland and colleagues at the Royal Adelaide Hospital demonstrated in the 1990s that dietary n-9 eicosatrienoic acid inhibits leukotriene B4 synthesis in rats and modifies plasma lipid composition in ways consistent with reduced inflammation. Soh et al. showed beneficial effects of n-9 eicosatrienoic acid on experimental bowel lesions. These studies, cited in Peat's newsletters, remain isolated findings rather than the foundation of a research field because no commercial interest exists to build on them.

## Practical Implications

Peat never claimed that restoring Mead acid production was quick. The suppression threshold is so low (0.6% of calories as PUFA) that even small amounts shut the system down, and the body stores years of accumulated PUFA in its tissues, releasing it under stress even after dietary intake stops.

Minimizing PUFA intake is the necessary first step: eliminating seed oils, choosing low-PUFA fats like [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], [[butter|butter]], and [[tallow]], and favoring tropical fruits over grains. Over time, as stored PUFA is depleted, the desaturase enzymes that produce the n-9 series become less suppressed. Aspirin serves as a bridge, occupying the same protective niches that Mead acid would fill while the body works through its PUFA backlog. [[Vitamin E|Vitamin E]] slows the oxidative breakdown of whatever PUFA remains.

Supporting the biosynthetic pathway also matters. Adequate [[sugar]] intake (from fruit, juice, honey) provides glucose for de novo lipogenesis. Adequate [[thyroid]] function keeps the desaturases active. [[Progesterone]] and the other protective steroids ([[pregnenolone]], [[DHEA]]) support the hormonal environment in which these pathways run efficiently.

The endpoint would be the metabolic state of a healthy newborn: tissues dominated by saturated and omega-9 fats, inflammation limited by Mead acid derivatives rather than amplified by prostaglandins. Peat's conclusion in the "Membranes" newsletter was characteristically plain: "I think the 'problem' is simply that they allow us to live at a higher energy level, with greater resistance to stress, better immunity, and quicker healing."
