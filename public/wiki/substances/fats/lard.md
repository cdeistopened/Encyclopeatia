# Lard

The word "lard" still conjures images of wholesome farmstead cooking -- a grandmother's pie crust, a cast-iron skillet's seasoning. But the fat inside that jar has been quietly replaced at the molecular level. Modern lard is, for all practical purposes, liquid corn oil in solid form.

Lard -- rendered pig fat -- occupies a cautionary position in Ray Peat's nutritional framework. Unlike [[tallow]] from ruminant animals, whose multi-chambered stomachs destroy dietary [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] before they reach tissue, lard faithfully reflects whatever the pig consumed. And for the past half-century, that has meant corn and soy. The result is an animal fat with a polyunsaturated content that approaches the very [[seed-oils|seed oils]] health-conscious consumers are trying to escape.

## The Monogastric Problem

The difference between a cow and a pig, from the standpoint of fat quality, comes down to stomach architecture. Cattle are ruminants: their specialized digestive system biohydrogenates polyunsaturated plant fats into saturated forms before those fats ever reach adipose tissue. Pigs are monogastric -- single-stomached omnivores that absorb and deposit dietary fatty acids with minimal transformation.

Peat was blunt about what this means. In a [[2010-12-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-radiation|2010 KMUD episode on radiation]], he warned listeners to "avoid pork and non-ruminants because those reflect whatever they ate and are usually highly unsaturated and anti-thyroid." The phrase "anti-thyroid" is key: polyunsaturated fats interfere with [[thyroid]] function at every level -- blocking hormone release from the gland, inhibiting transport in the blood, and impairing cellular reception. A fat high in [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] is not merely neutral; it actively suppresses the metabolic rate.

Sarah Murray reinforced this point in a [[2012-08-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hot-flashes-night-sweats-the-relationship-to-stress-aging-pm|2012 KMUD discussion]]: "And avoid pork, chicken, and turkey fat. Grass-fed beef and lamb are better." The grouping is telling -- pork, chicken, and turkey are all monogastric animals whose fat composition is dictated by feed. Beef and lamb, as ruminants, are exempt from this vulnerability.

## What Modern Lard Actually Contains

Modern commodity lard from confined feeding operations runs approximately 40% saturated, 45% monounsaturated, and 11% polyunsaturated -- with linoleic acid being the dominant PUFA. Compare this to [[tallow]], which holds its polyunsaturated content to roughly 4%, or [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], which contains less than 2%. Lard's 11% PUFA may sound modest in absolute terms, but in a Peattian framework where even small amounts of polyunsaturated fat accumulate in tissues over time, it represents a meaningful and ongoing source of the very molecule Peat identified as metabolically toxic.

The 11% figure also understates the practical concern. Lard is often used generously -- for deep frying, baking, and as a general cooking fat. A tablespoon of lard delivers more linoleic acid than a tablespoon of [[butter]] by a wide margin. Over weeks and months of regular use, this accumulation matters. As Peat noted in a [[2014-10-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-longevity|2014 discussion on longevity]], "our muscles quickly oxidize the saturated fats" while the polyunsaturated fats get stored -- meaning the body selectively retains the problematic component.

## The Historical Lard Was Different

It is worth acknowledging that traditional lard was not the same product. Pigs foraging on acorns, chestnuts, whey, and kitchen scraps produced fat substantially lower in linoleic acid and higher in monounsaturated [[oleic-acid|oleic acid]] and [[stearic-acid|stearic acid]]. The iberico pigs of Spain, raised on acorn-rich dehesa pastures, still produce fat closer to this historical profile -- predominantly monounsaturated with relatively low PUFA.

The transformation happened when industrial agriculture shifted pig feed to cheap corn and soybean meal. Corn oil is approximately 55% linoleic acid; soybean oil is roughly 50%. Pigs eating these feeds deposit those fatty acids directly into their tissues, and the lard rendered from those tissues carries the same high-PUFA signature. The name stayed the same, but the chemistry inverted.

This distinction matters for anyone evaluating ancestral diet claims about lard. Historical lard from traditionally raised pigs was a reasonable cooking fat -- not as protective as ruminant fats, but not actively harmful either. Modern commodity lard is a different substance wearing the same label.

## Peat's Practical Approach to Pork Fat

Peat did not avoid pork entirely, but he took specific measures to minimize PUFA exposure. He described his approach to bacon in correspondence: "I fry the bacon to remove some of the fat, and then refry it in coconut oil, to remove most of the PUFA." This double-cooking technique -- rendering out the pig's own fat and replacing it with a safe saturated fat -- illustrates just how seriously Peat took the monogastric fat problem. He enjoyed the protein and flavor of pork but treated its fat as a contaminant to be removed rather than consumed.

This approach also highlights a broader principle in Peat's dietary recommendations: the goal is not zero PUFA (which is impossible) but systematic reduction from every available source. Replacing lard with [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], [[butter]], or [[tallow]] in cooking is one of the highest-impact substitutions available, precisely because cooking fat is consumed in volume and heated to temperatures that accelerate [[lipid-peroxidation|lipid peroxidation]] of whatever unsaturated bonds are present.

## The Awkward Middle Ground

Lard occupies an uncomfortable position in dietary discourse. Advocates of traditional foods celebrate it as a wholesome alternative to industrial seed oils. Proponents of low-fat diets avoid it because it is animal fat. In Peat's framework, both camps miss the point. Lard is better than soybean oil or canola -- its lower total PUFA and its monounsaturated content make it less aggressively anti-metabolic. But it is substantially worse than butter, tallow, or coconut oil, all of which hold polyunsaturated content below 5%.

For those following Peat's principles, the safest approach is to treat modern commodity lard as a moderate PUFA source and avoid it as a primary cooking fat. If lard is used, it should come from verified pasture-raised sources with documented low-PUFA feed -- and even then, understood as a compromise rather than an ideal. The true Peattian cooking fats are those whose saturation is guaranteed either by ruminant biology or by the intrinsic chemistry of the plant itself.
