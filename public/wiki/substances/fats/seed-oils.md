# Seed Oils

If Ray Peat's dietary framework has a central villain, it is seed oils. Canola, soybean, corn, sunflower, safflower, cottonseed---these industrially extracted fats represent, in Peat's analysis, the single most destructive change to the modern diet. As his long-time collaborator Andrew Murray summarized in a [[2008-08-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-metabolism-and-coconut-oil|2008 episode on thyroid and coconut oil]], "Dr. Ray Peat advises us to get acquainted with pasture-fed butter, lard, tallow, and coconut oil. Throw away the canola, corn, sunflower, safflower, sesame, and soy oils." This advice runs directly counter to decades of mainstream nutritional guidance---and Peat spent his career explaining exactly why the mainstream got it wrong.

## From Industrial Waste to Cooking Oil

The seed oils now dominating American kitchens were never intended as food. Their entry into the diet is a story of industrial opportunism. Cottonseed oil---a byproduct of the cotton industry---was originally used for candles, soap, and machinery lubrication. As Peat explained in a [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 discussion on stress and trauma]], "The cottonseed industry was a major power behind that... They found that they could squeeze oil out of the cottonseed, and by chemically hardening it, they could sell it as artificial butter."

That chemically hardened cottonseed oil became Crisco, introduced by Procter & Gamble in 1911---the first hydrogenated seed oil marketed for cooking. The product succeeded not because it was healthful but because it was cheap. Soybean oil, corn oil, canola oil, safflower oil, and sunflower oil followed over the subsequent decades, each riding the same economic logic: industrial waste streams could be repackaged as premium food products if the marketing was compelling enough.

The paint and plastics industry accelerated this shift. When petroleum-based solvents replaced seed oils and [[fish-oil]] in the production of paints and varnishes, farmers suddenly lost their primary market. Rather than let the crop go unsold, the industry pivoted to food---rebranding these oils as "vegetable oils" and positioning them as modern, heart-healthy alternatives to traditional [[saturated-fat]].

## The PUFA Problem

What makes seed oils harmful is their [[PUFA]] content. All of the major seed oils---corn, soy, canola, safflower, sunflower, cottonseed---are high in polyunsaturated fatty acids, primarily [[linoleic acid]], an omega-6 fat with two vulnerable double bonds in its carbon chain. These double bonds are sites where oxygen can attack, making the molecule chemically reactive in ways that prove damaging inside the warm environment of the human body.

Peat catalogued the full roster across multiple interviews. In a [[2012-06-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cellular-repair|2012 episode on cellular repair]], he listed "all of the things you see widely advertised: corn oil, canola, soybean oil, safflower oil, sunflower oil, walnut oil," with his co-host Sarah adding "sesame oil, cottonseed oil" to round out the collection. In a [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 episode on sugar]], Sarah described these as "powerfully thyroid-toxic oils"---a characterization Peat endorsed throughout his career.

The biological consequences of consuming these fats are extensive. Once absorbed, polyunsaturated fatty acids suppress [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]] at multiple levels: blocking hormone release from the gland, inhibiting transport through the bloodstream, and impairing action at the cellular receptor. They promote [[inflammation]] by converting into prostaglandins and other inflammatory mediators. They accumulate in tissues---brain, liver, adipose---where their ongoing [[oxidative-stress|oxidation]] generates toxic aldehydes and contributes to [[lipofuscin|age pigment]] formation. And they embed so deeply in cell membranes that complete clearance takes an estimated four years of strict avoidance.

## Cholesterol: The Misleading "Benefit"

The seed oil industry's most powerful marketing tool was the discovery that these oils lower blood [[cholesterol]]. This single finding---presented as proof of cardiovascular benefit---drove decades of dietary guidelines urging Americans to replace butter with margarine and lard with corn oil. But Peat saw the cholesterol-lowering effect as evidence of harm, not health.

As he stated in an interview on cholesterol with East West Healing, "the only biological effect established for the seed oils, other than sterility and dementia, was that they lowered blood cholesterol." The mechanism behind the reduction is not improved cardiovascular function but rather thyroid suppression and cellular stress. Cholesterol is a precursor to [[progesterone]], [[vitamin-d]], and other protective steroids; lowering it by poisoning the metabolic machinery that produces it is, in Peat's framework, equivalent to calling a fever "cured" because the patient has died.

The cholesterol campaign worked because it aligned with existing fears. Ancel Keys's lipid hypothesis---the idea that dietary fat causes heart disease through elevated cholesterol---gave the seed oil industry a scientific veneer for what was fundamentally an economic project. Industry-funded researchers like David Kritchevsky produced studies showing these oils lowered cholesterol temporarily, while ignoring their effects on [[cancer]], [[inflammation]], immune suppression, and metabolic dysfunction.

## Thyroid Suppression and the Obesity Epidemic

The connection between seed oil consumption and thyroid suppression offers one of the clearest explanations for the modern obesity epidemic. The same property that made seed oils profitable for the livestock industry---their ability to fatten animals cheaply by slowing [[metabolic-rate|metabolic rate]]---operates identically in humans. Pig farmers discovered decades ago that feeding polyunsaturated fats to their animals produced rapid weight gain on less feed. The animals' metabolisms simply slowed, converting more food into fat storage.

Peat argued that the parallel rise of seed oil consumption and obesity rates in the United States is not coincidental. When thyroid function is chronically suppressed by dietary PUFA, the body's capacity to oxidize fuel diminishes. Cells shift toward inefficient [[lactic-acid-production|lactic acid production]] rather than full [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]]. Energy that should be dissipated as heat is instead deposited as fat. The person feels tired, cold, and hungry---not because they lack willpower but because their metabolic engine has been chemically throttled.

## The Contamination Problem

Perhaps the most practical challenge Peat identified is the near-impossibility of avoiding seed oils in the modern food environment. Restaurants cook with them because they are cheap. Processed foods contain them as default ingredients. Even foods marketed as "healthy"---salad dressings, granola bars, plant-based alternatives---are typically saturated with soybean or canola oil.

This contamination extends beyond obvious sources. Modern poultry and pork are fed corn and soy, making their fat significantly higher in PUFA than the animals of previous generations. Only ruminant animals---cattle, sheep, goats---reliably produce [[saturated-fat]] regardless of their feed, because their digestive systems hydrogenate dietary fats before absorption.

Peat's practical recommendation was straightforward: cook with [[coconut-oil]], butter from pasture-fed cows, or beef tallow. Avoid eating out when possible. Read ingredient labels carefully. Accept that modern food systems make some PUFA exposure inevitable, and focus on keeping total intake below the threshold of approximately four grams per day---the level above which [[cancer]] risk begins to rise measurably.

## The Long Road to Clearance

Because PUFA integrates into cell membranes and adipose tissue, simply stopping seed oil consumption does not produce immediate results. Peat estimated the average fat molecule in the body is four years old, meaning a complete dietary changeover requires roughly that long to fully reflect in tissue composition. During this transition, any stress that triggers [[lipolysis]]---fasting, intense exercise, emotional distress---releases stored PUFA back into circulation, temporarily reintroducing the very toxins the person is trying to eliminate.

This explains why Peat's dietary advice emphasized frequent eating and adequate sugar intake alongside PUFA avoidance. Keeping blood sugar stable suppresses the [[stress]] hormones that mobilize stored fat. [[Coconut oil]] at meals provides immediate metabolic fuel that counteracts the anti-thyroid effect of whatever PUFA is released. [[Vitamin E]] and [[aspirin]] offer additional protection against PUFA oxidation during the years-long detoxification process.
