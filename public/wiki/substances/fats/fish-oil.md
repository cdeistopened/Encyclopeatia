# Fish Oil

Ray Peat considered fish oil more dangerous than the [[seed-oils|seed oils]] he spent decades warning against. While the mainstream narrative positions omega-3 supplements as among the safest and most broadly beneficial substances a person can take, Peat argued that [[PUFA|DHA and EPA]] are the most unstable fatty acids in common use, that their celebrated "anti-inflammatory" effect is produced by their own toxic breakdown products, and that the entire omega-3 marketing campaign was manufactured by an industry that lost its original market when petroleum replaced fish oil in paints and varnishes. "In declaring EPA and DHA to be safe, the FDA neglected to evaluate their antithyroid, immunosuppressive, lipid peroxidative, light sensitizing, and antimitochondrial effects, their depression of glucose oxidation, and their contribution to metastatic cancer, lipofuscinosis and liver damage, among other problems," he wrote in his newsletter [[the-great-fish-oil-experiment|"The Great Fish Oil Experiment" (2007)]]. For a substance taken daily by tens of millions of people, this is a sweeping indictment, and Peat supported it with evidence spanning industrial history, immunology, oncology, and neuroscience.

## The Industrial Origin of the Omega-3 Narrative

Peat's critique of fish oil begins not in a laboratory but in a factory. Fish oil was used for centuries as varnish, lamp fuel, and fertilizer. Its tendency to react rapidly with oxygen and harden into a polymer made it an excellent drying oil for paints. When petroleum-derived compounds displaced fish and seed oils from the paint industry in the 1950s and 1960s, the producers needed a new market.

In a [[2008-07-21-politics-and-science-politics-science-fats|2008 *Politics and Science* episode on fats]], Peat traced the sequence: "Around 1950, the petroleum chemists found a new use for petroleum other than fuel: making paints and plastics. Where fish oil and linseed oil had been the major basis for making paints and varnishes, now those markets were lost. The linseed oil industry looked for new ways to sell their products. That was when the idea of oil as an 'essential nutrient' started being promoted."

The rebranding required a pivot in scientific messaging. By the 1970s, [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] (omega-6) was being recognized as a cause of heart disease and cancer. The industry's response was not to abandon the essential fatty acid doctrine but to shift its center of gravity. As Peat explained in a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on diabetes and saturated fats]]: "Since they had sold the public on the idea of essential fatty acids, they just changed the story. They said, 'Well, fish oils or linseed oil are a *different* kind of fat. They aren't the Omega-6 oils like the deadly linoleic acid; they're the Omega-3.' But those had already been incriminated with yellow fat disease and [[lipofuscin]]."

In his newsletter [[sales-pitch-for-linseed-oil|"Sales Pitch for Linseed Oil" (1989)]], Peat described the economics plainly: "U.S. consumption of seed oils had been almost doubling every decade since the first world war, but the technological advances of the 1960s which allowed paints to be made from petroleum derivatives, rather than from linseed oil, safflower and soy oil, stimulated the redirection of large amounts of these substances from paint production into the food market. Clever marketing tricks are in some cases creating price mark-ups of 10000%."

The industry's influence extended to editorial boards and journal policy. Peat noted a peculiar literary device in published research: "Around the end of the century, we begin to see a strange literary device appearing, in which research reports on the toxic effects of omega-3 oils are prefaced by remarks to the effect that 'we all know how great these oils are for good health.' I think I detect groveling and shuffling of the feet by authors who want to get their work published."

## Anti-Inflammation as Immunosuppression

The central claim of the fish oil industry is that omega-3 fatty acids reduce inflammation. Peat's response was not to deny the observation but to reframe it. The anti-inflammatory effect is real, he argued, but it is produced by the same mechanism that makes X-ray therapy "work" for arthritis: destruction of the immune cells that produce the inflammatory response.

In a [[2010-11-18-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-science-behind-the-dangers-of-polyunsaturated-fats|2010 *EastWest Healing* episode on polyunsaturated fats]], Peat laid out the sequence: "When you suppress the cells that produce inflammation, you get an interruption of an existing inflammatory process. However, researchers have found that it is only the oxidized, broken-down form of the fish oil that has this effect. When you prolong the feeding, you start seeing that immunodepression is the long-range result."

In the same interview, he compared it directly to X-ray therapy: "The present advocacy of fish oil for its anti-inflammatory effect is very similar to what the X-ray industry was doing 80 years ago. They treated arthritis, acne, and psoriasis with X-rays because the immediate effect is to create lipid peroxides, which are anti-inflammatory immediately, but immunosuppressive and toxic in the long run." In "The Great Fish Oil Experiment," he predicted that if the mechanisms are the same, "we can expect to see another epidemic of fibrotic conditions and cancer in about 15 to 20 years."

The immunosuppressive potency of EPA specifically was documented in research Peat cited in his newsletter [[suitable-fats-unsuitable-fats-issues-in-nutrition|"Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats"]]: "The proteolytic enzymes are an essential part of innate immunity, and the highly unsaturated fatty acid, EPA, which is the most immunosuppressive of the fats, strongly inhibits proteolysis in some cells." In "The Great Fish Oil Experiment," he cited a striking figure: "The cytotoxicity caused by EPA and its metabolites (15 mg of EPA per liter killed over 90% of a certain type of macrophage) isn't inhibited by [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]]."

The immunosuppression is not a side effect that the medical establishment has overlooked. It is, in specific contexts, the product. Fish oil emulsions are marketed for organ transplant patients to prevent graft rejection. "The deadly effect of the long-chain unsaturated fats on the immune system has led to the development of new products containing short and medium-chain saturated fats for intravenous feeding," Peat noted in "Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats." The same substance sold to healthy people as a daily supplement is sold to transplant patients as an immunosuppressive drug.

## Oxidation Before Absorption

DHA and EPA are among the most highly unsaturated fatty acids found in food. The omega-3 double bonds, positioned far from the acidic end of the molecule, make them exceptionally reactive with oxygen. In a [[2008-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-and-polyunsaturated-fatty-acids|2008 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and polyunsaturated fatty acids]], Peat described what this means inside the body: "Fish oil is so unstable that most of it doesn't survive to reach your bloodstream, or it would inhibit your [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]]. It breaks down into other compounds which are actually toxic. The first thing you see affected is the immune system. The breakdown products of the spontaneously oxidizing fish oil include acrolein, which is a carcinogen, and ethane."

In the same year's *Politics and Science* interview, he stated it more starkly: "In your body, it's mostly oxidized before it reaches your bloodstream. Those so-called beneficial anti-inflammatory effects are actually produced by the oxidative breakdown products of the fats."

The practical demonstration is simple. In a [[2008-09-18-eluv-eluv-good-fats|2008 ELUV interview on fats]], Peat described a kitchen experiment: "Anyone can do a simple experiment: put a cork in a bottle of unsaturated vegetable oil with a small rubber tube leading into a glass of water. At room temperature, the oil acts like it's respiring. It burns oxygen, breaks down into a varnish-like material, and sucks water up the tube. Fish oil is even worse than standard seed oils."

In "The Great Fish Oil Experiment," he provided the quantitative context: "Without antioxidants, fish oil is massively degraded within 48 h, and even with a huge amount of antioxidant there is still considerable degradation." And in the newsletter [[oils-in-context|"Oils in Context"]], he noted that vitamin E supplementation cannot solve the problem: "According to Draper, et al., enrichment of the tissues with highly unsaturated fatty acids results in an increase in lipid peroxidation *in vivo* even in the presence of normal concentrations of vitamin E."

## The Eskimo Data Revisited

The popular case for fish oil rests on observations of Greenland Inuit, reported to have low rates of heart disease despite a marine-fat-heavy diet. In the 2008 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and PUFA, Peat offered a direct counter: "That basically comes from a study of Greenland people where they claimed there was very little heart disease. But a more recent study of Alaska Eskimos showed that the ones who eat a lot of fish have no less heart disease than the ones who don't." He added a telling correlation: "A study in Canada found that if you look at the amount of Omega-3 fats in their tissues, the pollution, such as PCBs and mercury, exactly corresponds to the amount of fish oil."

In the 2009 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and oils, a caller added historical context that Peat found consistent with his view: "Farley Mowat, author of *People of the Deer*, noted that the Inuit avoided fish. The Canadian government gave them nets, but they wouldn't use them because they knew if they ate just those cold-water fish, they would die."

Large-scale studies also failed to confirm the fish oil hypothesis. Peat cited two in "The Great Fish Oil Experiment": "In 2004, a study of 29,133 men reported that the use of omega-3 oil or consumption of fish didn't decrease depression or suicide, and in 2001, a study of 42,612 men and women reported that after more than 9 years the use of cod liver oil showed no protective effect against coronary heart disease."

## Cancer: From Cod Liver Oil to DHA

In [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]], Peat cited a foundational finding: "In the late 1940s, experimenters used it as the main fat in dogs' diet, and found that they all died from cancer, while the dogs on a standard diet had only a 5% cancer mortality." In [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic"]], he restated the figure: cod liver oil "increased their death rate from cancer by 20 times, from the usual 5 to 100%."

The mechanisms are multiple. In "The Great Fish Oil Experiment," Peat noted: "[[natural-killer-cells|Natural killer cells]], which eliminate cancer cells and virus infected cells, are decreased after eating fish oil, and T suppressor cells are often increased." The suppression of tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-1 persists after supplementation stops. In a [[2012-11-23-world-puja-world-puja-foundational-hormones|2012 *World Puja* episode on foundational hormones]], he was blunt: "There are many studies people aren't told about where fish oil increases metastatic cancer and has serious immunosuppressive effects."

Peat also identified a paradox in the marketing. In "Fats and Degeneration," he noted that researchers would "test these fats in tissue culture dishes, and demonstrate that they were poisonous, cytotoxic, to the fast growing cancer cells. Although they caused cancer in animals, if they could be shown to kill cancer cells in a dish, they could be sold as anticancer drugs/nutrients." The in-vitro cytotoxicity and the in-vivo carcinogenicity coexist: both are consequences of the same oxidative instability.

## The Brain: From "Brain Food" to Neurotoxin

The claim that DHA is essential for brain development rests on the brain's high concentration of polyunsaturated fats. Peat inverted this reasoning. In "Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats," he wrote: "The concentration of polyunsaturated fats in the brain has led many people to say that the 'nutritionally essential fatty acids,' especially the omega-3 fatty acids, are essential for brain development. The types of argument they use simply ignore the real evidence: Cells can multiply indefinitely in culture dishes without the essential fatty acids, insects can multiply for generations on diets without the unsaturated fats, forming normal synapses and brains, and mammals fed diets with extremely small amounts of the unsaturated fats grow with perfectly normal, possibly superior, brains."

The prenatal evidence was particularly striking. In the 2012 *World Puja* interview, Peat described a study that inverted the conventional wisdom: "Just recently, a prenatal study compared the intelligence of the fetus with the amount of fish oil in the mother's diet. They found that the only things corresponding to better memory and learning were the *absence* of the so-called essential fatty acids and long-chain fish oils."

In a [[2022-08-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-lipofuscin|2022 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on lipofuscin]], Peat elaborated on the animal data: "Those studies were done over the last 70 or 80 years in rats, dogs, and other animals. They showed that in proportion to the amount of PUFA in the diet, their brains are reduced in size and learning ability after birth. The 'deficient' animals without the PUFA have bigger brains, are higher metabolizers, and learn much faster. Some studies in the 70s suggested the average rodent deficient in PUFA had the equivalent of a 140 IQ compared to its EPA-drugged siblings."

The placenta, Peat argued, provides biological evidence of the body's own assessment of these fats. In the same lipofuscin episode, he noted: "Even when the mother is eating fish, grains, and unsaturated fats, babies demonstrate at birth that they are making some of the anti-inflammatory, natural [[mead-acid|Mead acid]]. This shows they were assimilating very little of the PUFA from the environment." Andrew Murray, his co-host, summarized the implication: formula companies, "defining the newborn baby as 'essential fatty acid deficient,'" had argued for adding fish oil extract to infant formula. "Within a few days of reaching the grocery store, these fats are degrading, oxidizing, and becoming toxic. The evidence suggests that what you're doing is feeding the baby rancid fish oil products."

On the other end of the lifespan, DHA's breakdown products accumulate in the aging brain. In the 2012 *World Puja* interview, Peat connected the two endpoints: "I avoid them because of the toxic effects. In the last few years, breakdown products called neuroprostanes have been found in the brain, highly associated with [[dementia|Alzheimer's]] dementia. Their origin can be traced directly to the DHA and EPA of fish oils. So, looking at the prenatal effect and the Alzheimer's effect, both ends incriminate fish oil as a toxin."

In "The Great Fish Oil Experiment," Peat described the neurotoxic cascade: DHA "facilitates excitatory (glutamate) nerve transmission," and its breakdown product acrolein "inhibits mitochondrial function by poisoning the crucial respiratory enzyme, cytochrome oxidase." In "Fats and Degeneration," he shared a personal observation: "About ten years ago I met a young man with a degenerative brain disease, and was interested in the fact that he (working on a fishing boat) had been eating almost a pound of salmon per day for several years."

## Thyroid Suppression and Metabolic Interference

Peat's framework places thyroid function at the center of metabolic health. Fish oil opposes thyroid at multiple levels. In the 2008 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and PUFA, he stated the connection directly: fish oil is "so unstable that most of it doesn't survive to reach your bloodstream, or it would inhibit your thyroid function."

Andrew Murray, in a [[2015-02-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-uses-of-urea|2015 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on urea]], added the specific term: fish oils "are also specifically thyrotoxic (toxic to the thyroid)."

The antithyroid effect connects to the broader metabolic suppression. In "Fats and Degeneration," Peat described the foundational observation: the "essential fatty acid deficient" rats in George Burr's original experiments "were metabolizing 50% faster than rats that were given linoleic and linolenic acids as part of their diet." Fish oils, being more unsaturated than seed oils, produce this metabolic suppression at least as powerfully.

In "The Great Fish Oil Experiment," Peat connected thyroid and immune suppression through [[cortisol]]: "The polyunsaturated fatty acids linoleate, arachidonate, and eicosapentaenoic, are especially toxic to thymic cells by preventing their inactivation of cortisol, increasing its action." The thymic cells that fish oil destroys are the same T cells the immune system needs to fight infections and eliminate cancer.

## Yellow Fat Disease and the Suppressed History

Before the omega-3 marketing campaign began, the toxicity of fish oil to warm-blooded animals was well documented under the name "steatitis" or "yellow fat disease." In his newsletter "Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats," Peat provided the timeline: "By the 1940s, the polyunsaturated oils, including fish oils, were known to cause deterioration of the brain, muscles, and gonads in a variety of animals, and this was found to be caused mainly by their destruction of vitamin E."

In the 2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on diabetes and saturated fats, he described the agricultural context: "In the 1950s, they were feeding a lot of fish to mink in farms, and they were producing what was called 'yellow fat disease,' which was related to the age pigment lipofuscin, a brown pigment that develops from the breakdown of the polyunsaturated fats." In the 2022 lipofuscin episode, he broadened the scope: "Early studies feeding fish to pigs, horses, dogs, and mink identified 'yellow fat disease,' in which the animal's fat became a source of dangerous inflammation."

The formation of lipofuscin in proportion to PUFA consumption was documented as early as 1967. Peat cited the finding in "Fats and Degeneration": Hartroft and Porta observed in *Present Knowledge in Nutrition* that "the 'age pigment,' lipofuscin, was formed in proportion to the amount of polyunsaturated fat and oxidants in the diet."

## Liver Disease

In "Fats and Degeneration," Peat wrote: "Although publicity has emphasized the anti-inflammatory effects of fish oil, experiments show that it is extremely effective in producing alcohol-related liver cirrhosis." The critical finding: "In the absence of polyunsaturated fats, alcohol doesn't produce cirrhosis." Peat cited research showing that "a diet enriched in saturated fatty acids effectively reverses alcohol-induced necrosis, inflammation, and fibrosis despite continued alcohol consumption." From the 2008 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and PUFA: a researcher named Nanji "tried that on his patients and found that their liver condition improved if he gave them a lot of sugar and saturated fat, and got worse if he fed them fish oils or seed oils."

The widely cited claim that fish oil lowers blood triglycerides is, in Peat's reading, itself a sign of liver toxicity: "The triglycerides in the blood are decreased because of the fish oil's toxic effects on the liver."

## Reproductive Toxicity and the Sperm Count Experiment

In "Oils in Context," Peat recounted a self-experiment: "An oil researcher spent 100 days eating what he considered to be the 'Eskimo diet,' seal blubber and mackerel paste. He observed that his blood lipid peroxides (measured as malondialdehyde, MDA) reached a level 50 times higher than normal, and although MDA is teratogenic, he said he wasn't worried about fathering deformed children, because his sperm count had gone to zero." The reproductive toxicity of PUFA was among the earliest effects documented in the animal literature, where fish oils were shown to cause "deterioration of the brain, muscles, and gonads."

## Even Fish Suffer from Fish Oil

In the 2008 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and PUFA, Peat described a finding that undercuts the entire premise: "Even the salmon on a pure fish oil diet had less endurance than when they were getting chicken fat or a less polyunsaturated fat. Fish oil isn't even so great for fish."

In a [[2019-01-24-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fats-and-questions|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on fats]], Adam Bergstrom offered a vivid comparison: "A salmon is high in DHA and is lucky if it lasts nine years. An Orange Roughy is the lowest fish in DHA and lives to 149 years." Peat responded: "I've heard up to 300 years. When they feed fish fish oil, they lose endurance and age quickly."

## Two Illusions: Longevity and Deficiency

An early argument for fish oil was a set of animal experiments from around 1970 showing increased longevity. Peat, citing Alex Comfort, identified the confound: the animals simply ate less because the food was repulsive. "The animals were very reluctant to eat the food containing smelly fish oil, and were eating so little food that their longevity could be accounted for by their reduced caloric intake." Caloric restriction masquerading as a nutrient effect.

The second illusion involves tissue levels. Research often finds low levels of EPA and DHA in diseased tissues and interprets this as a deficiency. Peat read the data in the opposite direction: "Those oils are extremely susceptible to oxidation, so they tend to spontaneously disappear in response to tissue injury, cellular excitation, the increased energy demands of stress, exposure to toxins or ionizing radiation, or even exposure to light. Their 'deficiency' in the tissues frequently corresponds to the intensity of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation; it is usually their presence, rather than their deficiency, that created the disposition for the disease." The low tissue PUFA is a consequence of oxidative damage, not a cause of disease.

## Practical Position: Lean Fish, No Supplements

Peat did not recommend avoiding all fish. His practical advice, stated in "Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic," was concise: "Fish oils are usually highly unsaturated; 'dry' types of fish, and shellfish, used once or twice a week, are good. Avoid cod liver oil." The distinction is between lean, low-fat fish (which provides protein and minerals without large amounts of PUFA) and fatty fish or fish oil supplements (which deliver concentrated doses of the most unstable fats in the human diet).

The protective alternatives are the fats the body makes from sugar. In the 2009 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and oils, Peat described them: "If we eat only sugar for calories, we synthesize monounsaturated fat, oleic acid, the main fat of olive oil. From oleic acid, we can make Mead acid. An Australian researcher found that Omega-9 fats are powerfully anti-inflammatory and don't break down or oxidize easily." In "The Great Fish Oil Experiment," he summarized the alternative: "The fats that we synthesize from sugar, or [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], or oleic acid, the omega-9 series, are protective against the inflammatory PUFA, in some cases more effective even than vitamin E."
