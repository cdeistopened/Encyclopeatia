# PUFA (Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids)

The seed oils lining supermarket shelves represent one of the most significant dietary changes in human history---and in Ray Peat's analysis, one of the most damaging. While mainstream nutrition has spent decades promoting these polyunsaturated fats as "heart-healthy," Peat's work reveals them as potent suppressors of [[metabolism]], drivers of [[inflammation]], and accelerators of [[aging]]. The average American now consumes approximately 30 grams of PUFA daily, when the safe threshold appears to be under 4 grams. This mismatch, Peat argues, underlies much of the modern epidemic of degenerative disease.

## The "Essential" Fatty Acid Myth

The belief that humans require dietary polyunsaturated fats traces to experiments by George and Mildred Burr in the 1930s. Yet as Peat frequently explains, this research was fundamentally flawed. In a [[2008-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-and-polyunsaturated-fatty-acids|2008 discussion on thyroid and PUFA]], he noted that "people working on B vitamins showed that what the Burrs had created was a B vitamin deficiency by not feeding their animals a balanced diet."

The Burr experiments found that rats on fat-free diets developed skin problems and reproductive issues. But subsequent research demonstrated these symptoms resulted from deficiencies of B6 and zinc---not missing fatty acids. Crucially, "Burr himself demonstrated that animals without the unsaturated fats had an extremely high metabolic rate, about 50% higher than average." The polyunsaturated oils were simply suppressing metabolism so that rats needed fewer nutrients overall.

Human cells in culture can multiply indefinitely without any dietary PUFA, manufacturing their own fatty acids from glucose. When deprived of environmental linoleic and arachidonic acid, cells produce Mead acid---an omega-9 polyunsaturated fat with, as Peat describes it in [[2022-08-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-lipofuscin|a 2022 interview]], "a long saturated tail---just the opposite of fish oil." This internally synthesized fat is relatively resistant to oxidation, unlike the vulnerable omega-3 and omega-6 fats from food.

The placenta's selective filtering reveals biology's judgment on these fats. Even when mothers consume fish, grains, and seed oils heavily, "babies demonstrate at birth that they are making some of the anti-inflammatory, natural Mead acid," showing they absorbed minimal PUFA from their environment. The newborn brain contains very little DHA---the supposed "brain fat"---and this is not deficiency but protection.

## Dietary Sources: The Industrial Flood

PUFA entered the American food supply through an accident of industry. As Peat explained in his [[2008-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-and-polyunsaturated-fatty-acids|2008 thyroid episode]], "This really started with the paint and plastics industry learning how to make paint, varnish, and plastics out of petroleum. Previously, fish oil and seed oil---linseed oil in particular---had been the basis of paints." When petroleum chemists learned to make paint cheaply, farmers and the seed oil industry lost their market.

Their solution was rebranding industrial oils as food. Cottonseed oil---once used for candles and machinery---became "Crisco." Soybean oil, corn oil, safflower, canola, and sunflower oil followed, all marketed as modern, heart-healthy alternatives to the [[saturated fat]] of traditional diets. The transformation happened within a single generation.

The major dietary sources of PUFA include:

- **Seed oils**: Safflower, cottonseed, corn, soy, canola, sunflower, hemp, flaxseed
- **Fish oils**: Particularly the omega-3 supplements marketed for brain health
- **Poultry and pork fat**: Modern chickens and pigs are fed corn and soy, making their fat high in PUFA---unlike the saturated fat of ruminants
- **Nuts and seeds**: Especially when processed into oils or consumed in large quantities
- **Processed foods**: Nearly all contain seed oils as cheap ingredients

As Peat noted, "Safflower oil, cottonseed oil, corn oil, flax, and soy" top the list of problematic fats. "Canola isn't quite so bad, but it's still toxic. Sunflower is pretty bad." Olive oil, being predominantly monounsaturated, stands apart from this category.

## Why PUFA Is Harmful: The Oxidation Problem

Polyunsaturated fats have multiple double bonds in their carbon chains---sites where oxygen can attack. This makes them useful for paints (which need to harden through oxidation) but dangerous for warm-blooded animals. As Peat put it:

> "If you put these fats---which are designed to be sensitive to heat so they stay flexible---into a very hot animal (98 degrees Fahrenheit), they become extremely unstable."

The instability begins immediately upon absorption. In a [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-managing-hormones-and-cancer-treatment-with-nutrition|discussion on cancer and nutrition]], Peat described how "some of the oxidative breakdown is almost immediate. Just as soon as it gets absorbed from your intestine and shows up in your bloodstream, you can find the highly oxidized breakdown fragments of the polyunsaturated fats like fish oil."

This spontaneous oxidation generates a cascade of toxic compounds:

- **Aldehydes** like acrolein and malondialdehyde---known carcinogens and mutagens
- **Isoprostanes and neuroprostanes**---inflammation markers that accumulate in dementia
- **Free radicals** that damage DNA, proteins, and cell membranes

For the detailed chemistry of this breakdown process, see [[lipid peroxidation]].

The oxidation products of PUFA create the "age pigment" [[lipofuscin]]---the brown spots visible on aging skin that also accumulate invisibly in the brain, heart, and other organs. Studies feeding fish to pigs, horses, dogs, and mink identified "yellow fat disease"---a condition where the animals' fat became so oxidized it triggered dangerous inflammation throughout their bodies.

## Thyroid Suppression: The Metabolic Brake

PUFA's most insidious effect may be its comprehensive attack on [[thyroid]] function. Peat identified multiple points where polyunsaturated fats interfere:

"They antagonize the thyroid function at several levels," he explained in [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|a 2009 episode]]. "For example, they inhibit the proteolytic enzymes in the gland itself which are needed to form and secrete the hormone. They bind to the protein in the bloodstream that transports thyroid, preventing transport to the tissues. Finally, the polyunsaturated fats block several of the active sites in the cell where thyroid should bind to enliven the cell."

This explains why pig farmers discovered that feeding polyunsaturated fats to their animals caused rapid weight gain with less food---the fats suppressed metabolism so efficiently that the animals fattened cheaply. The same mechanism operates in humans, helping explain why the obesity epidemic parallels the rise of seed oil consumption.

The thyroid-blocking effect also explains the apparent benefits some people notice initially when eliminating PUFA. As tissues release their stored polyunsaturates, thyroid function gradually improves. "When you have eliminated most of the polyunsaturated fats in your tissues," Peat noted in [[2019-03-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-viruses|a 2019 interview]], "then your tissues are extremely sensitive to thyroid hormone. So your gland doesn't have to work very hard to keep you in a high metabolic condition."

For how PUFA blocks glucose oxidation specifically, see [[Randle cycle]].

## The Estrogen Connection

PUFA and [[estrogen]] form a mutually reinforcing cycle of metabolic damage. Peat's doctoral research at the University of Oregon established that "the effects of estrogen on biochemistry are in many ways exactly the same as those of ionizing radiation, and also parallel the effects of too much polyunsaturated fat in the diet." This synergy isn't coincidental---the mechanisms interlock.

Estrogen increases the accumulation of polyunsaturated fats in tissues and amplifies their activity. Simultaneously, PUFA increases estrogen's effects. As Peat explained in a [[2020-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vibrations-frequencies-part-2|2020 interview]], "It has continued to be confirmed that estrogen increases the accumulation of polyunsaturated fats in the body and increases their activity. Those fats simultaneously increase the effect of estrogen."

This explains why women---with higher estrogen levels---show greater susceptibility to certain degenerative diseases. "Very typically, women are more susceptible than men to several of the degenerative inflammatory diseases," Peat observed. The omega-3 fats particularly concentrate under estrogen's influence, creating the conditions for neurodegeneration.

Fat cells themselves produce estrogen through the aromatase enzyme, and polyunsaturated fats make this conversion more likely. "The polyunsaturated fats increase the deposition and storage of fat, and since they break down to form the inflammation-promoting prostaglandins, they make the fat cells more likely to produce excess estrogen." The result is a vicious cycle where PUFA increases body fat, and that fat produces more estrogen, which concentrates more PUFA.

For the inflammatory signaling cascade, see [[inflammation]].

## Storage and the Four-Year Elimination Timeline

Unlike water-soluble nutrients that wash through the body quickly, polyunsaturated fats embed themselves in tissues. This creates one of the most challenging aspects of dietary change.

"If you totally change your diet from saturated to unsaturated," Peat explained in [[2008-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-and-polyunsaturated-fatty-acids|2008]], "it takes about four years for the complete change to be reflected in your stores. So you can figure that the average fat molecule is four years old."

In a [[2017-09-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-proposition-65|2017 interview]], he provided more specific guidance: "In animal experiments, it takes about four years for a complete exchange when you stop eating PUFA. It takes about two years to get a 50% reduction, which is when you will start feeling your thyroid more consistently."

The storage creates an ongoing problem. Every time blood sugar falls---whether from skipping meals, exercise, or sleep---the body mobilizes stored fat for fuel. If that stored fat contains PUFA, the release reintroduces thyroid-blocking, inflammation-promoting compounds into circulation. This explains why people changing their diet often experience temporary setbacks: stress releases their stored toxins.

"The complete changeover takes about four years," Peat explained in [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011]]. "But if you eat frequently and thoroughly avoid stress-causing foods---don't let yourself get hungry enough to call on stress hormones to liberate fat---you can very quickly shift over to efficient metabolism. Frequent feeding, always with sugar and absolutely no PUFA, allows the slow disposition of the unsaturated toxic fats. The liver treats PUFA like other toxins; if it has the energy, it detoxifies them for excretion."

The brain presents special challenges. "By the time a person is 45, the body is really soaked in polyunsaturated fats," Peat noted. "They talk about the omega-3 fatty acids as being predominant in the brain, but that's only an old brain. A healthy young five-year-old brain is highly saturated in comparison to a middle-aged brain."

With aging, DHA and other polyunsaturated fats steadily accumulate in neural tissue. "If you look at the aging brain, the amount of PUFA steadily increases from the so-called deficient state at birth up to a heavy overload state in old age and dementia." This concentration interferes with cholesterol synthesis and drives the formation of the protein tangles seen in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease.

## Brain Degeneration and Protein Misfolding

The accumulation of PUFA in neural tissue has profound consequences. DHA---the omega-3 fat marketed for brain health---actually drives the protein misfolding seen in neurodegenerative diseases.

"DHA activates the excitotoxic process," Peat explained in [[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-genetics-x-environment|a 2012 episode]]. "It increases the glutamate excitatory system, increasing free glutamic acid in the brain fluid. In all these brain degenerative diseases, you can see increased breakdown products of the fish oil type of polyunsaturated fats in the cerebrospinal fluid and serum."

The mechanism connects directly to the "genetic" diseases often considered inherited:

"Unsaturated fatty acids cause misfolding of the prions in CJD and Mad Cow Disease. Radiation and polyunsaturated fatty acids are known to accelerate the misfolding of the protein, making it act like an infectious thing that spreads from cell to cell. In Parkinson's and Alzheimer's, similar folding of proteins causes the symptoms."

Saturated fats show the opposite effect. Test-tube experiments demonstrate that while DHA induces misfolding of the alpha-synuclein protein implicated in Parkinson's, saturated fatty acids block this misfolding. The infant brain---low in PUFA and high in saturated fats---maintains proper protein structure and supports the remarkable learning capacity of early life.

## The Safe Threshold: Under 4 Grams Daily

Given that complete avoidance of PUFA is nearly impossible in modern food environments, Peat identified a practical threshold. In [[2017-09-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-proposition-65|the 2017 interview]], he stated: "The inflection point where you see cancer correlating closely to the amount of PUFA in the diet is around 4 grams per day. You are safe if you keep it under that."

Achieving this requires significant dietary modification:

- **Eliminate seed oils**: No corn, soy, canola, safflower, sunflower, or cottonseed oil
- **Avoid fish oil supplements**: Despite marketing claims, these accelerate oxidative damage
- **Limit poultry and pork**: Their fat is high in PUFA from grain-fed practices
- **Choose dairy carefully**: Low-fat or skim milk reduces PUFA while preserving nutrition
- **Moderate egg consumption**: One or two eggs daily from grain-fed chickens; more from pastured birds
- **Use [[saturated fat]] for cooking**: [[coconut oil]], butter from grass-fed cows, beef tallow

The transition need not feel restrictive. "You can do this by eating low-fat or skim milk, fruit, low-fat cheese, and eggs," Peat suggested. The emphasis shifts from avoiding all fat to selecting the right fats.

## Protective Strategies

While elimination takes years, several strategies can mitigate ongoing damage:

**[[Vitamin E]]** protects against PUFA oxidation. Originally recognized as an anti-estrogen and fertility-extending compound, vitamin E was rebranded as "merely an antioxidant" when the seed oil industry found this positioning convenient. Both alpha and gamma tocopherols help oppose estrogen and protect cellular membranes.

**[[Aspirin]]** blocks the conversion of PUFA into inflammatory prostaglandins. Its protective effects against sunburn, radiation damage, and various degenerative diseases trace partly to this anti-PUFA action.

**Frequent feeding** prevents the stress-induced mobilization of stored fats. When blood sugar remains stable, the body has less need to release its PUFA stores into circulation. Sugar in the diet actually helps by suppressing lipolysis.

**[[Coconut oil]]** provides short and medium-chain saturated fats that the body can burn immediately, helping displace stored PUFA over time. Its metabolic-boosting effect supports thyroid function directly.

**[[Progesterone]]** opposes estrogen's concentration of PUFA in tissues and helps stabilize cells against oxidative damage.

## Historical Industry Manipulation

The promotion of polyunsaturated fats as healthy represents one of the more successful corporate campaigns to reshape dietary beliefs. In Mark Twain's time, as Peat noted, merchants were already discussing "how cheap cottonseed oil was and that they were going to teach Americans to eat that instead of butter."

The shift accelerated after World War II when the seed oil industry funded research showing these oils lowered serum cholesterol---a finding now understood to result from thyroid suppression and increased cellular stress rather than any health benefit. Researcher David Kritchevsky, funded by industry, performed studies that Peat describes as showing polyunsaturated oils lowered cholesterol "temporarily" while ignoring their other effects.

By the 1960s, researchers discovered that polyunsaturated fats prolong graft survival by suppressing the immune system. This immunosuppression---initially seen as useful for organ transplants---also explains PUFA's cancer-promoting effects and its false appearance of reducing inflammation. The oils don't resolve inflammation; they paralyze the immune response.

"The polyunsaturated fats work by exactly the same mechanism that X-rays do," Peat observed: "creating an immune deficiency and an inability to produce inflammation."
