# Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFA)

No category of substance occupies a more central position in Ray Peat's framework than the polyunsaturated fatty acids. They are the slow-moving but decisive lever underneath nearly every modern degenerative process he investigated: [[cancer]], [[hypothyroidism]], [[diabetes]], [[dementia]], [[fibrosis]], heart disease, autoimmunity, infertility, and the general phenomenon of aging itself. The argument is structural rather than rhetorical. PUFA, in Peat's analysis, is not merely an unhealthy nutrient that one ought to eat less of; it is a class of molecules whose physical properties (multiple double bonds, susceptibility to oxidation, capacity to displace thyroid hormone and vitamin A from their carrier proteins, ability to generate prostaglandins on demand) physically reorganize cellular respiration over the course of decades. The body that consumes seed oils for forty years becomes, at the level of mitochondrial cardiolipin, adipose stores, brain phospholipids, and blood-stream free fatty acids, a chemically different organism than the body that does not. "All systems of the body are harmed by an excess of these oils," Peat wrote in his newsletter [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic" (2007)]]. "There are three main kinds of damage: one, hormonal imbalances, two, damage to the immune system, and three, oxidative damage."

This article is the hub. The specific molecules and their applications live on their own pages — [[linoleic-acid]] for the dominant omega-6 dietary PUFA, [[fish-oil]] for the marine omega-3s, [[seed-oils]] for the industrial delivery vehicle, [[mead-acid]] for the protective omega-9 substitute the body makes in PUFA's absence, and [[age-pigment]] for the lipofuscin endpoint where PUFA damage becomes visible. What unifies them, and what gives Peat's framework its peculiar coherence, is the case for treating the entire class as a single problem with a single solution: stop adding it, accelerate its slow elimination, and substitute the saturated and monounsaturated fats the body actually thrives on.

## The Inversion at the Center of the Framework

Mainstream nutrition treats linoleic and linolenic acid as essential nutrients and treats the omega-3s (EPA and DHA) as protective. Peat held the opposite position across his entire career, and the inversion is not rhetorical hedging. He argued that the body synthesizes its own unsaturated fats from sugar and protein whenever dietary PUFA are absent ("we are able to synthesize our own unsaturated fats when we don't eat the 'EFA,' so they are not 'essential'" — [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic"]]), that the symptoms attributed to PUFA deficiency in the foundational 1929 Burr experiments turned out to be B-vitamin deficiencies, and that the actual properties of organisms raised without dietary PUFA are not the wasting, infertile, kidney-degenerated rats of textbook caricature but unusually robust animals with elevated metabolic rates, stable mitochondria, and remarkable resistance to stress, shock, infection, and cancer.

The animal-deficiency profile is one of Peat's most-repeated and most underappreciated set of observations. In [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids: Nutritionally Essential, or Toxic?" (2008)]] he listed what actually happens to animals on PUFA-free diets that are otherwise nutritionally complete: "Their metabolic rate is very high. Their nutritional needs are increased. They are very resistant to many of the common causes of sickness and death. They are resistant to the biochemical and cellular changes seen in aging, dementia, autoimmunity, and the main types of inflammation." In [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration" (2009)]] he made the same point with sharper edges: PUFA-deficient animals "consumed oxygen and calories at a very high rate, their mitochondria were unusually tough and stable, their tissues could be transplanted into other animals without provoking immunological rejection, and they were very hard to kill by trauma and a wide variety of toxins that easily provoke lethal shock in animals on the usual diet." If these are the properties of "deficient" animals, the doctrine of essentiality is upside down.

This is the inversion. The condition labeled deficiency is, in Peat's reading, the high-energy default state of mammalian biology; the condition labeled sufficiency is chronic exposure to a class of slow-acting tissue toxins that the seed oil and fish oil industries spent the second half of the twentieth century marketing as nutrients. The article that follows is the case for that inversion.

## A Brief History: Crisco, Cottonseed, and the Pivot

The seed oils that dominate the modern American diet did not enter it as food. Their origins are industrial. Cottonseed oil, the first large-volume vegetable seed oil sold for human consumption, was a byproduct of the cotton industry that had previously been used for lamp fuel, soap, and machinery lubrication. Procter & Gamble's chemical hydrogenation of cottonseed oil produced Crisco, marketed from 1911 as a butter substitute. The full history of the industrial pivot — paint and varnish markets collapsing when petroleum chemistry took over in the 1950s, the redirection of the crop into food supply, the manufacture of the "heart-protective oils" narrative — is treated in detail on the [[seed-oils]] page.

The pivot point Peat returned to most often was the 1940s discovery that polyunsaturated fats fatten livestock cheaply. In an [[2010-11-18-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-science-behind-the-dangers-of-polyunsaturated-fats|EastWest Healing interview on the science behind PUFA dangers (2010)]] he described the sequence: "The misconception started very quickly in the 1940s when agricultural chemists found that they could fatten their animals faster if they poisoned their metabolic systems with an antithyroid drug. They would get fat on very little food. However, that turned out to leave a carcinogenic residue in the meat. They then found that corn and soybeans contained these polyunsaturated fats that would suppress the respiration of the animals and make them get fat on very little food." The mechanism is identical in humans. Modern pork and poultry, raised on corn and soy, contain PUFA concentrations utterly unlike the animal fats of previous centuries — Peat estimated that "hogs fed soybeans can have fat containing over 30% linoleic acid."

The transition from animal feed to human food required reframing the substance as a nutrient. The Burr experiment, originally an obscure 1929 paper claiming linoleic acid prevented a particular dietary disease in rats, was retrieved from twenty years of irrelevance and pressed into service. As Peat described in the same EastWest interview: "In 1929, the Burrs (researchers working for the agricultural industry) claimed that linoleic acid and linolenic acid were essential nutrients. Previously, researchers had demonstrated that animals could live perfectly without those if they were given vitamin-rich, protein-rich food." The "Burr's disease" syndrome of dermatitis, sterility, growth failure, and kidney damage turned out, when Roger Williams' laboratory revisited it, to be a vitamin B6 deficiency that could be cured without adding any fat at all. By the time this was established, in [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]] Peat's words, "Around that time, the seed oil industry was in crisis because the use of those oils in paints and plastics was being displaced by new compounds made from petroleum. The industry needed new markets, and discovered ways to convince the public that seed oils were better than animal fats."

By the 1970s evidence of PUFA toxicity had become difficult to suppress, but rather than abandon the essential-fatty-acid doctrine the industry simply changed the molecules. Omega-3 fish oil, originally a paint and lamp oil whose petroleum displacement created an identical surplus, took up the marketing slot that linoleic acid had vacated. Peat traced this pivot in a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 KMUD interview on diabetes and saturated fats]]: "Since they had sold the public on the idea of essential fatty acids, they just changed the story. They said, 'Well, fish oils or linseed oil are a *different* kind of fat. They aren't the Omega-6 oils like the deadly linoleic acid; they're the Omega-3.' But those had already been incriminated with yellow fat disease and lipofuscin." The omega-3 pivot, in his reading, was not a course correction but a rebrand. Full treatment of fish oil's specific harms — its oxidation before absorption, its immunosuppressive effect through the destruction of natural killer cells, its connection to alcoholic liver cirrhosis, the neuroprostane breakdown products implicated in Alzheimer's disease, the Inuit data on which the original "anti-inflammatory" claim rested — lives on the [[fish-oil]] page.

## The Burr Inversion: What "Deficiency" Actually Produced

The 1929 Burr paper deserves a closer look because the inversion at its center clarifies the entire framework. George and Mildred Burr fed rats a diet they considered fat-free and observed dandruff, dermatitis, slowed growth, sterility, and fatal kidney degeneration. They attributed the syndrome to a deficiency of linoleic and linolenic acids and proposed adding the two compounds to nutritional reference works as essential nutrients on the model of vitamins. In [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids: Nutritionally Essential, or Toxic?"]], Peat noted what they did not understand: "In 1929, most of the B vitamins and essential trace minerals were unknown to nutritionists. The symptoms the Burrs saw are easily produced by deficiencies of the vitamins and minerals that they didn't know about."

Williams' laboratory at the Clayton Foundation Biochemical Institute at the University of Texas later reproduced the syndrome on the Burrs' exact diet and reversed it with B6 alone — no fat added. The "essential fatty acid deficiency" was an undiagnosed B-vitamin deficiency in a research design from a year when most of the B vitamins had not yet been identified.

The hidden detail in the Burr work is what they observed but did not interpret. As Peat described in [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]]: "A few years after the first experiments, Burr put one of his 'essential fatty acid deficient' rats under a bell jar to measure its metabolic rate, and found that the deficient animals were metabolizing 50% faster than rats that were given linoleic and linolenic acids as part of their diet. That was an important observation, but Burr didn't understand its implications. Later, many experiments showed that the polyunsaturated fats slowed metabolism by profoundly interfering with the function of the thyroid hormone and the cellular respiratory apparatus." A rat metabolizing 50% faster than a control rat is not, by most reasonable definitions, deficient in anything. The Burr finding, properly read, was a demonstration that removing linoleic acid restored metabolic rate to a level the standard PUFA-containing diet suppressed.

The endpoint of this rereading is cell culture. In a [[2022-08-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-lipofuscin|2022 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on lipofuscin]] Peat made the cleanest version of the case: "You can grow human cells in culture. If you are growing huge masses of cells, you know exactly what they are being fed. You can feed them a diet absolutely excluding unsaturated fatty acids. They multiply enormously on a diet of no environmental unsaturated fats. Obviously, any unsaturated fat they contain is something they have made themselves." Mammalian cells synthesize their own omega-9 unsaturated fats (see [[mead-acid]]) from glucose whenever their desaturase enzymes are not suppressed by exogenous linoleic acid. The very enzymatic suppression that orthodox medicine takes as evidence of dietary essentiality, Peat read as evidence of toxicity.

## The Structural Mechanism

PUFA harm operates through a small number of physical mechanisms that radiate into every body system. The four most important are (1) molecular instability and lipid peroxidation, (2) displacement of thyroid hormone from its carriers and inhibition of cellular respiration, (3) generation of inflammatory prostaglandins through the arachidonic acid cascade, and (4) activation of aromatase and amplification of estrogen's effects. Each operates through ordinary physical chemistry; together they produce the syndrome Peat called accelerated aging.

### Lipid peroxidation: the molecular instability

Saturated fats hold all the hydrogen atoms their carbon chains can hold. PUFA, by definition, do not — they have one or more pairs of carbons where the hydrogens have been removed and a double bond installed. The chemistry of those double bonds is the entire problem. As Peat wrote in [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic"]], "When an oil is saturated, that means that the molecule has all the hydrogen atoms it can hold. Unsaturation means that some hydrogen atoms have been removed, and this opens the structure of the molecule in a way that makes it susceptible to attack by free radicals. Free radicals are reactive molecular fragments that occur even in healthy cells, and can damage the cell. When unsaturated oils are exposed to free radicals they can create chain reactions of free radicals that spread the damage in the cell, and contribute to the cell's aging."

The temperature dimension matters more than is generally appreciated. Coconut oil, which has almost no double bonds, can sit on a countertop in the tropics for a year without becoming rancid; safflower or fish oil at the same temperature degrades within days. Body temperature is hotter still. Peat used a seed-germination analogy in the EastWest Healing interview: "If you think of where corn or soybeans normally grow, the seed sits on the ground through the winter. It is basically kept in refrigeration. When it warms up, it sprouts... However, if you kept the seed warm all winter at the temperature of a human body, these oils would react with oxygen and the seed would be dead by springtime. In your body at 98 degrees, that oxidation happens even faster than at room temperature." The unsaturation that lets a temperate seed remain mobile at refrigerator temperatures is, in a 98°F mammal, a chemistry experiment running continuously across every tissue compartment that stores those fats.

The breakdown products are catalogued in [[the-great-fish-oil-experiment|"The Great Fish Oil Experiment" (2007)]] and form the chemical bridge between PUFA and downstream disease: "acrolein, malondialdehyde, hydroxynonenal, crotonaldehyde, ethane, pentane, and the neuroprostanes, which are prostaglandin-like molecules formed from DHA by free radical lipid peroxidation products, especially in the brain and at a higher level in Alzheimer's disease." Acrolein, in particular, "inhibits mitochondrial function by poisoning the crucial respiratory enzyme, cytochrome oxidase, resulting in a decreased ability to produce energy. Besides inhibiting the ability of nerve cells to produce energy from the oxidation of glucose, acrolein inhibits the ability of cells to regulate the excitatory amino acid glutamate."

One of Peat's most cited reframings concerns the "advanced glycation end products" (AGEs) generally blamed on sugar. In [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids"]] he wrote: "The so-called 'advanced glycation end products,' that have been blamed on glucose excess, are mostly derived from the peroxidation of the 'essential fatty acids.' The name, 'glycation,' indicates the addition of sugar groups to proteins, such as occurs in diabetes and old age, but when tested in a controlled experiment, lipid peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids produces the protein damage about 23 times faster than the simple sugars do." The full mechanism of how peroxidation-derived aldehydes generate the protein cross-links and lipofuscin granules conventionally attributed to "free radical damage" lives on the [[age-pigment]] page, where the chemistry is treated in detail. The summary version is that lipofuscin, the brown granular pigment that accumulates in every aging tissue and is visible as "liver spots" on the hands of old people, is largely a product of PUFA peroxidation and would not exist in tissues that contained predominantly saturated fats. ([[lipid-peroxidation]] will hold the mechanism page once canonicalized.)

### Thyroid suppression: every level of the system

The interference with [[thyroid]] function is one of PUFA's most consequential effects because thyroid function gates oxidative metabolism. In [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic"]] Peat described the scope: "Their best understood effect is their interference with the function of the thyroid gland. Unsaturated oils block thyroid hormone secretion, its movement in the circulatory system, and the response of tissues to the hormone."

The mechanism repeats at every checkpoint. In a [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and PUFA]], Peat described the multi-level interference: "They antagonize the thyroid function at several levels. For example, they inhibit the proteolytic enzymes in the gland itself which are needed to form and secrete the hormone. They bind to the protein in the bloodstream that transports thyroid, preventing transport to the tissues. Finally, the polyunsaturated fats block several of the active sites in the cell where thyroid should bind to enliven the cell." Production, transport, reception: all three are throttled.

The displacement effect on transport proteins is dose-responsive in the most literal molecular sense. From [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids"]]: "A series of studies about 20 years ago showed that the functions of the thyroid hormone are all inhibited by unsaturated fats, with the inhibition increasing in proportion to the number of unsaturations (double bonds) in the fat molecule." Linoleic acid (two double bonds) is bad; arachidonic acid (four) is worse; EPA (five) and DHA (six) are worse still. The thyrotoxicity climbs with each additional unsaturation, which is why the omega-3 marketing campaign was, in Peat's reading, more dangerous than the omega-6 campaign it complemented.

The downstream effect is the suppression of oxidative metabolism. From the EastWest Healing interview: "Constantly, when we draw on stored unsaturated fats, all functions of our thyroid system are inhibited. We produce less hormone, transport it less effectively, and it enters the mitochondria less effectively. If you interrupt that supply of stored unsaturated fats, you immediately feel a surge of respiratory activity." The body that has been chronically PUFA-suppressed and is suddenly given a small dose of saturated fat (a half-ounce of coconut oil, in Peat's worked example) experiences a measurable rise in pulse, temperature, and respiratory rate within an hour and a half. The suppression had been continuous, and removing it for an hour produces a visible therapeutic effect.

The thyroid suppression also operates at the mitochondrial inner membrane through cardiolipin composition. From [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions" (2013)]]: "The crucial mitochondrial respiratory enzyme, cytochrome c oxidase, declines with aging, as the lipid cardiolipin declines, and the enzyme's activity can be restored to the level of young animals by adding cardiolipin. The composition of cardiolipin changes with aging, 'specifically an increase in highly unsaturated fatty acids.' Even supplementing old animals with hydrogenated peanut oil restores mitochondrial respiration to about 80% of normal." The aged mitochondrion is, at the level of its own membrane lipids, structurally PUFA-poisoned; saturating its cardiolipin restores its respiration. This is the connection point to [[oxidative-metabolism]] — PUFA is the lipid-level mechanism by which mitochondrial respiration is throttled across the lifespan.

### Prostaglandin generation and the arachidonic acid cascade

[[Arachidonic-acid|Arachidonic acid]] is a 20-carbon, four-double-bond omega-6 fat that the body synthesizes from dietary linoleic acid and stores in cell membrane phospholipids. The presence or absence of arachidonic acid in cellular phospholipids determines whether stress mobilizes inflammatory [[prostaglandins]] or whether it does not. The full role of arachidonic acid in linoleic acid metabolism lives on the [[linoleic-acid]] page; the relevant point for the hub article is that arachidonic acid is the substrate from which the inflammatory eicosanoids — prostaglandins, leukotrienes, thromboxanes — are made. In PUFA-deficient animals there is no arachidonic acid to mobilize, no prostaglandins to generate, no inflammatory amplification of ordinary tissue signaling. In PUFA-loaded humans the cascade runs constantly.

The Mead acid substitute system is the elegant counter-mechanism: when dietary PUFA are absent, the body's desaturase enzymes synthesize a different unsaturated fat (5,8,11-eicosatrienoic acid, the omega-9 analog of arachidonic acid) whose derivatives are antiinflammatory and act on the cannabinoid receptor. The full case for Mead acid as the protective substitute and aspirin as its chemical surrogate is treated on the [[mead-acid]] page. The point here is that the body has a built-in protective lipid signaling system that PUFA suppresses by occupying its biosynthetic precursor sites.

### Aromatase activation and estrogen amplification

PUFA and [[estrogen]] form a mutually reinforcing pair that figures prominently in Peat's accounts of cancer, fibrosis, and reproductive disease. The mechanism has two directions. PUFA-derived prostaglandins activate aromatase, the enzyme that converts androgens to estrogen, increasing estrogen production. Estrogen, in turn, activates the phospholipase that liberates arachidonic acid from cell membranes, feeding the prostaglandin cascade. Each amplifies the other.

In [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic"]] Peat put the connection plainly: "There are interesting interactions between these oils and estrogen. For example, puberty occurs at an earlier age if estrogen is high, or if these oils are more abundant in the diet. This is probably a factor in the development of cancer... Unsaturated fats intensify estrogen's harmful effects." The EastWest Healing interview added the bidirectionality: "Estrogen favors the storage of polyunsaturated fats, and these fats favor the influence of estrogen."

There is also a sex steroid binding globulin (SSBG) component. From [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]]: "While the competition by PUFA for protein binding sites blocks the effects of thyroid hormone and vitamin A, the action of PUFA on the sex steroid binding protein (SBP, or SSBG, for sex steroid binding globulin) increases the activity of estrogen. That's because the SSBG neutralizes estrogen by binding it, keeping it out of cells; free PUFA keep it from binding estrogen. People with low SSBG/estrogen ratio have an increased risk of cancer." The same physical displacement mechanism that throws thyroid hormone off its carrier protein throws estrogen off its binding globulin, leaving more free hormone to act on cells.

## The Randle Cycle and Energy Suppression

Beyond thyroid suppression at the membrane level, PUFA suppresses energy production at the substrate-competition level. The [[randle-cycle]] is the immediate, minutes-scale mechanism by which free fatty acids in the bloodstream block cellular glucose oxidation, forcing cells from efficient aerobic respiration (~36 ATP per glucose) into glycolytic fermentation (~2 ATP, producing lactic acid).

In a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on energy production and diabetes]], Peat described the cycle: "The essence of diabetes was pointed out by Randle in 1963 when he observed that if you increase the free fatty acids in the blood, you very quickly make the cells unable to use glucose... There are several points where free fatty acids activate adrenaline, ACTH, cortisol, TSH, and glucagon — all of which increase the release of free fatty acids from your fat storage. It seems illogical for the body to create those vicious circles where an energy failure turns on exactly what caused it. But it turns out that it's only the *polyunsaturated* fatty acids that have those terrible anti-energy effects."

The key clinical contrast: "If you compare stearic acid (butter/beef fat) and linoleic acid (corn oil), the butter turns off adrenaline and cortisol, while the corn oil turns them on." Saturated fats damp the stress response; PUFA amplifies it. In the EastWest Healing interview Peat made the link to the [[randle-cycle]] explicit: "The immediate effect of the presence of unsaturated fats is to block the oxidation of glucose. This is called the Randle Cycle. It immediately impairs the ability to oxidize glucose, leading to diabetes-like issues and oxidative damage to the mitochondria." Full treatment of the substrate-competition mechanism — how injected soy oil produces hyperglycemia within 15 minutes, how diabetes is fundamentally a PUFA disease misdiagnosed as a sugar disease — lives on the [[randle-cycle]] page.

The vicious-circle dimension is what makes PUFA storage particularly insidious. From [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]]: "When we don't eat for many hours, our glycogen stores decrease, and adrenaline secretion is increased, liberating more glucose as long as glycogen is available, but also liberating fatty acids from the fatty tissues. When the diet has chronically contained more polyunsaturated fats than can be oxidized immediately or detoxified by the liver, the fat stores will contain a disproportionate amount of them... In good health, especially in children, the stress hormones are produced only in the amount needed, because of negative feedback from the free saturated fatty acids... But when the fat stores contain mainly PUFA, the free fatty acids in the serum will be mostly linoleic acid and arachidonic acid... These PUFA stimulate the stress hormones, ACTH, cortisol, adrenaline, glucagon, and prolactin, which increase lipolysis, producing more fatty acids in a vicious circle. In the relative absence of PUFA, the stress reaction is self limiting, but under the influence of PUFA, the stress response becomes self-amplifying."

A self-limiting stress response in a PUFA-deficient animal and a self-amplifying stress response in a PUFA-loaded animal: this single difference, repeated billions of times across cellular events over decades, accounts for much of what the framework treats as "accelerated aging."

## The 600-Day Half-Life: Why It Takes Four Years

Of all the practical consequences of PUFA's role in the body, the storage half-life is the most operationally important. Adipose tissue does not turn over quickly. The fat you eat today becomes the fat you carry for years, and the fat in your tissues is the fat that gets mobilized into your bloodstream every time you stress, exercise, fast, get cold, or sleep poorly.

The canonical number comes from [[oils-in-context|"Oils in Context" (2007)]]: "The half-life of fats in human adipose tissue is about 600 days, meaning that significant amounts of previously consumed oils will still be present up to four years after they have been removed from the diet. According to Draper, et al., enrichment of the tissues with highly unsaturated fatty acids results in an increase in lipid peroxidation *in vivo* even in the presence of normal concentrations of vitamin E. Fasting for more than 24 hours also results in an increase in MDA excretion, implying that lipolysis is associated with peroxidation of the fatty acids released."

The four-year figure is not metaphorical. From a [[2008-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-and-polyunsaturated-fatty-acids|2008 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on thyroid and PUFA]]: "If you totally change your diet from saturated to unsaturated, it takes about four years for the complete change to be reflected in your stores. So you can figure that the average fat molecule is four years old. If you've ever left your cooking oil bottle out of the fridge, you've noticed it gets sticky. That hardening, oxidizing process would be much faster at body temperature."

There is a release asymmetry that makes the situation worse than a straight four-year decay implies. From [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]]: "Fat cells preferentially oxidize saturated fats for their own energy, and the greater water solubility of the PUFA causes them to be preferentially released into the bloodstream during stress." The fat cells burn the saturated fat they store and dump the unsaturated fat into circulation. Over time, the storage ratio shifts continuously toward more unsaturated tissue: every stress event preferentially removes saturated fat from stores and leaves PUFA behind. The 50-year-old's adipose tissue is more saturated with linoleic acid than the 25-year-old's, even on identical diets.

The brain dimension is particularly worrying. From a [[2019-01-24-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fats-and-questions|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on fats and questions]]: "Up to the age of 20, the body is diluting the PUFA in the diet, so the brain isn't seriously impaired. When you stop growing in size and volume, the brain and other tissues begin increasing their concentration of PUFA. From 20 to the mid-40s, the brain becomes more polyunsaturated and more at risk of stress. When you're under stress, you release stored PUFA into the bloodstream where they can become oxidized and toxic. Some of it reaches your brain and is stored for years. Every night your brain is renewing itself, but unfortunately, during the night, free fatty acids in the blood rise. By the time you're 40, the brain finds polyunsaturated fatty acids in the bloodstream that add to the damage."

The four-year horizon, the daily nocturnal release of stored PUFA into the bloodstream, and the preferential retention of unsaturated over saturated fat in adipose tissue are the structural reasons that depletion is a multi-year project rather than a months-long one. The dedicated [[pufa-depletion]] protocol page treats the practical sequence in operational detail; the hub-level point is that the half-life is the timeline on which PUFA's harm and its undoing both operate. There is no faster path.

## The Aging Mechanism

The accumulation dynamics dovetail with several other Peat themes to produce what amounts to a unified PUFA-centered theory of aging. From [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]]: "As the fat tissues become more burdened with arachidonic acid, they release it more easily in response to moderately lipolytic stress signals. This could explain the increased levels of free fatty acids and lipid peroxidation that occur with aging. In animals that are 'deficient' in the polyunsaturated fatty acids, adrenalin doesn't have the lipolytic effect that it does in animals on the standard diet. With aging, there is not only a tendency to have chronically higher free fatty acids in the blood, but for those fatty acids to be more unsaturated. The phospholipids of mitochondria and microsomes become more unsaturated with aging. In the human retina there is a similar accumulation of PUFA with aging, which implies that the aged retina will be more easily damaged by light. Several studies suggest that a high degree of unsaturation in the fats is fundamentally related to the aging process, since long lived species have a lower degree of unsaturation in their fats."

The cross-species data are striking. From [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]]: "Animals that naturally have a relatively low level of the highly unsaturated fats in their tissues have the greatest longevity. For example, the naked mole rat has a life expectancy of more than 28 years, about 9 times as long as other rodents of a similar size. Only about 2% to 6% of its phospholipids contain DHA, while about 27% to 57% of the phospholipids of mice contain DHA." From [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids"]]: "Birds' mitochondrial fats are much less polyunsaturated than those of mammals, and birds' metabolic rates are much higher, and they live much longer than mammals of a similar size."

The fish corollary is the cleanest version of the argument. In the 2019 One Radio Network episode, Peat noted that "A salmon is high in DHA and is lucky if it lasts nine years. An Orange Roughy is the lowest fish in DHA and lives to 149 years." When researchers fed fish their own fish oil, "they lose endurance and age quickly." A salmon's life expectancy is, in this reading, a direct function of its tissue PUFA load.

Caloric restriction — the only intervention reliably shown to extend mammalian lifespan — operates partly through PUFA. From [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids"]]: "Caloric restriction slows the accumulation of the highly unsaturated fatty acids in mitochondria, and reduces peroxidation." Eating less means storing less PUFA, which means slower accumulation in mitochondrial cardiolipin, which means slower decline of the respiratory chain.

The endpoint of the accumulation is [[age-pigment]] (lipofuscin), the brown granular pigment that builds up in every aging tissue and that Peat treated as the visible terminal toxin of PUFA peroxidation. From an EastWest Healing discussion of the substance: "All the organs develop this pigment with aging, but it develops in proportion to the amount of unsaturated fats in the diet. The essential toxic effect is that it consumes oxygen even faster than the unsaturated fatty acids. It acts as an enzyme equivalent, directly wasting energy and oxygen, producing hydrogen peroxide and a variety of toxic effects. It also poisons the respiratory enzymes and the cellular cleanup systems — the proteolytic enzymes that should remove defective proteins." The full mechanism of lipofuscin formation, its five principal accelerators, and the chemistry by which it amplifies its own production lives on the [[age-pigment]] page.

## The Range of Harm

The list of conditions in which Peat identified PUFA as a structural cause is long enough that it functions as an inversion test on modern medicine. In [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]] he listed them in one paragraph: "The different fatty acids are directly involved, very often with opposite effects, in cell division and growth, cell stability and dissolution, the organization of cells, tissues, and organs, the regulation of pituitary hormones, adrenalin and sympathetic nervous activation, histamine and serotonin synthesis, adrenal cortex hormones, thyroid hormones, testosterone, estrogen, activators of the immune system and inflammation (cytokines), autoimmune diseases, detoxification, obesity, diabetes, puberty, epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, other degenerative nerve diseases and Alzheimer's disease, cancer, heart failure, atherosclerosis, and strokes. In each of these situations, the PUFA have harmful effects."

The cancer case is the most categorical. From [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic"]]: "Cancer can't occur, unless there are unsaturated oils in the diet. Alcoholic cirrhosis of the liver cannot occur unless there are unsaturated oils in the diet. Heart disease can be produced by unsaturated oils, and prevented by adding saturated oils to the diet." The German research from 1927 that found a fat-free diet prevented almost all spontaneous cancers in rats, the Ip et al. dose-response work showing cancer incidence varying with dietary "essential oil" content from 0.5% to 10%, the Los Angeles veterans study showing three times the cancer mortality in the vegetable-oil group at eight years, the dogs fed cod liver oil dying of cancer at twenty times the rate of dogs on standard diets — Peat cited each of these repeatedly in support of the categorical claim. The 1948 cod liver oil dog study, in particular, was a 20-fold cancer-mortality increase: "100%" of cod-liver-fed dogs developed cancer versus 5% of controls.

Immunosuppression is the same substance from a different angle. From [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic"]]: "Vegetable oil is recognized as a drug for knocking out the immune system. Vegetable oil emulsions were used to nourish cancer patients, but it was discovered that the unsaturated oils were suppressing their immune systems. The same products, in which vegetable oil is emulsified with water for intravenous injection, are now marketed specifically for the purpose of suppressing immunity in patients who have had organ transplants. Using the oils in foods has the same harmful effect on the immune system. Unsaturated fats directly kill white blood cells." The fish oil "anti-inflammatory" effect is, in this reading, the same effect from a different brand: PUFA breakdown products knocking out immune function. Full treatment of the immunosuppression mechanism is on [[fish-oil]].

Liver damage from alcohol requires PUFA as a cofactor. From [[oils-in-context|"Oils in Context"]]: "Both experimental and epidemiological studies have shown that dietary linoleic acid is required for the development of alcoholic liver damage. Animals fed tallow and ethanol had no liver injury, but even 0.7% or 2.5% linoleic acid with ethanol caused fatty liver, necrosis, and inflammation." Switching to a saturated-fat-enriched diet reverses alcohol-induced cirrhosis even with continued alcohol intake.

Brain damage operates through the same peroxidation chemistry. From [[oils-in-context|"Oils in Context"]]: "When pregnant mice were fed either coconut oil or unsaturated seed oil, the mice that got coconut oil had babies with normal brains and intelligence, but the mice exposed to the unsaturated oil had smaller brains, and had inferior intelligence. In another experiment, radioactively labeled soy oil was given to nursing rats, and it was shown to be massively incorporated into brain cells, and to cause visible structural changes in the cells." A French prenatal study cited in the 2019 One Radio Network episode found that "babies learned more slowly in utero if they had extra PUFA added to the diet, and they were born with smaller brains."

Shock — the systemic decompensation that follows severe trauma, sepsis, or burns — is in Peat's reading largely a PUFA phenomenon. From [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]]: "When stress is very intense, as in trauma or sepsis, the reaction of liberating fatty acids can become dangerously counter-productive, producing the state of shock. In shock, the liberation of free fatty acids interferes with the use of glucose for energy and causes cells to take up water and calcium (depleting blood volume and reducing circulation) and to leak ATP, enzymes, and other cell contents, in something like a systemic inflammatory state often leading to death. The remarkable resistance of 'essential fatty acid deficient' animals to shock shows that the polyunsaturated fats are centrally involved in the maladaptive reactions of shock." The clinical observation that PUFA-deficient animals survive endotoxin doses that are 100% lethal to controls (Peat quoted a 24% mortality versus 100%) is a structural argument: shock is what happens when stress liberates accumulated PUFA into the bloodstream. The animal that has no PUFA to liberate does not shock.

The PUFA-and-radiation analogy is one of Peat's most generative framings. From [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]]: "PUFA and x-rays have many biological effects in common. They are immunosuppressive, but they produce their own inflammatory reactions, starting with increased permeability of capillaries, disturbed coagulation and proteolysis, and producing fibrosis and tumefaction or tissue atrophy. This isn't just a coincidence, since ionizing radiation attacks the highly unstable polyunsaturated molecules, simply accelerating processes that ordinarily happen more slowly as a result of stress and aging." X-rays attack PUFA; PUFA, given time, produces the same damage at a slower rate. The toxicology of ionizing radiation and the toxicology of dietary unsaturated fats are, at the molecular level, the same toxicology operating on different timescales.

## The Depletion Protocol in Outline

The practical implication of the framework is a protocol of slow elimination, and the dedicated [[pufa-depletion]] page treats it in full operational detail. The hub-level summary has five elements.

First, stop adding PUFA. All industrial [[seed-oils]] (soy, corn, canola, safflower, sunflower, cottonseed, rice bran, grapeseed), all products fried in them, all processed foods containing them, all fish oil supplements, and most restaurant cooking. The contamination is more pervasive than most people realize: even pork and chicken raised on corn and soy carry substantially elevated linoleic acid content compared with animals of previous centuries.

Second, replace dietary fat with saturated and monounsaturated sources. From [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic"]]: "Coconut and olive oil are the only vegetable oils that are really safe, but butter and lamb fat, which are highly saturated, are generally very safe (except when the animals have been poisoned). [[coconut-oil|Coconut oil]] is unique in its ability to prevent weight-gain or cure obesity, by stimulating metabolism. It is quickly metabolized, and functions in some ways as an antioxidant." Even "good" fats like olive oil run 8-10% PUFA; butter and coconut oil run 2-3%. The cleanest practical target is the lowest-PUFA fat that can be consistently sourced.

Third, support [[thyroid]] function. PUFA suppresses thyroid; thyroid accelerates PUFA oxidation. The two are coupled. From a [[2019-01-24-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fats-and-questions|2019 One Radio Network episode]]: "Thyroid is our natural antidote to the accumulation of polyunsaturated fats, which block the production, transportation, and use of thyroid hormone." Restoring thyroid function — through adequate iodine, fruit sugar, protein, and where appropriate exogenous T3/T4 — is part of the depletion mechanism rather than a separate intervention.

Fourth, suppress lipolysis. Frequent eating with sugar prevents the stress-hormone surges that pull stored PUFA into circulation. [[Niacinamide]] and [[aspirin]] both inhibit lipolysis at the adipocyte level and reduce free fatty acid release. [[Vitamin E]] partially protects whatever PUFA does circulate from peroxidation, though it cannot fully compensate for excess intake.

Fifth, accept the timeline. The 600-day half-life cannot be hacked. Weight loss should be gradual, not aggressive, because every pound of fat mobilized releases stored PUFA into circulation. The transition is measured in years.

Peat's own self-experiment with coconut oil, recounted in the EastWest Healing interview, illustrates the magnitude of what is being undone: "About 20 years ago, I decided to experiment by adding half an ounce to an ounce of coconut oil to my food. The immediate thing I noticed was that for about an hour and a half after eating it, my heart was running at a higher rate, I was breathing harder, and my skin was pinker. After a couple of weeks, I saw I was losing weight quickly, even though I was eating more calories." The shift from PUFA-suppressed to saturated-fat-fueled metabolism produced a measurable thermogenic effect within an hour and a sustained metabolic increase within weeks. The full restoration takes years; the directional improvement begins immediately.

## Contemporary Clinical Implications

The PUFA framework provides a unifying account of conditions that mainstream medicine treats as unrelated. [[Hypothyroidism]] becomes a function of cumulative PUFA suppression at the hormone-transport and tissue-reception levels. [[Diabetes]] becomes a Randle-cycle phenomenon in which stored fatty acids block glucose oxidation, misdiagnosed for sixty years as a sugar disease. [[Cancer]] becomes the predictable consequence of immune suppression, aromatase activation, and the membrane instability that allows tumor cells to mobilize their substrate. The slow rise in chronic fatigue, anxiety, and depression across the population maps to the same accumulation curve as the rise in average tissue linoleic acid content over the past century.

The radiation analogy generalizes the implication. Modern degenerative disease, in Peat's reading, is the chronic version of what acute radiation exposure produces acutely. Cancer rates, fibrotic conditions, immunosuppressive illness, neurodegeneration, premature aging — these are not separate epidemics but expressions of the same molecular damage from the same class of substrate, operating on the timescale of a lifetime rather than a single exposure.

The clinical translation is operationally simple even if individually difficult. The substances that protect against PUFA — [[aspirin]] (blocks prostaglandin generation and lipolysis), [[vitamin-e|Vitamin E]] (limits peroxidation), [[progesterone]] (inhibits the phospholipase that liberates arachidonic acid), [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]] (competes with PUFA at the mitochondrial level), [[thyroid]] (accelerates oxidation of what is stored), [[niacinamide]] (lowers free fatty acid release), [[caffeine]] (antilipolytic), [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] (stabilizes tissues against peroxidation) — are also the substances that Peat recommended most consistently across his career. The coherence of the recommendations follows from the coherence of the underlying lesion: a single class of accumulated tissue toxin that operates through a small number of mechanisms across nearly every body system.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream consensus against Peat's PUFA position is the deepest disagreement in modern nutrition science, and the framework demands honest engagement with what is being contradicted.

**The essential fatty acid orthodoxy.** Linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid have been classified as essential nutrients in mainstream biochemistry textbooks since the consolidation of Burr's work in the mid-20th century, and the case rests on more than the original 1929 paper. Animals fed completely fat-free diets do develop characteristic skin and growth abnormalities. The dispute is whether those abnormalities reflect a true PUFA deficiency or a downstream consequence of unrecognized cofactor deficiencies in early experimental diets — Peat's position — or whether they reflect a genuine essentiality at very low intakes that becomes irrelevant at the much higher intakes characteristic of the modern diet. Even sympathetic reviewers of Peat's work tend to find his claim that PUFA are entirely non-essential stronger than the underlying evidence sustains; the more defensible reading is that physiological requirements are met at extremely low intakes (well under 1% of calories) and that the doses encouraged by mainstream guidelines (often 10%+ of calories) are pharmacological rather than nutritional.

**The omega-3 cardiovascular consensus.** Large-scale epidemiology and several major intervention trials have associated marine omega-3 intake with reduced cardiovascular mortality. Peat's position is that the apparent benefit reflects displacement of more harmful omega-6 fats and confounding with other dietary variables (the Inuit comparison fails when Alaska Eskimo data are examined, in his reading), and that fish oil's anti-inflammatory effect is produced by immunosuppression rather than genuine resolution of inflammation. Recent meta-analyses (VITAL, ASCEND, REDUCE-IT) have produced mixed results, and the most recent large randomized trials of marine omega-3 supplementation for primary cardiovascular prevention have generally not shown clear mortality benefits. The disagreement is unsettled even within mainstream nutrition.

**"Anti-inflammatory" fish oil for autoimmunity and arthritis.** The clinical observation that EPA/DHA reduces inflammatory markers in rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and similar conditions is real. Peat's reframing — that the anti-inflammatory effect is the same effect that immunosuppressive drugs and X-ray therapy produce, with the same long-term consequence of chronic immune dysfunction and increased cancer susceptibility — is plausible mechanistically but has not been tested in head-to-head trials against placebo-plus-PUFA-elimination, which is the comparison the framework actually predicts would be favorable.

**The cell membrane fluidity argument.** Standard biochemistry teaches that membrane phospholipids require omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids to maintain appropriate fluidity, particularly in neural tissue. Peat's counter is that the body synthesizes omega-9 unsaturated fats (the [[mead-acid]] series) endogenously to perform this function when dietary PUFA is absent, and that cells in culture multiply normally on completely PUFA-free media. The mainstream rejoinder — that this works in cell culture but cannot scale to whole-organism function over decades — has not been directly tested, because no large human cohort has lived without dietary PUFA long enough to be studied.

**The Mediterranean diet evidence.** Long-running cohort and intervention studies of Mediterranean diets show cardiovascular and longevity benefits in populations consuming significant amounts of olive oil and some fish. Peat's framework can absorb this (olive oil is monounsaturated, not polyunsaturated; modest fish intake is much lower-PUFA than supplementation; the protective effects may come from polyphenols, vegetables, and reduced processed-food intake rather than from PUFA per se) but the protective interpretation of these populations does not require his framework, and most mainstream nutrition science reads them as supporting the moderate-PUFA hypothesis rather than the no-PUFA hypothesis.

**The methodological critique.** Peat's case rests heavily on animal studies from the mid-20th century, epidemiological correlations interpreted causally, and the personal observations of a researcher operating outside the institutional apparatus. None of these are decisive on their own. The strongest version of the framework requires accepting that the absence of large-scale randomized controlled trials of PUFA elimination in humans reflects industry suppression and the four-year-plus time horizon rather than genuine equipoise.

## Data gaps

Several questions are not resolved by the Peat corpus itself and remain open even within the framework.

**Pediatric and developmental dosing.** Peat's position that newborns are healthiest with low PUFA tissue concentrations is consistent across his career, but he gave less specific guidance for how a parent should structure a child's diet during the years when the brain is undergoing rapid lipid incorporation. The fish oil critique is categorical; the positive recommendation is less specific.

**The lower bound.** Peat sometimes implied a 0.5% or 0.6% of total calories ceiling at which PUFA harms become measurable, but he did not systematize a personal-intake target the way protocols for other nutrients usually do. Whether the optimum is "as close to zero as practically achievable" or "below 2% of calories" or "below 4 grams per day" varies across his statements.

**Genetic variation in PUFA metabolism.** FADS1/FADS2 desaturase polymorphisms produce substantial individual variation in arachidonic acid synthesis from linoleic acid. Whether the framework's universal claims need modulation for different genetic backgrounds is not addressed in the corpus.

**Pregnancy and lactation.** Peat warned against fish oil supplementation in pregnancy and cited animal evidence that maternal PUFA supplementation reduces fetal brain growth. He did not address what a pregnant woman doing serious [[pufa-depletion]] should expect for the fetus, whose lipid stores are largely shaped by maternal stores accumulated before pregnancy.

**The transition risk window.** PUFA depletion involves mobilizing stored unsaturated fats from adipose tissue into the bloodstream, where they are vulnerable to peroxidation. The first 12-18 months of serious depletion may carry elevated risk of stress-related symptoms (fatigue, mood disturbance, skin changes) as stored PUFA enters circulation. Peat addressed this with general recommendations for [[aspirin]], [[niacinamide]], and frequent eating, but the specific risks during the transition are underspecified.

**Modern measurement.** Standard lipid panels do not measure tissue PUFA content, only circulating cholesterol and triglycerides. Tools for tracking actual progress on PUFA depletion (red blood cell membrane fatty acid composition, adipose biopsy) exist but are not in routine clinical use. The framework's emphasis on a four-year horizon is harder to operationalize without measurable intermediate endpoints.

**Recent science the corpus does not cover.** Microbiome research, FADS polymorphism interactions, oxylipin signaling, the specialized pro-resolving mediators (resolvins, protectins, maresins) derived from omega-3 fats — these post-2020 developments map onto Peat's framework in ways that have not been worked out. Whether resolvins represent a genuine homeostatic mechanism (mainstream reading) or another set of PUFA breakdown products being marketed as solutions (Peat's likely reading) is not directly addressed by Peat's writings.
