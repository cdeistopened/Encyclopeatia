# Cholesterol

The modern war on cholesterol represents one of the most damaging medical campaigns in history. In Ray Peat's framework, cholesterol is not a toxin clogging arteries but the essential raw material from which the body manufactures every protective steroid hormone: [[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], [[DHEA]], [[testosterone]], cortisol, and vitamin D. Lowering it with statins does not prevent disease—it prevents the body from making the substances that prevent disease. As Peat explained in a [[2008-12-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cholesterol|2008 episode]], "Cholesterol is the raw material for all protective steroids. Artificially lowering it is dangerous."

## The Steroid Precursor

Cholesterol's primary metabolic role is as the starting material for steroidogenesis. Under the influence of [[thyroid]] hormone and [[vitamin A]], mitochondrial enzymes cleave cholesterol's side chain to produce [[pregnenolone]]—the mother steroid from which all others derive.

In a [[2008-09-11-politics-and-science-politics-science-thyroid-and-regeneration|2008 episode on thyroid and regeneration]], Peat described a revealing experiment: "An isolated ovary showed that progesterone output corresponded directly to cholesterol input." The more cholesterol available, the more protective steroids produced. Limiting cholesterol directly limits the body's ability to protect itself.

This is why [[hypothyroidism]] causes elevated cholesterol—not because the body is producing too much, but because it cannot convert cholesterol into steroids efficiently. Thyroid hormone drives the conversion; without it, cholesterol accumulates unused. As Peat noted, "Thyroid lowers cholesterol naturally by producing more protective hormones: progesterone, pregnenolone, DHEA." The high cholesterol that doctors treat with statins is often a sign that thyroid support is what the patient actually needs.

## Cholesterol and the Brain

The brain contains roughly 25% of the body's total cholesterol, and its maintenance depends on ongoing cholesterol synthesis and transport. Peat cited the Framingham study data repeatedly. In a [[2013-06-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-heart-ii|2013 episode]], he noted: "People over 50 with cholesterol above 200 were less likely to have dementia. Lowering it with statins below that is damaging."

The mechanism is direct: the brain uses cholesterol to produce neurosteroids (pregnenolone, DHEA, progesterone) locally at concentrations ten times higher than blood levels. When cholesterol is artificially lowered, the brain cannot produce enough of these protective steroids, leading to cognitive decline, depression, and eventually dementia.

As Peat described in a [[2011-09-29-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-cholesterol-and-saturated-fats|2011 episode]], "Low cholesterol leads to violence, suicidal depression, and dementia. Cholesterol is intrinsic to chromosome and DNA function." The epidemiological data consistently shows that elderly people with the highest cholesterol live the longest—the opposite of what the statin industry claims.

## Statins: Blocking the Entire Pathway

Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase—the enzyme that catalyzes an early step in cholesterol synthesis. But this enzyme is not exclusive to cholesterol production. It also produces coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), which is essential for mitochondrial energy production.

As Peat explained, "Statins interfere with the whole chain of synthesis, including CoQ10 production, likely causing rhabdomyolysis"—the dissolution of muscle tissue that is one of statins' most serious side effects. The muscle damage is a direct consequence of mitochondrial energy failure from CoQ10 depletion, not an idiosyncratic reaction.

The broader irony is that statins, by lowering cholesterol, suppress the production of the very hormones (progesterone, DHEA, pregnenolone) that protect against the cardiovascular disease they claim to prevent. A person on statins has lower cholesterol numbers but also lower protective steroids, impaired mitochondrial function, and reduced brain cholesterol—a trade that worsens outcomes on every dimension except the lab report.

## The Correct Range

Rather than treating a number, Peat's approach addresses what the number means. Elevated cholesterol usually indicates insufficient thyroid function—the body is producing cholesterol but cannot convert it to steroids. The solution is thyroid support, not cholesterol reduction.

In his emails, Peat suggested "keeping cholesterol in the range of 160-220" as a rough guide, noting that this requires "enough thyroid to keep TSH low." Low cholesterol (below 160) combined with even slightly low thyroid means "protective steroids are not produced in normal amounts, leading to inflammatory processes, connective tissue pain, waist fat, constipation."

The practical approach: if cholesterol is high, check thyroid function first. If cholesterol is low, the situation may actually be worse—the body lacks the raw material for hormonal protection.

## Dietary Cholesterol

Peat did not advocate cholesterol-rich foods for the sake of raising cholesterol levels, since the body synthesizes most of its cholesterol internally. But he emphasized that dietary cholesterol (eggs, shellfish, liver) is not harmful and provides a ready supply of the steroid precursor.

The raw carrot salad works partly through this pathway: by reducing intestinal reabsorption of [[estrogen]] and [[cortisol]], it improves thyroid function, which accelerates "the conversion of cholesterol into protective hormones," as Peat described. The carrot does not lower cholesterol directly—it improves the metabolic conditions that allow cholesterol to be used properly.

Sweet fruits also support healthy cholesterol metabolism. As Peat noted in his emails, "Sweet fruits are anti-inflammatory and help keep cholesterol in a good range"—not by lowering it but by supporting the liver metabolism that processes it.
