# Butter

In Ray Peat's hierarchy of acceptable fats, butter sits on the protective side of the ledger alongside [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], beef fat, and lamb fat. It was one of the handful of fats he considered "generally very safe," and he ate it daily for decades. But his endorsement was always comparative and never ecstatic. Where Peat wrote whole newsletters on coconut oil and returned to it in almost every interview, butter appeared as a supporting character: a stable cooking fat, a source of fat-soluble vitamins, a carrier of short-chain fatty acids with modest benefits. "Use coconut oil, butter, and olive oil," he wrote in the summary of his most influential anti-seed-oil article. The order was not accidental. Butter protects against the worst of what the seed-oil era has done to human metabolism, but it is not, for Peat, the therapeutic fat. That role belongs to the tropical oil that competes most directly with the [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] stored in our tissues.

## A Saturated Fat That Escapes the Indictment

The central claim Peat made about butter was defensive rather than offensive: it does not do the damage that [[seed-oils|seed oils]] do. In his 2007 article *"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic,"* he answered the practical question directly. "Coconut and olive oil are the only vegetable oils that are really safe, but butter and lamb fat, which are highly saturated, are generally very safe (except when the animals have been poisoned)." The parenthetical is important. Butter's safety depends on what the cow ate, and in the modern feedlot economy that condition is no longer automatic.

The safety is compositional. Like beef fat, lamb fat, and coconut oil, butter contains only about two percent polyunsaturated fat. A diet built around butter, coconut oil, milk, cheese, and ruminant meats keeps total PUFA intake low without any exotic discipline. In an email on diet, Peat described his own rule: "If the fat is mostly saturated, from milk, cheese, butter, beef, lamb or coconut oil, I think it's usually o.k. to get about 50% of the calories from fat, but since those natural fats typically contain around 2% polyunsaturated fats, I try to minimize my PUFA intake by having more fruit, and a little less fat, maybe 30 to 35%." The two percent floor was treated as the practical limit of dietary purity.

Peat also offered a comparative point about cholesterol that cut against popular assumption. "There is much less cholesterol in an ounce of butter than in a lean chicken breast," he wrote in the same 2007 article, citing R. Reiser of Texas A & M on the ratio: about one-fifth as much cholesterol in fat as in lean meat on a calorie basis. The image of butter as a concentrated cholesterol bomb is wrong on the arithmetic alone, before the metabolic case for dietary cholesterol even enters the picture (see [[cholesterol]] for Peat's broader reframe).

## The Vitamins That Come With It

What butter brings to the table that coconut oil does not is a set of fat-soluble vitamins in their natural matrix. Peat summarized the benefit concisely: "Butter contains natural vitamin A and D and some beneficial natural hormones. It is less fattening than the unsaturated oils." The phrase "natural vitamin A" carries weight in Peat's framework. He distinguished repeatedly between the retinol found in animal fats, the beta-carotene found in plants, and the synthetic vitamin A palmitate used in supplements. Butter from pasture-fed animals delivers retinol embedded in the fat matrix that allows for its proper absorption and storage (see [[vitamin-a|vitamin A]]).

The "beneficial natural hormones" Peat referenced include the trans-isomers of unsaturated fatty acids unique to ruminant fat. In his 2009 article *"Fats and Degeneration,"* he wrote that "trans-isomers of the unsaturated fatty acids (found in butterfat, and convertible into conjugated linoleic acid) can be protective against cancer." This is one of the few places in the corpus where a trans-fat receives an endorsement, and the distinction is biochemical: the trans-isomers produced in the cow's rumen by bacterial fermentation are structurally different from the trans-fats produced by industrial hydrogenation.

CLA (conjugated linoleic acid) is the most-studied of these ruminant isomers. Peat was measured in his enthusiasm. Asked about CLA and butyric acid in an email exchange, he replied: "They aren't necessary, but the CLA in cream and butter are probably responsible for some of their good effects. I use 1% milk, some butter and hydrogenated coconut oil, to keep polyunsaturated fats to a minimum." The word "probably" did characteristic work. Peat was unwilling to claim that CLA alone accounted for butter's benefits when the overall composition, the vitamin content, and the displacement of seed oils could all contribute. But he cited animal research on CLA's effects, noting that "rats given butter fat and supplements of CLA demonstrated an increased rate of bone formation and reduced ex vivo bone PGE2 production." The mechanism connects CLA to the broader anti-inflammatory story of saturated fats displacing the arachidonic acid cascade.

The [[vitamin-k|vitamin K2]] content of pasture-fed butter, which Weston Price's dental research brought to the attention of a later generation of nutritionists, did not feature prominently in Peat's writing under the name "K2" or "activator X." But the logic he applied to other vitamins fits the K2 story: a cow grazing on rapidly growing grass produces milk fat with a qualitatively different cofactor profile than a cow eating corn and soybean meal in a feedlot. The "feed matters" principle runs through all of Peat's discussion of animal fats, and it applies with particular force to butter.

## Butyric Acid and the Gut

Butter takes its name from butyric acid, the four-carbon short-chain fatty acid that gives it its characteristic smell and much of its reputation in the functional medicine world. Peat treated butyric acid the way he treated most isolated compounds: with interest in the mechanism but a preference for the whole food. In his article *"Coconut Oil,"* he noted that "butyric acid is used to treat cancer, lauric and myristic acids to treat virus infections, and mixtures of medium-chain fats are sold for weight loss," then added: "I think the whole natural product, used as a regular food, is the best way to protect health."

The mainstream case for butyrate protecting the colon involves its production by gut bacteria fermenting resistant starch and fiber. Peat did not center this pathway in his own writing, partly because he was skeptical of fiber-heavy diets and the [[endotoxin|endotoxin]] load they can produce in a compromised gut. The butyric acid in butter, unlike the butyrate produced by colonic fermentation, arrives pre-formed and absorbs higher in the digestive tract.

His broader point about butter and digestion came from fat's role in intestinal function. In email exchanges on diet, he described experiments on isolated intestinal loops showing that "relatively simplified mixtures of nutrients were poorly digested. Fat, protein, sugars, and minerals, in combination, activated the intestine, increasing the digestion of all of them, when they were present at the same time. If the fats are mostly saturated, as in butter, coconut oil, or beef or lamb fat, roughly a third of the calories is good." Butter's contribution to digestion in this view is not primarily about butyrate. It is about fat acting as a signal to the intestine that food has arrived.

## Feed Matters: The Grass-Fed Question

Peat's conditional endorsement ("except when the animals have been poisoned") pointed at a problem that has grown worse since he first wrote about fats in the 1970s. Commercial butter comes from cows fed largely on corn and soybean meal, and the fatty-acid composition of the milk fat reflects what the cow eats. Grass-fed butter contains a different profile: more CLA, more vitamin K2, more carotenoids from the green plants the cow grazed on. The color is visibly more yellow.

In his 2007 article *"Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats,"* Peat traced the historical shift. Around 1950, "corn and soybeans were widely considered to be optimal feeds for maximizing profits in the production of meat animals," because the seed oils in those feeds "had the opposite effect, of producing a rapid fattening of the animal, while decreasing feed consumption." Dairy cattle followed the same logic. The butter that resulted was still saturated enough to escape the worst PUFA problems, but its micronutrient profile drifted away from the vitamin-rich ruminant fat that traditional cultures had prized.

Peat did not insist on grass-fed sourcing as a precondition for eating butter. His personal diet included standard supermarket butter, and he rated regular butter as "generally very safe" when the alternative was seed oil. But his endorsement of butter's vitamin A, CLA, and "beneficial natural hormones" referred most accurately to butter from cows that had eaten grass.

## Cooking and Peat's Own Pattern

Butter's stability as a cooking fat follows from its saturation. The high saturated-fat content means it resists the oxidation that destroys unsaturated oils at cooking temperatures. When describing his own meals, Peat mentioned "cod fried in butter" alongside "smoked oysters, crab, ox-tail soup, parmigiano reggiano" as routine foods, while "completely avoiding unsaturated fats, such as canola and mayonnaise." The milk solids in butter do burn at high temperatures, which is why some cooks prefer clarified butter ([[ghee|ghee]]) for high-heat work, but Peat did not make much of this distinction.

The most accurate picture of where butter sat in Peat's life comes from his own dietary descriptions. In one email, he listed his daily rhythm as "eggs and orange juice, milk and oysters, and a raw carrot. For variety, smoked oysters, crab, cod fried in butter, ox-tail soup, parmigiano reggiano, sapotas, lychees, liver." Butter appeared as a quiet regular, never the centerpiece. The centerpiece was [[milk|milk]], orange juice, and the egg-fruit-coffee base that defined the pro-metabolic pattern.

For anyone trying to raise testosterone, Peat placed butter in the standard saturated-fat protocol. In an email on exercise, he wrote: "A high protein diet is helpful, and avoiding polyunsaturated fats helps to increase testosterone (coconut oil, butter, maybe MCT instead)." The logic connects to the broader [[estrogen|estrogen]] story: PUFA drives the conversion of testosterone into estrogen via aromatase, and replacing PUFA with stable saturated fats removes that pressure on the hormonal system. Butter is one of the obvious tools for doing that replacement in a Western diet, supporting [[thyroid|thyroid]] function indirectly by removing the PUFA brake.

## The Modest Endorsement

What makes Peat's position on butter distinctive is not its enthusiasm but its proportion. Butter appears in his recommendations as a reliable, protective food. It does not appear as a panacea or the central fat of a therapeutic diet. That role, when Peat assigned it to any single food, went to coconut oil, because of coconut oil's specific ability to compete with the PUFA stored in body tissue and to support thyroid function through its medium-chain fatty acids in a way that butter does not quite replicate. Butter is approximately 11 percent short-chain and medium-chain fatty acids combined, meaningful but not dominant. Coconut oil is roughly 65 percent medium-chain.

Peat did not have a single "best fat." He had a framework for distinguishing safe fats from dangerous ones, and within the safe category he recognized that different fats did different things. Butter is good for vitamins A and K and for the CLA and butyric acid it carries. Coconut oil is good for thyroid and weight and for its antioxidant displacement of PUFA. Olive oil is good for its polyphenols. Beef and lamb fat are good for their pure saturation. Peat's advice was to use all of them while avoiding the seed oils, the [[fish-oil|fish oils]], and the commercial substitutes that replaced them in the twentieth century. Butter is part of the foundation. The specific metabolic work is done by other foods operating on the foundation it helps provide.
