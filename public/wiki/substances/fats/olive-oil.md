# Olive Oil

Olive oil occupies an unusual position in Ray Peat's dietary framework---neither condemned outright like the [[seed-oils]] nor embraced as a staple like [[coconut-oil]] and butter. Peat himself used olive oil, notably in his recommended [[carrot salad]] dressed with "olive oil, vinegar, and salt," as he described in a [[2020-01-16-jodellefit-jodellefit-insulin-resistance-vegans-low-cortisol-bone-broth-and-coconut|2020 conversation on insulin resistance and coconut]]. But his endorsement came with important qualifications. Olive oil is better than corn oil or canola---dramatically so---yet it is not the metabolically neutral fat its proponents imagine. Its 10 to 20 percent [[PUFA]] content means that liberal use can quietly accumulate the same polyunsaturated fatty acids Peat spent his career warning against.

## A Different Category from Seed Oils

The crucial distinction between olive oil and industrial seed oils lies in fatty acid composition. As Peat explained in a [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode on dementia and progesterone]], "Olive oil is not considered a PUFA because it only has about 10% PUFA and the other 90% is monounsaturated." This is a meaningful difference. Where soybean oil delivers roughly 55 percent [[linoleic acid]] and sunflower oil can reach 70 percent, olive oil's dominant fatty acid is oleic acid---an omega-9 monounsaturated fat with only one double bond in its chain.

That single double bond makes oleic acid substantially more stable than the multiply-unsaturated linoleic and linolenic acids that characterize seed oils. It does not undergo the same rapid oxidative breakdown inside the body, does not generate the same cascade of toxic aldehydes, and does not suppress [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]] with the same potency. In Peat's hierarchy of dietary fats, oleic acid sits in a tolerable middle ground---neither protective like the short and medium-chain [[saturated-fat|saturated fats]] of coconut oil, nor destructive like the polyunsaturated fats of corn and soy.

## The PUFA That Remains

Tolerance, however, is not endorsement. The 10 to 20 percent of olive oil that is polyunsaturated---primarily linoleic acid---accumulates in tissues just as effectively as the same fatty acid from any other source. A tablespoon of olive oil contains roughly one to two grams of PUFA. For someone using olive oil as a primary cooking fat---drizzling it on salads, sauteing vegetables, dipping bread---daily PUFA intake from olive oil alone can approach or exceed the four-gram threshold Peat identified as the inflection point for increased [[cancer]] risk.

This arithmetic is what separates Peat's perspective from the mainstream Mediterranean diet narrative. Advocates of olive oil consumption often cite studies showing cardiovascular benefits in Mediterranean populations, but Peat observed that those populations also consumed substantial quantities of [[saturated-fat]] from sheep's milk cheese, lamb, and goat---fats that are metabolically protective and that rarely feature in the American interpretation of "Mediterranean eating." The olive oil was a component of a diet rich in saturated fats, not a replacement for them.

## Context-Dependent Acceptability

Peat's practical advice on olive oil was consistent: use it in moderation, particularly where its flavor matters, but do not treat it as a health food or a primary fat source. His co-host Sarah Johannesen Murray captured this position in a [[2009-10-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food-additives|2009 episode on food additives]], advising listeners to "eat coconut oil and butter, avoid other oils, use olive oil in moderation."

The carrot salad illustrates the appropriate context. A tablespoon of olive oil dressing a raw carrot---eaten specifically for its intestinal antiseptic properties---contributes a modest PUFA load in the service of a broader therapeutic goal. The oil aids absorption of fat-soluble nutrients and makes the salad palatable. This is fundamentally different from using olive oil as one's default cooking fat, where the quantities involved multiply the PUFA exposure considerably.

For cooking, Peat consistently recommended fats with higher saturated content. [[Coconut oil]]'s medium-chain fatty acids burn cleanly and support [[metabolic-rate|metabolic rate]]. Butter from grass-fed cows provides protective short-chain fats along with fat-soluble vitamins. Beef tallow is almost entirely saturated and monounsaturated, with minimal PUFA. All three withstand heat better than olive oil, whose lower smoke point can generate additional oxidation products at cooking temperatures.

## Extra Virgin versus Refined

The distinction between extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) and refined olive oil adds another layer of complexity. EVOO retains the polyphenols, tocopherols, and other minor compounds stripped from refined versions during processing. These phenolic compounds---including oleocanthal, which produces the characteristic throat-burning sensation---have demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties in isolation.

However, EVOO also contains more [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] than refined olive oil, a consequence of minimal processing. Free fatty acids are metabolically active in ways that intact triglycerides are not; they circulate more readily and can interfere with cellular processes. Peat was generally cautious about free fatty acids regardless of their source, viewing their elevation in the bloodstream as a marker of [[stress]] and metabolic dysfunction.

The practical resolution is straightforward: if using olive oil, choose a good extra virgin for its phenolic content, but keep quantities moderate. The polyphenols offer some real protection against [[oxidative-stress|oxidation]], but they do not neutralize the underlying PUFA load at high intake levels.

## The Mediterranean Diet Misunderstanding

Peat's critique of olive oil extends to a broader skepticism about how the Mediterranean diet has been interpreted and marketed. The original observations by Ancel Keys and others noted that certain Mediterranean populations---particularly on Crete---had lower rates of heart disease. But the diets of those populations bore little resemblance to what American nutritionists prescribe under the "Mediterranean" label.

Traditional Cretan diet included large amounts of cheese from sheep and goats (high in saturated fat), lamb and goat meat (again, saturated), wild greens, fruit, and modest amounts of olive oil. The modern American "Mediterranean diet" replaces the saturated animal fats with more olive oil and whole grains---a substitution that inverts the original fat profile. Peat viewed this transformation as another instance of the dietary establishment's long campaign against [[saturated-fat]], using olive oil as a palatable vehicle for the same low-saturated-fat ideology that gave rise to the [[seed-oils|seed oil]] catastrophe.

## Practical Position in the Fat Hierarchy

In Peat's framework, dietary fats arrange themselves along a clear spectrum from protective to harmful:

1. **[[Coconut oil]]** --- medium-chain saturated fats that boost metabolism directly
2. **Butter and tallow** --- long-chain saturated fats that are metabolically stable
3. **Olive oil** --- predominantly monounsaturated, acceptable in moderation
4. **Poultry and pork fat** --- variable PUFA depending on animal diet
5. **[[Seed oils]]** --- high PUFA, actively damaging at any intake level
6. **[[Fish oil]]** --- highly unsaturated omega-3s, the most oxidation-prone of all

Olive oil sits squarely in the middle: not something to fear in small amounts, not something to celebrate or pour liberally. For someone already working to reduce tissue PUFA stores, replacing seed oils with olive oil represents a significant improvement. But replacing olive oil with coconut oil or butter represents a further improvement still.
