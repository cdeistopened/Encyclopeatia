# Ghee

Ghee is what remains when you remove everything from [[butter]] except the fat itself -- and for a subset of people, that subtraction solves a real problem without sacrificing butter's core metabolic value.

Ghee, or clarified butter, is produced by slowly heating butter until the water evaporates and the milk solids -- [[casein]], [[lactose]], and whey proteins -- separate and can be strained away. What remains is pure butterfat: a golden, shelf-stable oil with the same ruminant-origin [[saturated-fat|saturated fatty acid]] profile that made Peat list butter first among "the safe oils" in a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 KMUD episode on energy production and saturated fats]].

## Butter's Fat Without Butter's Proteins

Peat recommended butter extensively and consistently throughout his career. It appears at the top of nearly every list of safe fats he offered, valued for its saturation, its ruminant origin, and its content of protective short-chain fatty acids like [[butyric-acid|butyric acid]]. But butter is not pure fat -- it contains roughly 80% fat, 15% water, and 5% milk solids. Those milk solids include casein and whey, the dairy proteins that provoke immune responses in sensitive individuals, as well as lactose, the milk sugar that causes digestive distress in those lacking adequate lactase.

Ghee eliminates this problem. By removing the protein and sugar fractions, it retains butter's fatty acid profile -- the [[stearic-acid|stearic]], [[palmitic-acid|palmitic]], [[oleic-acid|oleic]], and short-chain fatty acids -- while becoming tolerable for people who react to dairy proteins. For someone following Peat's dietary principles but experiencing skin reactions, digestive issues, or inflammatory responses to butter, ghee offers the same metabolic substrate without the immunological trigger.

This is not a trivial distinction. Peat recognized that dairy sensitivity was common and that forcing butter consumption on someone who reacted poorly to it defeated the purpose. The goal was always to supply stable, saturated fats that support [[thyroid]] function and [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]] -- and ghee accomplishes that as effectively as whole butter for the fat-soluble components.

## What Ghee Keeps and What It Loses

The clarification process preserves everything that is fat-soluble and removes everything that is water-soluble or protein-bound. Ghee retains [[butyric-acid|butyric acid]] (a short-chain fatty acid with anti-inflammatory properties), [[conjugated-linoleic-acid|conjugated linoleic acid]] (CLA, produced by ruminant digestion), and the fat-soluble [[vitamin-A|vitamins A]], D, E, and [[vitamin-K2|K2]] -- though concentrations of the vitamins may be somewhat reduced compared to whole butter, since some vitamin A and K associate with the milk solid fraction.

What ghee loses is meaningful in a different way. The whey proteins in butter contain bioactive peptides. The casein, while problematic for some, provides calcium and other minerals. Lactose, though a concern for the intolerant, is a source of [[galactose]] -- a sugar Peat discussed in the context of brain metabolism and glycoprotein synthesis. These are not critical losses for someone eating a varied diet that includes other dairy products like [[milk]] or [[cheese]], but they mean ghee is nutritionally narrower than whole butter.

The practical tradeoff is clear: ghee is the right choice when dairy proteins are the problem, and whole butter is preferable when they are not.

## Cooking Advantages

Ghee has a meaningfully higher smoke point than butter -- roughly 250 degrees Celsius versus 175 -- because it is the milk solids that burn and smoke at lower temperatures, not the butterfat itself. This makes ghee more versatile in high-heat cooking applications like searing, roasting, and sauteing, where whole butter would brown and eventually burn.

From a Peattian perspective, this thermal stability matters because it means the fat remains chemically intact during cooking. Ghee's fatty acid profile -- predominantly saturated and monounsaturated, with very low [[PUFA]] content -- resists [[lipid-peroxidation|lipid peroxidation]] at high temperatures. Combined with the absence of milk solids that would generate acrolein when scorched, ghee provides a clean cooking medium that delivers none of the oxidative byproducts associated with heating [[seed-oils|polyunsaturated cooking oils]].

Ghee is also shelf-stable at room temperature for extended periods. Without water or protein to support microbial growth, it resists rancidity far longer than whole butter. In warm climates -- notably in the Indian subcontinent, where ghee has been a culinary staple for thousands of years -- this stability was originally its primary practical advantage. Traditional Ayurvedic medicine used ghee extensively, and Indian cultures developed an entire culinary and medicinal tradition around it, long before anyone could explain the biochemistry of saturated fat stability.

## Ghee in Peat's Framework

Peat did not discuss ghee extensively in interviews or writing -- he tended to recommend butter directly, and when pressed on dairy sensitivity, would suggest addressing the sensitivity rather than abandoning dairy fat. But ghee fits comfortably within his principles. It is a ruminant-origin saturated fat, low in polyunsaturated content, thermally stable, and free of the anti-metabolic properties he attributed to [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]]. It belongs in the same category as [[tallow]], butter, and [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]] -- fats that support rather than suppress [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]].

For those building a Peattian kitchen, ghee serves as the dairy-safe alternative to butter and the high-heat alternative to coconut oil. It is not a superfood or a special supplement -- it is simply butterfat, isolated and purified, doing what ruminant saturated fats do: providing stable fuel for cellular respiration without the metabolic interference of polyunsaturated fatty acids.
