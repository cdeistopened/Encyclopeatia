# Arachidonic Acid

Arachidonic acid is the molecular pivot of the [[pufa|PUFA]] framework — the point where a dietary fat stops being a passive contaminant and becomes an active, signal-generating substrate for the body's inflammatory machinery. It is a 20-carbon fatty acid with four double bonds (20:4 ω-6), elongated and desaturated inside human cells from dietary [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] and stored, esterified into the sn-2 position of membrane phospholipids, in essentially every tissue. There it waits. When [[phospholipase|phospholipase A2]] is activated by stress, estrogen, or injury, arachidonic acid is cleaved free and enters the cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase (LOX) cascades, where it is converted to [[prostaglandins]], thromboxanes, prostacyclins, and leukotrienes — the eicosanoids that mediate pain, swelling, platelet aggregation, fever, immune suppression, aromatase induction, aldosterone synthesis, capillary leak, fibrosis, tumor growth, and the systemic decompensation of shock.

The bulk of Peat's PUFA argument routes through arachidonic acid. [[Linoleic-acid|Linoleic acid]] itself is harmful (it displaces thyroid hormone from carriers, inhibits glucuronidation, suppresses oxidative metabolism), but the molecule that does the most acute work — that converts the slow chronic insult of seed-oil consumption into the daily cellular events of inflammation and aging — is its elongation product. "Arachidonic acid is made from linoleic acid, and so those two oils were considered as roughly equivalent in their ability to meet our nutritional needs," he wrote in his newsletter [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration" (2009)]]. The "balance" between omega-3 and omega-6 that occupies mainstream nutrition writing is, in his reading, almost beside the point: linoleic acid in the diet becomes arachidonic acid in the tissues regardless of how much fish oil is added in parallel.

This page covers the 20:4 molecule itself — its biochemistry, sources, what Peat said about it that distinguishes his position from the mainstream, and what remains unresolved. The PUFA-wide case lives on the [[pufa|hub]]; the dietary precursor on [[linoleic-acid]]; the protective alternative on [[mead-acid]]; the eicosanoid signaling on [[prostaglandins]]; the principal pharmacological countermeasure on [[aspirin]].

## Biochemistry: 20:4 ω-6

Arachidonic acid is all-cis-5,8,11,14-eicosatetraenoic acid: twenty carbons, four cis double bonds, the first counting from the methyl end at carbon-6 (hence ω-6). Its four double bonds make it the substrate for two principal enzyme families: cyclooxygenases (COX-1 and COX-2), which convert it to prostaglandin H2 and downstream to PGE2, PGD2, PGF2α, prostacyclin (PGI2), and thromboxane A2; and lipoxygenases (5-LOX, 12-LOX, 15-LOX), which convert it to the leukotriene and HETE series. The cytochrome P450 epoxygenase pathway produces a third class of arachidonic-acid-derived signals (the EETs).

The body does not need to eat arachidonic acid. Given linoleic acid in the diet, delta-6 desaturase converts it to gamma-linolenic acid, an elongase adds two carbons to yield [[dgla|dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid (DGLA, 20:3 ω-6)]], and [[delta-5-desaturase|delta-5 desaturase]] installs the final double bond to produce arachidonic acid. DGLA is itself a precursor for the 1-series prostaglandins (including PGE1), which are largely anti-inflammatory — meaning the delta-5 step is a critical regulatory branchpoint that determines whether the same dietary linoleic acid feeds the inflammatory 2-series cascade or the protective 1-series. Peat did not write extensively about DGLA specifically, but the broader principle (elongation and desaturation rates govern eicosanoid output) is consistent with his framework.

Storage matters as much as conversion. Arachidonic acid sits esterified into the sn-2 position of phospholipids, particularly phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine. It does not float freely in cytoplasm; it is held in place until [[phospholipase|phospholipase A2]] cleaves it loose. Without phospholipase activation there are no free fatty acids to oxidize; without free arachidonic acid there are no eicosanoids; without eicosanoids the prostaglandin-mediated cascade of inflammation, aromatase induction, capillary leak, and fibrosis does not run. Peat treated phospholipase A2 as one of estrogen's most consequential downstream tools.

## Sources: Animal Foods Versus Endogenous Elongation

This is where Peat's framework diverges most sharply from mainstream low-arachidonic-acid recommendations. The mainstream advice translates into reducing liver, egg yolks, and red meat — foods that contain pre-formed arachidonic acid at perhaps a few hundred milligrams per day. The high-leverage source of arachidonic acid in modern tissues, in Peat's reading, is endogenous conversion from the much larger background load of dietary [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]].

Mainstream isotope tracer studies estimate human linoleic-acid-to-arachidonic-acid conversion at 0.1% to 6%, and these low fractions have been used to argue that direct dietary intake (animal foods) dominates tissue arachidonic acid levels. The Peat-aligned counter is arithmetic: even at low fractional conversion rates, 10-25 grams per day of seed-oil linoleic acid yields enough arachidonic acid to chronically saturate membrane phospholipids. Tissue arachidonic acid content tracks linoleic acid intake more strongly than direct arachidonic acid intake in most epidemiological work. The delta-6 desaturase step is rate-limiting in healthy adults but accelerates under stress and with FADS gene polymorphisms that vary substantially across populations.

The biological logic for tolerating animal-source arachidonic acid: pre-formed arachidonic acid in liver, egg yolk, or grass-finished meat comes embedded alongside saturated fats, protein, glycine, cholesterol, fat-soluble vitamins, and minerals — a nutritional context that supports thyroid function, glucuronidation, and mitochondrial respiration. Linoleic acid from seed oil arrives in bulk, often heated, oxidized, without those cofactors, and is converted to arachidonic acid inside cells simultaneously being suppressed by the same linoleic acid load. Peat did not endorse arachidonic acid as a positive nutrient — PUFA in general should be minimized — but he treated egg yolks, liver, and grass-fed animal fats as foods, not as the villains they had become in the lipid-hypothesis era. One or two eggs per day contributes perhaps 150-200 mg of arachidonic acid; that does not approach the chronic membrane saturation produced by 20-30 grams of seed-oil linoleic acid converting at even a few percent.

Modern industrial meat changes this calculus, as Peat noted repeatedly. Hogs and chickens fed corn and soybeans accumulate linoleic acid in their depot fat at levels that resemble the seed oils they ate — Peat estimated pork fat from soybean-fed hogs could exceed 30% linoleic acid. The same applies to industrial chicken. "Animal fat" from such animals is no longer the largely saturated fat traditional diets contained; it is a delivery vehicle for the same seed oils through a different chain. The protective contrast between animal-source arachidonic acid and endogenous arachidonic acid depends on the animal having been raised on its species-appropriate diet — grass-finished beef and lamb, pastured eggs, dairy from grazed cows.

## The COX/LOX Cascade

The mechanism by which arachidonic acid produces its downstream effects runs through the eicosanoid cascade. Arachidonic acid stored in phospholipid membranes is biologically inert; it becomes pathogenic only when liberated by phospholipase activation and converted by COX or LOX to active signals.

The COX pathway produces the 2-series prostaglandins. PGE2 — produced by both COX-1 and the inducible COX-2 — is the workhorse of inflammation in Peat's framework: it activates aromatase, suppresses immune surveillance against tumors, promotes vasodilation and capillary leak, induces fever, sensitizes pain receptors, and feeds the fibrotic remodeling that follows tissue injury. Thromboxane A2 (mainly platelet-derived) drives platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction; PGI2 opposes it from the endothelium. The LOX pathway produces the leukotrienes: LTB4 as a leukocyte chemoattractant; LTC4/LTD4/LTE4 driving bronchoconstriction, vascular permeability, and allergic disease. Both classes are quantitatively important in chronic inflammatory disease.

Peat's broader reading is that aspirin's interference is not limited to COX blockade — it also reduces phospholipase activation upstream and competes with arachidonic acid for binding sites throughout the cell. From [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|"Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer"]]: "The polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as linolenic, linoleic, arachidonic, EPA, and DHA, have many directly toxic, antirespiratory actions, apart from the production of the prostaglandins or eicosanoids. Just by preventing the release of these fatty acids, aspirin would have broadly antiinflammatory effects." Selective COX-2 inhibitors block one enzyme; aspirin attacks the system.

In [[regeneration-and-degeneration-types-of-inflammation-change-with-aging|"Regeneration and Degeneration"]], Peat connected arachidonic acid to the diet-dependence of adult inflammation: "In adults, prostaglandins are known to be involved in many of the harmful effects of inflammation. They are formed from the polyunsaturated fats, linoleic acid and arachidonic acid, which we are unable to synthesize ourselves, so the adult's exposure to the prostaglandins is influenced by diet." A fat the body cannot make from scratch is, in modern adults, ubiquitous because it elongates from a fat the body also cannot make but does ingest in bulk.

## The Stress Cascade

Arachidonic acid is the most important PUFA-derived mediator of the stress response in Peat's framework, and the framework treats stress and inflammation as essentially the same process viewed from different angles. From [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]]: "Increasingly, the liberation of arachidonic acid from tissues during stress is seen as a central factor in all forms of stress, either acute (as in burns or exercise) or chronic (as in diabetes or aging). And, as the fat stores become more toxic, it seems that they more readily liberate the free fatty acids."

The framework's key claim is that the stress response in a PUFA-loaded organism is qualitatively different from the stress response in an organism whose tissues contain primarily saturated and omega-9 fats. Saturated fats released from adipose tissue during fasting or exercise provide energy and turn off further lipolysis through negative feedback. Arachidonic acid released from PUFA-loaded tissues amplifies the stress signal, generates inflammatory prostaglandins, and drives further lipolysis in a positive-feedback loop.

In [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]]: "In good health, especially in children, the stress hormones are produced only in the amount needed, because of negative feedback from the free saturated fatty acids, which inhibit the production of adrenalin and adrenal steroids... But when the fat stores contain mainly PUFA, the free fatty acids in the serum will be mostly linoleic acid and arachidonic acid... These PUFA stimulate the stress hormones, ACTH, cortisol, adrenaline, glucagon, and prolactin, which increase lipolysis, producing more fatty acids in a vicious circle. In the relative absence of PUFA, the stress reaction is self limiting, but under the influence of PUFA, the stress response becomes self-amplifying."

The aldosterone connection is one of the more underappreciated parts of the framework. From [[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|"When Energy Fails"]]: "Although many factors influence the production of the adrenal steroids, arachidonic acid, even without being converted to prostaglandins, is an important activator of aldosterone synthesis. Adrenalin, produced in response to a lack of glucose, liberates free fatty acids from the tissues, so when the tissues contain large amounts of the polyunsaturated fatty acids, the production of aldosterone will be greater than it would be otherwise." This connects arachidonic acid directly to hypertension and edema — not through prostaglandins but through the free fatty acid itself, before conversion. The PUFA-loaded tissue continuously primes the adrenal cortex toward aldosterone overproduction whenever lipolytic stress occurs, which in modern life is essentially continuously.

## Estrogen, Phospholipase, and the Mutual Amplification

The relationship between arachidonic acid and [[estrogen]] is one of the most operationally important parts of the framework. The two form a mutually reinforcing system: estrogen activates phospholipase A2, which liberates arachidonic acid; freed arachidonic acid is converted by COX to prostaglandins, particularly PGE2, which activate aromatase, which converts androgens to more estrogen. Each step amplifies the next. [[Progesterone]] inhibits the phospholipase, breaking the cycle at its starting point.

From [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]]: "Estrogen activates the phospholipase which liberates arachidonic acid, and progesterone inhibits that phospholipase." The same newsletter develops the preferential-liberation point: "Estrogen, with its known contribution to the formation of blood clots and edema and fibrosis and tumors, achieves part of its effect by maintaining a chronically high level of free fatty acids, preferentially liberating arachidonic acid, rather than saturated fatty acids."

In [[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|"Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer"]], Peat extended the mechanism to a broader set of inflammatory pathways: "Estrogen increases lipid peroxidation, and maintains a chronically high circulating level of free fatty acids, mainly PUFA, activates the phospholipases that release arachidonic acid from cells leading to formation of prostaglandins and isoprostanes, and increases the enzymes that form the inflammation-promoting platelet activating factor (PAF) while suppressing the enzymes that destroy it, and increases a broad range of other inflammatory mediators, interleukins, and NF-kappa B."

This is the connection point between arachidonic acid and the broader Peat framework on cancer, infertility, fibrosis, and the menstrual-cycle pathologies. Each estrogen surge is also an arachidonic-acid-liberation event, and each arachidonic-acid-liberation event is also a prostaglandin-driven aromatase-activation event. The cycle does not stop until either estrogen is reduced (through progesterone, hepatic glucuronidation, or aromatase inhibition by [[aspirin]]) or the supply of stored arachidonic acid is depleted (through [[pufa-depletion|years of PUFA depletion]]).

## Cell-Wide Permeability and Brain Injury

Arachidonic acid's effects are not limited to its role as a precursor for soluble eicosanoids. The free fatty acid itself, in concentrations achieved by phospholipase activation under stress, acts directly on cellular protein structure. From [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]]: "Linoleic acid and arachidonic acid, which are said to 'make the lipid membrane more permeable,' in fact make the whole cell more permeable, by binding to the structural proteins throughout the cell, increasing their affinity for water, causing generalized swelling, as well as mitochondrial swelling (leading to reduced oxidative function or disintegration), allowing more calcium to enter the cell, activating excitatory processes, stimulating a redox shift away from oxidation and toward inflammation, leading to either (inappropriate) growth or death of the cell."

The brain-injury data sharpens the point. From the same newsletter: "When the brain is injured, DHA and arachidonic acid contribute to brain edema, weakening the blood-brain-barrier, increasing protein breakdown, inflammation, and peroxidation, while a similar amount of stearic acid in the same situation caused no harm." A saturated fatty acid at equivalent concentration causes no damage; the highly unsaturated fatty acids produce the brain swelling that drives much of the secondary injury after stroke and traumatic brain injury. This is one of the mechanistic foundations for Peat's recommendation of [[progesterone]] in head injury — the steroid inhibits the phospholipase that would otherwise liberate the arachidonic acid that drives the secondary inflammation.

## Glucuronidation Suppression

One of the more elegant feedback loops in the PUFA framework involves arachidonic acid's suppression of the liver's primary detoxification pathway. From [[fats-functions-malfunctions|"Fats, Functions and Malfunctions"]]: "One of the essential protective functions that decline with aging is the liver's ability to detoxify chemicals, by combining them with glucuronic acid, making them water soluble so that they can be excreted in the urine. The liver (and also the intestine and stomach) efficiently process DHA by glucuronidation. Oleic acid, one of the fats that we synthesize ourselves, increases (about 8-fold) the activity of the glucuronidation process. However, this system is inhibited by the PUFA, arachidonic acid, and also by linoleic acid, in one of the processes that contribute to the accumulation of PUFA with aging."

The loop is self-reinforcing. The liver glucuronidates a wide range of substrates, including [[estrogen]] (a major route by which estrogen is inactivated and excreted) and the PUFA themselves. Arachidonic acid inhibits glucuronidation; impaired glucuronidation leaves more estrogen and more PUFA in circulation; more estrogen activates more phospholipase and liberates more arachidonic acid; more circulating PUFA accumulates in tissues and further suppresses glucuronidation. The loop closes on itself across decades, and is one of the mechanisms behind the age-related decline in detoxification capacity that figures so prominently in Peat's account of aging.

## Aging: Accumulation and Stress-Triggered Release

The lifespan dynamics of arachidonic acid are the same as the broader PUFA accumulation curve, with one additional twist: as the storage burden in adipose tissue rises, the threshold for lipolytic release falls. From [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]]: "As the fat tissues become more burdened with arachidonic acid, they release it more easily in response to moderately lipolytic stress signals. This could explain the increased levels of free fatty acids and lipid peroxidation that occur with aging. In animals that are 'deficient' in the polyunsaturated fatty acids, adrenalin doesn't have the lipolytic effect that it does in animals on the standard diet. With aging, there is not only a tendency to have chronically higher free fatty acids in the blood, but for those fatty acids to be more unsaturated. The phospholipids of mitochondria and microsomes become more unsaturated with aging."

The peroxidation dimension compounds the membrane-composition shift. From [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids"]]: "With aging, the highly peroxidizable fatty acids, arachidonic and docosahexaenoic acid, increase greatly in a variety of tissues, and lipid peroxidation increases with aging. Peroxidation slows mitochondrial respiration, lowering the metabolic rate. Caloric restriction slows the accumulation of the highly unsaturated fatty acids in mitochondria, and reduces peroxidation." Caloric restriction slows the accumulation rate; complete dietary PUFA elimination would reverse it on a four-year timescale (see [[pufa-depletion]]).

## Fibrosis and Vascular Leak

The fibrotic consequences of arachidonic acid liberation are central to Peat's account of why so many degenerative diseases share the same underlying tissue pathology. From [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]]: "The stress-induced liberation of arachidonic acid causes blood vessels to leak, and this allows fibrin to escape from the blood stream, into the basement membrane and beyond into the extracellular matrix, where it produces fibrosis. (Cancer, autoimmune diseases, and heart disease involve the same inflammatory, thrombotic, fibrotic processes as the nominal fibroses.)"

A single underlying lesion — accumulated PUFA being released as free arachidonic acid under chronic stress — produces the heterogeneous clinical pictures of cancer, autoimmunity, cardiovascular disease, and fibrosis as a discrete condition. The capillary-leak step is the bridge: arachidonic-acid-derived prostaglandins increase vascular permeability; serum fibrinogen extravasates; fibrin polymerizes in the interstitium; collagen deposition follows; the tissue stiffens. The same sequence runs in liver, lung, kidney, heart, breast, and almost any other organ that experiences chronic low-grade arachidonic acid release. From [[fibrosis|"Fibrosis"]]: "The accumulation of heavy metals and of the unstable unsaturated fats (linoleic, linolenic, and arachidonic acids) can be slowed or reversed by careful dietary choices. The calorie-restricted diets that slow the aging process reduce the accumulation of the unstable fats and the heavy metals."

## Why Peat Didn't Fear AA from Foods

The mainstream nutritional position on arachidonic acid is straightforward: arachidonic acid is the principal omega-6 fatty acid in animal tissues; it is the primary substrate for inflammatory eicosanoid synthesis; dietary arachidonic acid should be reduced to lower inflammatory markers; the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio of the diet should be lowered toward 1:1 or 2:1; supplemental fish oil should provide EPA and DHA, which compete with arachidonic acid for the same enzymes.

Each step has empirical support, but the policy implications run in a different direction than Peat's. The mainstream position implies that the chief operational targets are (1) reducing animal-food consumption and (2) adding fish oil supplements. The Peat framework inverts both. Reducing animal-food consumption while continuing to consume seed oils does little to lower tissue arachidonic acid because most of it comes from linoleic acid elongation. Adding [[fish-oil|fish oil]] adds a separate class of highly peroxidizable fats whose acute anti-inflammatory effect (through competition with arachidonic acid at COX) is achieved at the cost of long-term immunosuppression, lipid peroxidation, and the formation of neuroprostanes implicated in Alzheimer's pathology.

Peat's nuance, articulated repeatedly: arachidonic acid from animal foods in the context of an otherwise low-PUFA, high-saturated-fat diet is a minor problem. Arachidonic acid generated from chronic linoleic acid consumption in the context of a high-PUFA modern diet is a major problem. The two situations look identical on a "tissue arachidonic acid" lab value but reflect entirely different metabolic states. The egg yolk and the liver are foods; the seed oil is a slow tissue toxin that happens to feed the same pathway.

The framework is also clear that arachidonic acid in animal-source foods comes accompanied by cofactors that mitigate its effects: cholesterol, saturated fats that compete for membrane esterification, glycine in connective tissue, vitamin A and choline in liver, fat-soluble vitamins in egg yolk, the [[conjugated-linoleic-acid|CLA]] in ruminant fats that competes with the normal omega-6 series. The metabolic context determines what the molecule does.

## Clinical Implications: Aspirin and the Cascade

The PUFA framework treats [[aspirin]] as the most important pharmacological countermeasure to the arachidonic acid cascade. Aspirin's mechanism operates at multiple levels: it acetylates COX-1 and COX-2, blocking conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins and thromboxanes; it inhibits phospholipase activation, reducing the upstream liberation of arachidonic acid in the first place; through structural similarity to PUFA, it competes for binding sites throughout the cell. The broader Peat reading is that aspirin functions as a chemical surrogate for the absent [[mead-acid|Mead acid]] series, occupying the protective binding sites that an omega-9-dominant tissue would fill endogenously.

The cascade-level claim: "Linoleic acid, linolenic acid, arachidonic acid: Their toxicity is potentially prevented by the Mead acids, and their eicosanoid derivatives, which behave very differently from the familiar prostaglandins, as far as they have been compared; can be drastically reduced by dietary changes. Prostaglandins, prostacyclin, thromboxane: Formation is blocked by aspirin and other antiinflammatory drugs." Aspirin's range of benefits — cancer prevention, dementia protection, cardiovascular risk reduction, anti-fibrotic effects — follows from its capacity to interrupt the arachidonic acid cascade at multiple points simultaneously.

The fish oil approach to the same cascade — using EPA and DHA to competitively inhibit arachidonic acid metabolism — is a separate matter Peat treated with consistent skepticism. From [[the-great-fish-oil-experiment|"The Great Fish Oil Experiment"]]: "Despite the nutritional value of those vitamins, fish oils are generally much more immunosuppressive than the seed oils, and the early effects of fish oil on the 'immune system' include the suppression of prostaglandin synthesis, because the more highly unsaturated long chain fats interfere with the conversion of linoleic acid into arachidonic acid and prostaglandins. The prostaglandins are so problematic that their suppression is helpful, whether the inhibition is caused by aspirin or vitamin E, or by fish oil." The suppression is genuinely helpful in the short run, but the side effects of using more-unsaturated PUFA to suppress less-unsaturated PUFA accumulate into long-term immunosuppression and neurological damage. Aspirin achieves the suppression without the lipid burden.

The practical translation: minimize dietary linoleic acid (the rate-limiting input to endogenous arachidonic acid synthesis), tolerate the small amounts of arachidonic acid present in well-sourced animal foods, support glucuronidation and thyroid function so the liver can dispose of what does accumulate, suppress lipolysis with frequent small meals and the supportive triad of [[aspirin]], [[niacinamide]], and [[caffeine]], inhibit the phospholipase with [[progesterone]] when appropriate, and accept the multi-year timescale of full depletion. Avoiding eggs and liver while continuing to eat at restaurants frying with seed oils is approximately useless. Avoiding seed oils while eating eggs and liver is approximately what Peat recommended.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream nutritional and biochemical consensus on arachidonic acid differs from Peat's reading in several important ways.

**The direct-source-is-primary position.** Mainstream lipid biochemistry has tended to argue that tissue arachidonic acid content is determined primarily by direct dietary intake (from meat, eggs, and liver) rather than by endogenous synthesis from linoleic acid, based on isotope tracer studies showing low fractional conversion rates in healthy adults. Peat's framework requires the opposite — that the much larger absolute volume of dietary linoleic acid in modern diets, even at low conversion percentages, produces more tissue arachidonic acid than direct intake from animal foods. Both positions can find supporting data, and the empirical resolution depends on which population and which background diet is being studied.

**The omega-3/omega-6 ratio framework.** Mainstream nutrition treats the ratio of dietary omega-3 to omega-6 as the operational target. Peat's framework is largely uninterested in this ratio, on the grounds that adding more omega-3 to an already PUFA-loaded tissue compartment substitutes one set of inflammatory mediators for another set of equally peroxidizable substrates. The mainstream rejoinder is that omega-3 fats produce genuinely anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving mediators (resolvins, protectins, maresins) whose existence Peat largely did not address.

**The "AA is essential for development" position.** Pediatric nutrition has incorporated arachidonic acid into infant formula on the basis of its presence in breast milk and its role in neural development. Peat's framework treats this as an example of pathologizing the normal mammalian developmental state — newborns make their own unsaturated fats from glucose via the [[mead-acid|Mead acid]] pathway, and high tissue arachidonic acid in formula-fed infants reflects the formula's composition rather than a developmental need. Neither position has been tested in a randomized trial that would resolve the question.

**The prostaglandin-as-essential-mediator position.** Mainstream physiology treats arachidonic-acid-derived prostaglandins as essential signaling molecules in functions ranging from kidney perfusion to gastric mucus production to platelet function. The Peat framework treats them as overamplified analogs of what an omega-9-dominant tissue would produce from Mead acid derivatives. The pharmacological observation that aspirin (which blocks prostaglandin synthesis) produces broadly beneficial effects rather than the severe physiological derangements one would predict if prostaglandins were truly essential supports the Peat reading, but does not settle the question.

**The DGLA exception.** [[Dgla|DGLA]], the immediate precursor to arachidonic acid in the elongation pathway, is itself a substrate for 1-series prostaglandins (PGE1) that are largely anti-inflammatory. This complicates the linear "linoleic acid → arachidonic acid → bad" narrative — at the [[delta-5-desaturase]] step, DGLA can either be converted to arachidonic acid (feeding the 2-series inflammatory cascade) or be retained for PGE1 production. The relative activities of delta-5 and delta-6 desaturase, and the cofactors that modulate them, become critical regulatory nodes that the broader Peat framework does not directly address.

**The methodological concern.** Peat's case rests on a synthesis of mid-20th-century animal data, epidemiological observations interpreted causally, and mechanistic inferences from the broader PUFA framework. Direct head-to-head comparisons of arachidonic-acid-restricted versus PUFA-restricted diets in humans are not available.

## Data gaps

Several questions are not resolved by the Peat corpus and remain open within the framework.

**Quantitative conversion rates in humans.** The actual rate at which dietary linoleic acid is converted to tissue arachidonic acid is highly variable and depends on FADS polymorphisms, baseline diet, age, hormonal status, and stress. The framework's claim that endogenous conversion dominates over dietary intake is plausible but not directly demonstrated at population scale.

**Membrane versus free-pool dynamics.** Arachidonic acid exists in both phospholipid-esterified (storage) and free fatty acid (active signaling) pools, with continuous flux mediated by phospholipase and acyltransferase activity. The relative kinetics under varying stress, hormonal, and dietary conditions are incompletely characterized.

**The DGLA-AA branchpoint.** The factors that determine whether DGLA is converted to arachidonic acid (feeding inflammation) or retained for PGE1 production (anti-inflammatory) are not addressed in Peat's writings. Selectively favoring DGLA retention would be a potentially valuable intervention if the regulatory factors were understood.

**Genetic variation.** FADS1 and FADS2 polymorphisms produce substantial individual variation in the linoleic-acid-to-arachidonic-acid conversion pathway. Whether the framework's universal claims require modulation for different genetic backgrounds is not addressed.

**Tissue-specific eicosanoid endpoints.** Different cells and tissues preferentially produce different eicosanoids from the same arachidonic acid substrate — platelets favor thromboxane A2, endothelial cells favor prostacyclin, immune cells favor leukotrienes. The systemic effects of arachidonic acid liberation therefore depend on which tissues are doing the converting; the framework treats arachidonic acid liberation as broadly inflammatory without distinguishing the tissue-specific eicosanoid profile.

**The pregnancy-and-fetal-brain question.** The mainstream position that arachidonic acid is critical for fetal brain development has substantial clinical support. Whether the Mead-acid-substitute model proposed for the broader PUFA framework also handles the developing brain's specific arachidonic acid requirements is not addressed in Peat's corpus.

**Specialized pro-resolving mediators.** The post-2010 literature on resolvins, protectins, and maresins (omega-3-derived mediators that actively resolve inflammation) and lipoxins (omega-6-derived mediators that do the same) represents a more nuanced picture than the simple "inflammatory eicosanoid" framework. Whether these resolution-phase mediators support the mainstream reading or fit naturally into the Peat framework has not been worked out.

**Measurement and monitoring.** Standard lipid panels do not measure tissue or membrane arachidonic acid content. Red blood cell membrane fatty acid composition can be measured but is not in routine clinical use. The framework's recommendations are difficult to operationalize without measurable intermediate endpoints.
