# Alcohol

Alcohol is one of the oldest drugs in human civilization, and Ray Peat's assessment of it reflects that complexity. Unlike the cultural binary of temperance versus indulgence, Peat's position on ethanol was conditional, specific, and rooted in endocrinology rather than morality. Pure ethanol, in small amounts, can spare protein, promote immunity, and exert a genuine antioxidant effect. But the beverages through which most people consume ethanol deliver something far more consequential than the alcohol itself: [[estrogen]]. Beer contains estradiol produced by the yeast that ferments it. Wine carries estrogenic phenolics from grape skins and yeast alike. Even dark whiskey retains enough estrogenic residue to be harmful. "Small amounts of alcohol can have some good antioxidant effects," Peat wrote in email correspondence, "but beer, wine, and dark whiskey, etc., contain enough estrogen to be harmful." The practical result is a hierarchy that surprises most people: the "healthiest" way to drink, in Peat's framework, is the way most associated with alcoholism. Pure, colorless, highly distilled spirits are the safest form of alcohol, and the culturally respectable drinks of moderation, beer and wine, are the most estrogenic.

## Beer: An Estrogen Delivery System

Beer is the worst alcoholic beverage in Peat's framework, and the reason has nothing to do with calories or carbohydrates. The problem is biological. Yeast, the organism responsible for fermentation, produces [[estradiol]], the most potent human estrogen. This is not a trace contaminant or a theoretical concern. As Peat explained in his 1985 newsletter on immunity and yeast, "estradiol, the most powerful human estrogen, has been extracted from a yeast." When Andrew Murray asked on a [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] whether the breast development seen in male beer drinkers was caused by hops or environmental estrogen mimics, Peat's answer was direct: "Yeast can produce estradiol. They think the yeast fermenting is a major source of the breast development in beer drinkers. They are probably more harmful than the hops."

The hops compound the problem. Hops belong to the Cannabaceae family, and Peat regarded them as potent phytoestrogens. "Hops is a very powerful estrogen," he told listeners in a [[2013-07-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-heart-iii|2013 episode on heart health]]. "Almost every plant has some estrogenic material as a defense mechanism." Between the yeast-derived estradiol and the hop-derived [[phytoestrogens]], beer delivers a double estrogenic payload that no other common beverage matches.

The clinical consequences are visible and well-documented. In a [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 episode on hair loss and inflammation]], Peat connected the hormones in beer directly to two of the most recognizable features of heavy beer drinkers: "The hormones in beer, and to a lesser extent in wine, along with the estrogen from the yeast, will stimulate the adrenals to produce both estrogen and cortisol. Cortisol makes the big belly, and the estrogens contribute to breast growth." The "beer belly" is not simply a matter of excess calories being stored as visceral fat. It is an endocrine phenomenon driven by [[cortisol]] elevation, and the gynecomastia (male breast enlargement) that accompanies it is an estrogen effect independent of liver damage. For decades, mainstream medicine attributed male breast development in heavy drinkers to cirrhosis-mediated estrogen retention, but as Peat noted in his [[ray-peat-newsletters/immunity-hormones-and-yeast-infections|"Immunity, Hormones and Yeast Infections" (1985)]] newsletter, "enough estrogen was found in beer and wine to account for the condition, independently of any alcohol damage to the liver."

The cancer connection follows from the estrogen connection. In the same newsletter, Peat observed that "the increased rate of cancer which has been observed in beer drinkers might have something to do with estrogen," and proposed a natural experiment: "It would be interesting to compare the cancer incidence among bread eaters with the incidence in people who eat tortillas, pasta, or potatoes." The comparison is revealing because bread, like beer, is yeast-fermented, while tortillas and pasta are not. If yeast-derived estrogen contributes to cancer, then any yeast-fermented food becomes suspect.

Peat tested this on himself, inadvertently. In an email exchange on cancer, he reported: "Once when I had four ounces of beer daily for a few weeks several moles grew rapidly, and I realized it was probably the estrogen in the beer that was responsible, and two weeks after I stopped the moles dried up and fell off." Four ounces of beer, roughly a third of a standard bottle, consumed daily for a few weeks was enough to produce visible estrogenic effects on his skin. The dose was small. The biological response was not.

## Wine: A Lesser but Real Estrogenic Burden

[[Wine]] occupies a middle position in Peat's hierarchy. It shares beer's fundamental problem of yeast-derived estradiol, since Saccharomyces cerevisiae is the fermentation agent in both beverages. But wine lacks hops, removing one layer of the estrogenic assault. Red wine introduces its own complications through longer skin contact during fermentation, which extracts phenolic compounds, many of which function as phytoestrogens, along with significant quantities of [[histamine]]. White wine, with minimal skin contact, is less problematic than red, though it remains estrogenic through its yeast content.

The cultural prestige of wine as a health food rests largely on the resveratrol narrative, which Peat considered a case study in pharmaceutical marketing disguised as nutritional science. Resveratrol is a stilbene, structurally similar to diethylstilbestrol (DES), the pharmaceutical estrogen prescribed to millions of pregnant women before being recognized as a carcinogen. It is also a fungicidal compound produced by the grape vine as a defense chemical. A substance that is both structurally estrogenic and biologically a plant toxin is, in Peat's framework, precisely the wrong thing to celebrate as a longevity molecule.

## Spirits: The Least Harmful Form

When asked directly about the least estrogenic form of alcohol, Peat's answer was unambiguous: "Pure colorless highly distilled alcohol is the safest." Vodka is the paradigm case. The distillation process strips away the yeast-derived estrogens, phenolics, histamine, and other biologically active compounds that survive in fermented beverages. What remains is ethanol and water, which is the only context in which alcohol's genuine antioxidant properties can operate without being overwhelmed by its estrogenic baggage.

Peat qualified this recommendation: even pure distilled alcohol "can have antioxidant effects, but in some people it can interfere with the respiratory enzymes and lower blood sugar." The individual response to ethanol depends heavily on [[thyroid]] function. Someone with adequate thyroid activity metabolizes alcohol efficiently and maintains blood sugar stability. Someone with hypothyroid tendencies is more likely to experience the hypoglycemic, pro-oxidative effects. Dark-colored spirits, including aged whiskey and bourbon, retain enough estrogenic compounds from the barrel aging and production process to be meaningfully worse than clear spirits, placing them closer to wine than to vodka on Peat's hierarchy.

## Ethanol's Metabolic Effects

The pharmacology of ethanol itself, stripped of the estrogenic cargo of its typical delivery vehicles, reveals a substance with real biological complexity. Ethanol inhibits the oxidation of sugars, fats, and amino acids. As Peat described in his [[ray-peat-articles/sugar-issues|"Sugar Issues"]] article, "ethyl alcohol is a common potentially toxic substance that can have this effect, causing oxidative damage by allowing an excess of electrons to accumulate in the cell, shifting the cells' balance away from the stable oxidized state." The language is precise: alcohol does not directly oxidize tissues. Instead, it pushes cells into a [[reductive-stress|reductive state]] by interfering with the electron transport chain, and this accumulation of reducing equivalents is what produces the downstream damage.

This metabolic shift explains alcohol's relationship to fatty liver disease. In an email exchange on liver pathology, Peat wrote that "alcohol shifts metabolism away from oxidation, and that metabolic shift is responsible for aneurysms as well as fatty liver." The liver, deprived of its normal oxidative capacity, begins accumulating fat instead of burning it. The treatment Peat identified follows directly from the diagnosis: "Aspirin, [[thyroid]], vitamin D, and some of the flavonoids in [[orange-juice]] help to restore normal oxidation."

The interaction between alcohol and dietary [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] is especially dangerous. "If a person eats polyunsaturated fats, alcohol is more likely to cause oxidative reactions between iron and the fats," Peat warned. The combination creates a synergistic assault: alcohol damages [[mitochondria|mitochondrial]] respiration while PUFA provides abundant substrate for [[lipid-peroxidation]]. Conversely, animals raised on diets free of polyunsaturated fats proved remarkably resistant to alcohol's worst effects. In his [[ray-peat-articles/fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]] article, Peat described PUFA-free animals as "very hard to kill by trauma and a wide variety of toxins," noting specifically that "they weren't susceptible to various fibrotic conditions, including alcoholic liver cirrhosis." The implication is stark: a significant portion of what we attribute to alcohol toxicity may actually be PUFA toxicity, amplified by alcohol's metabolic disruption.

## Fructose as Protection

One of the most practical findings in Peat's discussion of alcohol is the protective role of [[fructose]]. In "Sugar Issues," he reported that "fructose has been known for many years to accelerate the oxidation of ethanol (by about 80%)." The mechanism works at the level of cellular redox chemistry: "Besides removing the alcohol from the body more quickly, it prevents the oxidative damage, by maintaining or restoring the cell's redox balance, the relatively oxidized state of the NADH/NAD+, lactate/pyruvate, and GSH/GSSH systems."

This means that drinking alcohol with fruit juice is not merely a matter of taste or social convention. Orange juice, with its high fructose content and additional protective [[bioflavonoids]], directly counteracts the metabolic disruption that ethanol produces. Fructose accelerates ethanol clearance by nearly double, prevents the reductive shift that causes oxidative damage, and restores the cellular redox ratios that alcohol disturbs. When Peat identified the safest context for drinking, "pure colorless highly distilled alcohol" with fructose, he was describing a vodka and orange juice, a screwdriver, the cocktail most dismissed by wine connoisseurs as lowbrow.

## The Nutritional Case for Alcohol

Peat recognized a genuine nutritional dimension to alcohol that the temperance tradition suppresses. In his [[ray-peat-newsletters/biological-balance-and-addictions|"Biological Balance and Addictions" (1991)]] newsletter, the single most comprehensive source in his corpus on the subject, he wrote that "alcohol can make a powerful and positive contribution to nutrition by sparing protein, and by modifying nutrition-related hormones." He noted pointedly that although some of this research had been published in the *Journal of the American Medical Association*, "the stigma attached to alcohol has prevented its adoption as a most useful drug/nutrient, even in terminal cancer patients, where its promotion of immunity could have great value." The parenthetical that followed was characteristically sharp: "The abusive stigmatizing of useful substances is an example of unhealthily exaggerated social cycles."

The protein-sparing effect is significant because protein is expensive metabolically and nutritionally. In contexts of illness, wasting, or limited food access, a substance that reduces the body's protein requirements while simultaneously promoting immune function has obvious therapeutic value. That this substance happens to be culturally stigmatized does not change its biochemistry.

## Addiction as Biology, Not Moral Failure

Peat's framework rejects the prevailing cultural model of addiction as a matter of willpower, character, or compulsive craving. In "Biological Balance and Addictions," he dismantled what he called "probably the biggest error in our popular culture of addiction": the idea that "craving is triggered by the presence of the substance in the body, and that cleansing the substance from the body will stop the craving." He described this belief as a cult with "ramifications into theories of obesity, allergy, sexual dependency, and violence," unified by "a distrust of the organism, a body-rejecting attitude that craving is necessarily bad, leading to a blurring of the difference between need and addiction."

The distinction between need and addiction is central. "The fact that something makes you feel better, and that you feel worse when you stop using it, shouldn't be taken as evidence of its 'addictiveness,'" Peat wrote, "but many people feel that way about thyroid hormone, coffee, vitamins, and even the most ordinary foods." Withdrawal symptoms are not evidence of addiction. They are evidence that the body had adapted to a substance and now must readapt to its absence. The physiological swing Peat described is a compensatory rebalancing, not a moral collapse.

This reframing has immediate therapeutic consequences. If alcoholism reflects an underlying endocrine or nutritional deficiency rather than a character disorder, then the treatment is to identify and correct the deficiency, not to demand abstinence through sheer resolve. As Peat observed, painters and musicians "have sometimes said that they use alcohol or other drugs to help them find their 'own way.' Moving against the tide of the culture is an adaptive effort, and they find some neurological support in drugs." He validated the impulse while questioning the vehicle: "They are right in struggling against the current of imbecile conformity to find some creative truth, but there are better ways to sustain our energy and adaptive capacity."

## Pregnenolone and Progesterone: Better Ways

The "better ways" Peat identified were hormonal, and the case studies he reported are among the most striking in his entire body of work.

The first involved a man who had struggled with alcoholism for thirteen years while unhappy in his work. Peat suspected a deficiency in [[pregnenolone]], the master precursor hormone from which [[progesterone]], [[DHEA]], cortisol, and the sex hormones are all synthesized. "A few minutes after he took 100 mg of pregnenolone, his depression and anxiety disappeared," Peat reported. "Later, he said 'that's the feeling I've been trying to get from alcohol, but it never worked.' Pregnenolone, more powerfully than alcohol, lowers cortisol while stabilizing other systems." The man had been self-medicating an endocrine deficiency with ethanol for over a decade. The actual substance he needed cost a few dollars and took effect in minutes.

The second case was more dramatic. A musician who had used heroin and alcohol for forty years, and had been continuously drunk for six weeks, came to Peat asking to sober up for an important job. He had serious cirrhosis of the liver. Peat gave him approximately 100 mg of [[progesterone]] dissolved in [[vitamin-e]] at bedtime, along with [[triiodothyronine]] (T3, the active form of [[thyroid]] hormone), reasoning that the musician's damaged liver was probably producing very little of it. The next morning, the man "was smiling and very happy looking. He said he had never had this experience before, of waking up without a hangover after heavy drinking."

The musician stayed sober, completed his performances, and continued taking progesterone over the following months, consuming roughly 30 grams in vitamin E over several months along with 50 micrograms of Cytomel (T3) morning and night. His doctors gave him magnesium injections. The outcome was remarkable: "His doctors noticed that his liver was getting smaller, and about a year and a half later they said it was normal, without cirrhosis." A liver destroyed by forty years of alcoholism regenerated under the influence of progesterone, thyroid hormone, and magnesium. Peat noted that "since then other people have noticed similar effects on inflamed and enlarged livers, when taking progesterone with vitamin E and thyroid."

The mechanism connecting these interventions is energy production. "Both progesterone and triiodothyronine have the function of increasing the tissue's energy supply, and in suitable doses can have a steadying, calming effect," Peat wrote. The correct dose of T3 with [[magnesium]] is, in his assessment, "a reliable treatment for insomnia, cramps, and anxiety, whether these symptoms are caused by fatigue, or aging, or alcohol withdrawal." The implication is that alcohol withdrawal syndrome shares a physiological basis with aging and chronic fatigue: all three reflect insufficient cellular energy production, and all three respond to the same hormonal interventions.

## The Intestinal Dimension

Alcohol's damage extends beyond direct metabolic disruption to the gut barrier. "Aging, stress, and heavy consumption of alcohol increase the permeability of the intestine, causing increased absorption of microbial toxins," Peat wrote. When the intestinal lining becomes permeable, bacterial [[endotoxin]] enters the bloodstream and triggers systemic inflammation, further burdening the liver and activating the [[serotonin]]-cortisol-estrogen cascade that Peat identified as a core engine of degenerative disease.

His remedies for intestinal permeability were characteristically practical: "Laxatives, carrot fiber (not carrot juice), activated charcoal, and a small amount of sodium thiosulfate decrease the formation and absorption of toxins, increasing the organism's adaptive capacity." The carrot salad, one of the most distinctive recommendations in the Peat dietary canon, serves as a gentle intestinal disinfectant that binds endotoxin and excess estrogen for excretion.

A related phenomenon, the "auto-brewery" syndrome, illustrates how yeast colonization of the gut can produce alcohol endogenously. As Peat described in a [[2019-09-10-jodellefit-jodellefit-how-to-fix-your-digestion-poop|2019 episode on digestion]], "around 1955 or 1960, there were cases of people thought to be drunkards because they reeked of alcohol. It turned out they had a little brewery in their stomach. If they ate starch, their enzymes produced enough sugar for yeast to grow, producing alcohol and carbon dioxide." The yeast-sugar-alcohol connection that makes beer estrogenic can, in susceptible individuals, establish itself inside the body.

## Alcohol, Thyroid, and Serotonin

The individual response to alcohol maps closely to thyroid status in Peat's framework. "Have you ever had a thyroid test or tried a thyroid supplement?" he asked one correspondent. "High [[serotonin]] activity is often present in hypothyroidism, and alcohol can probably provide temporary compensation for that." This observation suggests that some proportion of heavy drinking represents self-medication for undiagnosed [[hypothyroidism]]. The serotonin excess that accompanies low thyroid function produces anxiety, depression, and sleep disruption, and ethanol temporarily suppresses serotonin activity, providing relief that no other readily available substance matches.

The protective factors Peat identified against alcohol's harmful effects read like a summary of his entire dietary framework: "Protein, thyroid, sugars, and [[saturated fats]] are protective against both," he wrote, referring to alcohol and cannabis simultaneously. A well-nourished person with adequate thyroid function, consuming [[saturated fats]] rather than PUFA, and maintaining blood sugar through regular intake of simple sugars, is positioned to metabolize alcohol efficiently and resist its pro-oxidative effects. A hypothyroid person eating a PUFA-rich diet with unstable blood sugar is positioned to be damaged by even moderate drinking.

## Protective Strategies

Peat's practical recommendations for those who drink can be assembled from his various discussions:

The hierarchy of beverages is clear: pure distilled spirits (vodka, clear rum, unaged spirits) are least harmful; white wine is preferable to red; beer is worst. "Pure colorless highly distilled alcohol is the safest," Peat stated. Dark spirits, including whiskey and bourbon, retain estrogenic compounds and should be avoided.

Consuming fructose alongside alcohol accelerates ethanol clearance by approximately 80% and prevents the reductive shift that causes oxidative damage. Orange juice is the ideal accompaniment, providing fructose, [[bioflavonoids]], and [[aromatase]] inhibitors simultaneously.

Avoiding PUFA reduces the substrate for the iron-mediated lipid peroxidation that alcohol promotes. A diet built on saturated fats is protective in the context of alcohol consumption, as it was for the PUFA-free animals that proved resistant to alcoholic liver cirrhosis.

Adequate thyroid function and protein intake provide the metabolic foundation for efficient alcohol metabolism. Maintaining blood sugar through regular meals of simple sugars prevents the hypoglycemia that amplifies alcohol's pro-oxidative effects.

Carrot fiber, activated charcoal, and laxatives can reduce the intestinal permeability and endotoxin absorption that heavy drinking promotes.

For those struggling with alcohol dependence, Peat's case studies suggest that pregnenolone (100 mg), progesterone (in vitamin E), T3, and magnesium address the underlying endocrine deficiencies that drive the compulsion to drink. These are not substitutes for addressing the situational and emotional dimensions of addiction, but they correct the physiological substrate on which the addiction rests.
