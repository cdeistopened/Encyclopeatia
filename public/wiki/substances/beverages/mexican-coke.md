# Mexican Coke

No single food item better illustrates the distance between Ray Peat's framework and mainstream nutrition than Mexican Coca-Cola. A mass-produced soft drink---one that public health authorities condemn as liquid candy---turns out, on Peat's analysis, to deliver a precise combination of metabolically supportive factors: [[sucrose]] for immediate fuel, [[caffeine]] for metabolic stimulation, [[carbon dioxide]] for respiratory support, and coca leaf extractives that Peat considered genuinely bioactive. The reaction it provokes in conventional dietitians is itself instructive: the horror reveals how deeply anti-sugar dogma has replaced metabolic reasoning.

## More Than Sugar Water

The standard explanation for why Peat communities favor Mexican Coke focuses on its use of cane sugar instead of high-fructose corn syrup. This is true but incomplete. In a [[38-in-defense-of-mexican-coke-the-authoritarian-metabolism-symbols-of-an-alien-sky-astrology|Generative Energy episode dedicated to the topic]], Peat made a distinction that most of his followers overlook: "I think sucrose has great virtues therapeutically. In the case of an animal in shock, they would pour a mouthful of honey in its mouth, and in a few minutes, the animal would be up. But if you compare just coffee with added sucrose, I don't think you get quite the effects you do with Mexican Coke. I think the coca leaf extractives are really biologically active."

This is a remarkable statement. Peat was not merely defending sugar or caffeine---he was arguing that Coca-Cola's proprietary coca leaf extract contributes genuine anti-inflammatory activity beyond what sugar and caffeine alone provide. Modern Coca-Cola uses decocainized coca leaf extract processed by the Stepan Company in New Jersey, and while the cocaine alkaloid is removed, other bioactive compounds from the coca leaf remain. Peat's assessment, based on his understanding of plant biochemistry, was that these residual extractives contribute meaningfully to the beverage's biological effects.

He reinforced this in his email correspondence: "The coca leaf and cola seed extracts are valuable anti-inflammatories. The amount of phosphate is very small compared to the amount in meat, fish, beans, nuts, and grains."

## The Sugar Question: Sucrose Versus HFCS

The distinction between Mexican Coke (cane sugar) and American Coke (HFCS) matters, though Peat was characteristically nuanced. "I prefer Mexican coke with real sugar (it tastes very different)," he wrote, "but metabolically there isn't much difference." This surprised many followers who assumed the sucrose-versus-HFCS distinction was fundamental.

The practical difference lies primarily in taste and absorption kinetics. [[Sucrose]] is a disaccharide---glucose and [[fructose]] bound together in a 50-50 ratio---that must be cleaved by the enzyme sucrase in the small intestine before absorption. HFCS (typically 55% fructose, 45% glucose) delivers these sugars already separated, allowing faster absorption that may overwhelm hepatic fructose processing in susceptible individuals. The metabolic endpoint is similar, but the rate of delivery differs.

What matters far more than the sweetener type is the role of sugar itself. Peat defended sugar against decades of demonization, particularly the anti-fructose campaign led by Robert Lustig. In a [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|2011 episode on sugar myths]], Peat and his hosts discussed fructose's protective effects on the liver---the exact organ Lustig claimed fructose was destroying. Fructose supports liver [[glycogen]] storage, suppresses [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]], and provides the substrate for efficient [[oxidative metabolism]]. A Coca-Cola delivers this fructose alongside glucose, caffeine, and CO2 in a form people will actually consume regularly.

## Carbonation as Carbon Dioxide Therapy

The bubbles in Coca-Cola are not merely aesthetic. Dissolved [[carbon dioxide]] is a metabolically active gas that Peat considered protective across numerous biological systems. CO2 inhibits [[lactic-acid]] production, supports mitochondrial function, helps maintain proper [[pH]], and counteracts the effects of [[hyperventilation]] and metabolic alkalosis.

Peat noted that "in a crisis situation, it (or baking soda in water) can be helpful"---referring to carbonated water as a source of CO2. The carbonation in Mexican Coke creates carbonic acid upon dissolution, providing a mild CO2 load that supports cellular respiration. This is not a dramatic therapeutic dose, but as a regular dietary habit, the cumulative effect of consuming CO2-rich beverages aligns with Peat's broader emphasis on maintaining adequate tissue carbon dioxide levels.

The carbonation also improves gastric emptying and may enhance sugar absorption, making the energy from the drink available more quickly. For someone experiencing [[hypoglycemia]] or stress-induced energy depletion, the combination of rapidly available sugar and dissolved CO2 addresses two metabolic problems simultaneously.

## The Phosphoric Acid Question

The most common objection to Coca-Cola from within Peat communities concerns its phosphoric acid content. Excess [[phosphate]] relative to [[calcium]] promotes [[parathyroid hormone]] secretion and can contribute to [[calcification]] of soft tissues while weakening bones. Peat acknowledged the concern but placed it in proportion: "The amount of phosphate is very small compared to the amount in meat, fish, beans, nuts, and grains."

A 12-ounce Coca-Cola contains roughly 40-50mg of phosphorus. A serving of chicken breast contains over 200mg. A cup of black beans contains nearly 250mg. In the context of a diet that includes these foods, the phosphoric acid in an occasional Coke is quantitatively trivial. The real phosphate problem in the modern diet comes from processed meats, grains, and legumes---not soft drinks.

That said, pairing Mexican Coke with calcium-rich foods like [[milk]], [[cheese]], or [[ice cream]] makes metabolic sense. The calcium offsets whatever phosphoric acid the drink contains, and the combination of sugar, caffeine, calcium, and protein creates a more complete metabolic support package.

## The Quality Question

Peat was alert to the possibility that not all Coca-Cola labeled as "Mexican" actually contains cane sugar. A chemist who tested products in Mexico reported that "the Mexican Coca-Cola in Mexico will have sugar sometimes and corn syrup other times," as discussed in a [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 episode on sugar]]. The consistency of labeling is not guaranteed, particularly for products sold domestically in Mexico versus those exported to the United States.

When asked whether Coke had any advantage over Pepsi, Peat responded with characteristic honesty: "Does Coke have any advantage over Pepsi nutritionally or allergy wise? That has been my impression, but I haven't seen any organized tests of it." His preference for Coca-Cola was partly experiential and partly based on the coca leaf and cola nut extracts unique to the Coca-Cola formula.

## Context and Proportion

Mexican Coke works within Peat's framework not as a health food but as a practical metabolic tool. It provides sugar when blood sugar is falling, caffeine when metabolic rate needs support, CO2 when tissues need respiratory assistance, and anti-inflammatory coca extractives as a bonus. It is shelf-stable, widely available, and---critically---something people enjoy consuming.

This last point matters more than purists might admit. A theoretically optimal beverage that sits unconsumed provides zero benefit. Fresh-squeezed [[orange-juice]] offers superior nutrition---potassium, bioflavonoids, vitamin C, and no phosphoric acid---but it requires refrigeration, preparation, and has a limited shelf life. Mexican Coke requires nothing but a bottle opener.

The principle extends beyond the brand. Any cane-sugar soda with caffeine and carbonation delivers similar metabolic support. But Coca-Cola's unique coca leaf and cola nut extractives, which Peat considered "really biologically active," give it a specific advantage that generic sugar-caffeine-CO2 combinations may not match.

The deeper lesson of Mexican Coke in Peat's framework is that metabolic reasoning trumps food moralism. A drink condemned by every public health institution on earth turns out to align more closely with sound metabolic principles than organic kombucha (which promotes [[endotoxin]] and [[lactic-acid]]), green juice (which delivers [[iron]] and goitrogens), or coconut water (which provides a problematic potassium-to-sodium ratio). Understanding why requires abandoning the calorie-counting paradigm and thinking instead about what the body actually needs to maintain [[oxidative metabolism]].
