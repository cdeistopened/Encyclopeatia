# Coffee

Most people who search "is coffee good for you" expect a hedged answer: moderate consumption may have benefits, but watch the caffeine. Ray Peat's answer was not hedged. Coffee was, in his view, one of the most protective substances available to a human being, and the case against it was built almost entirely on experimental errors and cultural prejudice. He drank five or more cups a day for decades, recommended it to patients who were afraid of it, and argued in his 2006 newsletter [[caffeine-a-vitamin-like-nutrient-or-adaptogen-questions-about-tea-and-coffee-cancer-and-other-degenerative-diseases-and-the-hormones|"Caffeine: A Vitamin-Like Nutrient, or Adaptogen" (2006)]] that caffeine itself should be reclassified from drug to nutrient. "Nutrition textbooks flatly describe caffeine as a drug, not a nutrient, as if it were obvious that nutrients can't be drugs," he wrote. "Any of the essential nutrients, if used in isolation, can be used as a drug, for a specific effect on the organism that it wouldn't normally have when eaten as a component of ordinary food." The distinction between drug and food, in his framework, was not a fact about chemistry but an artifact of institutional habit. Coffee was food, and unusually good food at that.

Peat placed coffee alongside [[oranges|orange juice]] and [[milk|milk]] as one of three foundational beverages in a pro-metabolic diet. Where orange juice supplied [[sugar]], [[bioflavonoids]], and [[aromatase]] inhibition, and milk supplied [[calcium]], [[protein]], and lactose, coffee supplied [[magnesium]], [[niacinamide|niacin]], [[caffeine]], and a set of protective mechanisms that touched nearly every system Peat cared about: [[serotonin]] suppression, [[progesterone]] elevation, [[estrogen]] opposition, [[cancer]] protection, [[iron]] chelation, and support for [[thyroid]]-driven [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]].

## A Nutritional Source, Not a Stimulant

The mainstream framing of coffee treats it as a delivery vehicle for caffeine and nothing else. Peat's framing began by correcting this. Coffee is a meaningful source of minerals and B vitamins. In his [[coffee|email exchanges on coffee]], he noted that "dry instant coffee is close to 0.5% magnesium, so a cup of strong coffee has about 40 mg," and that coffee contains "mostly niacin" alongside smaller amounts of other essential nutrients. In a [[2019-03-19-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-health-of-the-human-body|2019 *One Radio Network* interview]], he confirmed that even decaffeinated coffee, "if it is decaffeinated with carbon dioxide rather than solvents," retains its nutritional profile: "Coffee contains potassium, magnesium, niacin, and B vitamins. It is a significant nutritional source."

The magnesium content alone is clinically relevant. Five cups of strong coffee provide roughly 200 mg of magnesium, a substantial fraction of the daily requirement. The niacin content connects coffee to the broader purine metabolism that Peat considered central to understanding caffeine's effects.

Peat did not consider instant coffee inferior to brewed. "The antioxidants in very fresh coffee might have some special value," he wrote, "but I think instant coffee is on average just as good as brewed coffee." The critical variable was not brewing method but whether coffee was consumed with food. "It's important not to drink coffee on an empty stomach," he warned, "since it increases the metabolic rate, and can deplete glycogen stores." Coffee increases the cellular consumption of [[glucose]], and when glucose is unavailable, the body compensates by releasing [[adrenaline]] and [[cortisol]]. The shaky, anxious feeling some people attribute to caffeine sensitivity is often a blood sugar crash masquerading as a drug reaction.

## Caffeine and the Purine System

Peat's argument for caffeine as nutrient rested on its place in purine chemistry. "To talk about caffeine, it's necessary to talk about uric acid," he wrote in 2006. "Uric acid, synthesized in the body, is both a stimulant and a very important antioxidant, and its structure is very similar to that of caffeine." Purines are the building blocks of DNA and RNA, but they also function as signaling molecules. "In general, substances related to purines are stimulants, and substances related to pyrimidines are sedatives."

Caffeine is trimethyl xanthine: the purine xanthine with three methyl groups attached. Theophylline, the related compound in tea, is dimethyl xanthine. The body has enzyme systems that add and subtract these methyl groups; babies given theophylline can convert it into caffeine. This interconvertibility was one reason Peat resisted the clean separation between endogenous molecule and exogenous drug. Uric acid restrains the enzyme xanthine oxidase, which under stress becomes "a dangerous source of free radicals." Caffeine also restrains this enzyme, reinforcing Peat's case that it functions as a protective compound rather than a mere stimulant.

## Cancer Protection

The evidence that coffee protects against cancer was among the strongest in Peat's case for the beverage. "Many studies over the last 30 years have shown caffeine to be highly protective against all kinds of carcinogenesis, including estrogen's carcinogenic effects on the breast," he wrote in 2006. "There are substances in the coffee berry besides caffeine that protect against mutations and cancer, and that have shown strong therapeutic effects against cancer. Although many plant substances are protective against mutations and cancer, I don't know of any that is as free of side effects as coffee."

The most dramatic evidence came from animal studies. In a [[2014-06-02-its-rainmaking-time-it-s-rainmaking-time-energy-protective-materials|2014 *It's Rainmaking Time* episode on energy-protective materials]], Peat described research from the 1970s: "A monograph showed the cancer-protective effects of caffeine. Researchers found that adding caffeine to carcinogenic smoke reduced cancer rates in mice from 95% to 5%. It protected against radiation, viruses, and chemical carcinogens." The magnitude of that reduction, from near-universal cancer to near-zero, is striking. Even accounting for the difference between animal models and human epidemiology, an effect that large demands attention.

The mechanism connects to one of coffee's less-discussed properties: PARP inhibition. PARP is an enzyme system that repairs genetic damage, but its activation drains cellular energy so severely that it can kill the cell. "Niacin and caffeine can inhibit this enzyme sufficiently to prevent this characteristic kind of cell death, without preventing the normal cellular turnover; that is, they don't produce tumors by preventing the death of cells that aren't needed." This is a delicate balance. Caffeine protects cells from energy depletion during stress without conferring the kind of immortality that characterizes cancer cells. It is protective without being promotional.

## Liver Protection and Heavy Metal Chelation

Coffee's relationship to the liver was one of Peat's most frequently cited arguments for the beverage. In a [[2021-08-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-august-2021-kmud|2021 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]], he stated: "Coffee is the only one I feel safe with. It has been shown to reduce the bound heavy metals in kidneys and other crucial organs, especially the liver. Many studies show that coffee drinkers have low ALT, an indicator of liver stress. Heavy coffee drinkers have an extremely low incidence of all kinds of liver diseases, including liver cancer."

The chelation mechanism distinguishes coffee from conventional chelating agents. Standard chelators "pull metals from safe stores into circulation," redistributing toxins rather than eliminating them. Coffee works within the body's own excretory stream, supporting the outward movement of cadmium, aluminum, and excess iron. In his 2006 newsletter, Peat confirmed that "coffee drinkers have been found to have lower levels of cadmium in their kidneys than people who don't use coffee, and coffee is known to inhibit the absorption of iron by the intestine, helping to prevent iron overload."

He elaborated on this in the 2021 KMUD episode: "Meat, and liver in particular, has very low calcium and very high iron. It's extremely important to have coffee when you're eating those to block the iron absorption." If you are not getting enough calcium to suppress [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]], he added, "you're going to retain more iron." Drinking coffee with meals containing red meat or organ meats was, in Peat's view, a simple strategy for preventing the iron accumulation he associated with [[aging]], cancer, and degenerative disease. Murray asked whether capsules of instant coffee would work. "Sure," Peat replied.

## Serotonin, Progesterone, and the Hormonal Effects

Coffee's hormonal effects constitute some of its most significant actions in Peat's framework. Caffeine suppresses serotonin, elevates progesterone, and mimics several of the actions of thyroid hormone. These are not incidental side effects; they are the core of coffee's therapeutic value.

On serotonin, Peat wrote: "The observation that coffee drinkers have a low incidence of suicide, for example, might be physiologically related to the large increase in suicide rate among people who use the newer antidepressants called 'serotonin reuptake inhibitors.'" The SSRIs work by preventing serotonin from being cleared from the synapse. Coffee does the opposite: it "stimulates the uptake (inactivation or storage) of serotonin, increases metabolic energy, and tends to improve mood. In animal studies, it reverses the state of helplessness or despair, often more effectively than so-called antidepressants." Peat placed coffee's anti-serotonin action in a broader physiological context: "Coffee's effects on serotonin are very similar to carbon dioxide's, and the thyroid hormone's." All three promote the clearance of serotonin, and all three support oxidative metabolism. The parallel was not coincidental in Peat's view; it reflected a deep alignment between coffee's chemistry and the body's own pro-metabolic signaling.

On progesterone, the connection is direct. "Women spontaneously drink more coffee premenstrually," Peat wrote, "and since caffeine is known to increase the concentration of progesterone in the blood and in the brain, this is obviously a spontaneous and rational form of self-medication, though medical editors like to see things causally reversed, and blame the coffee drinking for the symptoms it is actually alleviating." Women drink more coffee premenstrually because their bodies need more progesterone, and coffee helps provide it. "Some women have noticed that the effect of a progesterone supplement is stronger when they take it with coffee." Peat attributed this synergy to caffeine's ability to "locally activate thyroid secretion by a variety of mechanisms, increasing cyclic AMP and decreasing serotonin in thyroid cells."

As an aromatase inhibitor, coffee reduces the conversion of androgens into estrogen. This connects it to the broader anti-estrogenic strategy that runs through Peat's entire framework. Coffee, aspirin, progesterone, and thyroid hormone all oppose estrogen through overlapping pathways.

## Blood Sugar, Metabolic Rate, and the Hypoglycemia Myth

The most common objection to coffee in health-conscious circles is that it raises cortisol and destabilizes blood sugar. Peat's response was that this objection confuses the effects of coffee with the effects of fasting. "The bad effects ascribed to coffee usually involve administering large doses in a short period of time," he wrote. "Experimenters typically ignore essential factors." The increased metabolic rate that caffeine produces "increases the cellular consumption of glucose, so experiments that study the effects of coffee taken on an empty stomach are measuring the effects of increased temperature and metabolic rate, combined with increased adrenaline (resulting from the decrease of glucose), and so confuse the issue of caffeine's intrinsic effects."

The solution is simple: drink coffee with food. "Often, a woman who thinks that she has symptoms of [[hypoglycemia]] says that drinking even the smallest amount of coffee makes her anxious and shaky," Peat wrote. "Sometimes, I have suggested that they try drinking about two ounces of coffee with cream or milk along with a meal. It's common for them to find that this reduces their symptoms of hypoglycemia, and allows them to be symptom-free between meals." The sugar and fat in the meal provide the substrate that coffee's metabolic acceleration requires. Without it, the acceleration burns through glycogen and triggers a stress response.

Sarah Murray, Peat's frequent interviewer on KMUD, illustrated the practical logic in a [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|2011 episode on sugar]]: "The fact that he's adding white sugar to his coffee is probably protective. Coffee lowers your blood sugar. If he has pure black coffee, he'll end up lowering his blood sugar." Adding sugar to coffee is not an indulgence; it is a metabolic strategy that prevents the very stress response that gives coffee a bad reputation.

In a 2019 interview, Peat confirmed: "Coffee, aspirin, and correcting thyroid help you go longer without food stress." Coffee extends the window of stable blood sugar between meals by supporting the same metabolic processes that thyroid hormone supports. The people who do worst with coffee are typically [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid]]. In his email correspondence, Peat noted: "I suspect that [coffee withdrawal] happens mostly with hypothyroidism, because in the 1970s I averaged dozens of cups a day, and thought about it as soon as I woke up, then suddenly after I took some thyroid, I didn't feel any need for it." The compulsive quality that people associate with caffeine addiction may reflect the body's attempt to compensate for inadequate thyroid function.

## Vascular Protection, Parkinson's, and Platelet Effects

Coffee protects the cardiovascular system through its effects on platelet aggregation and vascular tone. Caffeine "tends to restrain stress-induced platelet aggregation," Peat wrote. When platelets clump, they release serotonin, which produces "vascular spasms and increased blood pressure, blood vessel leakiness and inflammation." Caffeine interrupts this cascade at multiple points: it inhibits the aggregation itself and also inhibits the serotonin release that follows. On blood pressure, Peat challenged the standard claim that coffee raises it: "Drinking plain water can cause an extreme rise in blood pressure, especially in old people, and eating a meal (containing carbohydrate) lowers blood pressure." The experimental context matters more than the isolated pharmacological effect.

Coffee's promotion of carbon dioxide production further supports vascular function. Caffeine "increased the blood volume in the brain, while decreasing the resistance of the vessels," Peat noted, an effect he attributed to "stimulation of brain metabolism and the consequent increase in carbon dioxide, which dilates blood vessels." Higher CO2 means lower vascular resistance, better perfusion, and less work for the heart.

The epidemiological protection against Parkinson's disease was among the strongest findings Peat cited. A study "found that high uric acid levels seem to protect against the development of Parkinson's disease," but "coffee drinking, which lowers uric acid levels, nevertheless appeared to be much more strongly protective against Parkinson's disease than uric acid." Coffee's protective mechanism goes beyond simple antioxidant action, likely involving the serotonin suppression and metabolic support that pervade its other effects.

## Fibrocystic Breast Disease: The Myth

One persistent myth holds that caffeine causes fibrocystic breast disease. Peat addressed this directly: "One of the most popular misconceptions about caffeine is that it causes fibrocystic breast disease. Several groups demonstrated pretty clearly that it doesn't." He went further, arguing that "caffeine's effect on the breast resembles that of progesterone, opposing estrogen's effects." The myth likely arose from the same causal reversal Peat identified in premenstrual coffee consumption: women with estrogen-dominant breast conditions may drink more coffee because their bodies are seeking the progesterone-like, anti-estrogenic effects of caffeine. The coffee is the treatment, not the cause.

## How Peat Made His Coffee

In [[Carbon Dioxide, Redox Balance, and The Ketone Body Ratio with Ray Peat  [Generative Energy #26]|*Generative Energy* #26 on CO2 and redox]], Danny Roddy asked Peat to describe his brewing method. The answer revealed the same metabolic logic that informed everything else. Peat used a paper filter and started with water below boiling: "When the water is getting hot, I start and moisten the coffee without really getting it very hot. As the water gets hotter and hotter, I keep adding. So the first coffee that drips through is cool and very mild tasting, with very little caffeine in it, but it doesn't evaporate or degrade some of the delicate aldehyde-type flavor molecules." These volatile aromatics are "supposedly the most antioxidant part and they're destroyed if you put the boiling water directly onto the grounds." Only at the end did he use boiling water: "Just the last half cup or so of water," to extract maximum caffeine.

The result was a brew optimized for both flavor compounds and caffeine, with mineral extraction throughout the temperature range. "I think it comes out all along the way," he said of the magnesium. "I think it's loose so you get it even if you soak it and make cold coffee." His daily five cups delivered antioxidants, minerals, vitamins, and caffeine in proportions that no supplement could replicate.
