# Wine

Wine occupies a peculiar position in Ray Peat's framework: celebrated by mainstream health culture as the sophisticated, cardioprotective drink, yet revealed on closer inspection to be a delivery vehicle for [[estrogen]], [[histamine]], and a compound structurally similar to one of the most notorious pharmaceutical carcinogens ever manufactured. The "French Paradox"---the idea that red wine explains French longevity despite a high-fat diet---is, in Peat's analysis, a marketing narrative built on estrogenic molecules masquerading as antioxidants.

## The Estrogenic Burden of Fermented Drinks

The fundamental problem with wine is not the alcohol itself but what accompanies it. As Peat wrote, "small amounts of alcohol can have some good antioxidant effects, but beer, wine, and dark whiskey, etc., contain enough estrogen to be harmful." The estrogenic load in wine comes from multiple sources: the [[phytoestrogens]] naturally present in grapes, the phenolic compounds extracted from skins and seeds during fermentation, and the yeast itself.

Peat explained the yeast problem in a [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 discussion on inflammation]]: "It's not only the phytoestrogens from the hops, but the yeast required for alcoholic drinks. Yeast produces estradiol, the powerful human hormone." While he was speaking primarily about [[beer]], the same yeast biology applies to wine fermentation---Saccharomyces cerevisiae produces [[estradiol]] as a byproduct of its metabolism regardless of the beverage it is brewing.

Red wine compounds the problem. Longer skin contact during fermentation extracts more tannins, polyphenols, and phenolic compounds---many of which function as phytoestrogens in human tissue. Red wines also "contain their own histamine, which can cause symptoms in a lot of people," as Peat noted in a [[2014-12-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-you-are-what-you-eat-2014|2014 episode on diet]]. The histamine in red wine can trigger headaches, flushing, nasal congestion, and gut disturbance---symptoms often blamed on sulfites but more accurately traced to biogenic amines produced during fermentation.

White wine, with minimal skin contact, contains less histamine and fewer phenolic compounds. It remains estrogenic through its yeast-derived estradiol and grape-derived phytoestrogens, but it represents a meaningfully lesser burden than red.

## Resveratrol: A Stilbene in Disguise

Peat devoted an entire September 2009 newsletter to dismantling the resveratrol narrative, just one year after GlaxoSmithKline purchased the resveratrol-focused biotech company Sirtris for $720 million. The scientific and financial infrastructure promoting resveratrol as a longevity molecule was enormous, and Peat saw through it with characteristic precision.

The structural argument alone should give pause. "Resveratrol is a stilbene, similar in structure to diethylstilbestrol, DES, the famously toxic carcinogenic pharmaceutical," Peat wrote. DES was prescribed to millions of pregnant women before being discovered to cause cancer and reproductive abnormalities in their children. That resveratrol shares the same molecular backbone---the stilbene scaffold---is not a trivial coincidence. Stilbenes interact with [[estrogen]] receptors precisely because their geometry mimics estradiol's.

Resveratrol's biological origin reinforces the concern. It is "a defensive fungicidal antibiotic, or phytoalexin," Peat explained, "so it's reasonable that it would be perceived as a stressor by yeast. It is also a phytoestrogen." The grape vine produces resveratrol to kill fungi. When humans consume it, the compound acts as both a cellular stressor and an estrogenic signal---two properties that Peat's framework identifies as fundamentally aging.

The longevity gene claims that fueled the resveratrol industry rested on a misunderstanding of yeast biology. The Sir2 gene, activated by resveratrol, was said to extend yeast lifespan. But as Peat pointed out, citing Kennedy et al. (2005) and Fabrizio et al. (2005), "increased Sir2 activity shortens the actual chronological lifespan of non-dividing yeast." The apparent "longevity" was an increase in replicative capacity---more cell divisions---which is not the same as longer life. In mammals, increasing SirT1 (the human equivalent) "might contribute to the development of cancer."

> "Resveratrol inhibits the formation of progesterone," Peat noted, connecting it directly to the suppression of the body's primary anti-aging, anti-stress hormone. A compound that blocks [[progesterone]] synthesis while activating estrogen receptors is, by Peat's criteria, a pro-aging substance regardless of what it does to sirtuins.

## Niacinamide: The Anti-Resveratrol

One of the more striking contrasts in Peat's newsletter was between resveratrol and [[niacinamide]]. Where resveratrol suppresses Sir2 activity was claimed to extend life, niacinamide was shown by resveratrol advocates to have the "opposite effect"---suppressing Sir2. Yet niacinamide "protects nerves, vascular cells, insulin-producing cells in the pancreas, and a variety of other types of cell from cell death produced by lack of oxygen, excitotoxicity, endotoxin, and a variety of stressors and toxins." Peat summarized the relationship: "Niacinamide acts in many ways as a negation of resveratrol."

Where resveratrol interferes with insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells and suppresses mitochondrial respiration, niacinamide protects mitochondrial function and extends the replicative lifespan of human cells in culture. Where resveratrol promotes [[nitric oxide]] production---which Peat considered a central pro-inflammatory mediator that poisons mitochondrial respiration---niacinamide blocks endotoxin-induced nitric oxide formation. The two substances represent opposing metabolic philosophies: resveratrol suppresses energy production in the name of "stress resistance," while niacinamide supports it.

## The Calorie Restriction Parallel

The resveratrol story is inseparable from the broader calorie restriction narrative. Peat drew a devastating parallel to the early fish oil longevity claims: researcher Alex Comfort "watched the rats that were being given the fish oil in their food. The food smelled bad, and the rats ate it only when they were very hungry, so they ate less and were leaner than the animals that were getting the standard diet. Their increased longevity was the result of avoiding obesity." The same reasoning, Peat argued, applied to resveratrol's apparent benefits---it made animals eat less and weigh less, and the resulting caloric deficit, not any magical gene activation, produced the observed effects.

## Alcohol's Conditional Toxicity

Setting aside wine's specific problems, Peat recognized that alcohol's metabolic impact depends heavily on individual context. "People have very different reactions to it, probably depending on [[thyroid]] activity," he observed. "It can have an antioxidant effect, but it can also cause [[hypoglycemia]] with pro-oxidative effects." Someone with robust thyroid function metabolizes alcohol more efficiently and maintains blood sugar better than someone with hypothyroid tendencies.

The interaction with dietary fat is critical. "Heavy drinking inhibits cellular respiration and sets up an inflammatory process, involving [[iron]], which will still be harmful, but less so than in the presence of [[PUFA]]." Alcohol combined with [[polyunsaturated-fats]] creates a synergistic oxidative assault---the alcohol damages mitochondrial function while the PUFAs provide abundant substrate for [[lipid-peroxidation]].

One of Peat's more striking observations involved a friend who was addicted to both morphine and alcohol. Starting [[progesterone]] at bedtime and stopping the wine, "he said it was the first time he didn't have a hangover in the morning." This suggests that progesterone's anti-stress, pro-metabolic effects can address some of the physiological dysfunction that drives alcohol dependence---and that wine's estrogenic properties may actively worsen the addictive cycle by opposing progesterone.

## If You Must Drink

Peat's hierarchy was unambiguous: "Least estrogenic? Pure colorless highly distilled alcohol is the safest." Vodka, gin, or white rum deliver ethanol without the estrogenic compounds, histamine, and phytoalexins that survive in wine and [[beer]]. Mixed with [[orange-juice]] or another fruit juice, clear spirits provide sugar alongside the alcohol, helping prevent the hypoglycemia that amplifies alcohol's pro-oxidative effects.

If wine specifically is the only option, white is preferable to red---less histamine, fewer phenolic phytoestrogens, less skin-derived resveratrol. Small amounts with food, particularly food containing [[calcium]], [[saturated-fat]], and [[gelatin]], provide some metabolic buffering. But the foundational message remains: the compounds celebrated as wine's health benefits---the polyphenols, the resveratrol, the "antioxidants"---are precisely the compounds that make it problematic.
