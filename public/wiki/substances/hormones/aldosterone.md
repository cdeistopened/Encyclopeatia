# Aldosterone

Aldosterone is the adrenal steroid that lets the kidney trade potassium for sodium. Conventional endocrinology treats it as a tidy salt-balance regulator: blood pressure falls or sodium drops, the kidney releases renin, the renin-angiotensin cascade synthesizes angiotensin II, angiotensin II tells the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex to make aldosterone, aldosterone tells the distal nephron to reabsorb sodium and excrete potassium, blood volume rises, blood pressure normalizes, and the system shuts itself off. Ray Peat read the same hormone differently. In his framework aldosterone is a stress hormone — chemically a mineralocorticoid, functionally a marker of adrenergic drive, inflammation, and energy failure — and the conditions that elevate it chronically are precisely the conditions that medicine usually creates while trying to lower blood pressure: salt restriction, diuretics, low-sodium "heart-healthy" diets, hypothyroidism, estrogen dominance, PUFA-loaded membranes, and the catecholaminergic state these jointly produce. The salt-retention is real, but it is downstream. The thing to lower is not sodium intake. The thing to lower is the signal that called for the sodium retention in the first place.

That inversion of cause and effect is the load-bearing claim of this article. Peat argued, and Tom Brewer demonstrated clinically in obstetrics, that salt restriction *increases* the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), and that chronic RAAS activation is the actual upstream driver of vascular damage, cardiac and renal fibrosis, magnesium and potassium wasting, capillary leakiness, sympathetic overactivation, and the inflammatory tail that constitutes most late-life chronic disease. The drugs mainstream cardiology uses to suppress aldosterone — ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, eplerenone — work in the direction Peat predicted; they are imitating progesterone. The framework reaches the same destination by eating salt to taste, restoring thyroid function, raising progesterone, and lowering polyunsaturated fat. Foundational mechanisms — the cortisol cascade, progesterone's broader steroidogenic role, the estrogen mechanism — are referenced through [[cortisol]], [[progesterone]], and [[estrogen]] rather than restated here.

## Biochemistry

Aldosterone is synthesized in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex, from cholesterol via pregnenolone and progesterone. The committed enzyme is aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2), present only in the glomerulosa. Cortisol is governed by ACTH from the pituitary; aldosterone is governed primarily by angiotensin II and by extracellular potassium concentration, with ACTH playing a secondary, mostly acute role. The two can dissociate under stress — chronic stress eventually pushes both, with the protective sex steroids (DHEA, progesterone, the androgens) suppressed.

The classical target is the principal cell of the distal nephron, where aldosterone induces the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) and the Na/K ATPase that drive sodium into the blood and potassium and hydrogen ions into the urine. The net effect is sodium and water retention, potassium and magnesium wasting, and metabolic alkalosis at the extreme.

What Peat emphasized — and what the older "salt-balance hormone" framing did not predict — is that the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) is expressed far beyond the kidney: on cardiomyocytes, cardiac fibroblasts, vascular smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, macrophages, hippocampal neurons, colonic epithelium, and adipocytes. Aldosterone acting through the MR on these non-renal tissues is responsible for the cardiac fibrosis, vascular remodeling, inflammation, and oxidative stress that constitute most of its long-term harm. The hormone's pulse follows a circadian curve that peaks around dawn; the night-time rise is exaggerated in the elderly and in salt-restricted subjects, driving Peat's "nocturnal stress cluster": "Aldosterone secretion increases during the night, and its rise is greater in [the aged]... During aging, salt restriction can produce an exaggerated nocturnal rise in aldosterone" ([[water-swelling-tension-pain-fatigue-aging|"Water: swelling, tension, pain, fatigue, aging"]]).

## The Low-Sodium-Diet Paradox

The single observation that anchors Peat's reading is that low-sodium diets *raise* aldosterone, not lower it. The mechanism is straightforward: dietary sodium restriction drops plasma volume slightly; renal perfusion drops; the juxtaglomerular cells release renin; renin cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; ACE cleaves angiotensin I to angiotensin II; angiotensin II constricts vessels, raises blood pressure, and tells the zona glomerulosa to make aldosterone. The body has done exactly what physiology textbooks describe — detected a sodium deficit and corrected it by retaining more of what remains. What the textbooks do not emphasize is that this correction carries substantial downstream costs: hypertension, magnesium and potassium wasting, capillary leakage, oxidative damage to cardiac tissue, fibrosis of the heart and kidneys, and a chronic adrenergic state that disturbs sleep, raises blood viscosity, and accelerates atherosclerosis.

Peat made the point repeatedly. With mineral specificity: "Since a very low sodium diet increases the loss of magnesium, by increasing aldosterone synthesis, simply increasing the amount of sodium in the diet can help some people to balance their minerals and minimize stress" ([[calcium-and-disease-hypertension-organ-calcification-shock-vs-respiratory-energy|"Calcium and Disease"]]). Structurally: "It has been known for many years that decreasing sodium intake causes the body to respond adaptively, increasing the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). The activation of this system is recognized as a factor in hypertension, kidney disease, heart failure, fibrosis of the heart, and other problems" ([[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, energy, metabolic rate, and longevity"]]).

The clinical observation that pinned this down was the cyclic edema of premenstrual women. The 1970s gynecologic advice was salt restriction with or without a thiazide diuretic; in Peat's direct experience, hundreds of women followed that advice and continued to swell. From [[the-edema-of-stress|"The Edema of Stress"]] (1989): "Seeing that salt restriction never helped women with cyclic edema, I explained the mechanisms of water regulation, first to just one woman. Within a month, she was salting her food to taste (i.e., salting it heavily in the premenstrual week), and having no trouble with edema. Since then (1978) I have explained this to many women, and it always works for estrogen-induced cyclic edema, and also for the edema of pregnancy induced by excess estrogen." Detail at [[pms]].

The broader physiology corroborates. Salt restriction raises catecholamines (norepinephrine excretion 77% higher in one cited trial, VMA 40% higher), raises serotonin synthesis, raises PAI-1, raises prolactin, decreases insulin sensitivity, decreases vascular compliance, and produces in the elderly a disturbed sleep architecture that itself raises night-time cortisol and aldosterone further. The "BP-lowering" benefit, when measurable, is a few millimeters of mercury at the cost of these systemic disturbances. From [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, energy, metabolic rate, and longevity"]]: "Salt restriction, according to a review of about 100 studies (Alderman, 2004), lowers the blood pressure a few points. But that generally doesn't relate to better health. In one study (3000 people, 4 years), there was a clear increase in mortality in the individuals who ate less salt. An extra few grams of salt per day was associated with a 36% reduction in coronary events."

The volume-and-albumin story closes the loop. Sodium ions associate with the negative charges on serum albumin; together they exert the colloid osmotic pressure that holds water inside the blood vessel. Without adequate sodium the albumin's tethering of water weakens, water leaves the vessel, blood volume falls, the kidney senses it, and RAAS fires. From [[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|"When Energy Fails"]]: "While increased salt in the diet tends to lower both aldosterone and VEGF, reducing the leakiness of blood vessels, sodium also has a direct effect that tends to prevent the leakage of water and albumin out of the blood vessels, helping to maintain the blood volume which is needed to perfuse the kidneys, preventing them from producing signals to increase blood pressure and aldosterone."

## What Else Raises Aldosterone

The RAAS is not the only switch. Peat catalogued substances and states that activate aldosterone independently of, or in synergy with, the renin path. **Serotonin** directly stimulates the zona glomerulosa: "It stimulates the production of both cortisol and aldosterone. It also activates aldosterone secretion by way of the renin-angiotensin system. Angiotensin is an important promoter of inflammation" ([[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|"Tryptophan, serotonin, and aging"]]). **Prolactin** — elevated in hypothyroidism, estrogen excess, and pregnancy — compounds aldosterone secretion and worsens edema. **Nitric oxide** does it counterintuitively: "One of the things it does is increase aldosterone, the adrenal salt-regulating steroid, which produces inflammation and fibrosis as a side effect... too much aldosterone gives you high blood pressure" ([[2015-10-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-current-trends-on-nitric-oxide|October 2015 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]]). **Arachidonic acid** activates aldosterone synthesis even before conversion to prostaglandins; adrenaline liberates free fatty acids in glucose deficit, and "when the tissues contain large amounts of the polyunsaturated fatty acids, the production of aldosterone will be greater than it would be otherwise" ([[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|"When Energy Fails"]]). **Estrogen** drives the menopausal surge: "An increase in the blood aldosterone content in menopause appears to be due to the hyperestrogenic phase (the first menopausal phase in women) and estrogen-stimulated aldosterone synthesis" (Bekker & Svechnikova 1981, cited in [[water-swelling-tension-pain-fatigue-aging|"Water: swelling, tension, pain, fatigue, aging"]]). **Growth hormone, ACTH, vasopressin, and catecholamines** all push aldosterone upward acutely; the chronic adrenergic state of aging is the chronic aldosterone state of aging. **Hypothyroidism** acts indirectly through the estrogen-to-progesterone ratio and the lowered metabolic rate that drops blood volume. **Coronavirus spike protein** occupies and downregulates ACE2, shifting balance toward angiotensin II and aldosterone: "ACE2 is a defensive, anti-inflammatory enzyme that destroys angiotensin... Things that block the AT1 receptor (like Losartan) also increase the ACE2 enzyme, which is protective" ([[2020-03-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-coronavirus|March 2020 coronavirus episode]]).

The list maps onto Peat's broader stress cluster. Anything that produces glucose deficit, free fatty acid mobilization, sympathetic activation, inflammation, or estrogen elevation will, sooner or later, route through aldosterone.

## Progesterone as Endogenous Aldosterone Antagonist

Progesterone and aldosterone are both derived from pregnenolone, both bind the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR), and they oppose each other at it. Progesterone occupies the MR without activating it strongly, displacing aldosterone and blunting its sodium-retentive, potassium-wasting, fibrogenic action. Functionally, progesterone is the body's built-in MR antagonist — a spironolactone secreted by the corpus luteum, the placenta, the adrenal cortex, and the brain.

Peat treated this as a central fact of physiology. From [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, energy, metabolic rate, and longevity"]]: "Progesterone's antagonism to aldosterone has been known for many years, and the synthetic antialdosterone drugs are simply poor imitations of progesterone." From the email on atrial fibrillation: "Progesterone and those other steroids have opposite effects (progesterone is a natural aldosterone antagonist, too)" ([[sources/emails/heart.md|Peat email on the heart]]).

The historical primacy is important. From [[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|"When Energy Fails"]]: "Even before aldosterone was identified, progesterone's role in regulating the salts, water, and energy metabolism was known, and after the functions of aldosterone were identified, progesterone was found to protect against its harmful effects." Hans Selye demonstrated that progesterone "will normalize sodium retention even when there is no aldosterone at all, following removal of the adrenal glands." The body is not arranged so that aldosterone makes sodium retention possible and progesterone "antagonizes" it — progesterone is the default sodium-and-water-regulating steroid; aldosterone is the emergency override. When steroidogenic resources are adequate, progesterone keeps the system stable. When they are not — when stress, PUFA, or estrogen has shunted pregnenolone toward cortisol — aldosterone is what the adrenal can still make, and the consequences read out in the long tail of degenerative disease.

The estrogen-to-progesterone ratio is therefore a more reliable readout of aldosterone activity than aldosterone itself: "The ratio of estrogen to progesterone — regardless of age or gender — is an important factor in regulating minerals and water, cell energy metabolism, and blood pressure" ([[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|"When Energy Fails"]]). Mechanism detail at [[progesterone]]. [[Pregnenolone]] enters one step upstream: "Pregnenolone isn't a hormone, but it normalizes the steroid hormones, preventing excess cortisol and helping to normalize aldosterone" ([[sources/emails/cancer.md|Peat email on cancer]]).

## Spironolactone, Eplerenone, ACE-Is, ARBs

Mainstream cardiology arrived at the same conclusion by the longer route. RALES (1999, spironolactone in severe heart failure), EPHESUS (2003, eplerenone post-MI), and EMPHASIS-HF (2011, eplerenone in mild HFrEF) showed mortality reductions from aldosterone-receptor antagonists that exceeded what BP-lowering alone would predict. The mechanism cardiology settled on is the one Peat had been writing about for thirty years: aldosterone is directly fibrogenic at the myocardium and renal vasculature through the MR expressed on cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and vascular smooth muscle.

**Spironolactone (Aldactone)** is a non-selective MR antagonist, structurally similar to progesterone, with off-target action at the androgen and progesterone receptors (the source of its gynecomastia profile in men). It is the agent Peat referred to most often: "the synthetic antialdosterone drugs are simply poor imitations of progesterone." **Eplerenone (Inspra)** is selective. **ACE inhibitors** (lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril) block conversion of angiotensin I to II. **ARBs** (losartan, valsartan, candesartan) block AT1R directly, leaving the protective ACE2 / Angiotensin 1-7 arm intact — Peat singled this class out as particularly clean throughout the COVID newsletters.

Where mainstream and framework converge: Peat endorsed lisinopril for hypertension and heart failure on the explicit basis that it interrupts the angiotensin-aldosterone-TGF-β axis without immune suppression. Detail at [[fibrosis]] and [[heart-disease-protocol]]. Where they diverge: the framework would rather raise progesterone and lower the upstream stress signals (low sodium, hypothyroidism, PUFA, estrogen) than block a single receptor. Drug therapy points the right direction through a narrower pipe.

Cyproheptadine combines several relevant blockades in one molecule: "Cyproheptadine, 2 to 4 mg at bedtime, would help with his sleep as well as the cancer. It also has calcium blocking action, aldosterone antagonism, and antagonizes serotonin's antidiuretic effect" ([[sources/emails/cyproheptadine.md|Peat email on cyproheptadine]]). Drospirenone in newer combination oral contraceptives also has weak anti-aldosterone activity, but as the [[pms]] page notes, "the package it comes in is wrong" — the appropriate target is endogenous progesterone, not a synthetic progestin riding piggyback on ethinyl estradiol.

## Clinical Applications

**PMS edema and cyclic water retention.** The luteal phase is the diagnostic window. Estrogen elevates aldosterone; progesterone is supposed to oppose it; when progesterone collapses — because thyroid is low, because PUFA loads the membranes, because chronic stress shunts pregnenolone toward cortisol — the residual estrogen drives aldosterone unopposed. The result is bloating, breast tenderness, three-to-five-pound weight gain in a week, irritability, headaches, and sleep disturbance. Peat's intervention pattern, established in 1978: salt to taste (heavily in the premenstrual week), progesterone supplementation, thyroid support, magnesium, and dietary PUFA reduction. Full protocol at [[pms]].

**Pregnancy and preeclampsia.** Pregnancy requires nearly doubling blood volume by the third trimester; volume expansion requires sodium plus serum albumin plus thyroid hormone. The standard American diet plus "watch your salt" obstetric advice prevents that expansion; the underperfused kidney releases renin; aldosterone retains sodium in tissues rather than blood, producing edema; the placenta is underperfused; the fetus is hypoxic; the maternal sympathetic nervous system activates; preeclampsia results. Tom Brewer demonstrated this in tens of thousands of pregnancies between 1950 and 1980 and was largely responsible for the gradual abandonment of low-salt-plus-diuretics obstetric treatment. From [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, energy, metabolic rate, and longevity"]]: "He explained that sodium, in association with serum albumin, is essential for maintaining blood volume. Without adequate sodium, the serum albumin is unable to keep water from leaving the blood and entering the tissues." Full protocol at [[pregnancy-nutrition]].

**Heart failure and cardiac fibrosis.** The application where Peat and mainstream cardiology most fully agree. HFpEF is characterized by diastolic stiffening; the histological substrate is interstitial fibrosis driven by the angiotensin-aldosterone-TGF-β axis acting on cardiac fibroblasts through the MR. Both angiotensin II and aldosterone directly induce TGF-β in cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts; the RALES / EPHESUS / EMPHASIS-HF mortality benefits exceed what BP lowering alone would predict because the anti-fibrotic effect compounds over years. From [[fibrosis]]: "aldosterone retains sodium, raises blood pressure, and *also* directly activates cardiac fibroblasts through the mineralocorticoid receptor expressed on those cells." Full detail at [[heart-disease]] and [[heart-disease-protocol]].

**Hypertension.** The framework reading: the kidney is signaling — via renin — that its perfusion is inadequate. The cause is usually low blood volume (which usually means low sodium, low albumin, or both), not high blood volume. The framework response is to raise blood volume by raising sodium and protein, restore thyroid function so the liver can make adequate albumin, raise progesterone, and lower the catecholamine and free-fatty-acid background that drives renin. Where pharmacotherapy is needed, ACE-Is and ARBs are the cleanest options. The fear of dietary salt in mainstream cardiology rests on epidemiological associations Peat considered statistically weak and mechanistically backwards. See [[heart-disease]].

**Menopause and post-viral COVID syndromes.** Aldosterone elevates in the perimenopausal "hyperestrogenic phase" (Bekker & Svechnikova 1981). Detail at [[menopause]] and [[menopause-protocol]]. In COVID, ACE2 downregulation shifts the renin-angiotensin balance toward angiotensin II and away from the protective Angiotensin 1-7 arm; losartan, candesartan, and the anti-serotonin agents (cyproheptadine, ketanserin) cluster as protective in Peat's reading because they all reduce the angiotensin-aldosterone-inflammation cascade.

**Atrial fibrillation.** Aldosterone-driven magnesium and potassium wasting destabilizes cardiac rhythm. From Peat's email correspondence on the heart: "Vitamins D and K, and calcium are important for stabilizing the heart rhythm... Progesterone and those other steroids have opposite effects (progesterone is a natural aldosterone antagonist, too). Thyroid is essential for helping cells to retain magnesium" ([[sources/emails/heart.md|Peat email on the heart]]).

**Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome).** The one place where the mainstream framing — that aldosterone itself can be the primary disease, in the form of an adrenal adenoma or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia — has the strongest evidence. The framework treatment would still involve progesterone, salt, and the metabolic substrate, but in the case of an autonomously secreting adenoma, surgical resection is reasonable and reliably curative.

## Counter-arguments

**The DASH-Sodium and SPRINT trials.** DASH-Sodium (Sacks et al., NEJM 2001) showed low-sodium DASH lowered BP more than standard DASH; SPRINT (NEJM 2015) showed aggressive BP lowering (target SBP <120 mmHg) reduced cardiovascular events. Mainstream cardiology takes both as evidence that lower sodium and lower BP are causal goods. The framework response: DASH-Sodium measured BP, not mortality or fibrosis, and the difference was a few millimeters of mercury at the cost of the systemic disturbances (catecholamines, serotonin, prolactin, PAI-1, insulin resistance) Peat catalogued. SPRINT used ACE-Is, ARBs, CCBs, and thiazides — all of which lower aldosterone or its downstream effects, all of which the framework endorses in direction. The trial did not test whether the *same* event reduction could be achieved by raising salt, thyroid, and progesterone.

**Aldosterone is necessary for life.** Patients with Addison's disease die without adrenal steroid replacement. The framework does not argue that aldosterone is uniformly harmful; it argues that *chronic elevation in response to stress signals* is the disease state.

**Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome).** The exception where the mainstream surgical view holds. Roughly 5-13% of carefully screened hypertensives have inappropriately autonomous aldosterone secretion; in those patients, spironolactone (in bilateral hyperplasia) or adrenalectomy (in adenoma) is appropriate. The framework critique applies to the larger "essential hypertensive" population in whom aldosterone is elevated *appropriately* to upstream stress signals.

**The "salt-sensitive hypertensive" subgroup.** Roughly a third of hypertensives show meaningful BP responses to salt loading. Peat's response: salt sensitivity correlates with the same metabolic disturbances (hypothyroidism, low magnesium, PUFA load, low albumin) that drive RAAS activation. The sensitivity is downstream; fix the upstream metabolism rather than restricting salt in everyone.

**Heart-failure pharmacology actually works.** Modern HFrEF is treated with beta-blockers, ACE-Is, ARBs, MRA antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, and ARNI, with substantial mortality reductions across multiple large RCTs. The reconciliation: mainstream HF pharmacotherapy is mostly aimed at suppressing the RAAS and the sympathetic nervous system — exactly what the framework also wants — and the framework's quarrel is with the prior steps (salt restriction, diuretics), not with the modern MRA / ACE-I / ARB combinations.

## Data gaps

**No head-to-head trial of Peat's protocol against guideline-directed HF therapy.** A randomized comparison of (high-salt, high-protein, thyroid + progesterone + niacinamide + low PUFA) versus (ACE-I + beta-blocker + spironolactone + SGLT2 inhibitor) in NYHA II-III heart failure has never been run and is unlikely to be funded.

**Aldosterone-vitamin K interaction.** Both aldosterone excess and vitamin K deficiency contribute to cardiac fibrosis and vascular calcification. Whether they act independently or one potentiates the other is not specified in the corpus. See open question at [[fibrosis]].

**Dose-response of progesterone as an MR antagonist.** Progesterone's affinity for the MR is roughly 4-10x lower than aldosterone's, and the systemic level required for meaningful MR blockade in vivo is not the same as the level required for endometrial protection or sleep. The clinical doses Peat recommended (transdermal 30-300 mg/d, oral 100-300 mg/d) are larger than gynecologic doses and may be the relevant ones for cardiac and renal fibrosis; titration to MR effect has not been systematically studied.

**Cardiac MR genomic vs non-genomic effects.** Aldosterone acts on cardiac fibroblasts through genomic MR signaling and through faster non-genomic membrane effects (GPR30, EGFR). The fraction of cardiac fibrogenesis attributable to each pathway is not settled; whether progesterone blocks both equally is uncertain.

**Aldosterone in long COVID.** The mechanism (ACE2 downregulation, shift toward angiotensin II) is well-described, but the longitudinal trajectory of aldosterone and the response to MR antagonism in post-acute COVID syndrome have not been mapped at scale.

**Primary aldosteronism prevalence.** Recent series suggest Conn syndrome may account for 5-13% of unselected hypertensives. The boundary between "appropriate RAAS activation" (the framework's reading) and "inappropriate autonomous secretion" (the surgical reading) is not as crisp as either side claims.
