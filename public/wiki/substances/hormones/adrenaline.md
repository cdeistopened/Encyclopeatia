# Adrenaline

Adrenaline is the body's emergency mobilization hormone—the chemical signal that says "we need energy now, regardless of long-term cost." In Ray Peat's framework, chronic adrenaline elevation is not a sign of vitality but of metabolic failure. When cells have adequate [[glucose]], [[thyroid]] hormone, and [[oxygen]], they do not need adrenaline to function. Its chronic presence indicates that the normal energy systems have failed and the body has shifted to emergency backup mode—a state that cannot be sustained without tissue destruction.

As Peat explained in a discussion of sleep and glycogen, "Without enough glycogen, stress hormones ([[cortisol]] and adrenaline) rise. Stress hormones catabolize fat and muscle for energy." The surge of energy adrenaline provides comes directly from breaking down the body's own structure.

## The Hypoglycemia Trigger

The most common trigger for adrenaline release is [[hypoglycemia]]—low blood sugar. A healthy person stores seven to eight ounces of glucose as glycogen in liver, muscles, and brain. When these stores are depleted—through fasting, inadequate carbohydrate intake, or impaired glycogen storage—blood sugar falls and adrenaline surges to mobilize alternative fuel.

As Peat described, "Hypoglycemia is a powerful activator of the stress system." The adrenaline released in response to low blood sugar activates lipolysis—the release of [[free fatty acids]] from adipose tissue. These fatty acids then block glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]], worsening the cellular fuel shortage that triggered the adrenaline release in the first place.

The vicious cycle is precise: hypoglycemia → adrenaline → lipolysis → free fatty acids → blocked glucose use → more hypoglycemia → more adrenaline. Breaking the cycle requires providing "enough glucose to stop the stress reaction."

## The PUFA Amplification

Not all fatty acids released by adrenaline-driven lipolysis are equally harmful. As Peat emphasized in a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 episode]], "Free fatty acids activate adrenaline, ACTH, cortisol, TSH, and glucagon. These hormones increase release of more free fatty acids." But critically, "only *polyunsaturated* fatty acids have the terrible anti-energy effects."

[[PUFA|Polyunsaturated fats]] stored in adipose tissue are preferentially released during stress. Once circulating, they suppress [[thyroid]] function, block glucose oxidation, promote [[inflammation]], and activate more stress hormone release. A person with high tissue PUFA stores enters a self-amplifying stress cycle that someone with lower PUFA stores—fed on saturated fats—would not experience to the same degree.

This explains why the same stressor affects different people differently. The person with decades of vegetable oil consumption has loaded their fat tissue with the very molecules that, once released, perpetuate stress. The person eating [[saturated fat]] and [[coconut oil]] releases fatty acids that do not create the same vicious feedback.

## Cortisol Follows Adrenaline

Adrenaline is the first responder; [[cortisol]] follows within minutes to sustain the emergency response. As Peat described in a [[2008-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-and-polyunsaturated-fatty-acids|2008 episode]], "When fatty acids don't provide adequate energy, the body produces cortisone. Cortisone usually follows a surge of adrenaline by a few minutes."

Cortisol is catabolic—it breaks down tissues to provide fuel. "Cortisone breaks down tissues—muscles first, thymus can dissolve in hours." The emergency mobilization that adrenaline initiates becomes tissue destruction if sustained. The [[thymus]], the immune organ most sensitive to stress hormones, can atrophy dramatically within days of chronic stress activation.

## Insomnia as a Daytime Problem

One of Peat's most practical insights connected adrenaline to sleep. Insomnia, he argued, is "a daytime disorder presenting at night." When daytime carbohydrate intake is insufficient to maintain liver glycogen through the night, blood sugar falls during sleep, triggering adrenaline and cortisol release that wakes the person.

The solution is not sleeping pills but adequate daytime nutrition—particularly carbohydrate intake that maintains glycogen stores. A bedtime snack of [[milk]] and fruit, providing sugar and [[calcium]] (which suppresses [[parathyroid hormone]], another nighttime stressor), often resolves insomnia that pharmaceuticals cannot address.

## The Catecholamine-Heart Connection

Adrenaline's effects on the heart illustrate the damage chronic stress causes. As Peat discussed in his newsletter *The Generality of Adaptogens*, catecholamines drive fatty acid oxidation in heart tissue. When those fatty acids are polyunsaturated, the rapid oxidation produces [[lipid peroxidation]] products that directly damage cardiac cells.

Carnitine analogs (betaines) protect against this catecholamine-induced cardiac damage, as do saturated fats. The combination of chronic stress, high PUFA intake, and inadequate protective factors creates the conditions for the [[heart disease]] that medicine then attributes to saturated fat and cholesterol.
