# Adrenaline

Adrenaline is the body's acute stress signal — the one that gets there first, in seconds, and announces itself as a pounding heart, cold sweat, tunnel vision, and the conviction that something has gone wrong. In Ray Peat's framework it is also one of the most misread hormones in physiology. The mainstream reading places adrenaline at the center of the "fight or flight" response and treats its surges as either situational (a near-miss in traffic) or psychiatric (panic disorder). Peat read the same surges, in the same people, as metabolic emergencies — specifically, as the body's response to falling blood sugar. The pounding heart at 3 AM, the morning anxiety before breakfast, the hand tremor an hour after a "healthy" salad, the chest tightness during a cold plunge: in Peat's analysis these are not character flaws or sleep disorders or anxious temperaments. They are adrenaline rescuing the glucose supply. Fix the glucose supply and the adrenaline subsides. This single reframe is the page's reason for existing as a separate hub from [[cortisol]] and [[stress]] — Peat consistently treated adrenaline as the *acute* stress signal (minutes, blood sugar emergencies) and cortisol as the *chronic* one (hours to days, glycogen exhaustion and tissue cannibalization), and the interventions for each look different.

## What adrenaline is

Adrenaline (epinephrine, in American pharmacology) is a catecholamine hormone secreted by the medulla of the adrenal gland. It is synthesized from the amino acid tyrosine through a short pathway — tyrosine to L-DOPA to [[dopamine]] to noradrenaline to adrenaline — with the final methylation step performed almost exclusively in the adrenal medulla under permissive control of [[cortisol]] reaching the medulla through the adrenal portal circulation. Its half-life in plasma is two to three minutes; this is the hormonal-system equivalent of a flare, not a fixture.

Peat consistently distinguished adrenaline (the circulating hormone released from the adrenal gland into the bloodstream) from noradrenaline (the closely related catecholamine that functions primarily as the neurotransmitter of the sympathetic nervous system). Both substances appear in his writing, often together — "noradrenalin and serotonin, and of other stress-related hormones" — and both contribute to the sympathetic, adrenergic state. But adrenaline is the hormone of the *systemic* alarm: when something has been deemed by the body to require a coordinated, whole-organism response, the adrenal medulla floods the bloodstream with adrenaline, and every tissue in the body that carries an adrenergic receptor responds at once. Noradrenaline can fire one nerve at a time. Adrenaline cannot.

What adrenaline does, mechanically, is mobilize energy. It accelerates the heart and dilates the airways, constricts blood vessels in the skin and gut and dilates them in skeletal muscle and the heart, dilates the pupils, and — the part of its action Peat returned to over and over — instructs the liver to break down glycogen and release glucose, instructs the fat cells to break down triglycerides and release [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]], and instructs the skeletal muscle to break down its protein into amino acids that the liver can convert into more glucose. Adrenaline is, in this metabolic reading, primarily a glucose-mobilization hormone whose other effects (heart rate, vasoconstriction, sweating) are downstream of the fact that it is preparing the body to burn a lot of fuel very fast.

## Peat's framework — adrenaline as glucose-rescue signal

The framing that makes adrenaline coherent in the rest of Peat's writing is that *adrenaline rises when blood sugar falls.* The body cannot tolerate low [[blood-sugar]] — the brain consumes glucose at a fixed, high rate and has no significant fuel reserve of its own — so when serum glucose begins to drop, the adrenal medulla releases adrenaline, which immediately commands the liver to dump glycogen and (if glycogen is depleted) commands skeletal muscle to break down its protein for gluconeogenic substrate. As Peat put it in [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|"TSH, Temperature, Pulse Rate, and Other Indicators in Hypothyroidism"]], "the speed of the pulse is partly determined by adrenaline, and many hypothyroid people compensate with very high adrenaline production. Knowing that hypothyroid people are susceptible to hypoglycemia, and that hypoglycemia increases adrenaline, I found that many people had normal (and sometimes faster than average) pulse rates when they woke up in the morning, and when they got hungry."

This sentence is the load-bearing observation of Peat's whole adrenaline framework. The pulse goes up when you wake up hungry not because waking is exciting but because blood sugar is falling and adrenaline is rising to meet it. The pulse goes up when you skip lunch not because you are stressed about a deadline but because the same thing is happening at noon that happens at 3 AM. The pulse goes up an hour after a high-protein, low-carbohydrate meal because, as Peat repeatedly described, "if protein is eaten without carbohydrate, it will stimulate insulin secretion, lowering blood sugar and activating the stress response, leading to the secretion of adrenalin, cortisol, growth hormone, prolactin, and other hormones" ([[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context"]]).

The implication, made explicit across hundreds of newsletters and transcripts, is that the great majority of what is diagnosed as anxiety in adults — and a significant fraction of what is diagnosed as panic disorder, social anxiety, generalized anxiety disorder, and "high-strung temperament" — is metabolic adrenaline in a body whose glucose supply is unreliable. The treatment is not to suppress the adrenaline (the SSRI strategy) or to teach the person to tolerate it (the exposure-therapy strategy) but to fix the supply that the adrenaline exists to defend. As Peat put it on the 2019 *One Radio Network* milk episode: "Low thyroid people chronically have anxiety. When energy production goes down — which happens to everyone during the night — the body compensates with increased adrenaline and cortisol. When your thyroid is chronically low, you run around in the daytime with elevated adrenaline and cortisol. I've seen people with several times the normal amount of adrenaline production. Some people can function normally with four times as much adrenaline as normal, but that's why some low thyroid people are anxious all the time."

The thirty-year-old who has had panic attacks since college, eats two meals a day, drinks coffee on an empty stomach, jogs to stay thin, and cannot understand why she still feels jumpy after a year of therapy is, in this framework, running on adrenaline because she is undereating, undersleeping, and underfed at exactly the times of day when her liver glycogen runs out. The anxiety is not the disorder. The metabolic state that requires the anxiety is the disorder.

## The 3 AM cascade

The single most-cited adrenaline event in the Peat corpus is the 3 AM wake-up — the involuntary, often frightening transition from deep sleep to full alertness in the dark, sometimes with heart pounding, sometimes with a cold sweat or a hot flash, almost never followed by easy return to sleep. Peat traced this event, in himself first, to overnight glycogen depletion. In [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|"Solving Some of the Problems of Aging" (1991)]] he described his own discovery: "In 1979... I began to wake up every night, usually between 2 and 4 AM... I got in the habit of drinking milk or orange juice to raise my blood sugar enough to lower my adrenalin (and cortisol) so that I could return to sleep."

The sequence, reconstructed across his later writing, runs like this. Liver glycogen is the body's overnight glucose buffer. In a well-fed person with a healthy thyroid, glycogen is sufficient to maintain blood sugar through eight or nine hours of sleep. In a hypothyroid person, an undereating person, a person who ate a high-protein dinner without enough carbohydrate, or a person whose dinner was simply too long ago, glycogen runs out somewhere in the middle of the night. As serum glucose drops, the adrenal medulla releases adrenaline. The heart accelerates. Cutaneous vasoconstriction produces the cold sweat. Bronchodilation produces the sense of being suddenly, fully awake. The hippocampus, sensing high adrenaline, encodes the moment with the emotional valence of threat. The person sits up in bed convinced something is wrong.

Shortly behind the adrenaline, cortisol rises — slower, broader, longer-acting — and begins breaking down muscle protein into amino acids for gluconeogenesis. By morning the person is awake but exhausted, having spent the second half of the night running a catabolic salvage operation. This is why, Peat noted, many of his correspondents reported their *worst* fatigue in the morning despite having "slept" through the night: they had not so much slept as endured a long internal emergency.

The direct intervention in Peat's framework is to refill liver glycogen before bed and (if the wake-up still happens) to refill it again on waking. In [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|the same 1991 newsletter]] and dozens of later transcripts he recommended a small bedtime snack of carbohydrate plus salt — orange juice with salt, milk and honey, ice cream, fruit, sometimes well-cooked starch — to keep blood sugar high enough through the night that adrenaline is not provoked. The [[eat-before-bed]] practice is built on this single mechanism. The 3 AM glass of orange juice is not a folk remedy but, as Peat once put it, pharmacology dressed up as a drink.

A related observation, made in [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism]], is that *body temperature* tracks this cascade: "In old people, who lose heat easily during the day, their extreme increase in the compensatory nervous and hormonal adrenalin activity causes their night-time heat regulation (vasoconstriction in the extremities) to rise to normal." Adrenaline is also a thermogenic signal; hypothyroid people with elevated adrenaline often have warm core temperatures but cold hands and feet, the signature of adrenergic vasoconstriction compensating for sluggish basal metabolism. Taking the temperature before breakfast and again after a glucose-and-salt breakfast can directly visualize the cascade — temperature drops after breakfast in a hypothyroid person whose pre-breakfast warmth was adrenaline-driven, because the meal lowered the adrenaline and the underlying low-metabolic state was revealed.

## What raises adrenaline (chronically)

Peat catalogued the inputs that drive chronic adrenaline elevation across newsletters, transcripts, and emails over four decades. The pattern is consistent. Anything that lowers blood sugar, or lowers the body's ability to use glucose, or lowers thyroid function, ends up raising adrenaline.

- **Undereating in general.** Frequent small drops in blood sugar drive frequent small adrenaline surges, which over weeks become the person's baseline state. The cumulative effect is what mainstream medicine calls "anxiety."
- **Low-carbohydrate and ketogenic diets.** Without sufficient carbohydrate the liver cannot keep glycogen stocked. Adrenaline becomes the substitute mechanism for keeping blood sugar up. People often describe the first few weeks of a low-carbohydrate diet as exhilarating; what they are describing is sympathetic activation.
- **Intermittent fasting.** "Every hour without food drives a predictable hormonal cascade: cortisol rises to catabolize tissue for fuel, adrenaline mobilizes free fatty acids from storage" ([[intermittent-fasting]]). The popularity of intermittent fasting and the popularity of anxiety as a chronic complaint of well-educated adults are, in Peat's reading, not unrelated facts.
- **Cold exposure.** Deliberate cold exposure raises both adrenaline and noradrenaline within seconds. The wellness-culture practice of cold showers and cold plunges is, mechanically, an adrenaline-induction protocol. The endorphin afterglow many people describe is the rebound from a large catecholamine surge. Peat treated this practice as net-negative for almost everyone; see [[cold-plunge-dont-do-it]].
- **Overexercise.** Exercise that depletes muscle glycogen forces adrenaline and cortisol to mobilize substrate. A person doing daily long-form cardio on insufficient carbohydrate is in a near-permanent adrenergic state.
- **Hypothyroidism.** This is the most under-recognized driver in the corpus and the one Peat returned to most often. "In hypothyroidism, there is an adaptive increase in the activity of the sympathetic nervous system, producing more adrenalin, which helps to maintain body temperature by causing vasoconstriction in the skin" ([[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|"Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities"]]). Hypothyroid people *run on adrenaline*. The thin, anxious, hyperactive hypothyroid presentation — what Peat called the patient who is "unlikely to be evaluated for hypothyroidism" because they don't fit the textbook overweight-and-sluggish picture — is a person whose adrenals are doing the work the thyroid won't.
- **High estrogen.** Estrogen inhibits monoamine oxidase, the enzyme that degrades catecholamines and serotonin. With MAO inhibited, adrenaline and noradrenaline are cleared more slowly. "Estrogen shifts the balance of the 'neurotransmitters' in the same direction, toward increased serotonin and adrenalin, for example by inhibiting enzymes that degrade the monoamine 'neurotransmitters'" ([[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease]]). PMS, perimenopause, and post-menopausal anxiety are partly explained by this single enzyme effect.
- **Low CO2.** Hyperventilation and the broader pattern Peat called "relative hyperventilation" lower tissue CO2, which in turn promotes the same lactic-acid, low-oxidative-metabolism state that triggers adrenergic compensation ([[autonomic-systems|"Autonomic Systems"]]).
- **Caffeine without food.** Coffee on an empty stomach lowers blood sugar through hepatic stimulation, then provokes a compensatory adrenaline surge. The jittery, anxious, can't-quite-relax feeling many people get from coffee is not the caffeine itself but the adrenaline the caffeine called for. Coffee with food, particularly with sugar and protein, looks pharmacologically very different.

## What lowers adrenaline

The interventions in the corpus point in one direction: raise blood sugar reliably, restore thyroid function, and remove the inputs that drive sympathetic activation. The repeated short-list:

- **Adequate sugar.** [[Sucrose|Sugar]], fruit, fruit juice, milk, honey, well-cooked starch — anything that the liver can convert quickly to glycogen. Peat was emphatic that this is not optional. In the 2009 newsletter he wrote that "raising the blood sugar permits the adrenalin and cortisol to fall." The mechanism is not optional and the dietary recommendation that follows from it is not optional either.
- **Salt.** "Salt, which helps to maintain blood sugar, also tends to lower adrenalin, and hypothyroid people often lose abnormal amounts of sodium" ([[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism]]). Sodium restriction activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, raises serotonin, and raises adrenaline; sodium adequacy reverses all three. See [[salt]].
- **[[Gelatin]] and glycine.** The amino acid profile of gelatin (glycine-heavy, tryptophan-free) is anti-inflammatory and shifts the protein side of the diet away from the [[tryptophan]]-serotonin-adrenaline loop.
- **Thyroid replacement.** For hypothyroid people, no amount of dietary management substitutes for restoring T3. "Correcting thyroid function can immediately correct many problems" ([[autonomic-systems]]) — including the chronic adrenaline overproduction that compensates for low metabolism.
- **[[Bag-breathing]].** Restores tissue CO2, lowers the lactic-acid load, and directly opposes the adrenergic state. The simplest acute intervention Peat recommended for anxiety attacks.
- **[[Magnesium]].** Calcium-antagonist, smooth-muscle relaxant, retained in proportion to thyroid activity. Hypothyroid people are usually magnesium-deficient and the deficiency itself contributes to adrenergic excitability.
- **[[Niacinamide]].** Lowers free fatty acids, supports oxidative metabolism, and acts as a mild sedative at higher doses.
- **[[Walking]].** Low-intensity, mostly aerobic, oxidative, restorative — the opposite metabolic pattern from running, which is partly glycolytic and partly adrenergic. Peat consistently recommended walking as the default form of movement.
- **Warmth.** Hot baths, warm rooms, warm feet at bedtime. "Increased body temperature improves sleep, especially the deep slow wave sleep. A hot bath, or even warming the feet, has the same effect as thyroid in improving sleep" ([[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease]]). Warm peripheral skin is incompatible with sustained adrenergic vasoconstriction.
- **Cyproheptadine.** The antiserotonin antihistamine Peat returned to for chronic anxiety states. It does not lower adrenaline directly but blunts the serotonin amplification of the adrenergic state and breaks the chronic-anxiety loop in many people.
- **Light.** Daily sunlight, especially morning sunlight, supports thyroid function and the broader oxidative-metabolic state that obviates adrenaline as the compensatory mechanism. See [[morning-sunlight]].

The intervention Peat recommended above all others for an active adrenaline event — say, the 3 AM wake-up itself, or a panic attack on the train — was the simplest one: eat something sweet and salty, sit down, breathe slowly. Not because the panic is "in the head" but because adrenaline's half-life is two to three minutes, and once the metabolic emergency that called for it is over, it clears.

## Tracking signal

Adrenaline is hard to measure directly — it is labile, secreted in bursts, and its plasma half-life is shorter than the time to draw and process a sample. Peat treated this as a feature rather than a bug: rather than chase serum catecholamines, he taught his correspondents to read the *downstream* signals that adrenaline produces, which are stable enough to track on a kitchen thermometer.

The composite tracking pattern, from [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism]]:

- **Pulse on waking.** A pulse higher than 75 on waking, in a person who is not athletic and not running a fever, is a strong signal of overnight adrenaline. The pulse should ideally rise into the 80s through the morning as thyroid and metabolism activate, not start high and stay flat.
- **Pulse after breakfast.** Pulse should be slightly higher after a glucose-and-salt breakfast than before; this is the metabolic uptick. If pulse *drops* after breakfast, the pre-breakfast pulse was being held up by adrenaline.
- **Temperature on waking versus after breakfast.** In simple hypothyroidism, the morning temperature is low and rises after breakfast. In adrenaline-compensated hypothyroidism, the morning temperature is normal or high and *drops* after breakfast, because the meal lowered adrenaline and exposed the underlying low temperature.
- **Hand and foot temperature.** Persistently cold hands and feet in a warm room are a signature of adrenergic vasoconstriction. Warming peripherally is itself a sign of falling adrenaline.
- **Sleep continuity.** Sleeping eight hours without waking, and waking up rested rather than wired, is the most direct functional indicator. Most adults in the corpus achieved this within a week of consistent bedtime carbohydrate-plus-salt.
- **Morning state.** No 3 AM wake-up, no morning anxiety, calm pulse, easy appetite for breakfast, hands warm by mid-morning, mood stable through to lunch.

A person who tracks pulse and temperature before and after breakfast for two weeks will, in this framework, see their adrenaline pattern more clearly than any laboratory measurement could deliver.

## Counter-arguments

The strongest competing framings of adrenaline excess come from three directions, each of which Peat addressed explicitly.

The **psychiatric framing** treats anxiety as a primary disorder of brain chemistry, generally a serotonin deficiency, treatable with SSRIs. Peat's response was that this entire framing inverts the actual physiology: serotonin is not low in anxious people, it is high, and the adrenaline that drives the symptoms is a metabolic event that no amount of serotonergic medication will resolve. "Estrogen is often said to achieve its wonderful effects by increasing the effects of serotonin" — but in the same passage Peat treats serotonin as a stress-amplifying signal, not a deficiency to be corrected. The psychiatric framing also misses the temporal pattern: psychiatric "panic" diagnoses generally do not ask the patient what they had for dinner, whether they ate before bed, or what their pulse was on waking. The metabolic framing makes those questions the first ones.

The **cardiology framing** treats catecholamines as relevant only at pathological extremes — pheochromocytoma (the catecholamine-secreting tumor), acute myocardial infarction, anaphylaxis. The day-to-day chronic adrenergic state of a hypothyroid, undereating thirty-year-old is invisible to this framing because plasma catecholamines, on the snapshot measurement available in a cardiology clinic, are within reference range. Peat's framework predicts that this person's adrenaline is producing a continuous, low-grade vasoconstrictive and pro-inflammatory pattern that, over decades, contributes to hypertension, atherosclerosis, and arrhythmia — but none of this is captured by any single cardiology measurement.

The **alternative-medicine framing of "adrenal fatigue"** holds that chronic stress eventually exhausts the adrenal glands, producing a syndrome of low cortisol, low energy, salt craving, and orthostatic hypotension that requires "adrenal support" supplements. Peat criticized this framing directly. In a 2011 *East-West Healing* exchange with Josh Rubin, he said: "I don't know where 'exhaustion' is, where the adrenal gland dies. You don't want to get to exhaustion, but the idea of fatigue seems to be something else." He pointed out that in Selye's original work, "exhaustion" was a terminal stage in lethally stressed animals, not a chronic syndrome in living humans, and that the actual pattern in most people complaining of "adrenal fatigue" is the opposite: their adrenals are *overactive*, secreting high adrenaline (and often high cortisol) to compensate for low thyroid function and low blood sugar. The supplements marketed for adrenal fatigue (licorice, ashwagandha, "adrenal cortex" extract) often work briefly by raising cortisol further, then leave the person worse than before. The right intervention is to treat the hypothyroidism and the hypoglycemia, not to flog already-overactive adrenals.

There is also a narrower technical counter-argument: *some* of what Peat attributed to adrenaline is more precisely attributable to noradrenaline, since the sympathetic nerves can fire locally without provoking systemic adrenal release. This distinction matters for a researcher; in the clinical pattern Peat described it largely does not, because the inputs that drive one drive the other and the interventions that lower one lower the other.

## Data gaps

Direct adrenaline measurement is genuinely difficult. Plasma adrenaline samples must be drawn through an indwelling catheter (the venipuncture itself raises adrenaline), processed immediately, and kept cold; spot samples in a clinic setting are nearly uninterpretable. Twenty-four-hour urine catecholamines integrate over a longer window but have wide reference ranges and lump adrenaline together with its metabolites and with noradrenaline. The result is that most clinical labs cannot distinguish the chronically adrenaline-elevated patient from the normal one, which is part of why the pattern Peat described has been so easy for mainstream endocrinology to overlook.

The individual variation in adrenaline:cortisol ratio is poorly characterized. Some people, faced with low blood sugar, produce a large adrenaline spike with relatively modest cortisol; others produce the opposite pattern. Peat noted that the temperament differences this might explain — the "anxious" presentation versus the "fatigued" presentation — track but do not perfectly map onto the underlying ratio. There is no useful clinical assay for the ratio itself.

The interaction between adrenaline and serotonin is incompletely worked out. Peat treated them as broadly co-secreted in stress and broadly potentiating each other (estrogen-MAO-monoamine pathway), but the cellular detail of how serotonergic and adrenergic signaling intersect in, say, the human hippocampus during a 3 AM panic episode is largely unmeasured.

Finally, the chronic-adrenaline-to-cardiovascular-disease link is biologically plausible — Peat argued for it across the corpus — but has not been directly tested in long-term studies, partly because the measurement problem above makes it nearly impossible to identify the chronically elevated group prospectively.
