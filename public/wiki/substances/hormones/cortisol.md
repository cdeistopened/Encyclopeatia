# Cortisol

Cortisol is the hormone that lets your body eat itself. Mainstream medicine frames it as the "stress hormone" and leaves it at that—sometimes elevated, sometimes low, measured by morning blood draws and managed with adaptogens. Ray Peat's framework recognizes cortisol as something more specific and more dangerous: the catabolic signal that breaks down tissue to maintain blood sugar when oxidative [[metabolism]] has failed. In acute emergencies this is lifesaving. As a chronic state—which it becomes for most people eating modern diets—it is the metabolic equivalent of burning the furniture to heat the house.

## The Catabolic Function

Cortisol's primary role is gluconeogenesis: converting amino acids from tissue proteins into glucose. When blood sugar falls and glycogen stores are depleted, cortisol rises to cannibalize the body's own structure for fuel. As Peat explained in his newsletter on stress and cancer, "The body's response to decreasing glucose is to produce larger amounts of adrenal hormones, especially cortisol. Cortisol raises the blood sugar by mobilizing amino acids from tissues and converting them to glucose, but this is at the expense of muscle and other tissue structure."

This tissue breakdown is not selective. Muscle wastes. Skin thins. Bone matrix dissolves. The thymus—the immune system's training ground—shrinks. The brain, which depends absolutely on glucose, suffers a particular irony: chronic cortisol elevation actually deprives the brain of the glucose cortisol was supposed to provide. As Peat's newsletter on estrogen, calcium, and nerve degeneration described, "In diabetes, inflammatory conditions, and under the influence of increased cortisol, brain cells are deprived of glucose"—because cortisol simultaneously impairs glucose transport into neural tissue while breaking down peripheral proteins.

The amino acids liberated by cortisol include [[tryptophan]] and cysteine. As Peat noted, "stress and cortisol increase circulating cysteine and tryptophan from muscle." Rising tryptophan feeds [[serotonin]] synthesis, which promotes further [[inflammation]] and stress—creating a self-amplifying cycle. Rising cysteine contributes to excitotoxicity. The breakdown products of cortisol's catabolic activity are themselves harmful.

## Acute Versus Chronic: The Critical Distinction

A brief cortisol spike during genuine danger is appropriate—it mobilizes energy for immediate survival. The problem in modern life is that cortisol elevation becomes chronic and often goes unrecognized.

Every time blood sugar drops—from skipping a meal, from a low-carbohydrate diet, from poor sleep, from emotional distress—cortisol rises. In a person with adequate glycogen reserves and healthy thyroid function, the rise is small and brief. In a person with depleted glycogen, suppressed [[thyroid]], and high stored [[PUFA]], cortisol must rise higher and stay elevated longer because the underlying metabolic machinery cannot restore blood sugar efficiently.

This chronic elevation produces the constellation of symptoms that Peat associated with metabolic decline: muscle wasting with abdominal fat accumulation, thin skin, poor wound healing, bone loss, immune suppression, cognitive impairment, insomnia (cortisol peaks prevent deep sleep), and mood instability. These are often attributed to "aging" or "genetics" when they are the predictable consequences of a metabolism that cannot maintain blood sugar without cannibalizing tissue.

The connection to [[cancer]] is direct. Cortisol's tissue breakdown feeds cancer's glucose hunger—cancer cells consume glucose at extraordinary rates through aerobic glycolysis, and cortisol provides it by destroying the host's own proteins. As Peat described, the body "interprets cancer as stress, since the waste of glucose is the key feature of stress." The cancer-cortisol relationship is circular: cancer creates glucose deficit, cortisol responds by breaking down tissue, the immune suppression from cortisol allows cancer to grow further.

## The Progesterone Relationship

The relationship between cortisol and [[progesterone]] is one of the most important in Peat's hormonal framework. Both are synthesized from [[pregnenolone]], which is itself made from [[cholesterol]] under the influence of [[thyroid]] hormone. When the body is well-nourished and metabolically efficient, pregnenolone flows primarily toward progesterone and the other protective steroids ([[DHEA]], pregnenolone itself). When metabolism falters and stress increases, production shifts toward cortisol.

The popular concept of "pregnenolone steal"—where cortisol production supposedly robs pregnenolone from progesterone synthesis—captures part of this dynamic but misrepresents it. As Peat explained, "thyroid hormone, vitamin A, and cholesterol support the formation of pregnenolone, and the well nourished body is able to make large adjustments in these, to minimize the need for cortisol." The problem is not a fixed supply of pregnenolone being diverted; it is inadequate total production due to thyroid deficiency, cholesterol deficiency, or nutrient depletion.

[[Hans Selye]]'s experiment with pregnant rats demonstrated this relationship dramatically. Pregnant rats whose adrenal glands had been completely removed—eliminating all cortisol production—survived stresses that killed non-pregnant adrenalectomized animals. The placenta's progesterone production was sufficient to maintain stress resistance without any cortisol at all. Selye found that non-pregnant adrenalectomized rats given progesterone supplements could likewise "live a normal, stress-resistant life span."

This means progesterone is not merely cortisol's hormonal cousin but its functional superior—providing stress protection without the catabolic tissue destruction. The body resorts to cortisol when progesterone is insufficient, not because cortisol is the preferred solution.

## Adrenal Function: Beyond "Fatigue"

The alternative health community's concept of "adrenal fatigue"—a state of exhausted adrenal glands that cannot produce adequate cortisol—does not align with Peat's understanding. As he wrote in his email correspondence, "It's popular to talk about 'weak adrenals,' but the adrenal cortex regenerates very well." The adrenal glands are not fragile organs that wear out from overuse.

What appears as adrenal insufficiency is usually a problem upstream: inadequate thyroid function, insufficient cholesterol (the raw material for all steroid hormones), or deficient nutrients needed for steroidogenesis. "Thyroid is needed for the adrenals to function well, and adequate cholesterol, as raw material," Peat explained. Treating "adrenal fatigue" with adrenal extracts or hydrocortisone addresses the symptom rather than the cause—and supplemental cortisol carries its own catabolic risks.

Selye himself distinguished between "adrenal fatigue" and "exhaustion." His exhaustion phase was not about tired glands but about an organism whose total capacity to maintain structural integrity had been overwhelmed by accumulated damage. Peat's refinement was that this accumulated damage comes primarily from specific substances—stored PUFA, excess estrogen, inflammatory mediators—rather than from a mysterious depletion of vital force.

## Testing: The Measurement Problem

Cortisol measurement presents difficulties that Peat addressed directly. Serum cortisol varies dramatically throughout the day (highest in early morning, lowest at night), in response to meals, and in reaction to the stress of the blood draw itself. A single morning measurement tells you relatively little.

Saliva cortisol testing, while popular in functional medicine, has its own limitations. As Peat noted, "Saliva hormone tests are not reliable for absolute cortisol levels." The concentration of cortisol in saliva reflects the free (unbound) fraction, which can change independently of total cortisol production depending on binding protein levels, liver function, and other variables.

Urine cortisol measurements reflect liver metabolism of cortisol more than its hormonal activity. A person with poor liver function may show low urinary cortisol not because production is low but because the liver cannot process it efficiently.

Rather than relying on laboratory numbers, Peat tended to assess cortisol status through its metabolic signatures: waking body temperature and pulse (low morning temperature suggests cortisol is rising to compensate for inadequate thyroid), sleep quality (cortisol peaks at 2-4 AM if blood sugar drops), muscle mass relative to abdominal fat, skin thickness, and the ease with which blood sugar is maintained between meals.

## Reducing Cortisol: Removing the Need

Peat's approach to elevated cortisol was not to suppress it pharmacologically but to eliminate the conditions that make it necessary. If cortisol rises because blood sugar cannot be maintained through normal metabolism, the solution is to restore normal metabolism.

**[[Thyroid]] support** comes first because thyroid hormone drives glucose oxidation at every step. When cells can oxidize glucose efficiently, they do not run out of fuel, and cortisol does not need to rise. Many people over 40 are functionally hypothyroid, creating a baseline cortisol elevation that they experience as "normal" stress.

**Frequent meals with sugar and protein** prevent the blood sugar drops that trigger cortisol release. The combination matters: sugar provides immediate glucose, while protein provides the amino acids that would otherwise be stripped from muscle by cortisol. Orange juice with a small amount of salt was one of Peat's most frequently recommended anti-stress interventions.

**[[Progesterone]]** provides anti-stress protection that reduces the body's reliance on cortisol. In women, progesterone supplementation during the luteal phase (or postmenopausally) directly opposes cortisol's catabolic effects. In men, pregnenolone serves a similar function.

**PUFA elimination** prevents the inflammatory cascade that both triggers and amplifies cortisol release. When stored fats are predominantly saturated, the fatty acids mobilized during minor stress do not suppress thyroid function or generate inflammatory prostaglandins—so the stress resolves quickly and cortisol returns to baseline.

**Adequate protein** prevents the amino acid deficit that cortisol tries to correct through tissue catabolism. Gelatin and bone broth are particularly useful because their amino acid profile (high in glycine, low in tryptophan) opposes the inflammatory amino acid pattern that cortisol-driven tissue breakdown produces.

**[[Aspirin]]** blocks the prostaglandin-mediated inflammation that activates the cortisol response. **[[Vitamin E]]** protects against the lipid peroxidation that PUFA generates during stress, reducing the inflammatory signals that call for cortisol.

The pattern is consistent: rather than managing cortisol directly, restore the metabolic conditions that make excessive cortisol unnecessary. A well-fed, thyroid-sufficient, progesterone-adequate organism produces cortisol only in genuine emergencies—which is what the hormone was designed for.
