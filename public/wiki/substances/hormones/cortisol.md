# Cortisol

Cortisol is the body's emergency demolition crew. When blood sugar falls and the liver's glycogen stores run out, cortisol rises to maintain fuel supply by cannibalizing the body's own tissue: muscle, skin, thymus, intestine, and eventually brain. This is not pathology; it is a survival mechanism. The problem, in Ray Peat's framework, is that modern life creates the conditions for this emergency response to run continuously. [[Hypothyroidism]], [[PUFA]] accumulation, chronic undereating, prolonged darkness, and psychological stress all drain glycogen and drive cortisol upward. When the emergency never ends, cortisol destroys the very tissues it was designed to protect temporarily. "Cortisol maintains your blood sugar level so that you can keep working," Peat explained in a [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|2019 *Jodellefit* episode on cortisol and testosterone]], "but it does it by converting your protein. Tissues, muscles, skin, thymus gland, lymphatic tissues in general, and finally the digestive system, liver and intestine, will be cannibalized to make sugar and energy under the influence of cortisol."

That single mechanism, tissue protein converted to blood glucose under hormonal command, connects cortisol to an extraordinary range of degenerative conditions: muscle wasting, skin thinning, immune collapse, osteoporosis, brain atrophy, depression, insomnia, insulin resistance, and accelerated aging. In Peat's view, the popular fascination with "how to lower cortisol naturally" actually points at the right question, even if the mainstream answers (adaptogens, meditation apps, cold plunges) miss the metabolic root entirely. Cortisol is not lowered by calming the mind. It is lowered by feeding the cell.

## The Cortisol Cycle

Peat described cortisol's action as a predictable cascade that begins with energy failure and ends with self-amplifying destruction.

The sequence starts when blood sugar drops. This can happen through fasting, through eating protein without carbohydrate, through consuming [[PUFA]] that block glucose oxidation, through overexercise that depletes glycogen, or simply through sleeping through the night without eating. As glycogen runs out, the body activates its emergency fuel system. [[Adrenaline]] mobilizes fatty acids from fat stores, and cortisol breaks down tissue protein into amino acids, some of which the liver converts to glucose. This is gluconeogenesis, the conversion of the body's own structure into fuel.

In a healthy person with adequate reserves, this emergency is brief. A meal restores glycogen, cortisol falls, and protective hormones resume their dominance. But several conditions prevent the return to normal. Low [[thyroid]] function slows the rate at which glycogen is restored. PUFA in the tissues competes with glucose for oxidation, keeping the cells fuel-starved even when glucose is available. [[Estrogen]], which rises when the liver cannot clear it efficiently, directly stimulates more cortisol production through the pituitary and hypothalamus. The cortisol itself suppresses thyroid function by converting T4 into [[reverse T3]] instead of the active T3, creating a functional hypothyroidism that does not appear on standard labs. As Peat summarized: "All of the stress hormones interfere with the conversion of T4 to T3. Instead of making the effective, respiration-promoting T3, they convert thyroxine into Reverse T3."

The result is a self-reinforcing loop. Low thyroid causes high cortisol. High cortisol suppresses thyroid. Estrogen rises because the hypothyroid liver cannot clear it. The rising estrogen stimulates still more cortisol. Each pass through the loop degrades the tissues that would, if healthy, produce the protective steroids needed to shut cortisol down.

## Fasting: The Voluntary Stress

One of Peat's most pointed critiques of mainstream health culture was directed at fasting, which he regarded as a voluntary imposition of the exact metabolic emergency that cortisol exists to manage. The data he cited was stark: "On a complete water fast for 10 days, they found that 80% or more of the weight loss came from the destruction of muscle and skin. But if they had a 500 or 600 calorie diet with adequate minerals, almost all of the weight loss was in the form of fat loss. So a complete fast is very destructive to the body."

The difference between these two outcomes is cortisol. A water fast depletes glycogen within 24 hours and then turns the entire catabolic machinery on full. Muscle and skin are consumed to make sugar. A small caloric intake, particularly one that includes carbohydrate, keeps glycogen partially stocked and cortisol partially suppressed. The fat loss occurs not because cortisol is driving it but because [[insulin]] remains moderate and the cells can oxidize fat without the emergency-mode tissue destruction.

Peat extended this logic to intermittent fasting, overnight fasting, and even the common habit of skipping breakfast. In his newsletter [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|"Solving Some of the Problems of Aging" (1991)]], he described his own solution: "I got in the habit of drinking milk or orange juice to raise my blood sugar enough to lower my adrenalin (and cortisol) so that I could return to sleep." The midnight glass of [[orange-juice]] was not indulgence but pharmacology.

## The Overnight Problem

Cortisol's diurnal rhythm is central to Peat's understanding of aging, sleep disorders, and bone loss. Cortisol rises through the night, peaking around dawn, and this nocturnal elevation is itself a response to the stress of darkness. In the same newsletter, Peat observed: "People who are awake in the dark have higher levels of cortisol than when they are asleep in the dark, that is, sleep is a partial defense against the stress of darkness. The cortisol (and adrenalin) secreted in darkness, or other stress, has the important function of maintaining the blood sugar level. The fact that cortisol causes the destruction of tissue to convert to glucose is the reason that stress and hypoglycemia are destructive."

This explains a cluster of symptoms that conventional medicine treats as separate conditions. Insomnia, night sweats, hot flashes, and nocturnal bone loss are, in Peat's analysis, all consequences of the same cortisol surge during sleep. His newsletter [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone" (2006)]] documented the bone connection directly: "In old age, the catabolic hormones such as cortisol are relatively dominant, and even in youth, cortisol rises during darkness, reaching its peak around dawn. Even in young women, bone loss occurs almost entirely during the night, when cortisol is high."

The standard medical claim that estrogen and [[growth hormone]] protect bones at night runs into an awkward fact: these hormones peak alongside cortisol in the nocturnal hours, yet bone loss still occurs during that window. If estrogen were truly bone-protective, the nighttime rise should prevent bone loss, not coincide with it. Peat read the evidence differently. Estrogen and growth hormone are themselves stress-responsive substances. Their nocturnal elevation reflects the same stress that elevates cortisol, and their co-occurrence with bone loss is consistent with Peat's classification of all three as catabolic or stress-promoting in context.

When [[progesterone]] is deficient, as it increasingly is after age thirty-five and universally after menopause, even "normal" cortisol levels become destructive. Peat noted that "insomnia and hot flushes commonly occur around the time of menopause. These and other symptoms of menopause can be produced by an excess of cortisol, as in Cushing's syndrome. The increase of cortisol during the night is probably responsible for 'night sweats.' When progesterone is deficient, cortisol, even in 'normal' amounts, has a stronger effect."

## The Protective Steroids

Cortisol does not operate in isolation. Its destructive potential is determined by its ratio to the protective steroids: [[pregnenolone]], progesterone, [[DHEA]], and [[testosterone]]. When these are abundant, cortisol's catabolic action is blunted. When they are depleted, even moderate cortisol levels inflict serious damage.

This ratio shifts with age. In young people, the brain contains very high concentrations of pregnenolone, DHEA, and progesterone, all of which "stabilize cells and protect against the effects of cortisol." By old age, Peat wrote, these fall "to about 5% of their normal concentration, leaving the brain exposed to the destructive action of cortisol." The consequence is the progressive brain atrophy that characterizes aging, which Peat connected directly to Marion Diamond's research showing that "stress causes very general brain damage, including to the cortex."

The protective steroids do not merely dilute cortisol's effects. They actively oppose its mechanism. In the Jodellefit interview, Peat was explicit: "All of these protective steroids block the effect of cortisol. You can take a cortisol pill or prednisone and have very little harm from it if you're fairly saturated with progesterone, DHEA, and testosterone, because they prevent the action as well as taking care of some of the things that cortisol would produce."

This explains why the pharmaceutical use of prednisone and other synthetic glucocorticoids produces such devastating side effects: moon face, osteoporosis, skin thinning, diabetes, depression. The prescribed doses vastly exceed what the adrenals produce naturally, and patients are not simultaneously supplemented with the protective steroids that would buffer the cortisol's action. In his email exchanges, Peat described the dosing error: "William Jefferies told people that, since the adrenals produce 20 mg of cortisol per day, they should take 30 or 40 mg, as a replacement dose, because only half of it is absorbed. They got fat faces quickly." The medical establishment's cortisol dosing, even by sympathetic physicians like Jefferies, was pharmacologically naive because it ignored the hormonal context in which cortisol acts.

## Cortisol, PUFA, and Immune Destruction

The interaction between cortisol and [[PUFA]] is one of the most damaging synergies in Peat's framework. Polyunsaturated fatty acids do not merely coexist with cortisol; they amplify its cell-killing capacity, particularly in the immune system.

Peat documented this synergy in his article [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context"]]: "The cells of the thymus gland are very sensitive to damage by stress or cortisol, but they too can be rescued by giving them enough extra glucose to compensate for the cortisol. Polyunsaturated fatty acids have the opposite effect, sensitizing the thymus cells to cortisol. This partly accounts for the immunosuppressive effects of the polyunsaturated fats."

The thymus gland, which produces T-cells and is the foundation of adaptive immunity, is one of cortisol's primary targets. This is why severe stress, chronic illness, and aging all produce thymic atrophy and immune suppression. Peat's observation about hamsters in his laboratory made the connection to darkness and seasons: "In 1971 I found that none of the sexually mature hamsters in our lab had any detectable thymus glands during the winter, though they all had them in the summer. Since estrogen and testosterone, the other thymus-shrinking hormones, are lower in the winter, I inferred that it was cortisone which caused their loss of thymus in the winter."

PUFA stored in the tissues makes every cortisol exposure more immunosuppressive than it would otherwise be. A person eating a high-PUFA diet who also fasts, undersleps, or lives under chronic stress is subjecting their immune system to a double assault: the cortisol itself plus the PUFA that potentiates its killing effect on immune cells.

## Cortisol and the Brain

Cortisol's neurotoxicity is well established in mainstream research but rarely connected to the metabolic context Peat emphasized. Excess cortisol makes nerve cells more vulnerable to excitotoxicity, the process by which excessive stimulation kills neurons. The hippocampus, which is critical for memory formation, is particularly sensitive.

Peat noted that this vulnerability can be offset by providing extra glucose: "Excess cortisol makes nerve cells more sensitive to excitotoxicity, but the cells are protected if they are provided with an unusually large amount of glucose." The protective mechanism is straightforward in his framework. Cortisol kills cells by depriving them of energy. Glucose restores energy. The cell that can maintain its oxidative metabolism survives; the cell that cannot, dies. This is the same principle that operates in the thymus, the muscles, and every other tissue cortisol targets.

The brain damage accumulates over years, particularly during the nighttime cortisol peaks when protective steroids are at their lowest. Peat drew on Diamond's work to argue that the brain atrophy attributed to "normal aging" is in large part cortisol-mediated and therefore preventable through the same interventions that lower cortisol systemically: adequate thyroid function, frequent carbohydrate intake, progesterone supplementation, and PUFA avoidance.

## Protein Without Carbohydrate

One of Peat's most counterintuitive observations was that eating protein by itself raises cortisol. The mechanism involves insulin: amino acids stimulate insulin secretion, which lowers blood sugar, which triggers the stress response. As Peat explained in "Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context": "If protein is eaten without carbohydrate, it will stimulate insulin secretion, lowering blood sugar and activating the stress response, leading to the secretion of adrenalin, cortisol, growth hormone, prolactin, and other hormones."

This has practical implications that run contrary to the high-protein, low-carb orthodoxy. A steak without a side of fruit or juice does not simply provide amino acids for muscle repair. It triggers a cortisol surge that cannibalizes existing muscle to stabilize blood sugar. In Peat's email correspondence, he was direct: "Eating protein by itself can cause a big surge of cortisol. Preceding the protein with some carbohydrate makes the protein go farther, otherwise under the influence of cortisol a lot of protein is used just for energy."

The [[tryptophan]] released from dietary protein (or from cortisol-driven muscle catabolism) converts to [[serotonin]], which further activates the pituitary stress hormones, increasing cortisol and intensifying the catabolic cycle. Serotonin also suppresses thyroid function, adding another turn to the self-reinforcing loop.

## Depression as a Cortisol State

Peat understood depression not as a psychological condition requiring cognitive reframing but as the subjective experience of cortisol-driven tissue destruction. "Depression. The whole stress system that ends up increasing your cortisol and decreasing DHEA, progesterone, and testosterone is related to inflammatory and degenerative processes. Depression is how we experience that process. The body senses that it's being destroyed by the stress hormones and it wants to stop everything to sort of hibernate until the stress is over."

His newsletter [[endotoxin-stress-depression|"Endotoxin, Stress, Depression" (2011)]] added nuance to this picture by distinguishing cortisol from CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone). While chronically elevated cortisol is common in depressed people, cortisol itself has some antidepressant effects because it suppresses the more directly depression-inducing CRH. "CRH is more directly associated with depression than cortisol is, and it by itself activates many inflammatory processes, including the release of [[histamine]], cytokines, and [[nitric oxide]]." The implication is that cortisol elevation in depression is partly compensatory, an attempt to suppress the CRH that is driving the inflammatory cascade. The real target is the upstream activation of CRH by estrogen, hypothyroidism, [[endotoxin]], and serotonin.

## The "Low Adrenal" Myth

Peat had little patience for the alternative medicine concept of "adrenal fatigue" or "weak adrenals." In his email exchanges, he dismissed it: "It's popular to talk about 'weak adrenals,' but the adrenal cortex regenerates very well... So I think the 'low adrenal' people are simply low thyroid, or deficient in cholesterol or nutrients." The adrenals need [[thyroid]] hormone to function and [[cholesterol]] as raw material. What appears as adrenal insufficiency is usually thyroid insufficiency. Fix the thyroid, supply adequate cholesterol from the diet, and the adrenals will produce cortisol normally.

The diagnostic tool Peat recommended was the morning temperature reading: "Taking your temperature before and after breakfast helps to interpret your circadian hormone cycle. Hypothyroid people often have very high adrenaline, cortisol, and other stress hormones during the night, causing the temperature to be higher before breakfast than after." A pre-breakfast temperature that exceeds the post-breakfast temperature indicates that nighttime stress hormones, not metabolic rate, are generating the heat. This simple observation can reveal a cortisol-dominated physiology that standard blood tests may miss entirely.

## How Cortisol Is Actually Lowered

In Peat's framework, cortisol is not lowered by addressing cortisol directly but by removing the conditions that make cortisol necessary. His email exchanges listed the practical toolkit: "pregnenolone, progesterone, aspirin, sugar, thyroid, calcium, etc." The target range was specific: "It's best to have cortisol no higher than the middle of the range, estrogen below the middle, testosterone above the middle."

Each intervention addresses a different node in the cortisol cascade. [[Sugar]] (particularly the combination of glucose and [[fructose]] in orange juice, [[milk]], or fruit) replenishes glycogen and directly removes the stimulus for cortisol release. [[Thyroid]] hormone restores the metabolic rate that prevents glycogen depletion in the first place and re-enables the liver's estrogen-clearing function. Progesterone and pregnenolone both directly suppress cortisol production at the brain, pituitary, and adrenal levels, and their presence in tissues buffers whatever cortisol remains. "Supplemental progesterone can reduce excessive cortisol production," Peat confirmed. "Cortisol is made from progesterone, but increasing the supply of progesterone reliably lowers cortisol synthesis." [[Aspirin]] reduces the inflammatory prostaglandins that activate CRH. [[Calcium]] stabilizes cells against the excitatory effects of stress hormones. [[Vitamin D]] "often helps to improve the balance."

The overnight cortisol surge is managed by eating carbohydrate before bed and, if waking occurs, drinking milk or orange juice to restore blood sugar. Light exposure during sleeping hours reduces the cortisol-elevating effect of darkness. Adequate protein, consumed with carbohydrate rather than alone, prevents the paradoxical cortisol spike that high-protein meals produce.

The pattern across all of these interventions is the same: supply the energy and hormonal substrates that make the cortisol emergency unnecessary, and cortisol falls on its own.
