# Melatonin

Millions of people take melatonin supplements to improve their sleep. Health food stores sell it as a natural sedative, an antioxidant, and a cancer preventive. Ray Peat considered it dangerous. In his framework, melatonin is not a beneficial sleep hormone but a stress hormone, a chemical signal of darkness, cold, and winter whose physiological effects include suppressing [[thyroid]] function, inhibiting [[progesterone]], stimulating [[estrogen]], shrinking the [[thymus]] gland, causing vasoconstriction in the brain and heart, and lowering body temperature toward a torpid, hibernation-like state. "Many health food stores are now selling melatonin, to reduce sleep and 'prevent cancer,'" he wrote in his [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|"Using Sunlight to Sustain Life" (1996)]] newsletter. "They have taken some information out of context, and don't realize how dangerous melatonin is. It makes the brain sluggish, causes the sex organs to shrink, and damages immunity by shrinking the thymus gland." That a hormone with this profile became America's favorite supplement is, in Peat's analysis, another case of market culture guiding research conclusions away from biological reality.

The mainstream story about melatonin is simple: darkness triggers the pineal gland to produce it, it makes you sleepy, and supplementing it helps you fall asleep. Every element of this story is technically present in Peat's account, but the framing is inverted. Darkness does trigger melatonin. But darkness, in Peat's framework, is a stressor. The sleepiness melatonin produces is not restorative relaxation but metabolic suppression. And the doses sold in supplements exceed the body's own production by orders of magnitude, flooding every tissue with a hormone whose chronic effects mirror the physiology of aging, winter depression, and immune collapse.

## The Hormone of Darkness and Winter

Peat's core characterization of melatonin ties it to the biological meaning of darkness rather than the subjective experience of sleep. The pineal gland produces melatonin in response to the absence of light, but also, crucially, in response to any stress that activates the adrenaline system. As he wrote in "Using Sunlight to Sustain Life": "Melatonin, or pineal hormone: the pineal gland in the brain responds to an absence of light (or to any stress which increases the adrenalin systems) by secreting a hormone called melatonin, which lightens the skin, makes the brain sluggish, turns off thyroid and progesterone production, and suppresses immunity and fertility." The parenthetical about stress is the key. Melatonin is not simply a darkness detector; it is a stress hormone that happens to be most reliably triggered by darkness.

This connection between melatonin and the adaptive physiology of winter was something Peat observed directly in his graduate work. In a [[2016-04-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-mitochondria-gaba-herbs-and-more|2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on mitochondria and GABA]], he described his hamster laboratory at the University of Oregon: "In our hamster lab, even though the lab was temperature-controlled with a 12-hour light cycle, the hamsters had essentially no thymus gland during the winter. That started me thinking about the potential risk of changing your whole endocrine system by supplementing something like that." The hamsters were not cold. They were not food-deprived. But something about the winter season, probably subtle environmental signals amplified by marginal light deprivation in their cage boxes, was activating the pineal gland sufficiently to cause the thymus to nearly disappear. The immune organ responsible for T-cell maturation was being dissolved by a seasonal signal, and melatonin was the mediator.

In his [[aging-eyes-infant-eyes-and-excitable-tissues|"Aging Eyes, Infant Eyes, and Excitable Tissues"]] newsletter, Peat placed this observation in a broader evolutionary context: "During the winter, when they were infertile, I found that their thymus glands practically disappeared. The mechanism seemed to include the increase of pineal gland activity (probably increasing melatonin synthesis) in the winter, under the intensified activity of the 'sympathetic nervous system' (with increased activity of adrenalin and other catecholamines), and the melatonin was apparently a signal for suppressing fertility during the stressful winter." The entire constellation of winter physiology, including reduced fertility, suppressed immunity, weight gain, depression, and metabolic slowing, maps to the effects of elevated melatonin. When Sarah Johanneson Murray remarked that "it doesn't seem like you'd want to take something that triggers the wintertime response," Peat replied simply: "That's what I've always thought."

## The Antarctic Experiment

One of the most vivid illustrations of melatonin's relationship to darkness-stress came from research on young sailors stationed in Antarctica. During the six months of continuous polar night, these otherwise healthy men developed physiological changes that mimicked the nighttime stress patterns of elderly people. Peat described the finding in "Using Sunlight to Sustain Life": "When young sailors spent 6 months in the continuous polar night of Antarctica, they developed the same signs of nocturnal stress that are common in old people during the night."

The connection between darkness and aging physiology runs through the mitochondria. "The energy-producing part of cells, the mitochondrion, shows signs of being increasingly damaged as the night progresses, but they are gradually restored to their normal condition during the daytime light hours," Peat explained. "This means that our greatest ability to resist stress is in the late afternoon, and we are most susceptible to injury at dawn." In the polar night, the restorative effect of daytime light never arrives. The cumulative mitochondrial damage that normally reverses each day instead accumulates, producing in young men the metabolic profile of old age.

This was not merely a curiosity about extreme environments. Peat extended the principle to ordinary winters, where shorter days mean less light exposure and more time spent with elevated stress hormones. "Nights are much longer in the winter, and even in the summer, death rates are higher during the night than in daytime. December 21 is the day with the fewest hours of sunlight, but the cumulative damage of prolonged darkness reaches its peak about a month later." The seasonal pattern of illness and death, conventionally attributed to cold temperatures, is in Peat's analysis primarily a function of light deprivation, operating through the same melatonin-mediated hormonal cascade that caused his hamsters' thymus glands to vanish.

## Suppressing Progesterone, Stimulating Estrogen

The hormonal consequences of melatonin place it squarely on the anti-metabolic side of Peat's framework. In "Aging Eyes, Infant Eyes, and Excitable Tissues," he cited direct experimental evidence: "In 1994 A.V. Sirotkin found that melatonin inhibits progesterone production but stimulates estrogen production, and it's widely recognized that melatonin generally inhibits the thyroid hormones, creating an environment in which fertilization, implantation, and development of the embryo are not possible."

This hormonal profile, low progesterone, high estrogen, suppressed thyroid, is the signature of what Peat called the stress state. Progesterone is the primary protective hormone in his framework: it supports mitochondrial respiration, opposes estrogen, stabilizes the nervous system, and protects against [[seizures]] and excitotoxic damage. Thyroid hormone drives the metabolic rate that keeps every cell adequately energized. When melatonin suppresses both while simultaneously stimulating estrogen, the result is a comprehensive degradation of the organism's defenses. As Peat wrote: "This combination of high estrogen with low progesterone and low thyroid decreases the resistance of the organism, predisposing it to seizures and excitotoxic damage, and causing the thymus gland to atrophy."

The thymus atrophy is worth emphasizing because it connects melatonin directly to immune function in a way that contradicts the supplement industry's marketing. Melatonin is sold as immune-supportive. Peat found that it destroys the organ responsible for immune competence. The apparent contradiction dissolves when you recognize that some research showing immune stimulation from melatonin involves cyclical or pulsed exposure at very specific doses, while the chronic, high-dose exposure typical of supplementation produces the opposite effect. As Peat noted in the "Aging Eyes" newsletter: "Cyclical exposure to melatonin can have an effect on the reproductive system opposite to that of chronic exposure, and the way exogenous melatonin is delivered to the animal can have unexpected effects on the actual amount of melatonin circulating in the blood."

## The Antioxidant Myth

One of melatonin's strongest selling points in the supplement market is its purported antioxidant activity. Peat directly challenged this claim. "There is a lot of talk about melatonin's function as an antioxidant," he wrote in "Aging Eyes, Infant Eyes, and Excitable Tissues," "but, like so many other 'antioxidants,' melatonin can act as a pro-oxidant at physiologically relevant concentrations; some studies have found that it, like estrogen, increases the activity of the pro-oxidative free radical [[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]] (which acts like melatonin on pigment cells, causing them to lighten)."

The comparison to estrogen is pointed. Peat spent years documenting how estrogen, also marketed as an antioxidant, actually catalyzes intense oxidations at the concentrations found in the body. The pattern is the same: a substance that shows protective effects at pharmacological extremes in laboratory settings behaves very differently at the concentrations that actually matter for human health. "The promoters of estrogen are also making claims that estrogen is a protective antioxidant, though that isn't true of physiological concentrations of estrogen, which can catalyze intense oxidations," he wrote. "The market culture seems to guide most research in these substances."

The radiation protection studies are illustrative. In antioxidant and radiation protection experiments, researchers have used concentrations a thousand or even a million times the physiological level. As Peat explained in the 2016 KMUD episode: "In antioxidant or radiation protection experiments, they've used a thousand or maybe a million times the physiological concentration. It can do amazing things like protect against gamma rays, but the outcome might have unexpected consequences." The fact that a gram of melatonin can protect a cell in a petri dish from gamma radiation tells you nothing about what five milligrams does to a living human being's thyroid, thymus, and reproductive system over months of nightly supplementation.

## Metabolic Suppression: Temperature, Vasoconstriction, Torpor

Melatonin's direct metabolic effects read like a checklist of everything Peat spent his career warning against. "Melatonin lowers body temperature, causes vasoconstriction in the brain, heart, and other organs, and slows reactions," he wrote in the "Aging Eyes" newsletter. "An antagonist to melatonin acts as an antidepressant, reducing 'behavioral despair' resulting from stress."

The fact that blocking melatonin reduces behavioral despair is telling. It suggests that melatonin itself contributes to the depressive, low-energy state that characterizes both winter and aging. The seasonal affective disorder that afflicts millions of people every winter is, in Peat's framework, partly a melatonin-driven phenomenon. In the 2016 KMUD discussion, he connected it directly: "This involution of reproductive glands and the thymus has behavioral consequences, like Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD), depression, and weight gain. Melatonin is the main thing that causes this involution."

The torpor dimension connects melatonin to hibernation physiology. "Since a hibernating animal under the influence of melatonin can become very cold, the light-sensitizing function of melatonin is probably related to the biological need to be roused out of the torpor occasionally," Peat wrote. Hibernation is a controlled metabolic shutdown. The animal's temperature drops, its heart rate slows, its energy production falls to minimal levels. That the same hormone governing this state is being taken nightly by millions of people who believe they are simply improving their sleep quality is a disconnection between marketing and biology that Peat found characteristic of the supplement industry.

Melatonin's vasoconstriction carries additional risks. "Melatonin's ability to cause vasoconstriction means that stress makes supplemental oxygen more toxic," he noted. For anyone using supplemental oxygen, whether in a hospital setting or for altitude sickness, melatonin creates a paradox: the constricted blood vessels cannot deliver the extra oxygen to tissues, and the resulting localized oxygen excess at the vascular level can produce oxidative damage.

Melatonin also shifts the body's fuel substrate toward [[free-fatty-acids]]. "Melatonin increases the concentration of free fatty acids during the night," Peat wrote. This nocturnal mobilization of fats is part of the stress response that occurs during sleep, when blood sugar falls and the body relies increasingly on fat oxidation. In Peat's framework, elevated free fatty acids are themselves toxic, interfering with glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] and promoting the very metabolic suppression that melatonin initiates through other pathways.

## What Melatonin Actually Is: A Serotonin Detoxification Signal

Despite his opposition to supplementation, Peat did not regard the body's own melatonin production as purely harmful. He understood it as serving a specific physiological function: detoxifying [[serotonin]]. In a [[2013-04-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hormone-replacement-therapy|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hormone replacement therapy]], he explained the mechanism: "Its function seems to be as a way of detoxifying serotonin. The trigger that turns the conversion in the pineal gland of serotonin into melatonin is the adrenergic stress-related nerves. During the night, your stress increases from the lack of light, and this adrenergic system causes the pineal to convert serotonin to melatonin."

Serotonin is, in Peat's framework, a far more dangerous substance than melatonin. It promotes [[inflammation]], stimulates cell division, contributes to fibrosis, and is implicated in conditions ranging from depression to cancer. The pineal gland's conversion of serotonin to melatonin is therefore a protective process, a way of reducing the body's exposure to a genuinely toxic molecule. "In general, melatonin is functioning as an antidote to serotonin. Biologically, it's evidence that you're responding to stress with a detoxifying process."

The enzyme that accomplishes this conversion is not limited to the pineal gland. Peat noted that "the enzymes that exist in the pineal that are activated by the stress can also be found in inflamed joint tissue and in breast cancer. Serotonin is involved in the pathology of both of those situations. If our enzymes could turn it more quickly into melatonin, there would be less of a serotonin problem." In a [[2011-05-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-misconceptions-relating-to-serotonin-and-melatonin|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on serotonin and melatonin]], he elaborated: "In these situations of inflammation, melatonin seems to be performing the same function that it does in the brain: a way of eliminating serotonin, which is the actual pro-inflammatory substance."

This framing has a crucial implication for supplementation. Taking exogenous melatonin may protect against circulating serotonin, but in an otherwise healthy person, the animal research shows it suppresses progesterone and thyroid while increasing estrogen. As Peat put it: "It's probably protecting against the circulating serotonin. But in itself, in an otherwise healthy person, animal studies showed it was capable of lowering both progesterone and thyroid while increasing estrogen." The supplemental melatonin addresses one problem while creating several others.

Murray's follow-up captured the practical implication: "So in a short-term emergency situation, it could be okay, but long-term it's going to have negative side effects." Peat agreed, adding the qualification that "it's much safer than serotonin." The comparison matters. Given a choice between elevated serotonin and elevated melatonin, melatonin is the lesser harm. But the real solution, in Peat's framework, lies upstream.

## The Dosage Problem

Perhaps the most straightforward argument against melatonin supplementation is the arithmetic. The body produces a fraction of a milligram of melatonin, and the most common supplement doses range from one to ten milligrams. On a [[2020-01-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-oxygen-saturation-lactic-acid-thyroid-vaccines-pufas|2020 *One Radio Network* episode on oxygen saturation and thyroid]], Peat was characteristically direct: "The body produces a fraction of a milligram. Even a 1mg dose is unphysiological. 5 or 10mg is risky. In animals, melatonin rises in winter due to darkness and causes the gonads and thymus to regress."

The gap between physiological production and supplemental doses is enormous. In the 2016 KMUD episode, Peat noted the range of estimates: "One publication refers to the physiological dose as equivalent to 5 micrograms per body in an average-sized adult. Others have measured it as up to 2 or 3 milligrams in the whole body." Even taking the higher estimate, a standard 5mg supplement delivers nearly double the body's total content. Taking the lower estimate, the supplement delivers a thousand times the physiological amount. "When a person takes 10 milligrams, it will put them to sleep, but it is probably doing lots of other things."

The cancer research presents a similarly muddled picture. Peat acknowledged that melatonin sometimes reduces tumors in animal studies, but the results are inconsistent. "Some of that varies with the variety of mouse used," he noted. "Sometimes it decreases tumors, sometimes it increases them. The effect on the immune system partly depends on the dose. At least two groups, one in China and one in Denver, are seeing very clear pro-inflammatory effects from physiological amounts of melatonin."

For those who insist on supplementing, Peat offered one concession in the 2020 interview: "If one is going to supplement, use very small doses, a tenth to a fourth of a milligram." This range, 100 to 250 micrograms, is a fraction of what health food stores typically sell, and even then Peat's tone was one of reluctant tolerance rather than endorsement.

## Working at the Other End

Peat's consistent recommendation was not to supplement melatonin but to address the conditions that make the body produce too much of it. When Andrew Murray asked directly whether melatonin supplementation would be useful for its anti-inflammatory effects, Peat replied on a 2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on serotonin and endotoxins: "I think it has anti-inflammatory effects, but it's much better to work at the other end, making it unnecessary by reducing your tryptophan intake and reducing starches and inflammation-producing things."

The logic flows directly from the serotonin-detoxification understanding. If melatonin's primary function is neutralizing serotonin, then reducing serotonin production makes melatonin less necessary. [[Tryptophan]], the amino acid precursor to serotonin, is concentrated in muscle meats and particularly in foods high in the amino acid relative to [[gelatin|glycine]]. Reducing tryptophan intake, avoiding [[starch]] that feeds gut bacteria capable of producing serotonin, and controlling [[endotoxin]] and inflammation all reduce the body's serotonin burden.

Light exposure is the other lever. As Peat wrote: "Adequate sun light suppresses the formation of melatonin." And from his email correspondence: "Having more very bright light (several hundred watts of incandescent bulbs) in the hours from sundown until bedtime will lower them a little more." The "them" refers to the entire nocturnal stress hormone profile: [[cortisol]], [[prolactin]], TSH, and melatonin, all of which rise during the night and can be partially suppressed by extending the body's exposure to bright light.

The protective approach also involves supporting [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]] directly. In the "Aging Eyes" newsletter, Peat noted that "thyroid and gelatin (or glycine, as in magnesium glycinate) are protective against the serotonin and melatonin toxicities." Adequate thyroid hormone keeps the metabolic rate high enough to resist the suppressive effects of both substances, while glycine directly opposes serotonin at the neurochemical level.

## Melatonin and the Fatigued Brain

Peat did acknowledge one specific context in which melatonin's suppressive effects serve a genuinely protective function. In the 2016 KMUD episode, he explained: "The good side is that in the fatigued state of the brain, the excitatory, de-energized condition, nitric oxide rises and starts a vicious circle where it activates acetylcholine, which activates nitric oxide synthesis. This can keep you awake. Melatonin is effective at turning off that cycle. So when your brain is in a bad fatigue state, melatonin can protect it by lowering nitric oxide and excitation."

This is consistent with Peat's broader view that melatonin is less toxic than the substances it counteracts. A brain trapped in a nitric oxide-acetylcholine excitatory loop is in immediate danger. Melatonin breaks the loop. The suppression that is harmful when applied to a healthy organism becomes protective when applied to an acutely distressed one. Similarly, when someone facing radiation therapy has no alternative, Peat conceded that "very large doses of melatonin are probably protective against the damaging effect." The caveat, as always, is that the protection comes with its own costs, and addressing the underlying problem remains preferable to managing it with a hormone that carries the signature of darkness and winter.
