# Serotonin

No substance in Ray Peat's framework reveals the gap between pharmaceutical marketing and biological reality more sharply than serotonin. The "happiness hormone"—the molecule SSRIs are designed to boost—is, in Peat's analysis, a stress mediator, an inflammatory promoter, and a driver of degenerative disease. When asked about the claim that serotonin is the happiness hormone, Peat called it "very, very silly" and added that "everything there is confused and backward," as he explained in a [[2018-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-2|2018 episode comparing progesterone and estrogen]]. The gut produces 95% of the body's serotonin—and Peat identified it as "the main promoter of stress hormones, [[inflammation]], pain, and anxiety."

## The Gut Hormone

The popular image of serotonin centers on the brain, but only about 5% of the body's serotonin exists there. The gastrointestinal tract manufactures the overwhelming majority, and this intestinal serotonin operates as part of the body's stress-response apparatus rather than its reward system.

As Peat explained in his email correspondence, "The gut makes 95% of serotonin, which is the main promoter of stress hormones, inflammation, pain, and anxiety." Serum serotonin levels fluctuate with intestinal irritation—meaning that gut problems do not merely correlate with mood disorders but directly increase the serotonin that drives them.

The connection between gut bacteria and serotonin runs through [[endotoxin]]—the lipopolysaccharide fragments released by gram-negative bacteria in the intestine. As Peat discussed in a [[2011-08-25-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-serotonin-and-endotoxin|2011 episode dedicated to serotonin and endotoxin]], bacterial endotoxin triggers serotonin production as part of a defensive cascade. When endotoxin absorption increases—from intestinal inflammation, impaired liver function, or bacterial overgrowth—serotonin rises throughout the body. The endotoxin simultaneously slows the liver's detoxifying processes, creating a vicious cycle: more endotoxin means more serotonin, more serotonin means more inflammation, more inflammation means more endotoxin.

This explains why so many "mood disorders" respond to dietary changes that improve intestinal function—not because the brain is receiving better nutrients, but because the gut is producing less serotonin.

## Stress Hormone, Not Happiness Hormone

Peat consistently grouped serotonin with [[cortisol]] and [[estrogen]] as the body's primary stress mediators. In a [[2019-09-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-education-and-reeducation|2019 episode]], he stated plainly: "If you stress an animal—injure it, suffocate it—you will increase the stress hormones: cortisol, estrogen, and serotonin. Chronic stress increases serotonin in the brain."

The mechanism connecting stress to serotonin involves [[tryptophan]], serotonin's amino acid precursor. As Peat's newsletter on serotonin and aging explained, "Increased free tryptophan in blood is the main factor determining serotonin production in the brain." Under normal conditions, tryptophan circulates bound to albumin in the blood, keeping brain serotonin synthesis low. But when [[stress]] mobilizes free fatty acids from adipose tissue—particularly [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]]—these fatty acids compete for albumin binding sites, displacing tryptophan into its free form. Free tryptophan crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to serotonin.

This is why every stress response increases brain serotonin: not through a specific "serotonin pathway" but through the general stress mechanism of free fatty acid mobilization. A person with high stored PUFA releases more tryptophan during every stress response, producing more serotonin. A person with predominantly saturated tissue fats releases less tryptophan and synthesizes less brain serotonin from the same stressor.

Cortisol amplifies this process further. As Peat noted, cortisol-driven tissue catabolism releases amino acids from muscle, including tryptophan and cysteine—feeding serotonin synthesis while the cysteine contributes to excitotoxicity. The stress hormones reinforce each other: cortisol releases tryptophan, tryptophan becomes serotonin, serotonin promotes inflammation that triggers more cortisol.

## Cardiovascular Damage

Serotonin's harmful effects are not theoretical—they were documented decades before the SSRI era. In a [[2011-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-inflammation-ii|2011 episode on inflammation]], Peat described how serotonin-producing tumors (carcinoid tumors) revealed the hormone's toxicity:

> "Serotonin excess causes pulmonary hypertension and heart valve degeneration—known since the 1950s from serotonin-producing tumors. Anti-serotonin drugs treat this."

The heart valve damage seen in patients taking the diet drug fenfluramine—which raises serotonin levels—was not an unexpected side effect but the predictable consequence of serotonin's known cardiovascular toxicity. The same mechanism operates more slowly in anyone with chronically elevated serotonin, whether from gut inflammation, chronic stress, or SSRI medication.

Serotonin constricts blood vessels, increases vascular permeability (promoting [[edema]]), and promotes platelet aggregation. As Peat discussed in a discussion of LSD and serotonin, serotonin acts by "constricting blood vessels in the brain, shutting off energy production"—the exact opposite of what a "feel-good" chemical should do.

Hyperventilation provides another window into serotonin's effects. Peat's newsletter on serotonin and coherence described how displacing carbon dioxide through hyperventilation causes platelets to release serotonin, "increasing sickness and mortality." This connects serotonin's effects to the broader pattern of metabolic stress: anything that depletes CO2 and shifts metabolism toward the fermentative state also increases serotonin.

## The SSRI Deception

Peat viewed the SSRI paradigm as commercially motivated rather than scientifically grounded. In a [[2018-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-evidence-based-medicine|2018 episode on evidence-based medicine]], he traced the history: the original antidepressants—MAO inhibitors—worked by raising multiple neurotransmitters simultaneously, including dopamine and adrenaline alongside serotonin. When patents expired, the pharmaceutical industry needed a new product. The solution was to isolate serotonin as "the" mechanism and develop selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors.

The scientific basis for this was never strong. Peat's newsletter on postpartum depression noted that SSRIs for postpartum depression showed a "lack of good-quality evidence comparing SSRIs with placebo," while simple interventions like massage and counseling appeared effective. The "serotonin deficiency" theory of depression—that depressed people lack serotonin and need drugs to increase it—inverts the actual relationship: depressed people often have elevated serotonin because they are under chronic metabolic stress.

The irony is that SSRIs' limited effectiveness may come not from raising serotonin but from their secondary effects. Some SSRIs have weak anti-inflammatory properties or interact with other receptor systems. The serotonin elevation itself, in Peat's analysis, would tend to worsen the metabolic conditions underlying depression rather than improve them.

## Melatonin: The Detoxification Product

The pineal gland's conversion of serotonin to [[melatonin]] represents the body's primary defense against serotonin toxicity. As Peat explained in a [[2011-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-endotoxins-stress|2011 episode on serotonin and stress]], the enzyme responsible for this conversion is linked to defense against "the inflammatory and carcinogenic effects of serotonin."

This reframes melatonin. Rather than being a "sleep hormone" produced for its own sake, melatonin is what the body makes to dispose of serotonin. Its production increases in darkness not primarily to induce sleep but because serotonin accumulates during the stress of nighttime (when blood sugar drops, cortisol rises, and stored PUFA is mobilized). The pineal gland converts this toxic surplus into the relatively benign melatonin.

People with high serotonin production—from gut inflammation, chronic stress, or PUFA-rich diets—need more melatonin production to handle the load. When the detoxification system is overwhelmed, serotonin accumulates, contributing to the insomnia, anxiety, and inflammatory conditions that characterize chronic stress.

## The Estrogen-Serotonin Axis

[[Estrogen]] and serotonin form a mutually reinforcing cycle. Estrogen increases serotonin by activating tryptophan hydroxylase (the rate-limiting enzyme in serotonin synthesis) and by reducing the enzymes that break serotonin down. Serotonin, in turn, promotes inflammation and tissue injury that activates aromatase—the enzyme that produces estrogen locally in tissues.

This axis explains why women experience more serotonin-related conditions (depression, anxiety, irritable bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia) during periods of estrogen dominance, and why these conditions often respond to progesterone supplementation—which opposes both estrogen and serotonin.

Peat's newsletter on premenstrual, postpartum, and seasonal serotonin described how inadequate light produces thyroid and progesterone deficiencies, allowing estrogen, PUFA, and stress hormones to rise—particularly during the long winter nights. The seasonal pattern of depression reflects this hormonal-metabolic reality rather than a mysterious "chemical imbalance."

## Dietary Sources and Triggers

Several common foods directly contain serotonin or increase its production:

- **Bananas** contain serotonin directly and were identified by Peat as problematic for serotonin-sensitive individuals
- **Potatoes** can feed gut bacteria that produce endotoxin and serotonin, though Peat considered them less harmful than bananas
- **Tryptophan-rich proteins** (especially muscle meats eaten without adequate carbohydrate) increase serotonin synthesis by raising free tryptophan
- **PUFA-rich foods** increase serotonin indirectly by displacing tryptophan from albumin during stress

Conversely, [[gelatin]] and bone broth—high in glycine and low in tryptophan—help reduce serotonin synthesis by altering the amino acid ratio in favor of inhibitory rather than excitatory pathways.

## Reducing Serotonin

Peat's approach to lowering serotonin addresses the conditions that produce it:

**[[Thyroid]] support** improves liver function (accelerating serotonin clearance), reduces endotoxin absorption, and opposes the metabolic conditions that trigger serotonin production.

**[[Progesterone]]** directly opposes serotonin's effects and reduces the estrogen that stimulates its production.

**Intestinal health**—raw carrot fiber, cooked mushrooms, bamboo shoots—reduces endotoxin absorption and the gut serotonin it triggers. A well-functioning liver clears serotonin from portal blood before it reaches systemic circulation.

**PUFA elimination** reduces the free fatty acid displacement of tryptophan during stress, lowering brain serotonin synthesis from each stress response.

**Adequate light exposure** supports thyroid and progesterone production while reducing the winter-pattern hormonal shifts that elevate serotonin.

**Air ionization** near the bed may help, as Peat noted that "the lungs are the main site of serotonin metabolism" and negative ions support this pulmonary clearance.

**Cyproheptadine**, a prescription antihistamine with potent anti-serotonin effects, was one of the few pharmaceuticals Peat recommended regularly—precisely because it blocks serotonin's inflammatory and vasoconstrictive effects.
