# Serotonin

This article covers serotonin in Peat's framework; the estrogen-serotonin-prolactin stress loop is covered in [[estrogen]] and [[prolactin]]. Everything the public has been told about serotonin is approximately the opposite of what the underlying physiology shows. It is not a happiness molecule, it is not concentrated in the brain, it is not in short supply in depressed patients, and the drugs that increase its activity are not, in any mechanistic sense, treating depression. Ray Peat spent decades arguing that serotonin is instead a damage-induced inflammatory mediator, the biological signal of injury, stress, and resource depletion. Its proper physiological role is to shift metabolism toward glycolysis when an organism is overwhelmed, the same shift that defines hibernation, social defeat, and cancer. "Injecting serotonin never does relieve depression," Peat wrote in [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|"Serotonin, Depression, and Aggression: The Problem of Brain Energy"]], "but sometimes adrenalin or dopamine does. Injecting serotonin, or increasing its activity, can cause sedation, helplessness, or apathy." The statement reorganizes the entire field of psychiatric medicine.

## What Serotonin Actually Is

The first fact that reorients the entire discussion is anatomical. Ninety-five percent of the body's serotonin lives in the intestine; the brain contains roughly one percent. The popular image of serotonin as a neurotransmitter deficient in depressed brains contradicts the basic distribution of the molecule: it is overwhelmingly a gut substance. In [[serotonin-effects-in-disease-aging-and-inflammation|"Serotonin: Effects in Disease, Aging and Inflammation"]], Peat placed the number in context: "The normal intestine contains about 95% of the serotonin in the body, and in the normal person only about 1% of the dietary tryptophan is converted to serotonin."

The second reorienting fact comes from what happens when serotonin is injected into an animal. The outcome is not euphoria but sedation, lethargy, and eventually helplessness. A 1994 study by Koren-Schwartzer and colleagues measured the effect directly: "Injection of serotonin to rats induced a dramatic fall in brain ATP level. Stimulation of anaerobic glycolysis was also reflected by a marked increase in lactate content in brain. All these serotonin-induced changes in brain are characteristic for cerebral ischemia." Serotonin produces, in metabolic terms, the same pattern of ATP collapse and lactic acid accumulation that defines a stroke.

The third fact is the one Peat returned to most often. Serotonin is an injury signal. "The body has many responses to injury, and serotonin and estrogen are two of the most basic, primitive responses to generalized injury," he explained in a [[2011-05-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-misconceptions-relating-to-serotonin-and-melatonin|2011 episode of *Ask the Herb Doctor*]]. "If you get sunburned, in the next few hours you can measure a large amount of serotonin in the urine. That same thing happens for any kind of injury, including ionizing radiation. Dental X-rays are enough to activate the production of serotonin." The substance appears in the bloodstream whenever tissues are damaged, and its concentration tracks the severity of the damage. This is the behavior of an alarm, not a happiness molecule.

## The Bioenergetic Action

Serotonin's mechanism centers on interference with oxidative metabolism. Where healthy cells produce energy through mitochondrial respiration and generate carbon dioxide as a waste product, serotonin-exposed cells shift toward glycolysis and produce lactic acid instead. The shift is immediate and reversible. Give an animal serotonin, and its brain ATP falls. Take the serotonin away, and the ATP recovers.

The "Brain Energy" article laid out the cascade: "In learned helplessness, the level of serotonin is high, and an excess of serotonin helps to create the state of learned helplessness. Serotonin activates glycolysis, forming lactic acid. Excess lactic acid tends to decrease efficient energy production by interfering with mitochondrial respiration." The loop is self-reinforcing: serotonin turns off oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis takes over, and the lactic acid produced by glycolysis further impairs the mitochondria.

The practical consequence is hypothermia. Serotonin lowers body temperature by decreasing the metabolic rate, producing a clinical picture indistinguishable from [[hypothyroidism]]. "Many of the symptoms produced by excess serotonin are also the symptoms of hypothyroidism," Peat observed. "Thyroid, progesterone, and high quality protein nutrition are central to protection against the serotonin syndromes." [[Thyroid]] hormone is the biological opposite of serotonin, supporting exactly the oxidative machinery that serotonin disables.

The temperature effect also explains serotonin's role in hibernation. "In hibernating animals, the stress of a declining food supply causes increased serotonin production. In humans and animals that don't hibernate, the stress of winter causes very similar changes. Serotonin lowers temperature by decreasing the metabolic rate." The hormone is the chemical signal that initiates torpor. In humans who cannot actually hibernate, the same signal produces the seasonal weight gain, depression, and cognitive slowing that pass for normal winter physiology.

## Learned Helplessness and the Authoritarian Personality

The learned helplessness paradigm provides a striking experimental model. When an animal is subjected to inescapable stress, it eventually stops trying to escape even when escape becomes possible. "Animals that would normally swim for hours will stop swimming after a few minutes and allow themselves to drown. They simply don't have enough mental or physical energy to overcome challenges." The physiological state accompanying this behavior is characterized by elevated serotonin and collapsed brain ATP.

Peat extended the observation to personality. "High serotonin production causes behavioral inhibition and harm avoidance, which are traits of the authoritarian personality, while anti-authoritarians tend to have novelty seeking personalities, with high [[dopamine]] and low serotonin functions." What serotonin produces is deference, withdrawal, and acceptance of subordinate status. A dominant animal has low serotonin and high dopamine; a defeated animal has the reverse. If serotonin mediates the biological state of defeat, raising serotonin is not a treatment for depression but an intensification of the underlying physiology. Learning itself suffers: "Increased serotonin interferes with the consolidation of learning," [[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|"Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging"]] argued. See [[learned-helplessness]] for the full behavioral model.

## The Gut Origin

Because the intestine contains nearly all of the body's serotonin, any serious discussion of serotonin physiology has to start with the gut. Almost anything that irritates or stresses the intestine will trigger a release. "Just friction, or scratching or stretching the intestine is enough to cause it to release serotonin into the bloodstream," the 2011 [[endotoxin-stress-depression|"Endotoxin, Stress, Depression"]] newsletter argued. "Serotonin increases the permeability of the intestine and blood vessels, and so is likely to be a major cause of the absorption of [[endotoxin]] during intestinal irritation or stress."

This creates a vicious loop. Irritation causes serotonin release, serotonin increases gut permeability, permeability allows endotoxin into the bloodstream, and endotoxin triggers more serotonin release, both locally and centrally. "Bacterial endotoxin increases serotonin release from the intestine, and increases its synthesis in the brain and liver. It also stimulates its release from platelets." Fiber fermentation adds another layer: "The formation of serotonin in the intestine is also stimulated by the lactate, propionate and butyrate that are formed by bacteria fermenting fiber and starch, but these bacteria also produce endotoxin." The "short-chain fatty acids" celebrated by the fiber-and-microbiome literature are, in Peat's framework, serotonin triggers produced by the same bacteria that produce endotoxin.

The raw carrot functions as a mechanical scrubber of the intestine, binding estrogen, serotonin, and endotoxin together. "As well as absorbing the estrogen, the raw carrot reduces the absorption of endotoxin and serotonin," Peat noted in a [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 *EastWest Healing* interview]]. The fiber in raw carrot is rigid enough to sweep the upper intestine without fermenting into the lactate and butyrate that drive serotonin production. A broader principle guides the dietary framework: "A diet that reduces fermentation and endotoxin, with an abundance of calcium, fruit, milk, and cheese, for example, can help to shift the balance away from lactic acid, estrogen, and serotonin, toward carbon dioxide, progesterone, and thyroid." The distinction between those two metabolic states is the central axis of Peat's nutritional system.

## The Cortisol Self-Perpetuation Loop

Beyond the intestinal cascade, serotonin participates in a second vicious loop involving the adrenal stress hormones. "Tryptophan is turned into serotonin which activates the pituitary stress hormones, increasing cortisol, and intensifying catabolism, which releases more tryptophan," [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context"]] argued. "It suppresses thyroid function, which leads to an increased need for the stress hormones. Serotonin impairs glucose oxidation, and contributes to many of the problems associated with diabetes."

Over-training is one clinical example. Muscle protein is broken down, releasing [[tryptophan]] into the bloodstream, which then crosses into the brain and drives serotonin synthesis. "The fatigue produced by over-training is probably produced by a tryptophan and serotonin overload, resulting from catabolism of muscle proteins and stress-induced increases in serotonin. Muscle catabolism also releases a large amount of cysteine, and cysteine, methionine, and tryptophan suppress thyroid function. Stress also liberates free fatty acids from storage, and these fatty acids increase the uptake of tryptophan into the brain, increasing the formation of serotonin." The athlete who over-trains and cannot recover is trapped in a chemical loop his own physiology is generating.

The same loop operates in the malnourished: "Malnutrition, and specifically protein deficiency, produces an inflammatory state that involves extreme serotonin dominance." [[Gelatin]] enters the picture here. Muscle meat contains high concentrations of tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine, all of which push toward serotonin and thyroid suppression; gelatin contains almost none of these amino acids, providing instead glycine and proline. Balancing muscle meat with gelatin or bone broth is a direct attempt to lower the overall tryptophan load of the diet.

## Inflammation, Clots, and Carcinoid

The classification of serotonin as an inflammatory mediator cuts against the mainstream image of serotonin as a neurotransmitter with incidental peripheral actions. "Serotonin is a mediator of stress that has to be systematically balanced by the systems that support high energy respiratory metabolism," Peat wrote. "The use of supplements of tryptophan, hydroxytryptophan, or of the serotonin promoting antidepressant drugs, seems to be biologically inappropriate." Released from platelets, serotonin causes vasoconstriction and promotes clotting. Released from mast cells, it produces edema, bronchoconstriction, and joint swelling alongside [[histamine]]. Both are stored in the same cells and released by the same stimuli.

In multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer's disease, "blood clots have been microscopically associated with brain lesions, and the platelets in clots release neurotoxic serotonin. The serotonin reuptake inhibitors are presumed to have the same effect on the brain that they have on blood platelets. They inhibit the ability of platelets to retain and concentrate serotonin, allowing it to stay in the plasma. This uptake-inhibited condition is a model of the platelet behavior seen in multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer's disease." SSRIs deliberately recreate the platelet condition associated with two of the most feared neurological diseases.

The carcinoid syndrome provides the purest clinical demonstration of what chronic high serotonin does to a human body. "In an advanced case of carcinoid, 60% of the tryptophan can be turned into serotonin. Especially if the tumor has invaded the liver, the serotonin will circulate in the bloodstream at high levels, producing symptoms of flushing, sweating, diarrhea, nausea, anxiety, reduced urination, muscle and joint pains, and, in late stages, very often cardiovascular disease (especially inflammation, fibroma and calcification of the valves in the right side of the heart) and aggressive behavior and psychosis." Every element of this syndrome is a concentrated version of what chronic moderate serotonin elevation produces more slowly in the general population.

## Autism and Movement Disorders

The pattern of elevated serotonin in specific neurological conditions provides further evidence for its identity as an injury marker. "Autistic children and their relatives have high concentrations of serotonin in their serum and platelets," Peat wrote. "Some studies have reported that thyroid supplements help autistic children, and anti-serotonin drugs have caused improvement in both children and adults." The connection extends to Parkinson's: "The benefits seen from increasing the concentration of dopamine could result from dopamine's antagonism to serotonin. Anti-serotonin drugs can alleviate the symptoms, and 5-hydroxytryptophan can worsen the symptoms."

Irritable bowel syndrome presents perhaps the cleanest example because the gut contains so much of the hormone. "Irritable bowel syndrome, another kind of movement disorder, can be treated effectively with anti-serotonin agents. This syndrome is very common in women, with premenstrual exacerbations, when estrogen is highest." The premenstrual worsening points directly to the estrogen-serotonin connection. Chorea, the involuntary dancing movement produced by certain neurological conditions, shows up as a side effect of oral contraceptives: the estrogen increases serotonin sufficiently to produce uncontrollable motor activity in susceptible women.

## Pineal Conversion to Melatonin

The pineal gland, under the influence of nocturnal adrenaline, converts serotonin to [[melatonin]]. Peat did not regard melatonin as safe, but he recognized that its production represented an attempt to neutralize a more toxic substance. "The body has enzymes activated by stress," he explained in the 2011 KMUD episode. "In the pineal gland, adrenaline is the signal that rises in darkness to activate the enzyme which converts serotonin into melatonin. This same enzyme system can be found in an inflamed breast or a rheumatic knee joint. In these situations, melatonin seems to be performing the same function: eliminating serotonin, which is the actual pro-inflammatory substance."

The finding that the same serotonin-to-melatonin enzyme operates in inflamed tissues is significant. The body recognizes serotonin as a pro-inflammatory compound that needs to be eliminated. "In general, melatonin is functioning as an antidote to serotonin. Biologically, it's evidence that you're responding to stress with a detoxifying process." The full relationship between the two molecules is covered in [[melatonin]].

## Anti-Serotonin Interventions

Peat's practical framework involves dietary foundations and a short pharmaceutical toolkit. The dietary layer includes adequate calcium (which suppresses parathyroid hormone, cortisol, and serotonin simultaneously), adequate high-quality protein favoring gelatin over muscle meat, avoidance of fermentable fiber and starch, and regular raw carrot to interrupt intestinal serotonin and endotoxin absorption. [[Thyroid]] supplementation is the single most effective anti-serotonin drug because thyroid hormone directly supports the oxidative metabolism that serotonin suppresses. [[Progesterone]] is the second primary intervention, inhibiting serotonergic nerve firing directly; Peat occasionally described its action as similar to LSD's but without the toxicity.

Cyproheptadine, an older antihistamine with strong anti-serotonin activity, was Peat's most-recommended pharmaceutical for serotonin excess. In "Endotoxin, Stress, Depression," he cited Selye's demonstration that "an interference with blood circulation that normally didn't produce serious harm would cause similar ulcers, and also paralysis of the hind legs, if he injected serotonin into the animal. If the anti-serotonin drug, cyproheptadine, was given to the animal before the serotonin, they were protected."

Tianeptine is perhaps the most interesting of the anti-serotonin drugs because it is a marketed antidepressant that works by promoting serotonin uptake rather than inhibiting it. Sold as Stabilon, it "promotes the uptake of serotonin, so it is less active in the synapse. It turns out to have beneficial effects on arthritis, diabetes, and inflammatory conditions related to high serotonin." A drug doing the exact opposite of SSRIs produces comparable antidepressant effects and additionally treats the inflammatory conditions that SSRIs worsen. Other anti-serotonin agents include bromocriptine, lisuride, and ondansetron. [[Aspirin]] functions at the margin by reducing the inflammatory prostaglandins that amplify serotonin's tissue-level effects.

## SSRIs

To the extent SSRIs raise synaptic serotonin, they intensify exactly the pathology serotonin produces. Their antidepressant effects, when real, come from unrelated mechanisms: increased adrenaline and dopamine activity, direct effects on enzyme systems, and notably the increase of allopregnanolone, a metabolite of progesterone that acts on GABA receptors. Three years before Prozac received FDA approval in late 1987, the German BGA refused to approve it based on Lilly's own studies showing a fivefold higher rate of suicides and suicide attempts in previously non-suicidal patients versus those on older antidepressants; Dr. David Healy later estimated that roughly 50,000 people had committed suicide on Prozac above the number who would have done so untreated. The drugs also cause bone loss through their effect on platelet serotonin release (see [[prolactin]] for the full mechanism), worsen inflammation in susceptible tissues, and contribute to the multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer's-like platelet conditions discussed above.

## Multiple Sclerosis and the Sex Difference

Women are much more likely than men to suffer from depression, irritable bowel syndrome, multiple sclerosis, premenstrual mood disturbance, and the cluster of inflammatory-neurological conditions Peat traced to serotonin. The reason runs through estrogen: "Serotonin and histamine are increased by estrogen, and their activation mimics the effects of estrogen."

Multiple sclerosis, which disproportionately affects women during their reproductive years, fits the pattern dramatically. "The high incidence of multiple sclerosis in women, and its onset during their reproductive years, is well known. The number of brain lesions is associated with the ratio of estrogen to progesterone. Estrogen activates mast cells to release histamine and serotonin, and activated mast cells can produce brain edema and demyelination. Blood clots have been microscopically associated with brain lesions like those in multiple sclerosis, and the platelets in clots release neurotoxic serotonin." The pathology of MS, in Peat's reading, is a direct expression of estrogen-driven serotonin and histamine release producing localized inflammatory damage in brain tissue. The protective factor is progesterone, which the reproductive years interrupt cyclically with high peaks during the luteal phase.

## Bystander Damage

A final observation places serotonin in a broader context of cellular damage biology. Peat described an experiment on the radiation bystander effect: cells in a culture dish given the X-ray exposure from dental imaging will emit photons for over an hour. "If you put unexposed cells in the water with those injured cells, the unexposed cells will begin mutating hours later due to the emissions, such as serotonin, released into the water. This bystander effect can cause mutations for 30 generations." Cells injured by radiation release serotonin into their environment, and neighboring cells absorb the serotonin and develop mutations of their own. The injury signal turns out to be the injury mechanism, continuing the damage thirty generations after the original exposure ended.

This places serotonin in the same conceptual category as [[linoleic-acid]] and other persistent metabolic disruptors. The initial insult creates a chemical environment that perpetuates itself long after the original cause has passed, and breaking the perpetuation requires active intervention against the propagating signal itself. Thyroid hormone and magnesium accelerate repair in this context because they support the oxidative metabolism that the serotonin signal is suppressing. This, finally, is why the serotonin story matters beyond psychiatry. It is not a mood molecule; it is a damage-propagation signal, and the conditions that look like separate diseases (depression, IBS, fibrosis, autism, MS, learned helplessness, osteoporosis, carcinoid) are variations on the single theme of elevated serotonin producing the metabolic state of a cell that is trying to shut itself down.
