# DHEA

DHEA (dehydroepiandrosterone) is the most abundant steroid hormone in the human body and one of Ray Peat's "basic stabilizing molecules" alongside [[pregnenolone]] and [[progesterone]]. Produced from [[cholesterol]] via pregnenolone, DHEA supports muscle anabolism, heat production, and neurological function while opposing [[cortisol]]'s catabolic, inflammatory effects. Its decline with age—falling roughly 2% per year after the mid-twenties—parallels the broader pattern of protective hormone loss that Peat connected to degenerative disease.

As Peat described, "DHEA is anabolic for the muscles, but it activates heat production. It causes you to 'waste' heat, but it isn't really wasted because it keeps your nerve function up and inflammation down."

## Position in the Steroid Cascade

DHEA sits at a branch point in steroid synthesis. From [[cholesterol]], the body makes pregnenolone—"the first hormone produced from cholesterol when our thyroid function is adequate." Pregnenolone can then convert either to [[progesterone]] (leading to cortisol and aldosterone) or to DHEA (leading to [[testosterone]] and [[estrogen]]).

This branching explains why Peat considered pregnenolone the safest first-line supplement: it "turns into either [progesterone or DHEA], according to need," allowing the body to direct hormone production based on actual requirements rather than forcing a specific pathway.

DHEA itself can convert to testosterone (protective) or, under stress conditions via aromatase, to estrogen (harmful). This conversion vulnerability—shared with testosterone but not with progesterone—explains Peat's caution about DHEA supplementation, particularly for women.

## The Neurosteroid Function

DHEA is synthesized directly in the brain, independent of adrenal production. As Peat described the process occurring in the substantia nigra: "Right in the nerves... this process is going on: energy, sugar, cholesterol, pregnenolone, progesterone. The endocrine process stabilizes the structure and the consciousness."

The brain's local production of DHEA and related neurosteroids suggests their importance extends beyond systemic hormone function. They appear to be essential for neural stability itself—maintaining the energized, organized state of neurons that Peat connected to consciousness and cognitive function.

## Why DHEA Falls

DHEA deficiency in the forties—often accompanied by pregnenolone deficiency—reflects the same systemic pattern Peat identified across all degenerative conditions. As he noted in email exchanges, this deficiency "usually [occurs] because of an imbalance of thyroid and [[estrogen]]."

The mechanism is circular: inadequate [[thyroid]] function reduces cholesterol-to-pregnenolone conversion; lower pregnenolone means less DHEA; accumulated PUFA and rising estrogen activate aromatase, converting what DHEA remains into more estrogen; estrogen suppresses thyroid function further. Each declining hormone reflects and reinforces the decline of the others.

When adrenal and thyroid hormones fall, as Peat explained, "energy decreases and the brain shifts to emergency drivers like [[adrenaline]] and [[serotonin]], which increase functions temporarily but risk harming vital functions." The shift from DHEA/pregnenolone-supported metabolism to stress-hormone-driven emergency function characterizes the progression from youthful vitality to aged degeneration.

## Supplementation Considerations

Peat's supplementation hierarchy placed pregnenolone first, DHEA second, and direct hormone supplementation (testosterone, progesterone) third. The logic: start with the most upstream intervention that allows the body to produce what it needs, rather than forcing specific hormones that may create imbalances.

For men, Peat considered ten milligrams of DHEA "pretty safe." For women, he was more cautious: "In women, too much DHEA can have a masculinizing effect, so it's best to work on the diet, or to use pregnenolone, which doesn't lead to an imbalance between progesterone and DHEA."

The masculinizing risk for women reflects DHEA's position as a testosterone precursor. While testosterone is protective for women in appropriate amounts, excessive DHEA supplementation can push androgen levels beyond the optimal range—producing acne, hair growth, and voice changes without the intended metabolic benefits.

## Comprehensive Support

Peat's approach to DHEA deficiency was characteristically systemic. As he wrote regarding recovery from PUFA exposure or heavy metal toxicity, "it helps to use all of the supports possible: thyroid supplements, pregnenolone supplements, possibly DHEA and progesterone, saturated fats, sugar, everything that works in the same direction."

DHEA supplementation in isolation, without addressing thyroid function, estrogen excess, and PUFA accumulation, misses the underlying pattern. The goal is restoring the metabolic conditions under which the body produces adequate DHEA itself—supplementation serving as a bridge while the deeper problems are addressed.
