# DHEA

DHEA is the third member of the protective steroid cascade that begins with [[cholesterol]], moves through [[pregnenolone]], and branches into [[progesterone]] and DHEA depending on tissue and demand. In Ray Peat's framework, these three substances form the youth-associated axis whose decline marks the territory of aging more reliably than any other hormonal signal. "The first barrier to harm is the group of steroids: pregnenolone, progesterone, DHEA, and testosterone," Peat explained in a [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|2019 *Jodellefit* interview]]. "All of these have broadly overlapping protective effects that protect your energy apparatus and sensory apparatus, keeping things stable and working. Injury simply doesn't happen when you have a great abundance of those things."

Yet DHEA occupies a different position in Peat's recommendations than progesterone or pregnenolone. Where he wrote about progesterone as the universally safe anti-stress hormone and pregnenolone as the self-regulating parent steroid, his view of DHEA was measured, qualified, and increasingly cautious as the decades passed. DHEA was the one protective steroid he effectively stopped recommending in favor of its upstream precursor, not because it failed but because it was too easy for users to overshoot the narrow window in which its benefits appeared without its liabilities. The anti-aging industry of the 1990s and 2000s marketed DHEA at 25 mg and 50 mg per tablet. Peat's recommendations hovered between 2 mg and 10 mg. That gap is the difference between restoring a youthful steroid profile and feeding the very estrogen conversion problem DHEA is supposed to help solve.

## A Brain Hormone, Not an Adrenal Byproduct

DHEA (dehydroepiandrosterone, sometimes written DHA) is most often described in textbooks as the dominant steroid produced by the adrenal glands. Peat found that framing incomplete. In his 2006 newsletter [[progesterone-pregnenolone-dhea-three-youth-associated-hormones|"Progesterone, Pregnenolone & DHEA"]], he noted that "many researchers still think of it as a substance produced by the adrenal glands, but experiments show that animals without adrenals are able to produce it in normal amounts. Much of it is formed in the brain (from pregnenolone), but it is probably produced in other organs, including the skin. The brain contains a much higher concentration of DHEA than the blood does."

The anatomical distribution is diagnostic. Like progesterone, DHEA concentrates in the brain at levels far higher than blood measurements suggest. This is the signature of a neurosteroid whose primary job is local protection of neural tissue, not an endocrine signal meant to travel through the circulation to distant targets. Peat's [[steroids-thyroid|Blake College "Steroids/Thyroid" newsletter]] laid out the branching in structural terms: "Depending on the tissue, pregnenolone will be converted by enzymes in the cytoplasm into either progesterone or DHEA. The brain contains much more pregnenolone, DHEA, and progesterone than do other organs or the blood, and these levels decrease progressively with age."

The decline is not a modest curve. "In old age, we produce only about 5% as much as we do in youth," Peat wrote. "This is about the same decrease that occurs with progesterone and pregnenolone. The other hormones (for example, cortisone) do not decrease so much; as a result, our balance shifts continually during aging toward dominance by hormones such as cortisone, which use up more and more body substance, without rebuilding it."

This is the aging thesis in compact form. What looks like "rising cortisol with age" is not the adrenals becoming dysregulated. It is the protective steroids collapsing, which leaves the catabolic stress hormones unopposed. A middle-aged person with cortisol at the high end of normal and DHEA at the low end of normal is not hormonally balanced. They are under siege.

## The Anti-Cortisol Function

DHEA's protective role is primarily about restraining cortisol and restoring what cortisol dissolves. "Starvation, aging, and stress cause the skin to become thin and fragile," Peat wrote. "An excess of cortisone, whether it is from medical treatment, or from stress, aging, or malnutrition, does the same thing. Material from the skin is dissolved to provide nutrition for the more essential organs. DHEA blocks these destructive effects of cortisone, and actively restores the normal growth and repair processes to those organs, strengthening the skin and bones and other organs."

The mechanism is not a single receptor interaction. DHEA operates as a broad-spectrum buffer against the entire catabolic program cortisol drives. In the *Jodellefit* conversation, Peat described the cumulative protection: "You can take a cortisol pill or prednisone and have very little harm from it if you're fairly saturated with progesterone, DHEA, and testosterone, because they prevent the action as well as taking care of some of the things that cortisol would produce. With age, if you're producing less of the protective steroids, even a moderate amount of cortisol begins eating up your muscles and skin, and eventually, it can start deteriorating your heart, lungs, and brain."

The list of tissues DHEA helps rebuild is long. Bone growth has been stimulated *in vitro*. Skin thickens. Hair follicles reactivate. Connective tissues regain their elasticity. Peat's most vivid statement on the last point came in a 2017 [[2017-01-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-precautionary-principle-part-1|*Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]]: "For connective tissue and discs, DHEA and thyroid are crucial. If thyroid function is low, all connective tissues tend to be waterlogged, soft, and lacking elasticity. Normalizing your serum level of DHEA, which might only take 5mg a day, can have very quick, dramatic effects on connective tissues, ligaments, tendons, and discs." The "very quick" is characteristic. Progesterone and DHEA, when they work, tend to work in hours or days, not months.

In the 1985 *Rejuvenation* article [[energy-entropy-and-estrogens-in-aging|"Energy, Entropy and Estrogens in Aging"]], Peat placed DHEA alongside progesterone as the twin anti-estrogen hormones whose decline defines the aging phenotype in both sexes: "The same pattern of estrogen dominance is seen in both sexes with aging, and in all species studied. The anti-estrogens, especially progesterone and dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) decline similarly with age in both sexes."

## The Estrogen Conversion Problem

This is where DHEA departs from progesterone in the practical imagination. Progesterone cannot turn into estrogen; the biosynthetic cascade runs in one direction and cannot be reversed. DHEA can turn into estrogen, and under the wrong conditions, that conversion dominates its effects. The enzyme responsible is [[aromatase]], whose upstream stimulation and industry history belong to the [[estrogen]] hub. What matters here is that DHEA sits directly upstream of the androgen and estrogen branch point, and every milligram of DHEA is a potential substrate for aromatase depending on what else is happening in the tissue.

The governing variables are thyroid function, progesterone status, and the overall stress signature. "If your thyroid is very low, you should be cautious with the DHEA, because stress hormones can cause it to turn to estrogen," Peat wrote in his email correspondence. The same theme runs through decades of transcripts. Sarah Johanneson Murray, his longtime co-host on KMUD, reinforced the point in the 2017 *Precautionary Principle* episode: "You must be careful with DHEA because it can raise your estradiol levels. You might want blood checks to make sure your hormone ratios are good." Andrew Murray added the operative condition: "The important thing with DHEA is to make sure your thyroid function is adequate, otherwise that conversion to estradiol is more likely to occur."

The mechanism Peat described involves cortisol as the direct activator of the conversion. In a 2013 [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|*Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] on aging, a caller named Gina asked whether taking pregnenolone would drive her already-high DHEA levels higher. Peat's answer cut against the intuition of anyone thinking in linear precursor-product terms: "No. When your estrogen is not being controlled, it likely over-activates your adrenals. DHEA tends to go up with cortisol, and cortisol is a major activator of aromatase. The function of pregnenolone is to lower the excess cortisol and prevent the overstimulation of aromatase."

Read carefully, the mechanism is circular and self-reinforcing in the worst way. Estrogen excess drives cortisol up. Cortisol activates aromatase. Aromatase converts DHEA to more estrogen. The high DHEA reading that looks protective on a lab report is actually the fuel feeding the very process destroying the person. Peat's solution was not to push DHEA down directly but to attack the upstream driver (cortisol) with pregnenolone, thyroid, and the nutritional corrections that restore oxidative metabolism.

Progesterone, at the steroid level, is the specific agent that prevents this conversion. In a 2011 [[2011-07-04-its-rainmaking-time-it-s-rainmaking-time-life-supporting-substances|*It's Rainmaking Time*]] conversation with Kim Greenhouse, Peat stated the relationship directly: "In a 1971 article, I mentioned DHEA as being one of the basic protective hormones like progesterone. Progesterone happens to be the main thing that protects against the conversion of DHEA into estrogen." This is why the early Peat protocols paired DHEA with progesterone and vitamin E rather than offering DHEA alone.

## Dosing, and Why Peat Stopped Recommending It

Young people produce about 12 to 15 mg of DHEA per day. That number is the ceiling on what a healthy endocrine system generates at peak function, and it was also the ceiling Peat used to size every recommendation he gave. "At the age of 50, about 4 mg. of DHEA per day will usually restore the level of DHEA in the blood to a youthful level," he wrote in the 2006 newsletter. "It is important to avoid taking more than needed, since some people (especially if they are deficient in progesterone, pregnenolone, or thyroid) can turn the excess into estrogen or testosterone, and large amounts of those sex hormones can disturb the function of the thymus gland and the liver."

The practical dose range Peat named repeatedly was 2 to 10 mg per day. Sarah Johanneson Murray summarized the sex- and age-specific version in the 2020 [[2020-09-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2020-kmud|*Ask the Herb Doctor*]] episode: "Dr. Peat usually recommends no more than 5 milligrams for a woman under 50, maybe up to 10 for a woman over 50, and for men, 10 to 20 milligrams." These numbers are an order of magnitude below what the anti-aging supplement industry has sold. In a 2012 [[2012-11-23-world-puja-world-puja-foundational-hormones|*World Puja*]] conversation, co-host Taya Skerry flagged the gap directly: "You only recommend one to two milligrams a day, which is infinitesimal compared to most supplements that start at 12, 25, or 50 milligrams." Peat's reply was blunt: "Teenage boys only make about 12 milligrams per day at the maximum. So if you take 10 milligrams when you're 30 or 40, some of it is likely to be turned into estrogen."

The problem is not only that over-the-counter doses exceed the body's endogenous production. It is that DHEA at subtly elevated levels makes people feel good in a way that is difficult to self-regulate. In the same 2012 conversation, Peat told the story of why he stopped selling it:

> Several years ago I stopped giving people DHEA because they felt so good they would keep taking more and more until one person enlarged his liver and had the estrogen level of a teenage girl. I shifted to recommending almost everyone use pregnenolone instead. In animal experiments, rats given a massive dose of pure pregnenolone showed no change in hormones if they were happy and healthy. But if the rat was under stress, it lowered the stress hormones. So no matter how much you take, it doesn't disturb your hormones, but if you're under stress, it will remove the stress hormones.

This is the pivot point in Peat's evolving view. Pregnenolone is self-regulating because it converts to whatever steroid the tissue actually needs and does not convert at all when no conversion is needed. DHEA sits one step downstream of that branching decision, already committed to the androgen and estrogen pathway, and in tissues with active aromatase it will simply become estrogen whether the person wants it to or not. The safer protocol is to supply the precursor and let the body decide.

Peat's 2006 newsletter reached essentially the same conclusion for a different reason: "One study has found that the only hormone abnormality in a group of Alzheimer's patients' brains was an excess of DHEA. In cell culture, DHEA can cause changes in glial cells resembling those seen in the aging brain. These observations suggest that DHEA should be used with caution. Supplements of pregnenolone and thyroid seem to be the safest way to optimize DHEA production."

## The Vitamin E Delivery System and the 1986 Patent

When Peat did recommend DHEA directly, he put it in the same delivery matrix he used for progesterone: dissolved in [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]]. The reasoning was that DHEA taken as crystalline powder or pressed tablets enters the liver first-pass, and the liver is where the conversion to estrogen most easily occurs. "I used to sell DHEA dissolved in Vitamin E," Peat told Skerry in 2012. "This makes it very quick-acting and controllable; it circulates and distributes without affecting your liver. If you take it in crystalline powdered form, your liver gets the first opportunity to metabolize it, and that's when it most easily turns into estrogen. If it's dissolved completely in oil, like olive oil, coconut oil, or butter, it helps keep it from going into the liver first."

Vitamin E also supplies its own anti-estrogenic and progesterone-sparing effects. The combination of DHEA plus tocopherol is therefore both a delivery improvement and a metabolic safeguard, and it is the composition at the core of Peat's 1986 [[ray-peat-patent-1986|US Patent 4,628,052]], "Pharmaceutical Compositions Containing Dehydroepiandrosterone and Other Anesthetic Steroids in the Treatment of Arthritis and Other Joint Disabilities."

The patent reads as a concrete expression of his thinking. The preferred oral composition was "about 10 mg of the active material, preferably DHEA, about 50 mg pregnenolone, about 5 mg progesterone in 1200 mg tocopherol." The preferred topical composition was 5 percent DHEA, 80 percent mixed tocopherols, up to 10 percent olive oil, and up to 3 percent progesterone. The attached case reports describe a 78-year-old man whose arthritic knees became comfortable within two hours of a single topical application, a 72-year-old woman whose rigid arthritic fingers regained flexibility within 40 minutes on the treated side while the untreated fingers remained stiff, and a 60-year-old woman who walked through a town sightseeing for two days after a single afternoon of applications, with no after-effects. These are not the outcomes typical of slow-acting anti-inflammatories. They are the outcomes of a hormone replacing something the body is missing.

The arthritis responsive to DHEA is the arthritis of cortisol dominance. When the stress hormones have been dissolving connective tissue faster than the protective hormones could rebuild it, a small supplement of the missing hormone tilts the ratio back and the tissue resumes repair. Peat framed it at the ratio level in the 2020 episode: "You need to get the ratio of cortisol to the protective DHEA, testosterone, progesterone, and pregnenolone correct. Lowering the ratio of cortisol allows connective tissues to repair themselves rather than being weakened by stress hormones."

## Regeneration, Diabetes, and Immunity

The regenerative signature extends beyond connective tissue. Peat cited animal experiments in which rabbits made experimentally diabetic with pancreas-toxic chemicals recovered after DHEA supplementation, with the insulin-producing cells visibly regenerated. "Diabetes can be produced experimentally by certain poisons which kill the insulin-producing cells in the pancreas," he wrote. "Rabbits were experimentally made diabetic, and when treated with DHEA their diabetes was cured. It was found that the insulin-producing cells had regenerated." The same mechanism that rebuilds connective tissue seems to rebuild secretory tissue when given the opportunity.

DHEA also functions as a thermogenic and fat-oxidation promoter. "DHEA, like the thyroid hormone, increases our heat production and ability to burn calories," Peat wrote. "This is one of the reasons that young people eat more without getting fat, and tolerate cold weather better." In a 2017 [[2017-04-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-2|*Ask the Herb Doctor*]] episode, he framed the metabolic consequence: "DHEA is anabolic for the muscles, but it activates heat production. It causes you to 'waste' heat, but it isn't really wasted because it keeps your nerve function up and inflammation down."

In his 1992 *Townsend Letter* piece [[failures-of-immunity|"Failures of Immunity"]], Peat placed DHEA among the "anesthetic steroids" whose deficiency predicts premature death from multiple causes: "A low level of the hormone DHEA is associated with premature death from various causes, including cancer, heart disease, and AIDS. In general, the things that lower vitality and immunity interfere with our ability to produce the protective steroids." The cancer protection, he noted, "probably involves many other functions" beyond the anti-estrogen effect alone, though the anti-estrogen effect was a central pillar and the very one that reversed when thyroid was inadequate.

## The Thyroid Prerequisite

Every practical discussion of DHEA in Peat's work routes back to thyroid function. Thyroid is required to convert cholesterol into the protective steroid cascade in the first place, and thyroid is required to prevent the stress-driven conversion of those steroids into estrogen. "Thyroid is necessary to produce these protective steroids," Peat explained in the *Jodellefit* interview. "Cholesterol is converted pretty massively into pregnenolone and the other protective steroids if you have Vitamin A and thyroid hormone adequately. But if you're deficient in either of those, the cholesterol will rise in your blood. That generally means that you're not making enough DHEA and testosterone."

Taking DHEA without correcting an underlying thyroid deficiency is doing the work in reverse. The hypothyroid state is exactly the state in which DHEA converts most readily to estrogen. Peat's preferred sequence was to restore thyroid function first, supplement pregnenolone second, and resort to direct DHEA only when those upstream interventions failed to produce the desired change. The 2006 newsletter captured the philosophy succinctly: "Supplements of pregnenolone and thyroid seem to be the safest way to optimize DHEA production."

For people determined to try direct DHEA supplementation anyway, Peat's guardrails were specific. Pair it with adequate thyroid. Keep the dose small (2 to 10 mg, taken with a little olive oil or butter, not the 25 to 50 mg industry standard). Use pregnenolone alongside it at roughly a 2:1 pregnenolone-to-DHEA ratio to buffer against stress-driven conversion. Monitor estradiol on bloodwork. Discontinue if conversion signs appear (oily skin, pimples, breast tenderness). Understand that the benefits disappear once the conversion starts, because the substance is no longer functioning as DHEA but as the very estrogen the protective steroids are supposed to oppose.

## The Measured Anti-Aging Position

The DHEA story illustrates something distinctive about Peat's overall stance on hormone supplementation. He was enthusiastic about progesterone and pregnenolone in almost every context, because neither can cause the downstream problems that misused DHEA causes. DHEA sat in its own category: a genuinely protective substance whose therapeutic window was too narrow and whose failure modes were too consequential for the way it was actually sold.

The anti-aging hype that surrounded DHEA in the 1990s treated it as a standalone fountain of youth in a capsule. Peat's reading was tighter and more specific. DHEA is part of a protective cascade whose collapse defines aging, and restoring that cascade is real medicine. But DHEA is also a single step away from estrogen in a hypothyroid, stressed, progesterone-deficient body, and at the doses the industry sold, it was as likely to deepen the estrogen problem as to correct it. The measured recommendation was to fix the foundation first (thyroid, nutrition, stress), take pregnenolone if more support was needed, and approach DHEA itself as a scalpel rather than a hammer.
