# Testosterone

Testosterone's central problem in Ray Peat's framework is that it is one enzymatic step away from becoming [[estrogen]], and the enzyme responsible, [[aromatase]], accelerates with every one of the conditions that define aging: stress, fat accumulation, inflammation, hypothyroidism, PUFA breakdown, and chronic cortisol elevation. The mechanism of that conversion is covered in detail in the [[aromatase]] article; what matters here is the consequence. The hormone that conventional medicine treats as the male counterpart of estrogen, the "masculinizing" steroid responsible for libido, muscle mass, and assertiveness, is in Peat's reading a protective anti-estrogen whose decline with age has been profoundly misunderstood. "Old men typically have more estrogen than women," as Peat noted in a 2022 discussion of aging physiology. The men whose testosterone is low are not men who have lost a "male hormone." They are men whose testosterone has been converted, inside the very organ meant to produce it, into the stress hormone it was supposed to oppose.

This inversion dominates Peat's view of male endocrine aging, testosterone replacement therapy, and prostate disease. It also explains why so much of his practical advice on testosterone deficiency does not begin with testosterone. It begins with the upstream conditions that either permit testosterone to remain testosterone or force it to become estrogen.

## Testosterone as Protective, Not Provocative

In the bioenergetic framework, testosterone belongs to the family of protective, anti-stress steroids alongside [[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], and [[dhea|DHEA]]. Peat's [[heart-and-hormones|"Heart and Hormones"]] article makes this explicit in the context of cardiac function. Where estrogen is a negative inotropic hormone that impairs the contraction of the heart and a negative lusitropic hormone that impairs its relaxation, "progesterone and testosterone are both positively inotropic and lusitropic, improving contraction and relaxation." The rapid alternation of contraction and relaxation that distinguishes a healthy heart from a failing one is sustained by the same hormones that are depleted by stress and estrogen dominance. "Shock and stress cause estrogen to increase, and decrease progesterone and testosterone," Peat observed in the same article. The acute clinical features of cardiogenic shock are, in this framing, the features of acute progesterone and testosterone deficiency.

Testosterone also protects against atherosclerosis, a finding that contradicts a generation of cardiology textbooks blaming male heart disease on the male hormone. In [[cholesterol-longevity-intelligence-and-health|"Cholesterol, Longevity, Intelligence, and Health"]], Peat noted that "there are many studies that show that testosterone and DHEA are protective against atherosclerosis. The LDL particle size is increased by androgens, and postprandial triglyceridemia is decreased." The large LDL particles that androgens produce are metabolically inert compared with the small, dense particles estrogen and PUFA generate, and the postprandial lipid surge associated with insulin resistance is blunted. Men with adequate testosterone are not men suffering a slow cardiotoxic insult; they are men whose circulation is still receiving the anti-stress, pro-oxidative signal that a youthful endocrine system provides.

The heart is only the clearest example. The same inotropic principle holds throughout the vascular tree, in skeletal muscle, in the nervous system, and in the immune organs. Testosterone's clinical reputation as a "virility hormone" is a downstream consequence of its more fundamental role as an energy-enabling, stress-opposing signal. A man with vigorous libido is not experiencing a specialized sexual effect of testosterone. He is experiencing the general effect of a metabolism that is producing enough ATP to run every system at full capacity.

## The Aging Inversion: Old Men Have More Estrogen Than Young Women

The conventional account of male aging is that testicular function declines and testosterone falls. Peat accepted the first half and inverted the second. "Since the time of Brown-Sequard and Eugen Steinach, it has been accepted that declining testicular function is a common feature of aging," he wrote in his [[prostate-cancer|"Prostate Cancer"]] article, "and testosterone was probably the first hormone that was clearly found to decrease consistently with aging." But testosterone's decrease is only half of the hormonal story, and the less important half. What rises in parallel, and what does most of the damage attributed to low testosterone, is estrogen.

"It was also recognized decades ago that estrogen rises in men during old age, as it rises in stress, disease, malnutrition, and hypothyroidism." The same article cites Siiteri and MacDonald's foundational work showing that fat tissue is a major source of estrogen production, and that fat "tends to increase with age, when thyroid and progesterone are deficient." The result is that "the conversion of testosterone to estrogen occurs in the testicle itself," amplified by the unfavorable hormonal environment that replaces the protective one of youth.

This is the rotation that Peat considered central to understanding prostate disease, sarcopenia, gynecomastia, cardiovascular decline, and the psychological flattening of old age. A man's total androgen pool does fall, but his tissue estrogen content climbs. He ends up, in Peat's summary, more estrogenized than a young woman. And because the tissues responsible for converting testosterone to estrogen include the testicles themselves, the decline in circulating testosterone is not simply a failure of production. It is a shift in the metabolic fate of the testosterone that is still being made. The raw material goes in, and estrogen comes out.

The age-related rise of luteinizing hormone feeds the loop. As LH rises to compensate for falling testosterone, it also stimulates aromatization. Peat noted in an email exchange that a man presenting with shrunken testes from exogenous testosterone and DHT "probably hasn't damaged" the underlying tissue, and that such testes "would resume functioning with good nutritional support," but he added a pointed diagnostic caution: "it would be good to have your LH and estrogen checked. If the LH is very high, using a little DHT for a while might be protective until it's more normal. Coffee, aspirin, vitamin D, milk and cheese would also protect against high LH." The protection offered by DHT in that specific situation is precisely its unavailability to aromatase, a point to which Peat returned often.

## The DHT Distinction

One of Peat's most practical observations about testosterone concerns what separates it from its more potent metabolite, [[dhea|dihydrotestosterone]]. "The special difference between testosterone and DHT is that testosterone is easily aromatized into estrogen, and DHT isn't," he wrote in his email correspondence. "There are several ways that the body can dispose of estrogen, but I haven't heard of that way of inactivating it; I don't think it happens in the body." The asymmetry is critical: DHT is downstream of testosterone, more androgenic at the receptor, and chemically unable to be converted back into estrogen. In a stressed man whose aromatase is already hyperactive, adding DHT directly avoids the leak that adding testosterone would open.

This explains one of the stranger pieces of conventional wisdom, the prescription of testosterone to men whose prostates are already being damaged by estrogen. The testosterone arrives, the damaged tissue converts a large fraction of it into the exact hormone driving the damage, and the physician interprets the resulting inflammation as a contraindication to future androgen support. The confusion is not pharmacological but ideological. Testosterone and estrogen are treated as opposites on a "sex hormone" axis, when in a body full of activated aromatase they are two ends of the same pipeline.

## TRT Pitfalls and the Aromatization Problem

Peat was not opposed to supplemental testosterone, but his recommendations for using it differed sharply from standard urological practice. "Pure testosterone on the skin is safe if the diet and thyroid function are good," he wrote in an email exchange, "but it's better to try supplements of pregnenolone first, and then DHEA, to normalize the testosterone production." The ordering matters. Pregnenolone, as the upstream precursor of every other steroid, allows the body to allocate the raw material where it is actually needed. DHEA provides an androgen reserve that the body can convert selectively. Topical testosterone is the most direct intervention but also the least informative, because it bypasses the regulatory logic that would otherwise determine which hormones the body makes.

The delivery route mattered to Peat as much as the order of intervention. "I think an oil or cream with real testosterone (and DHEA) is much better than the injections, which are usually an ester of testosterone in a toxic solvent." The esters used in intramuscular preparations, along with the solvents they are dissolved in, introduce pharmacokinetic and toxicological problems absent from straightforward topical administration. A small amount of real testosterone in oil, applied to the skin, reaches the bloodstream without the absorption spikes and solvent burden of the injected preparations.

The deeper problem with TRT, however, is one the prescribing protocols rarely address. If a man's low testosterone is the result of aggressive aromatization driven by fat accumulation, hypothyroidism, cortisol, or PUFA, then adding exogenous testosterone simply increases the substrate for the same enzymatic conversion. Some of the new testosterone will become estrogen exactly as the old testosterone did, and in some patients the serum estrogen climbs more than the serum testosterone. The apparent "failure" of TRT in these cases is not a failure of testosterone but a confirmation that the upstream conditions making the testosterone into estrogen were never addressed.

Peat's practical alternative to a purely supplement-based approach repeatedly returned to the environment in which the hormone would have to operate. "A high protein diet is helpful, and avoiding polyunsaturated fats helps to increase testosterone," he wrote in an email on exercise, "(coconut oil, butter, maybe MCT instead). Excess tryptophan can promote the catabolic cortisol, so supplementing gelatin might be helpful." The diet that supports endogenous testosterone is the same diet that suppresses aromatase and the same diet that protects thyroid function. There is no special testosterone diet; there is only the bioenergetic one.

## Thyroid as Upstream Support

The single most important upstream factor in male testosterone production is [[thyroid]] function. Peat framed this directly in a discussion of low testosterone and high cholesterol: "A small supplement of Armour thyroid or the equivalent could quickly lower the cholesterol, and since cholesterol is converted by thyroid into pregnenolone and DHEA, that would probably help the testosterone. Some shellfish (oysters, shrimp, squid, etc.) or low fat fish would provide trace minerals that might be lacking in your diet." The logic runs through the entire steroid cascade: thyroid hormone drives the conversion of cholesterol into pregnenolone, pregnenolone into DHEA and progesterone, and those into testosterone. When thyroid is low, the cascade stalls upstream of testosterone, and no amount of supplementation at the endpoint can compensate for the lost cascade behind it.

In the same email Peat warned that DHEA supplementation in a hypothyroid man could backfire for the same reason testosterone backfires in a stressed one: "Supplementing pregnenolone wouldn't have the risk of the DHEA being converted to estrogen, which tends to happen when thyroid function is low." Low thyroid does not merely reduce androgen production; it actively redirects the androgens that are produced down the estrogenic pathway. The same aromatase that is sensitive to cortisol and PUFA is also sensitive to thyroid status, and a hypothyroid man will aromatize a larger fraction of his available testosterone than a euthyroid one will.

The corollary is that a man with unexplained low testosterone should have his thyroid evaluated before his testicles, and that in many cases correction of the thyroid alone will restore endogenous testosterone production without any direct androgen intervention. This is why Peat's first line for a man with low T was almost never "get a prescription for testosterone." It was "check the thyroid, drop the PUFA, and try pregnenolone."

## The Prolactin and Cortisol Lowering Effect

Testosterone must also be interpreted against the hormones that suppress it. Peat was explicit in an email aside: "Testosterone -- it has to be interpreted in relation to cortisol, estrogen, and sex hormone binding globulin." A serum testosterone number means nothing on its own. The same value in a man with elevated cortisol and SHBG is functionally different from the same value in a man with normal cortisol and low SHBG. The free, bioavailable fraction, and the metabolic context around it, determine the clinical picture.

Two stress hormones in particular drive testosterone down. [[Cortisol]], the catabolic anti-inflammatory steroid that rises with chronic stress, opposes testosterone directly at multiple levels: it activates aromatase locally, suppresses LH centrally, and diverts the cholesterol substrate away from the gonadal pathway and into the adrenal one. [[Prolactin]], the lactation hormone that rises with estrogen and dopamine deficiency, also suppresses gonadal function. In the prostate cancer article, Peat cited survival data showing that "a high ratio of testosterone to estradiol or of testosterone to prolactin corresponded to better survival." The ratios matter because prolactin and estrogen are functionally on the same side of the equation, opposing testosterone, and their combined effect is captured in the ratio, not in any one number alone.

The clinical implication is that a man with low testosterone should have his prolactin checked as well, and that elevated prolactin is itself a signal of estrogen dominance, dopamine deficiency, or thyroid failure. Suppressing prolactin with bromocriptine or cabergoline, or more practically with the dopaminergic support of thyroid hormone and adequate sugar, often restores testosterone indirectly by removing the suppression that was driving it down.

## The Prostate Cancer Myth

Perhaps no corner of urology has been more distorted by the "testosterone as provocateur" framing than the management of prostate cancer. The standard teaching is that testosterone drives prostate growth, that high testosterone promotes prostate cancer, and that androgen deprivation is therefore the rational treatment. Peat's reading of the actual literature was the opposite on every point.

"It has seemed odd to many people," he wrote in the [[prostate-cancer|"Prostate Cancer"]] article, "that enlargement of the prostate should occur mainly in older men, if testosterone is the hormone that causes its growth, and estrogen is antagonistic to its growth. The nature of the growth of the old man's prostate is very different from its natural growth in youth." The prostate grows in youth under the influence of testosterone, and it grows again in age under conditions of declining testosterone. The two growth patterns cannot both be explained by testosterone. The hormone that is rising in the second case, and that is actually driving the pathological enlargement, is estrogen.

The survival data Peat cited make the inversion concrete. "Prostate cancer patients who had higher levels of LH and lower testosterone died most quickly. Considered separately, patients with higher testosterone levels had a better prognosis than those with lower levels, and patients with lower growth hormone levels did better than those with higher growth hormone levels." The observational correlation is the opposite of what the orthodoxy predicts. Men with more testosterone lived longer. Men with more of the stress markers, LH, growth hormone, prolactin, and estrogen, died faster.

Peat's proposed alternative, phrased in his characteristic question form, was that prostate cancer might respond to "testosterone therapy" or, "more practically, a generalized antiestrogenic therapy, using thyroid, progesterone, and pregnenolone." The conventional approach of androgen deprivation removes the one hormone that was still holding the tissue together and leaves the patient in a state of accelerated estrogenization. The bioenergetic approach does the opposite, reducing the aromatase activity and the estrogen burden while allowing testosterone to remain testosterone.

## Progesterone to Protect Testosterone

Progesterone's role in male physiology is covered in detail in the [[progesterone]] article, but one aspect bears directly on testosterone. Progesterone suppresses aromatase, opposes estrogen at the receptor, and reduces the cortisol output that would otherwise divert steroid synthesis away from the gonadal pathway. In Peat's framing, small doses of progesterone in men can "cure impotence" and have been used effectively to treat benign prostatic enlargement, which the standard account wrongly blames on testosterone. The mechanism is not that progesterone replaces testosterone but that progesterone removes the estrogenic pressure that was converting testosterone into its opposite. When aromatase calms down, the testosterone a man is already making stays testosterone for longer, and the functional androgen status improves without any change in the nominal production rate.

## Practical Considerations

Peat's practical recommendations for men with low testosterone converge on a sequence that is almost the reverse of standard practice. First, address the upstream conditions: correct hypothyroidism, eliminate PUFA, reduce stress and cortisol, improve protein intake with gelatin balancing tryptophan excess, and use saturated fats like coconut oil and butter as the fat base of the diet. Second, if direct supplementation is indicated, start with pregnenolone (100 mg was his typical reference dose) and add small amounts of DHEA (5 mg range) as the second step. Third, if testosterone itself is needed, use a topical oil or cream rather than an ester injection. Fourth, monitor the estrogen and LH alongside the testosterone, so that aromatization is visible rather than assumed to be absent. Fifth, use [[aspirin]], [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]], coffee, and [[progesterone]] as complementary anti-aromatase and anti-estrogenic tools rather than as optional extras.

The ordering reflects Peat's general principle that hormones should be interpreted in the context of the metabolic environment rather than in isolation. A testosterone value is a snapshot of a dynamic process, and the process is either operating in a youthful, low-aromatase, thyroid-supported state or in an aging, high-aromatase, thyroid-deficient state. The goal of intervention is not to move the number up but to move the state back toward the one in which the testosterone a man produces remains the hormone he thinks he is producing.
