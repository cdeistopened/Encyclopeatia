# Testosterone

The popular understanding of testosterone as simply "the male hormone" misses the metabolic complexity that Ray Peat emphasized. Testosterone is protective—it supports [[metabolism]], maintains muscle and bone, and opposes many of [[estrogen]]'s harmful effects. But unlike [[progesterone]], testosterone can be converted into estrogen by the enzyme aromatase. This vulnerability means that the conditions causing low testosterone in aging men—[[stress]], [[PUFA]] accumulation, [[inflammation]], poor [[thyroid]] function—often simultaneously increase the conversion of whatever testosterone remains into the estrogen that accelerates their decline.

As Peat explained, "Testosterone has protective effects, but progesterone is more protective in aging because it can't be converted to estrogen, whereas testosterone can."

## The Aromatase Problem

Aromatase is the enzyme that converts testosterone (and its precursor [[DHEA]]) into estrogen. In a healthy young man with good metabolic function, aromatase activity is low, and testosterone remains testosterone. But aromatase is activated by precisely the conditions that characterize aging and metabolic dysfunction: stress, inflammation, PUFA exposure, and cellular energy depletion.

As Peat described, "What causes DHEA and testosterone to be turned into estrogen excessively is anything causing stress, irritation, or inflammation. The enzyme that makes estrogen—aromatase—is activated by anything that stresses cells."

This creates a vicious cycle. Stress converts testosterone to estrogen. Estrogen increases [[serotonin]], which activates more aromatase, which produces more estrogen. The man experiences falling testosterone and rising estrogen simultaneously—not because his testes have failed, but because his metabolic environment is converting his androgens into the hormone that suppresses them.

## Thyroid: The Master Regulator

The relationship between [[thyroid]] and testosterone is bidirectional and delicate. As Peat explained, "Too much thyroid can create stress and waste testosterone. Not enough thyroid will cause testosterone to be converted into estrogen. Estrogen blocks the secretion of thyroid from the gland."

Adequate thyroid function—not too much, not too little—maintains the metabolic environment where testosterone can do its work without excessive conversion. Hypothyroidism promotes aromatase activity; hyperthyroidism can accelerate testosterone clearance. The goal is the stable, efficient oxidative metabolism that keeps all hormones in proper balance.

## Natural Aromatase Inhibition

Peat identified several natural aromatase inhibitors that help preserve testosterone without pharmaceutical intervention. [[Orange juice]] contains naringenin, and guavas contain apigenin—both flavonoids that inhibit aromatase and reduce testosterone-to-estrogen conversion. [[Aspirin]] also inhibits aromatase, adding another mechanism to its anti-estrogenic profile.

[[Progesterone]] opposes estrogen through multiple pathways without being converted to estrogen itself. For men concerned about testosterone-to-estrogen conversion, progesterone provides anti-estrogenic protection that testosterone cannot provide for itself.

## Supplementation Strategy

Peat's approach to testosterone optimization prioritized addressing root causes over direct supplementation. As he wrote in email exchanges, "Pure testosterone on the skin is safe if the diet and thyroid function are good, but it's better to try supplements of [[pregnenolone]] first, and then DHEA, to normalize the testosterone production."

The logic is elegant. Pregnenolone is the precursor to all steroid hormones—it "turns into either [progesterone or DHEA], according to need." Supplementing pregnenolone allows the body to make more of whatever it's deficient in, rather than forcing a specific hormone that may convert to estrogen or create imbalances.

DHEA comes next in Peat's hierarchy—it can convert to testosterone but also provides direct protective effects. Only if pregnenolone and DHEA are insufficient did Peat suggest direct testosterone: "A small supplement of testosterone will work in the same direction" as diet and thyroid optimization.

For men, Peat noted that "combining progesterone and DHEA or testosterone can produce the stabilizing effect without suppressing the libido"—addressing concerns that progesterone alone might reduce sex drive.

## The Deeper Pattern

Testosterone decline in aging men is not a localized testicular failure—it reflects the same systemic metabolic deterioration that produces rising estrogen, falling thyroid function, PUFA accumulation, and chronic stress activation in every tissue. Addressing testosterone in isolation, whether through supplementation or pharmaceutical aromatase inhibition, misses the opportunity to address the underlying pattern.

As Peat emphasized, "It's very common for people in their forties to become deficient in both pregnenolone and DHEA... usually because of an imbalance of thyroid and estrogen." The hormone deficiency is a symptom; the thyroid-estrogen imbalance is the cause.
