# Allopregnanolone

This article covers allopregnanolone — also written 3α-hydroxy-5α-pregnan-20-one or 3α,5α-tetrahydroprogesterone — the metabolite through which much of what people call "the progesterone effect" actually happens. The general framework for the parent hormone is on [[progesterone]]; this page is about the molecule that does the calming, the parts of psychiatric pharmacology that have been quietly reorganized around it, and the iatrogenic syndromes that follow when its synthesis is blocked.

Allopregnanolone is the most potent endogenous positive allosteric modulator of the GABA-A receptor known. It sits in the same regulatory site as benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and ethanol, and it potentiates GABA's chloride conductance more powerfully than any of them at physiological concentrations. The molecule is small, lipid-soluble, crosses the blood-brain barrier readily, and is synthesized de novo by astrocytes and oligodendrocytes — neural tissue makes its own supply in addition to whatever arrives from ovary, placenta, or adrenal. Mainstream neuroscience treats it as a curiosity of steroid biochemistry. Ray Peat treated it as the chemical signature of one of the body's most important regulatory systems — the explanation for why a drug that lowers serotonin can relieve premenstrual depression, why blocking a single enzyme can ruin a man's brain for life, and why a $34,000 hospital infusion exists for postpartum depression when a $30 bottle of progesterone produces the same molecule orally.

## Biochemistry: the two enzymes that make it

Allopregnanolone is two enzymatic steps downstream of progesterone. The first step is reduction of the double bond at carbon 4–5 of progesterone by **5α-reductase**, producing 5α-dihydroprogesterone (5α-DHP, the same enzyme that converts testosterone to dihydrotestosterone). The second step is reduction of the 3-keto group by **3α-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase** (3α-HSD), producing allopregnanolone proper. Both enzymes are expressed in the brain itself — allopregnanolone is therefore not just an ovarian or adrenal product imported from the bloodstream but a true *neurosteroid*, synthesized in situ by the cells that respond to it.

The first explicit statement of this pathway in the polished corpus comes from Andrew Murray's framing on the [[2018-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-1|2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on progesterone vs. estrogen]]: "Pregnanolone and allopregnanolone are synthesized *de novo* by astrocytes and oligodendrocytes from cholesterol. These are positive allosteric modulators of the GABA-A receptor, demonstrating anxiolytic, sedative, and anticonvulsant activities similar to benzodiazepines. When progesterone levels decrease, GABA-A receptor activity declines, contributing to excitotoxic effects, insomnia, and agitation." Peat accepted this characterization without modification — it is one of the few places in the corpus where the orthodox neuroscience and his own framework converge on the same description.

The substrate dependence matters clinically. Allopregnanolone synthesis runs [[cholesterol]] → [[pregnenolone]] → [[progesterone]] → 5α-DHP → allopregnanolone. Each step requires its precursor. If cholesterol is low (statins, low-fat diets, malnutrition), every downstream step is throttled. If 5α-reductase is blocked (finasteride, dutasteride), synthesis stops at 5α-DHP. From the [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|2012 *Serotonin* newsletter]]: "Since the production of allopregnanolone and progesterone depends on the availability of pregnenolone and cholesterol, a low cholesterol level would be one of the factors making this an inappropriate way to treat PMS." The molecule that does the work cannot be conjured at the end of an empty pipeline.

Brain allopregnanolone tracks cycle phase (highest mid-luteal, lowest early follicular), rises through pregnancy to 10–20× non-pregnant levels in the 3rd trimester, and collapses within hours of delivery. The postpartum collapse is what concerns the clinical literature on postpartum depression.

## Mechanism: positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A

Mechanism is the part of the story where allopregnanolone earns its own page rather than living as a footnote on [[progesterone]]. Progesterone binds the *nuclear* progesterone receptor (PR-A, PR-B), a transcription factor whose action is slow (hours to days) and gene-mediated. Allopregnanolone binds an allosteric site on the *membrane* GABA-A receptor — a ligand-gated chloride channel whose action is fast (milliseconds) and electrophysiological. Two completely different mechanisms belong to the same hormone's biology. The slow, transcriptional, anti-estrogenic, anti-cancer effects of progesterone run through the nuclear receptor. The fast, anxiolytic, sedative, anti-seizure effects run through allopregnanolone at GABA-A.

Peat made the mechanism explicit in the [[2018-01-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-female-hormones-progesterone|2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on female hormones]]. Asked whether allopregnanolone is more active than progesterone itself, Peat replied: "Since pregnenolone, the precursor, is pro-GABA, I suspect progesterone itself is too. It is converted to allopregnanolone, which seems to be the most powerful activator of the GABA system. But I think all of them act in the same direction. Besides activating GABA to reduce stress, progesterone also inhibits excitatory processes, including the glutamatergic and NMDA receptor systems." Three claims at once: pregnenolone, progesterone, and allopregnanolone all act in the same direction at GABA-A; allopregnanolone is the most potent; and the whole family also opposes the excitatory side (glutamate, NMDA) independently of GABA. Peat's preferred clinical strategy followed — load the upstream precursors, let the body convert at its own rate, rather than administer the metabolite directly.

Allopregnanolone's GABA-A modulation is the molecular reason progesterone shows up in the same regulatory frame as the benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and alcohol. From the [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|2012 *Serotonin* newsletter]]: "The neurosteroid, allopregnanolone, for which progesterone is the precursor, facilitates the inhibitory action of GABA, which is known to be deficient in some disorders of mood and movement. This suggests that progesterone will be therapeutic in the movement disorders, as it is in various mood problems." The chemistry that connects mood disorders, movement disorders, and seizure disorders into a single framework is the GABA-A system, and the endogenous molecule that powers it is allopregnanolone.

## Clinical: anxiolysis, sleep, postpartum, PMS, and the rest

The clinical footprint is wide. Allopregnanolone is involved in the anxiolytic effect of luteal-phase progesterone (and the rebound anxiety of premenstrual collapse), in pregnancy-induced calm (and postpartum-collapse depression), in the anti-seizure effect of progesterone in catamenial epilepsy, in ethanol sedation (alcohol acutely raises allopregnanolone — which is part of why it works, and why chronic alcoholism eventually produces severe anxiety as it depletes precursors), in progesterone's muscle-relaxant effects on smooth and cardiac muscle, and in its neuroprotection in traumatic brain injury. The parent literature originally attributed the effect to "progesterone"; subsequent work narrowed it to the allopregnanolone metabolite.

Two observations shape contemporary practice. First: caffeine raises allopregnanolone. From [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|"Bone Density: First Do No Harm"]] (Carney et al.): "Intraperitoneal injection of caffeine induced dose- and time-dependent increases in the concentrations of pregnenolone, progesterone, and 3α-hydroxy-5α-pregnan-20-one (allopregnanolone) in the cerebral cortex." Adrenalectomy plus orchiectomy abolished the effect — the raised steroids come from adrenals and gonads, not local brain synthesis. This is part of why coffee acts as a paradoxically calming agent in habitual users despite its stimulant pharmacology.

Second: allopregnanolone is a [[neurogenesis]] signal. The Brinton lab work cited in [[stem-cells-cell-culture-and-culture-issues-in-regeneration|"Stem Cells, Cell Culture and Culture Issues in Regeneration"]] showed that "the neuroactive progesterone metabolite, allopregnanolone … induced, in a dose dependent manner, a significant increase in proliferation of neuroprogenitor cells … derived from the rat hippocampus and human neural stem cells … derived from the cerebral cortex." Not only the brain's most potent acute anxiolytic, but a long-term stimulus to neuroprogenitor proliferation — relevant to post-stress brain recovery (postpartum, post-injury, post-stroke).

## The SSRI-as-allopregnanolone-raiser reframe

The most consequential reframe in the corpus is the Prozac-PMS story. In the orthodox account, fluoxetine (Prozac) was developed as a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor, found to alleviate depression by raising synaptic serotonin, and subsequently discovered to also help premenstrual symptoms — an off-label use that Lilly pursued and won FDA approval for. The mechanism, in the orthodox account, is the same serotonin-raising action that produces the antidepressant effect.

Peat read the same chain of events backward. In the [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|2012 *Serotonin* newsletter]]: "One of the many actions of the 'SSRI' (such as fluoxetine, Prozac), which aren't related to their effect on serotonin, is to increase the concentration of allopregnanolone in the brain, imitating the action of increased progesterone. Following this discovery, Lilly got Prozac approved as a treatment for premenstrual syndrome. Since the production of allopregnanolone and progesterone depends on the availability of pregnenolone and cholesterol, a low cholesterol level would be one of the factors making this an inappropriate way to treat PMS." On Peat's reading, the empirical claim is the same — fluoxetine helps PMS — but the mechanism is inverted. The molecule that fluoxetine actually elevates, and the molecule through which PMS relief actually occurs, is allopregnanolone, not serotonin. The serotonin elevation is a side effect of a drug whose useful clinical action runs through an entirely separate, accidentally discovered pathway.

The implications cascade. Oral progesterone, which raises allopregnanolone directly and more selectively, should work at least as well with fewer side effects. The SSRI's serotonin-raising action — which Peat treated as the *harmful* pharmacology — becomes an unwanted side effect of the actually-useful allopregnanolone effect. The "SSRI" brand identity is an FDA-approved misnomer around a chemically promiscuous molecule whose clinical utility runs through a side action unknown when the drug class was named. And any patient whose cholesterol is too low to make adequate allopregnanolone (statin users, low-fat dieters, malnutrition) will respond poorly to fluoxetine for the same reason they would respond poorly to oral progesterone — no substrate.

This reframe is the most important contribution of allopregnanolone to Peat's psychiatric-pharmacology critique. It explains SSRI efficacy in PMS/PMDD and postpartum cases without endorsing the serotonergic mechanism, and preserves the option of doing the same job more directly. ## Brexanolone (Zulresso) for postpartum depression

In March 2019 the FDA approved brexanolone — intravenous synthetic allopregnanolone, identical to the endogenous molecule — for postpartum depression. Brand name Zulresso, Sage Therapeutics, $34,000 per course, administered as a 60-hour hospital IV infusion because of sedation risk during titration. Peat addressed it on the [[2019-06-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-postpartum-depression|June 2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] three months later. Asked why a $34,000 IV when "a $30 bottle of progesterone or pregnenolone … quickly convert in the body to allopregnanolone," Peat: "If it was a natural therapy, it would be cheap and not patented, and no one would make several billion dollars a year from selling it. For the last 10 or 15 years, the FDA has acted as though it is in the business of creating monopolies."

The crucial mechanistic claim follows: "Someone tested that and found that just a tiny dose of oral progesterone — 20 milligrams — tripled the amount of allopregnanolone in the body" (same episode). If accurate, this dose response demolishes the rationale for the patented IV. The orthodox answer is that brexanolone bypasses conversion inefficiency. Peat's counter is that the bypass is the problem: "Allopregnanolone is essentially the end of a line of metabolites starting with cholesterol. Those are regulated at many steps before they come into existence. When you give the end product without looking at the condition of all the preceding steps, that is a great opportunity for throwing the whole system out of balance. For example, estrogen and aldosterone are other end products of steroid metabolism, and excess of those is very dangerous." Administer a precursor and the body adjusts conversion. Administer the active form directly and the regulatory feedback has nothing to act on.

Postpartum depression is, in part, a steroid-withdrawal syndrome. Through pregnancy, allopregnanolone runs at 10–20× non-pregnant levels. Within hours of delivery, the placenta is gone, progesterone collapses, and allopregnanolone with it. A woman with adequate cholesterol, thyroid, and adrenal substrate closes the gap; a woman whose substrate is thin does not. From the same episode: "Women with postpartum depression typically have reduced levels of cholesterol, so they cannot make enough progesterone. Vitamin A and thyroid are other limiting factors. A normal cycling woman produces about 30 milligrams of progesterone daily in the luteal phase. I think that would be a reasonable therapeutic amount postpartum." Full development on [[postpartum-depression]].

## The finasteride disaster

The mirror-image story is post-finasteride syndrome. Finasteride is a 5α-reductase inhibitor — the same enzyme whose first reaction is required for allopregnanolone synthesis. The dermatology textbook treats this enzyme as the testosterone-to-DHT converter and finasteride as a clean intervention in male-pattern hair loss. The neurosteroid pathway is invisible in the textbook. Peat made it the centerpiece of the critique.

From the [[#27： Thyroid Function & Pulse Rate ｜ Weaponized Culture ｜ Finasteride as an ＂Insane Decision＂|*Generative Energy* #27 (2022)]]: "First, no one *has* to take it. It's an irrational decision and probably means you're already abjectly obedient to authority. It's a very powerful intervention in your steroid chemistry, especially affecting your brain." The "especially affecting your brain" is the allopregnanolone clause. Blocking 5α-reductase blocks not just the testosterone→DHT step that finasteride is designed to block, but the parallel progesterone→5α-DHP→allopregnanolone step that the brain depends on for GABA-A modulation, mood, sleep, and stress tolerance. The collateral damage is the mechanism.

Peat returned to the same point in [[Safe Supplements with Ray Peat [Generative Energy #31]|*Generative Energy* #31]]: "Brain chemistry depends on it. The metabolism of progesterone as well as testosterone in your brain — if you block any of the enzymes used in making it, you're going to have a weird brain." Patients describe the result as depression, anhedonia, cognitive fog, loss of libido, loss of erection, suicidal ideation — the syndrome the FDA originally denied and now acknowledges under the "post-finasteride syndrome" label.

Dinkov, in the same #27 conversation, added a third axis: "Apparently the 5-alpha reductase enzyme is important for the synthesis of bile acids. If you inhibit it, you won't produce bile acids crucial for digestion." The enzyme does three jobs — DHT for follicle and prostate, allopregnanolone for brain GABA-A, bile acid for fat digestion. Finasteride is a "selective" inhibitor of none of these; the syndrome is the breadth of the inhibition. Full alternative protocol on [[hair-loss]] and [[hair-loss-protocol]].

## The paradoxical-dysphoria question (Bäckström)

The cleanest counter-argument is the Bäckström paradox. Torbjörn Bäckström has published for thirty years on the observation that allopregnanolone is not uniformly calming — at low-to-moderate physiological doses it is anxiolytic, at very high or very low concentrations it can produce anxiety, irritability, dysphoria. The dose-response is biphasic (inverted U). Bäckström's group argues this explains why some women experience luteal-phase progesterone surges as calming and others as triggering — and that PMS/PMDD may be a subtype-specific allopregnanolone hypersensitivity.

Murray raised this on the [[2018-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-2|May 2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]]: Bäckström's group claims allopregnanolone at GABA-A causes "the blues" similar to alcohol or sleeping pills. Peat's response was partly semantic: "That is an interesting confusion of the meaning of 'depression.' In the heart, progesterone *depresses* the spastic activity that leads to cardiac arrest and facilitates a good rhythm." The same word, "depression," means both mood disorder and physiological inhibition — progesterone's calming action looks like pathology only to a reader who conflates them.

The richer answer comes earlier in the June 2019 postpartum exchange. Murray cited a 2006 Swedish study showing allopregnanolone was biphasic; Peat: "That's the sort of thing you risk when using an end-of-line metabolite. Normally, it can never get above a certain level because it has to go through stages where each precursor produces a smaller amount of product. If you artificially give an amount that the body could never experience on its own, you might anesthetize some of the regulatory inhibitory nerves and release things that are less sensitive to inhibition. You are risking something the organism has never experienced before." The biphasic dose response is, on this reading, an artifact of supraphysiological dosing. The body's normal regulatory architecture caps allopregnanolone at a ceiling the metabolic chain enforces; administer the molecule directly and you exceed it. The fix is not "don't use allopregnanolone therapies" but "use precursors at physiological doses and let the body convert."

The Bäckström paradox has been replicated; the dose-response is real; the mainstream uses it against the entire neurosteroid-therapy approach. Peat's reply is principled but does not fully answer why a subset of women experience luteal-phase progesterone elevation — clearly within physiological range — as anxiogenic. The current best synthesis is that PMS/PMDD includes a subtype with abnormal GABA-A subunit composition (specifically α4/δ subunit changes) that converts the normally calming allopregnanolone signal into a destabilizing one. That subtype overlaps with the SSRI-responsive subtype — possibly because SSRIs raise allopregnanolone enough to override the subunit abnormality. The mechanism is unresolved.

## Counter-arguments

**The Bäckström biphasic-dose data.** Covered in the section above. At supraphysiological doses, allopregnanolone produces paradoxical anxiety in some patients, contradicting the clean "GABA-A potentiator → anxiolytic" story. Peat's counter — use precursors, not end-metabolites — is principled but incomplete; a subtype of PMDD patients shows the paradox even at physiological progesterone elevations, and the GABA-A subunit-composition hypothesis remains the best available mechanism for the subset. The brexanolone postpartum trial data also showed a small minority of patients with worsened symptoms, consistent with the biphasic literature.

**The brexanolone efficacy data.** Sage Therapeutics' Phase III trials of brexanolone for postpartum depression showed meaningful effect sizes (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale improvements of ~7 points vs. ~5 for placebo) and rapid onset (effect by 24 hours), supporting the orthodox claim that direct allopregnanolone administration is therapeutically active. The data does not, by itself, refute the Peat critique — Peat acknowledges the molecule is effective; the critique is about route, dose, regulatory capture, and the failure to study cheaper precursor alternatives in head-to-head trials. The trials that would resolve the question (oral progesterone vs. IV brexanolone in postpartum depression) have not been done, almost certainly because the patent-economic incentive points the other direction.

**The SSRI-mechanism debate.** The "fluoxetine raises allopregnanolone" claim is empirically supported (multiple animal and limited human studies show allopregnanolone elevation with fluoxetine and other SSRIs) but the magnitude of the elevation, and the degree to which it accounts for clinical efficacy, is contested. Mainstream psychiatry treats the allopregnanolone elevation as a downstream side effect of serotonergic action; Peat treats it as the actual mechanism. The two positions are not directly tested by any single experiment because the SSRIs cannot be separated cleanly from their serotonin-raising action in vivo. The empirical observation that survives both readings is that SSRIs work for PMS/PMDD; the disagreement is over which of the drug's pharmacologic actions explains the clinical effect.

**The post-finasteride syndrome (PFS) literature.** PFS remains contested. Some dermatology and urology groups attribute the persistent symptoms to confounding (depression preceding hair loss, nocebo, self-selection bias) rather than to drug-induced neurosteroid changes. Independent neurosteroid measurements in PFS patients are mixed — some studies show low CSF allopregnanolone in symptomatic patients, others do not replicate. The mechanism Peat described (finasteride blocks 5α-reductase → allopregnanolone collapse → brain dysfunction) is biochemically sound, but the syndrome's heterogeneity and the lack of clean prospective data make the orthodox skepticism partially defensible. The clinical practice question — whether the risk justifies finasteride for cosmetic hair loss in psychiatrically vulnerable patients — survives the mechanism debate.

## Data gaps

- **The head-to-head trial.** No published RCT directly compares oral progesterone (or pregnenolone) against brexanolone in postpartum depression. The Peat critique stands or falls on this comparison; the trial has not been done.
- **The Bäckström subtype.** Which women have GABA-A subunit compositions that convert allopregnanolone into a destabilizing rather than calming signal, how that subtype is identified clinically, and whether the subtype overlaps with PMDD, are unresolved in both orthodox and Peat literatures.
- **The fluoxetine-allopregnanolone dose response in humans.** The animal data is robust; the human data is thinner. The magnitude of the elevation and whether it correlates with clinical response has not been mapped in a clean cohort.
- **Long-term safety of brexanolone and zuranolone.** Zuranolone (oral analog, FDA-approved 2023) has short follow-up. GABA-A modulators historically produce tolerance, withdrawal, and abuse risk (benzodiazepines, barbiturates); the neurosteroid drugs have not yet been observed long enough to know.
- **Allopregnanolone in men.** Most of the clinical literature is on women. The PFS literature implies a substantial male role, but the cycle-phase and life-stage normative data is sparser than the female-side data.
- **Caffeine-allopregnanolone in habitual users.** The Carney et al. acute-dose data does not predict steady-state response in habitual coffee drinkers.
