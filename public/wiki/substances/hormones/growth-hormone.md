# Growth Hormone

In every wellness clinic somewhere between the testosterone-pellet menu and the peptide stack sits a pamphlet promising that recombinant human growth hormone will reverse the decline of middle age — restore lean mass, melt belly fat, thicken skin, sharpen sleep, recapture the metabolism of a twenty-year-old. The pamphlet has the science right in one narrow sense: GH does cause those short-term changes. It has the science wrong in the only sense that matters across a lifetime. In Ray Peat's reading of the same endocrinology textbooks, the rise of growth hormone in an adult is a *stress signal*, not a youth signal, and chronic elevation of GH — whether endogenous from a bad metabolism or exogenous from an injection vial — drives the same biochemistry that produces the visible features of aging: edema, basement-membrane thickening, fatty-acid mobilization, kidney degeneration, insulin resistance, connective-tissue cross-linking, and increased cancer. "The name 'growth hormone' is misleading," Peat wrote in [[growth-hormone-hormone-of-stress-aging-death|"Growth Hormone: Hormone of Stress, Aging, & Death?" (2013)]], the canonical source for this article. "Stress produces somatic growth, in a process called 'hormesis.' Exercise produces muscle edema, to a degree similar to that produced by GH; edema stimulates growth, but GH effect isn't limited to bone and muscle." The pamphlet has confused the muscle's *swelling* with the muscle's *building*. The two look identical at week six and diverge sharply at year six.

This page covers the adult framing. Children grow. GH deficiency in childhood is a real, treatable condition, and nothing here disputes that. The contrarian case is about what happens when a growth signal that was supposed to switch off at puberty is artificially turned back on at fifty.

## Prerequisites

- [[thyroid|Thyroid hormone]] and its role as the primary anabolic and respiration-supporting signal, distinct from GH
- [[cortisol|Cortisol]] and the broader stress-hormone family that GH belongs to
- [[adrenaline|Adrenaline]] and the fatty-acid mobilization that GH amplifies
- [[oxidative-metabolism|Oxidative metabolism]] versus the Warburg/glycolytic shift that GH drives toward
- [[aging|Aging]] as the metabolic cascade GH plays a documented role in accelerating
- [[prolactin|Prolactin]], the sister hormone in the same evolutionary family with overlapping effects
- [[estrogen|Estrogen]], which stimulates both GH and prolactin secretion

## What growth hormone does, in the textbook account

The anterior pituitary secretes growth hormone in pulses, with the largest pulses occurring during slow-wave sleep in the first hours of the night. GH circulates briefly (half-life around 20 minutes) and acts in two ways: directly on tissues bearing the GH receptor (adipose, liver, muscle), and indirectly by stimulating the liver to produce insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1, formerly called somatomedin C), which mediates most long-bone and cartilage growth. In a growing child this extends the long bones, builds lean tissue, and shifts substrate selection toward protein deposition. After epiphyseal closure in late adolescence the long bones can no longer elongate, but GH continues to be secreted, and continues to act on cartilage, connective tissue, fat, and kidney. The textbook adult effects are lipolysis (free-fatty-acid mobilization), insulin antagonism (the "diabetogenic" effect), and a permissive role in protein synthesis when other anabolic signals are adequate.

The textbook also lists, almost as a footnote, the conditions that *raise* GH: heat, running, hypoglycemia, fasting, sleep onset, trauma, hypoxia, shock, pregnancy, hyperestrogenic states, and critically — heart failure, where GH runs 11 to 22 times normal in untreated patients (Anand et al.). Modern endocrinology files all of these under "stressors that increase GH" without remarking that the unifying feature *is* stress. Peat remarked on it.

## Peat's reframe: the adult GH surge is a stress signal

Peat's central observation is that the list of states elevating growth hormone is virtually identical to the list of states elevating [[cortisol|cortisol]] and [[adrenaline|adrenaline]]. "Heat, hypoglycemia, running, and some types of shock are known to stimulate growth hormone secretion, sometimes to levels ten or twenty times higher than normal," he wrote. "(Two kinds of stress that usually don't increase GH are cold and stimulus-deprivation.) I consider the growth hormone to be, almost as much as prolactin, a stress-inducible hormone." This places GH in the same evolutionary family as [[prolactin|prolactin]] — roughly 20% sequence homology, common ancestral origin as an osmoregulatory peptide in fish. The two share downstream effects: breast-tissue stimulation, fertility suppression, fatty-acid mobilization, fluid retention, immune modulation. Bovine growth hormone, Peat notes with dry economy, is used to make cows give more milk. Human growth hormone is supposed to make men lean and muscular. Same family, opposite marketing.

The provocation is most direct in Peat's quarrel with Robert Sapolsky. Sapolsky, in radio interviews and in *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers*, described GH as *suppressed* by stress — the body shuts down growth and reproduction during fight-or-flight to conserve energy. Peat objected. The acute-stress framing is correct for rats and mice in laboratory time-windows of a few minutes. It is wrong for humans across most of the stress states they actually live in. "If an endocrinologist as good as Sapolsky can misunderstand GH to that degree, the public is even more likely to misunderstand the nature of the material, and to believe that it somehow acts just on muscle, fat, and bones."

The mechanism linking GH to stress runs through hypoglycemia. When blood sugar falls — through fasting, high-protein meals without carbohydrate, glycogen-depleting exercise, the nocturnal fall during sleep, or the hypothyroid liver's failure to maintain gluconeogenesis — adrenaline rises to mobilize free fatty acids, cortisol rises to convert protein to glucose, *and growth hormone rises alongside them*, doing approximately what adrenaline does to fat tissue while adding its own effects on water, salt, and basement-membrane synthesis. "When you have hypoglycemia, you increase your growth hormone," Peat said in his 2011 EastWest Healing interview. "It tends to come with other pituitary stress-responsive hormones. They are beneficial in the right amount at the right time, but harmful when they go on too long. People with acromegaly (overactivity of the pituitary) have an extremely high incidence of atherosclerosis." Acromegaly, the disease of GH excess, presents not as enhanced youthfulness but as enlarged hands and feet, thickened skin, joint pain, sleep apnea, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and roughly twice-normal cancer mortality. This is the natural-history experiment of chronic GH elevation.

The puberty observation reinforces the point. Insulin resistance — normally classed as a disease of middle age — *begins at puberty*, triggered by the pubertal GH surge. "At puberty, the increased GH creates a mild degree of diabetes-like insulin resistance, which tends to increase progressively with age." The biochemistry adolescents develop in their growth spurt (high free fatty acids, increased lactate, glucose intolerance) is a diluted version of the biochemistry of diabetic adults. Same hormone, same direction. The difference between a fourteen-year-old's growth spurt and a fifty-year-old's metabolic syndrome is the substrate the chemistry operates on — open growth plates and abundant thyroid in one case, closed plates, accumulated PUFA, and falling thyroid in the other.

## The longevity inversion

If GH were a youth hormone, animals with more GH should live longer than animals with less. The data run the other way.

W. D. Denckla, at the National Institute on Aging in the 1970s, removed the pituitary glands from aging rats and replaced only thyroid. The rats lived dramatically longer, in some experiments approaching twice the normal lifespan. Thyroid alone outperformed the intact endocrine system at protecting against aging. Denckla proposed a "decreasing oxygen consumption hormone" (DECO) secreted by the aging pituitary, gradually suppressing thyroid and driving metabolic decline. He never isolated a single molecule with all of DECO's properties; in his final publication he reported that the activity was "in the prolactin fraction" — the family of prolactin-like and GH-like peptides. "Growth Hormone may not be precisely the Death Hormone that Denckla was looking for," Peat summarized, "but it is very close to it."

A. V. Everitt extended the work. Removing the pituitary in young rats slowed collagen aging (tail-tendon cross-linking), age-associated proteinuria, and renal histopathology — and these effects were *greater* than those from caloric restriction in matched controls. "Hypophysectomy was found to have a greater anti-collagen aging effect than food restriction, when food intakes were the same in both groups" (Everitt 1983). The longevity benefit of caloric restriction — the most reproducible anti-aging intervention in the laboratory — works in significant part by reducing pituitary GH and prolactin secretion. The mTOR/IGF-1 pathway that mainstream gerontology has mapped is the molecular detail of a mechanism Denckla and Everitt identified at the organ level.

The mouse data run the same direction. The "little" mouse (GH at 5–10% of normal) has an abnormally long lifespan. The Ames and Snell dwarfs (lacking GH, prolactin, and TSH) live 40–70% longer than wild-type and show delayed cancer, cataracts, and connective-tissue aging. The Ghr-knockout mouse (rodent Laron syndrome) holds the published longevity record for laboratory mammals, with median lifespans over 50% longer than controls. Across species: less GH, more years.

The human case is Laron syndrome, in which mutations in the GH receptor render tissues unable to respond to GH. Affected individuals reach approximately 4 feet final height because their long bones never get the IGF-1 elongation signal, but the long-term health profile is remarkable. Walter Longo, tracking the Ecuadorean Laron cohort for over two decades, reports essential protection from cancer (zero cancer deaths in a cohort of ~100 patients over the follow-up window, against expected 17–20% mortality in matched relatives) and markedly reduced rates of type 2 diabetes despite obesity rates higher than unaffected siblings. Longo, who is not a Peat acolyte, now argues for pharmacological IGF-1 suppression as a longevity intervention — arriving by an independent path at the direction Peat pointed since the 1980s.

The inversion the anti-aging clinic industry cannot square: if chronic low GH/IGF-1 protects against the diseases of aging, the rational intervention is to *lower* IGF-1, not raise it. The Klatz-style clinics raise it.

## The Klatz industry and the anti-aging GH injection problem

In 1990, the *New England Journal of Medicine* published Daniel Rudman's small study of recombinant human growth hormone in twenty-one men aged 61 to 81. After six months of injections, the men had increased lean body mass by 8.8%, decreased adipose tissue by 14.4%, and modestly thickened their skin. The study had no measure of strength, no measure of function, no follow-up beyond six months, and a control group too small for reliable conclusions. Rudman himself cautioned that the cosmetic changes did not necessarily reflect functional benefit. None of these qualifications survived the journey from the journal to the strip mall. Within five years, Ronald Klatz and Robert Goldman had founded the American Academy of Anti-Aging Medicine, the Rudman paper had been laundered into the marketing copy of every GH clinic in North America, and an industry estimated at over a billion dollars annually was prescribing rhGH off-label to adults with no diagnosed deficiency, treating the age-related decline in GH (the "somatopause") as a disease.

The intervention has visible short-term effects. Adipose tissue decreases. Muscle *appears* to grow — though, as Peat documents at length, the increase in muscle weight is in significant part *water*, the same expansion of extracellular fluid that produces the periorbital edema, carpal tunnel symptoms, and joint pain that recur consistently in the GH-administration literature. The cosmetic results, at six months, are real.

The long-term results are what Peat's framework predicts. GH treatment in critically ill adults nearly doubled mortality in the largest randomized trial (Takala et al., NEJM 1999). The Rotterdam Study and the Physicians' Health Study both identified high-normal IGF-1 as a risk factor for prostate, breast, colon, and lung cancer; men in the top quartile of serum IGF-1 carry roughly twice the prostate-cancer risk of those in the bottom quartile, with similar effect sizes for premenopausal breast cancer. The mechanism is not mysterious: GH and IGF-1 stimulate cell proliferation including the proliferation of pre-malignant cells, suppress apoptosis, antagonize insulin, and drive the substrate-selection profile that cancer cells prefer. The Klatz-style intervention drives healthy adults' IGF-1 into the top quartile and keeps it there.

Other documented adverse effects include carpal tunnel syndrome, arthralgia, gynecomastia (GH and prolactin share breast-tissue effects), insulin resistance, sleep apnea, and the basement-membrane thickening that Peat identified as the central mechanism connecting GH to diabetic microvascular complications and renal degeneration. "It is clearly established that GH contributes to kidney degeneration," Peat wrote, "and the lack of GH, even the removal of the pituitary, is protective." The clinic's underlying premise — that the age-related fall in GH is a disease to be reversed — has the causality backwards. The fall is, if anything, an adaptive response that limits the damage GH would otherwise do to the basement membranes, kidneys, and lipid-peroxidation-prone tissues of an aged metabolism.

## What raises GH (mostly bad in adults)

The textbook list of GH secretagogues, read through Peat's lens, becomes a list of things adults should mostly avoid:

- **Fasting and hypoglycemia.** The largest physiological adult GH surges come from acute drops in blood glucose. Intermittent fasting protocols that promise GH-mediated benefits correctly describe the secretory response and incorrectly describe what it *means*. The lipolytic and gluconeogenic downstream effects are the same stress-metabolism Peat warns about everywhere else (see [[cortisol|cortisol]] and [[intermittent-fasting|intermittent fasting]]).
- **Sleep deprivation.** GH rises sharply when sleep is interrupted, as part of the stress response — not because the body benefits from the extra GH but because the metabolic state is bad.
- **High-intensity interval training and sustained running.** Both elevate GH substantially alongside [[adrenaline|adrenaline]] and cortisol. Peat's preferred exercise — walking, low-load resistance, brief concentric loading — does not trigger the GH-prolactin-cortisol surge that endurance work reliably produces.
- **Estrogen.** Directly stimulates GH secretion. HRT, oral contraceptives, and the endogenous estrogen elevations of an aging metabolism all push GH upward.
- **Hyperthermia.** Heat raises GH within minutes. Sauna's vascular effects may be beneficial in some respects, but the GH spike is not the youth-restoring mechanism the wellness press claims.
- **Hypothyroidism.** As thyroid falls, GH tends to rise — another sign the hormone responds to metabolic distress rather than directing healthy growth.

The partial exception is adequate slow-wave sleep in early adulthood, where the first nocturnal GH pulses are part of normal repair. Peat did not argue against them. His point was that adults trying to *enhance* the pulse through late-night exercise, late-night protein feeding, or GH-releasing peptides were intervening in a system whose adult-life adaptive setting was *less* GH, not more.

## What lowers GH (mostly good in adults)

The interventions Peat recommended throughout his work are, read against the GH literature, also GH-lowering interventions:

- **Adequate, frequent carbohydrate.** Sucrose, fruit, fruit juice, honey, dairy. Raising blood glucose suppresses GH within minutes. The midnight glass of orange juice Peat recommended for night-time stress symptoms raises glucose, suppresses adrenaline, cortisol, *and* GH simultaneously.
- **Protein eaten with carbohydrate, not alone.** Protein without carbohydrate triggers coordinated rises in adrenaline, cortisol, and GH through the hypoglycemia mechanism (see [[cortisol|cortisol]]).
- **Thyroid sufficiency.** Healthy [[thyroid|thyroid]] function stabilizes blood sugar between meals, preventing hypoglycemic GH spikes.
- **Walking and low-load resistance instead of HIIT.**
- **[[progesterone|Progesterone]] and the protective steroids.** Progesterone opposes [[estrogen|estrogen]] at the tissue level and therefore opposes one of GH's major upstream stimulators.

The pattern is consistent: the metabolic state that supports healthy aging — adequate fuel, adequate thyroid, low chronic stress, low estrogen, no PUFA — is the state in which GH stays at its physiological baseline. Peat's anti-aging interventions are GH-lowering. The Klatz clinics' interventions are GH-raising. Both sides have the directional sign right; they disagree about which direction is the youthful one.

## The pediatric case

Nothing in the contrarian frame applies to childhood GH deficiency. Children with documented hypopituitarism, Turner syndrome, or chronic kidney disease with measurable GH deficiency require GH replacement, and the long-term outcomes are generally good when dosing is appropriate. The pediatric indication is a genuine clinical success of recombinant DNA technology, and the modern formulations are safer than the cadaver-derived GH (Creutzfeldt-Jakob risk) used before the 1985 ban.

Peat's frame is about *adult* GH supplementation — off-label use in adults with no diagnosed deficiency, treating the normal age-related decline as a disease. The two cases should not be confused. A child with open growth plates and a working anabolic substrate (thyroid, insulin, adequate diet, low PUFA load) is in a metabolic situation in which GH does what its name advertises. A fifty-year-old with closed plates, accumulated PUFA, declining thyroid, and rising estrogen is in a situation where the same molecule produces a different outcome. Same molecule, different substrate, opposite effect — a recurring principle in Peat's framework. The mistake is continuing pediatric replacement into middle age "for muscle and skin," converting a legitimate pediatric case into a Klatz-style adult case.

## Tracking signal: the IGF-1 reading Peat would want

Adult IGF-1 (the integrated downstream signal of GH activity) is one of the few mainstream lab markers that fits Peat's anti-aging framework — but with the directional sign opposite to the way anti-aging clinics use it. The Klatz framing treats *low* IGF-1 as a deficiency to correct with injection. Peat's framing, consistent with Longo's and with the Laron-syndrome evidence, treats *high-normal* IGF-1 (above ~150–180 ng/mL in middle-aged adults; ranges vary by lab and age) as a risk factor for cancer and metabolic disease.

The cleanest Peat-aligned reading combines low IGF-1 with markers of metabolic robustness: IGF-1 in the lower half of the age-adjusted reference range (80–140 ng/mL for a middle-aged adult), pulse and oral temperature consistent with healthy thyroid function (see [[broda-barnes|broda-barnes]] and [[thyroid|thyroid]]), TSH below 1.0, low fasting [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]], and adequate progesterone and DHEA. This is *low IGF-1 on a robust metabolism*, not *high IGF-1 propped up by injections on a degrading metabolism*. The Laron cohort is the natural experiment: essentially undetectable IGF-1, otherwise reasonable metabolism, cancer rate approximately zero.

## Counter-arguments

**The anti-aging clinic and sports-medicine literature.** Rudman 1990, subsequent observational studies, and broad off-label clinical experience do show short-term improvements in body composition, energy, and patient-reported wellbeing. The Klatz-style framing has clinical "support" if support is defined as patient satisfaction and short-term cosmetic markers. Peat's response was that these markers respond to fluid retention and short-term lipolysis, neither of which predicts long-term outcome, and that the mortality data (Takala 1999; the IGF-1-and-cancer data from Rotterdam and the Physicians' Health Study) point the other way. The anti-aging clinics have not produced randomized long-term mortality data that supports continued adult GH replacement. The pediatric literature does not generalize.

**The bodybuilding and strength-sport community.** Performance-enhancing GH does produce measurable strength and lean-mass gains. Peat would not have disputed the short-term anabolic effect; he would have pointed at the cardiovascular and metabolic morbidity of long-term users (acromegaly-like cardiomyopathy, insulin resistance, soft-tissue overgrowth) as the long-term price. The community has decided the trade is acceptable; that decision cannot be generalized to anti-aging use in non-athletes.

**Geriatric sarcopenia framing.** Progressive muscle loss in elderly adults is a real concern. Peat's framework addresses it not with GH but with [[thyroid|thyroid]] replacement, adequate protein with carbohydrate, [[progesterone|progesterone]] for women, modest [[testosterone|testosterone]] replacement where indicated, walking and brief resistance loading, and the broader anti-PUFA program at [[aging|aging]]. The case for sarcopenia-specific GH replacement is weaker than for these levers, in both efficacy and safety.

**The mTOR/IGF-1 longevity literature.** This is where the mainstream converges with Peat rather than contradicting him. Longo's work, the Ames/Snell dwarf mice, the Ghr-knockout mouse data, the Laron cohort, and the caloric-restriction literature all show lower IGF-1 signaling correlating with longer life and less cancer. The remaining disagreement is mechanistic — Peat emphasizes mitochondrial respiration and lipid peroxidation, the rapamycin-pathway researchers emphasize signaling per se — but the directional prediction is the same.

## Data gaps

- **No long-term randomized adult GH-replacement trials with mortality endpoints.** The existing literature is dominated by short-term body-composition studies. The 1999 Takala finding in critically ill adults is the strongest randomized signal and points against GH replacement, but it does not transfer cleanly to elective off-label use.
- **Pulse-vs-continuous GH pharmacology is poorly characterized in the replacement context.** Endogenous GH is pulsatile; clinic dosing produces sustained elevation. The cancer and cardiovascular risk of clinical dosing may differ from the same area-under-the-curve delivered as pulses, but this has not been carefully studied.
- **The GH-PUFA interaction is mechanistically plausible but not directly studied.** Peat's framework predicts that GH-induced unsaturated-fatty-acid oxidation should be more damaging in PUFA-loaded tissues. A PUFA-depleted person might tolerate higher GH with less peroxidation downstream. The framework is well-motivated; the controlled experiment is missing.
- **GH and the iron/lipofuscin axis of [[aging|aging]] have not been integrated.** Peat's late aging writing emphasizes iron accumulation and age-pigment formation; the GH literature has not been crosswalked to that mechanism in any quantitative way.
- **Peat's own position over time.** The 2013 newsletter is the most complete published statement; a systematic comparison of his 1990s through 2010s framing could clarify whether his confidence in the contrarian position grew or weakened with later evidence.
