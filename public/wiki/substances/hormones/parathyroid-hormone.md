# Parathyroid Hormone (PTH)

Parathyroid hormone is the hormone the textbooks introduce as the gentle steward of blood calcium — a thermostat that flips on when serum calcium dips. Ray Peat read it the opposite way. In his framework PTH is a catabolic emergency signal, an anti-thyroid hormone, the calcium-mobilizing voice of chronic stress. Its job in a true emergency is to dissolve bone fast enough to keep the heart beating. Its job in the chronic state most modern adults live in is to slowly disassemble the skeleton, calcify the arteries, kill follicles, and lock cells into the same low-respiration, high-lactate mode that defines aging.

In ["Milk in Context: Allergies, Ecology, and Some Myths"](milk-in-context-allergies-ecology-and-some-myths) he wrote that "the parathyroid hormone (PTH) is an important regulator of calcium metabolism. If dietary calcium isn't sufficient, causing blood calcium to decrease, the PTH increases, and removes calcium from bones to maintain a normal amount in the blood. PTH has many other effects, contributing to inflammation, calcification of soft tissues, and decreased respiratory energy production." The "other effects" are why Peat regarded most adults at lab-normal PTH as functionally hyperparathyroid.

## Physiology: The Mobilizer

The parathyroid glands are four small bodies behind the thyroid that read calcium through a calcium-sensing receptor. When extracellular ionized calcium drops, PTH rises within seconds. It has three principal targets: bone (osteoclast activation, calcium release from hydroxyapatite), kidney (calcium reabsorption up, phosphate reabsorption down), and the kidney's 1-alpha-hydroxylase, which converts 25-hydroxyvitamin D to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol).

That much is orthodox. Peat's contribution was to extend the picture to mitochondria, cytokines, and the stress hormone family. In ["Calcium and Disease: Hypertension, Organ Calcification, Shock vs. Respiratory Energy"](calcium-and-disease-hypertension-organ-calcification-shock-vs-respiratory-energy) he wrote that "PTH (like estrogen and serotonin) inhibits cellular respiration and activates glycolysis, lowering the ATP level and shifting the cell's metabolism toward the production of lactic acid rather than carbon dioxide. PTH also causes bicarbonate to be lost in the urine." That single sentence relocates PTH from the calcium-balance chapter to the central pathology of [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]] failure. PTH does to a cell what hypothyroidism, [[estrogen]], [[serotonin]], and [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] do.

The "antimetabolic actions" he listed in the same newsletter read like a catalog of degenerative disease: "The antimetabolic actions of PTH mimic those seen in aging and diabetes, and surgical removal of the parathyroid glands has been known to eliminate diabetes. PTH can cause diuresis, leading to loss of blood volume and dehydration, hypertension, paralysis, increased rate of cell division, and growth of cartilage, bone, and other tissues." The parathyroidectomy-cures-diabetes claim implies that for some patients diabetes is downstream of chronic hyperparathyroidism, not primary.

PTH runs in tight feedback with the other stress hormones. "Prolactin... causes the body to lose calcium (drawing it from the bones), and it stimulates the secretion of PTH... Serotonin and IL-6 stimulate each others secretion, and PTH and serotonin each stimulate the others release." Stress raises [[prolactin]], prolactin raises PTH, PTH raises [[serotonin]] and IL-6, IL-6 and serotonin feed back. Once the loop closes nothing inside it relaxes without an external intervention.

PTH is also lipolytic. In ["Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes"](glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes) Peat noted that "the contribution of PTH to inflammation and degeneration is just being acknowledged, but the mechanism undoubtedly involves the fact that it is lipolytic, increasing the concentration of free fatty acids that suppress metabolism and interfere with the use of glucose." It pulls [[PUFA]] into circulation like [[adrenaline]] and [[cortisol]] do.

## Suppressors

The list of things that lower PTH reads like Peat's nutritional system in miniature. **Calcium** is the most direct. Adequate dietary calcium raises serum ionized calcium, which the parathyroid glands read as the signal to shut up. **Vitamin D** is the second pillar. As [[vitamin-d|vitamin D]] explains in detail, Peat regarded suppression of PTH as vitamin D's main function. The point at which PTH normalizes — he gave the rough number of 50 ng/mL of 25-hydroxyvitamin D — is the functional diagnostic for adequate D status, more important than any cutoff on the lab printout. In an [[vitamin-d|email exchange on vitamin D]] he wrote: "I think 50 ng/ml is a good goal. The point at which it lowers parathyroid hormone would be the right amount." The number is a surrogate; the lowering of PTH is what matters.

**Magnesium** is the third leg. "A deficiency of either calcium or magnesium can stimulate the parathyroid glands to produce more hormone," Peat wrote in the calcium and disease newsletter. Magnesium also stabilizes intracellular ATP, and when cells lose magnesium they lose ATP, depolarize, and take up calcium — the same intracellular pattern PTH itself produces. Calcium, vitamin D, and magnesium together hold PTH down; remove any of the three and the others cannot compensate fully.

**Carbon dioxide** is the fourth, and the most Peat-specific. "Respiration and carbon dioxide are the basic antagonists of the PTH," he wrote. "At birth, a baby has practically no PTH, probably because of the high intrauterine concentration of carbon dioxide, but within a few days the PTH rises." High-altitude living lowers PTH partly through the chronic mild hypercapnia it produces. [[Thyroid]] hormone, by raising the rate of oxidative phosphorylation, raises CO2 and therefore lowers PTH. The whole [[bioenergetics|bioenergetic]] frame converges here: anything that supports respiration suppresses the catabolic hormone.

**Sunlight** lowers PTH by raising 25-hydroxyvitamin D, but it has a second, faster action through the skin's production of cholecalciferol and possibly through direct effects on the parathyroid glands. In one [[2021-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2021-kmud|2021 Ask the Herb Doctor episode]] Peat noted that "just supplementing a moderate amount of vitamin D, like 5,000 units a day for an adult, can be enough. If you have calcium in your diet, it can be enough to lower the parathyroid hormone. Lowering the parathyroid hormone makes you put your calcium into the bones where it should go, keeping it out of the soft tissues."

Several other Peat staples lower PTH secondarily. **Vitamin K2** "blocks the removal of calcium from bone caused by parathyroid hormone and prostaglandin E2," he wrote in ["Osteoporosis, Aging, Tissue Renewal, and Product Science"](osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science). **Aspirin** "inhibits the actions of PTH, helping to prevent the calcification of inflamed tissues." **Niacinamide** lowers serum phosphate, which lowers PTH. **Vitamin A** "intake helps hold down parathyroid hormone," he told Andrew Murray in a [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 episode on hair loss and osteoporosis]]. **Frequent small meals** reduce PTH excursions — part of why Peat's advice to drink milk continuously through the day had a mechanistic rationale beyond preference.

## Drivers

The drivers mirror the suppressors. **Low calcium intake** is the most obvious. **Vitamin D deficiency** is next, and Peat saw it as the dominant driver in modern populations because diets that include enough calcium without dairy are uncommon and sunlight exposure has collapsed. **Excess phosphate** is the driver Peat thought was most underappreciated. In ["Phosphate, Activation, and Aging"](phosphate-activation-and-aging) he traced the trajectory: "High dietary phosphate increases serum osteopontin, as well as serum phosphate and parathyroid hormone, and increases the formation of tumors in skin." Phosphate-rich diets — cereals, beans, meat, nuts, processed meat injected with polyphosphates — keep PTH chronically elevated even when calcium intake looks adequate, because what the gland reads is not absolute calcium but the calcium-to-phosphate ratio.

**Low-protein diets** elevate PTH. "A low protein diet, similar to that eaten by a large proportion of women (0.8 g/kg of body weight) increases PTH, and so probably contributes to the development of osteoporosis and the diseases of calcification," Peat wrote in the calcium and disease newsletter. The advice to women to cut protein for bone health was, in his read, exactly backwards.

**Hyperventilation** raises PTH. "Hyperventilation increases phosphate and parathyroid hormone, and decreases calcium," he wrote in [phosphate-activation-and-aging](phosphate-activation-and-aging). [[Estrogen]] tends to cause chronic mild hyperventilation, which is part of how it contributes to the PTH-driven calcification picture in women. **Adrenaline** "strongly increases parathyroid hormone," he wrote in the osteoporosis newsletter. **Cortisol** raises PTH (the reference he cited was Lanna et al. on hypercortisolism). Anything in the stress cascade pulls PTH up.

**Aging itself** raises PTH: skin vitamin D synthesis declines, kidney function declines, and chronic dietary mistakes compound. In the osteoporosis newsletter Peat noted that "calcium activates mitochondrial respiration, and lowers adrenaline, parathyroid hormone, and prolactin." A diet that drifts away from calcium as a person ages produces a slow rise in PTH that the lab calls normal and the body experiences as aging.

## The Food-Source Hierarchy

Peat's practical advice for keeping PTH down was unambiguous and unfashionable: drink milk. In the [[2012-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-phosphate-and-calcium-metabolism|2012 Ask the Herb Doctor episode on phosphate and calcium metabolism]] he laid out the hierarchy. "Milk and cheese are about 1.3 to 1 calcium to phosphorus," he said. "Human milk is much better. I think if cows weren't given grain supplements, their milk would have an even higher ratio." A quart of milk delivers roughly 1,200 mg of calcium and just under a gram of phosphorus — the closest a single food gets to the one-to-one ratio Peat regarded as functionally protective. "A lot of people habitually eat a ratio of roughly five, six, or seven times as much phosphate as calcium. You probably shouldn't exceed about two parts of phosphate for each calcium. If you stick to a mostly milk and cheese-centered diet for your proteins, you'll stay close to a one-to-one ratio."

Leaves are the second-best source after dairy. "In plant materials, leaves really are a great source of calcium. Turnip greens, for example, have about 10 times as much calcium as phosphate." But "the cabbage family is anti-thyroid. All leaves have a fairly high unsaturated fat content, which can interfere with protein digestion." Bone-in fish such as sardines hold a respectable third place. Eggshells are a cheap backstop. The worst sources — most likely to drive PTH up — are exactly the foods modern nutritionism celebrates: lean meat (10:1 phosphate to calcium), whole grains, legumes, nuts, and processed meat with added phosphates. White rice and white bread have a *better* calcium-to-phosphate balance than whole-grain alternatives because the phosphate-rich germ has been removed.

Milk is the lever because no other food delivers high absolute calcium, favorable calcium-to-phosphate ratio, anti-inflammatory casein and whey peptides, and a form factor that allows continuous small doses through the day. In a [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-february-2022|February 2022 episode]] Peat added that "milk contains a lot of methionine, but parathyroid hormone is an activator of MTOR, and milk's high calcium content — working with vitamin D — lowers parathyroid hormone. So, milk contains its own antidote."

## Why "More Calcium" Sometimes Fails

People who add calcium supplements without thinking about phosphate or vitamin D often see no benefit, and the orthodox response is to conclude that calcium is unhelpful. Peat's reading was different. If a person was severely vitamin-D-deficient for years, the parathyroid glands physically enlarge, and PTH continues to be secreted even after calcium and D are restored. In the [[parathyroid-hormone-pth|emails on parathyroid hormone]] he wrote: "If your vitamin D was very low for a long time, I think your parathyroid glands probably enlarged, and might take some time to normalize under the influence of a generous amount of vitamin D and calcium."

A second failure mode is calcium without adequate carbohydrate — calcium pills on a low-carb diet do not produce the CO2 needed as the parallel PTH suppressor. A third is phosphate: supplemental calcium does not undo a diet of 6:1 or 10:1 phosphate-to-calcium meals.

## Clinical Conditions

The list of conditions Peat connected to chronically elevated PTH is long enough to function as an index of mid-life and late-life degenerative disease.

**Osteoporosis** is the textbook PTH-driven disease. Bone loss in postmenopausal women correlates with PTH elevation, and ["Osteoporosis, Aging, Tissue Renewal, and Product Science"](osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science) walks through the mechanism: "The parathyroid hormone, which removes calcium from bone, causes lactic acid to be formed by bone cells. Lactic acid produced by intense exercise causes calcium loss from bone, and sodium bicarbonate increases calcium retention by bone."

**Heart disease**, in Peat's frame, runs through PTH twice. First through the lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial damage caused by all the PTH-driven inflammatory mediators. Second, more directly, through vascular calcification. "The chronic elevation of PTH caused by calcium deficiency causes the heart and blood vessels to retain calcium, making them unable to relax fully," he wrote in the milk-in-context newsletter. "Intravenous infusion of calcium can relax blood vessels and improve heart function. The suppression of PTH is probably the main mechanism." The full vascular catalog he listed in the [[2016-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vitamin-d|2016 episode on vitamin D]] — "atrial fibrillation, enlargement of the ventricle, calcification of the valves, hardening of the arteries, calcification of the coronary arteries, and even calcification to the extent of what they call a 'heart stone'" — is what chronic hyperparathyroidism looks like read off an autopsy. The dedicated article on [[heart-disease|heart disease]] lists PTH as a Tier 1 mediator alongside [[PUFA]] and [[estrogen]].

**Hair loss** runs through PTH in a way that Peat singled out explicitly. In the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 episode on hair loss, inflammation, and osteoporosis]] he said: "The Vitamin D receptor is a regulatory material in practically every cell in the body, including bones and hair. The receptor itself is involved in making stem cells that produce new hairs. A couple of groups have discovered that if you inhibit the parathyroid hormone, you can increase hair growth. The natural inhibitors of the parathyroid hormone are Vitamin D and calcium." Asked whether parathyroid is inflammatory, he replied: "It interrupts the use of oxygen. It is essentially an anti-thyroid function. Thyroid enables you to use oxygen; parathyroid hormone can do useful things like shocking your bones into producing more cartilage by interfering with oxygen production for a moment. But if you keep your parathyroid hormone up continuously, you destroy the bone because it doesn't have enough energy to build." The follicle, like the bone, is one of the high-turnover tissues that cannot tolerate chronic anti-respiratory signaling. [[hair-loss|Hair loss]] lists PTH as a Tier 1 driver.

**Kidney stones** and kidney calcification both run through PTH. Chronic kidney disease produces severe secondary hyperparathyroidism, and PTH in that context is one of the classical "uremic toxins." But Peat's claim — repeated in the [[2016-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vitamin-d|2016 vitamin D episode]] — was that "too much phosphate and too little Vitamin D results in what amounts to an early stage of chronic kidney degeneration. Parathyroid hormone and phosphate are called uremic toxins in late-stage kidney disease, but people are starting that process very early when they don't eat enough calcium and Vitamin D." The lethal endpoint of kidney failure is a slow-moving version of the chronic moderate hyperparathyroidism most middle-aged people walk around with.

**Hypertension and metabolic syndrome** correlate with PTH. McCarron's work, which Peat cited often, showed that essential hypertension is associated with enhanced parathyroid function as a homeostatic response to a urinary calcium leak. The diabetes-hypertension-obesity cluster is associated with high PTH; Peat cited Hjelmsæth et al. in the milk newsletter on this point. PTH and aldosterone reinforce each other; PTH-driven calcium leakage activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.

**Cancer** appears through PTH-related protein (PTHrP), which "has the same functions as PTH, but can be produced in any tissue. It is responsible for the hypercalcemia of cancer, and is apparently involved in the frequent metastasis of breast cancer to the bones." Prostate and breast cancers exploit it.

**Multiple sclerosis** relapses "consistently occur at times of high PTH, and remissions consistently occur at times of low PTH," Peat wrote. Parathyroidectomy improves sleep in hemodialysis patients with severe hyperparathyroidism. "Chronically high PTH can produce anemia, by a variety of mechanisms."

## The Lab Marker

Peat's clinical interpretation of PTH was much tighter than the lab reference range. Most labs report a "normal" PTH range from roughly 10 to 65 pg/mL. Peat treated anything above the lower end of that as a warning sign. In the [[2012-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-phosphate-and-calcium-metabolism|2012 phosphate and calcium episode]] he said: "People who have good thyroid function and get enough calcium in their diet have a pretty low parathyroid hormone activity — towards the low end of the normal range. At the middle of the so-called normal range, people tend to suffer inflammatory diseases, muscle pains, and bone loss. The higher the parathyroid is, the more of these degenerative inflammatory diseases you have."

The implication is that a PTH around 30 pg/mL — well inside the lab's normal range — is already evidence of chronic stress, inadequate calcium, inadequate vitamin D, or all three. PTH approaching 50 or above, while still flagged "normal," indicates a person on a long trajectory toward osteoporosis, vascular calcification, and the rest. PTH around 10–20 is closer to what Peat regarded as physiological. The blood-tests email file makes the same point: "it might be worth having your parathyroid hormone tested. Despite your good vitamin D, you might not be getting enough calcium in relation to phosphate, and elevated PTH can cause generalized inflammation."

PTH should be interpreted alongside 25-hydroxyvitamin D, serum and ionized calcium, serum phosphate, magnesium, and (when available) the calcium-to-phosphate ratio of the typical diet. A high-normal PTH with a low-normal vitamin D and a high-normal phosphate is a single coherent picture: undersupplied with calcium and D, oversupplied with phosphate, headed slowly toward bone loss and arterial calcification. Lowering PTH into the lower third of the reference range was Peat's functional target.

## Counter-arguments

**Teriparatide and PTH analogs are approved osteoporosis treatments.** Recombinant PTH (1-34) — teriparatide — is FDA-approved for severe osteoporosis and demonstrably increases bone mineral density. This appears to contradict Peat's framing of PTH as bone-eating. The reconciliation is in dosing pattern: teriparatide is given as a once-daily injection, producing a transient PTH spike. The transient spike has anabolic effects on bone (it shocks osteoblasts into action) while chronic continuous elevation has the opposite, catabolic effect. Peat acknowledged this in the hair-loss episode: "Thyroid enables you to use oxygen; parathyroid hormone can do useful things like shocking your bones into producing more cartilage by interfering with oxygen production for a moment. But if you keep your parathyroid hormone up continuously, you destroy the bone." Teriparatide exploits the acute window. Chronic hyperparathyroidism — what most people experience — does the opposite. Mainstream osteoporosis pharmacology does not contradict Peat; it confirms his pulsatile-vs-chronic distinction.

**"Calcium supplements cause vascular calcification."** A series of 2010s studies associated calcium supplementation with increased cardiovascular events. Peat's response was that those studies confounded calcium with the form, timing, and accompanying nutrients, and ignored underlying PTH state. A calcium bolus on an empty stomach in a vitamin-D-deficient person produces transient hypercalcemia followed by rebound PTH spike — net effect can be worse than no supplement. The same calcium delivered as milk through the day, with adequate vitamin D, produces sustained PTH suppression. Peat's framework predicts harm from poorly delivered calcium and benefit from well-delivered calcium, exactly the pattern the literature actually shows.

**Primary hyperparathyroidism is a surgical disease.** Endocrinologists remove parathyroid adenomas. Peat did not dispute this. His point was that the surgical category captures only the extreme tail of the distribution, and that the larger population with secondary or chronic-stress hyperparathyroidism — driven by diet and lifestyle — is invisible to the endocrine system because their PTH falls inside the reference range. Peat treated lab-normal PTH as a continuum that most adults sit on the wrong end of.

**"Phosphate is essential; lowering it is dangerous."** Acute hypophosphatemia is serious. Peat's argument was not that phosphate should be removed but that the ratio of dietary phosphate to calcium in modern Western diets is grotesquely off (5–10:1) compared to the closer-to-1:1 ratio of dairy-centered diets. Shifting from whole grains and processed meat toward dairy, fruit, and leaves does not produce hypophosphatemia; it produces normal serum phosphate at normal PTH.

## Data gaps

The kinetics of parathyroid gland shrinkage after chronic stimulation are poorly characterized. Peat said "some time" without specifying months versus years; the rebound timeline matters clinically.

The minimum calcium intake required to suppress PTH in a vitamin-D-replete adult is not precisely defined. Peat used 2,000–2,500 mg as a target but the threshold below which PTH rises has not been mapped.

The phosphate-to-calcium ratio that defines "safe" is approximate. Peat's "don't exceed 2:1" is a working guideline; the dose-response curve between dietary ratio and steady-state PTH is not in the literature.

The role of PTHrP in non-cancer states is under-studied. Peat noted PTHrP can be produced in any tissue and contribute to inflammatory states, but assays outside the cancer context are limited.

The PTH-microbiome interaction — through phosphate absorption, vitamin D metabolism, and bile acid handling — is suggested by current research but was not in Peat's framework.

The diurnal PTH variation Peat described ("most loss of calcium from bones occurs during the night") is documented but practical implications are not well established.
