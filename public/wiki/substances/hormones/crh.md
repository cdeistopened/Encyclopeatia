# CRH (Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone)

The popular and clinical attention paid to cortisol is, in Ray Peat's reading of the stress literature, attention paid to the wrong tier. Cortisol is the late effector — the substance that converts muscle and skin into glucose when glycogen has run out. The hormone that decides whether the system runs that loop at all is one step upstream: corticotropin-releasing hormone, a forty-one–amino-acid peptide secreted from the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, traveling the hypophyseal portal vessels to the anterior pituitary, where it triggers ACTH and the adrenal cortisol surge the field calls "the stress response." But CRH is not only an upstream signal for cortisol. It acts directly on its own receptors in the brain stem, the limbic system, the gut, the skin, and on mast cells throughout the body — producing histamine release, cytokine amplification, nitric-oxide synthesis, vasodilation, and the visceral and behavioral features people recognize as anxiety. The cortisol the textbooks treat as the chemical signature of stress is, in this framing, a relatively late and partly compensatory event. CRH is the chemical signature of the disease.

Peat stated the inversion with unusual flatness in his 2014 newsletter [[endotoxin-stress-depression|*Endotoxin, Stress, and Depression*]]. After noting that cortisol-replacement therapy itself has antidepressant effects — a fact that should be impossible if the conventional cortisol-causes-depression story were complete — he wrote: "Chronically elevated cortisol is commonly seen in depressed people, but giving a supplement of cortisol is effective in relieving depression. Both cortisol and the pituitary corticotropic hormone that stimulates its production, ACTH, have some antidepressant effects, and they inhibit the hypothalamic corticotropin release hormone, CRH. CRH is more directly associated with depression than cortisol is, and it by itself activates many inflammatory processes, including the release of histamine, cytokines, and nitric oxide." That single paragraph contains the load-bearing claim of this page: the marker the field has been hunting lives one tier up.

The unique signal of this article is the CRH-as-direct-effector mechanism, the menstrual-postpartum-perimenopausal vulnerability it predicts, the failed pharmaceutical attempt to drug CRH at the receptor level, and the operational claim that meditation and "stress reduction" practices act downstream of glycogen, T3, salt, sugar, and progesterone. The broader stress cascade is at [[stress]]; the cortisol mechanism and protective-steroid buffering at [[cortisol]]; the SSRI inversion at [[anxiety-depression]]; the experimental model at [[learned-helplessness]].

## Biochemistry: the short axis

The pathway is anatomically compact. Parvocellular neurons of the paraventricular nucleus synthesize CRH and release it into the median eminence. The peptide travels down the hypophyseal portal vessels to the corticotrope cells of the anterior pituitary, where it binds CRHR1, triggers cleavage of pro-opiomelanocortin into ACTH, and provokes ACTH secretion into the systemic circulation. ACTH stimulates adrenal cortisol synthesis; the cortisol pulse peaks fifteen to thirty minutes later. The negative-feedback arm — cortisol acting on hypothalamic and pituitary glucocorticoid receptors — is what the textbooks call "the HPA axis."

The Peat reframe accepts the anatomy and questions the centrality of negative-feedback as the load-bearing story. Two facts trouble the conventional model. Exogenous cortisol *suppresses* CRH but does not necessarily suppress depression — sometimes the opposite, as supplemental cortisol has antidepressant effects in patients with elevated CRH (*Endotoxin, Stress, and Depression*). And CRH itself, not the downstream cortisol, drives many of the symptoms physicians attribute to "stress."

The list of things that drive CRH upward is a compact summary of Peat's account of disease. From the same newsletter: "CRH is promoted in the hypothalamus (and in many other tissues) by inflammation, endotoxin, serotonin, interleukins, and prostaglandins, but also by the perception of unavoidable difficulties. Besides cortisol, progesterone and androgens are internal factors that decrease the activity of CRH. Estrogen and hypothyroidism increase its activity." Five of the six promoters are bioenergetic, not psychological. The sixth — perception of unavoidable difficulties — is the [[learned-helplessness]] arm, which Peat reads as a metabolic event with a perceptual surface.

Clinically: CRH is lowered by progesterone and androgens, by adequate thyroid hormone (which lowers serotonin and estrogen), by suppression of gut endotoxin, and by restoration of the glycogen buffer.

## Direct effects: histamine, cytokines, nitric oxide, mast cells

The conventional reading of CRH treats it as an instruction-passing molecule whose job is to release ACTH and then disappear. The newer literature, and the part Peat emphasized, treats CRH as itself an inflammatory effector. CRH receptors are present in the skin, the gut, the mast-cell compartment, the bladder, the uterus, and the immune system; binding at these peripheral sites drives mast-cell degranulation (histamine, tryptase, TNF-α), upregulates inducible nitric-oxide synthase, increases vascular permeability, and amplifies the local cytokine cascade.

Peat compressed this in the 2014 newsletter: CRH "by itself activates many inflammatory processes, including the release of histamine, cytokines, and nitric oxide" ([[endotoxin-stress-depression|*Endotoxin, Stress, and Depression*]]). The "stress response" is therefore two parallel events: the slow cortisol arm that catabolizes tissue for fuel, and the fast CRH arm that directly inflames every tissue where CRH receptors are expressed. The patient feels the second arm — flushing, racing pulse, gut cramping, urticarial breakouts, migraine, anxiety — and the clinician measures the first.

The mast-cell connection links CRH directly to the diseases that share a mast-cell mechanism: migraine, urticaria, irritable bowel syndrome, interstitial cystitis, eczema, asthma, and "stress flares" in atopic and autoimmune conditions. Peat's general account of [[mast-cells|mast cells]] is that their activation is increased by "the pituitary hormones (including the thyroid stimulating hormone), and by estrogen" ([[autonomic-systems|*Autonomic Systems*]]). With CRH as the proximate trigger, the menstrual-cycle and postpartum vulnerabilities acquire a coherent signature: estrogen sensitizes the CRH neurons, CRH degranulates the mast cell, the mast cell releases the histamine that produces the flush, the cramp, the migraine, the panic episode.

The amplification by serotonin closes the loop. Peat in his [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|May 2017 *Ask the Herb Doctor* endocrinology episode]] put it directly: "serotonin increases CRH and prolactin; dopamine tends to inhibit them. Nitric oxide, endotoxin, and serotonin all shift your body toward distress. Progesterone and its derivatives in the brain, like allopregnenolone, are important for keeping the balance in the right direction." And in the [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|June 2019 *Jodellefit* episode on cortisol and low testosterone]]: "Serotonin is one of the most powerful activators of the cortisol stress hormone system. It acts powerfully on the hypothalamus to make CRH." Serotonin elevates CRH; CRH elevates serotonin's downstream effects; the loop has the shape of every other Peatian stress loop — positive feedback through inflammation, mediated by the substrate the cell does not have.

## The depression and anxiety connection

The cleanest reformulation of the depression literature in Peat's writing turns on the CRH measurement, not the cortisol measurement. The conventional psychiatric model reads the elevated cortisol of depressed patients as evidence that "stress causes depression," then treats the depression with serotonin-uptake inhibitors that elevate serotonin. The Peat reading is that the elevated cortisol of depressed patients is a marker of the chronically elevated CRH that the elevated serotonin (an SSRI's actual effect) is itself promoting. The drug raises the substance that drives the marker.

He stated the operational reading in the [[2019-09-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-education-and-reeducation|September 2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* education and reeducation episode]]: "the one that is suffering will have high cortisol and lower adrenaline. Cortisol is driven up by the corticotropin-releasing hormone, which is activated primarily by serotonin. So, in these miserable animals, their serotonin is higher." The causal chain — serotonin elevation → CRH elevation → cortisol elevation → tissue catabolism → behavioral suffering — runs through CRH as its load-bearing intermediate.

The vulnerability windows the framework predicts are the ones the clinical literature actually observes. CRH is hypersensitive to estrogen. Estrogen rises premenstrually relative to progesterone, drops abruptly after parturition, and becomes unopposed by progesterone after the menopausal collapse of the corpus luteum. Each of these is a window of disproportionate depressive and anxious morbidity — premenstrual dysphoric disorder, postpartum depression, perimenopausal mood disturbance. Peat noted the perimenopausal piece in [[hot-flashes-energy-and-aging|*Hot Flashes, Energy, and Aging*]]: "Hypoglycemia increases the brain hormone, corticotropin release hormone, CRH, which increases ACTH and cortisol. CRH causes vasodilation, and is more active in the presence of estrogen. Menopausal women are more responsive to its effects, and those with the most severe hot flushes are the most responsive." The hot flush is itself a CRH event, propagated through CRH's vasodilatory action and amplified by the estrogen that sensitizes the CRH neuron. The same mechanism, in a younger woman with unopposed estrogen and depleted progesterone, expresses itself as a panic attack rather than a hot flush.

The postpartum window is the strongest natural experiment. Placental CRH rises across pregnancy to a level that in any other context would indicate severe pathology. After parturition the placental source vanishes within hours, and the maternal hypothalamic CRH neurons — suppressed during pregnancy by the high placental output — are temporarily unable to fill the gap. Some women rebound smoothly; others do not, and the failure produces the syndrome the clinical literature calls postpartum depression. The framework predicts that rebound capacity tracks the protective-steroid pool: progesterone, allopregnanolone, pregnenolone, T3, DHEA. SSRIs do not address the substrate question. Progesterone, T3, sugar with salt, and allopregnanolone do.

## The CRH-antagonist drug story: a decade of failed trials

By the late 1990s the major pharmaceutical companies had read the CRH literature the same way Peat had — as the upstream signal whose elevation correlated more closely with depression and anxiety than any downstream measure. The strategic conclusion was to develop small-molecule antagonists at the CRHR1 receptor. Drugs were synthesized, animal models were positive, clinical trials proceeded. By the late 2000s the program had collapsed. None of the major CRHR1 antagonists made it through Phase II or III. The three best-characterized compounds:

- **Antalarmin** — non-peptide CRHR1 antagonist developed at NIH in the 1990s. Robust anxiolytic effects in rodent models, lowered ACTH and cortisol responses to exogenous CRH. Never moved past preclinical development.
- **CP-154,526** — Pfizer compound, structurally distinct from antalarmin but pharmacologically similar. Showed efficacy in fear-conditioning and stress-induced freezing models. Pfizer terminated development.
- **Pexacerfont (BMS-562086)** — Bristol-Myers Squibb's clinical candidate, the furthest-advanced. Reached Phase II trials for generalized anxiety disorder. 2010 data showed no significant difference from placebo. Development stopped.

The framework does not require a special hypothesis for the failure. CRH receptor blockade is a single-point intervention at a node whose elevation is driven by serotonin, endotoxin, inflammation, hypoglycemia, estrogen unopposed by progesterone, and hypothyroidism. Block CRHR1 and the upstream drivers continue to operate — redirecting through CRHR2, urocortin receptors, or vasopressin-coupled corticotrope responses, or saturating the blockade at higher CRH concentrations. A small molecule at one receptor cannot solve a problem whose actual cause is a substrate deficit.

This is not a Peat-stated claim about the specific molecules — he did not, in the indexed corpus, name antalarmin or pexacerfont. It is the application of his general framework to the case: pharmacology directed at a downstream effector while the substrate is uncorrected will produce small effects, large side-effect burdens, and trial failures. The same prediction holds for NK1-antagonist antidepressants, CCK-antagonist anxiolytics, and the long-term failure of statins once lipid-as-driver was shown incomplete.

## The Peat reframe: CRH is downstream of metabolic substrate

The operational claim that distinguishes this page from any conventional endocrinology textbook is that CRH is lowered, in clinical doses and within hours, by the simple restoration of substrate. Not by meditation, not by breathing exercises, not by a CRH-receptor antagonist. By sugar, salt, [[thyroid|T3]], [[progesterone]], an environment that is bright and warm, an absence of endotoxin from the gut.

Peat described the substrate dependence in the [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|*Jodellefit* episode]] with the thyroid arm foregrounded: "When your thyroid goes down from stress, your estrogen normally is being excreted and kept at a low level systemically by the thyroid activating liver metabolism... If your thyroid drops from stress, very quickly your estrogen will rise. Then the estrogen acts directly on the adrenals; it increases cortisol production. It activates your pituitary to stimulate cortisol production and activates your brain to release Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone (CRH). So just dropping the active thyroid, you very quickly turn on both the estrogen and the whole cortisol stress system." Restore the thyroid, estrogen falls, progesterone rises, CRH falls.

The glycogen arm is just as direct: "Hypoglycemia increases the brain hormone, corticotropin release hormone, CRH" ([[hot-flashes-energy-and-aging|*Hot Flashes, Energy, and Aging*]]). Every modality that prevents the dawn-glycogen crash — a glass of milk or [[orange-juice]] at midnight, a carbohydrate snack before bed, adequate daytime calories, sufficient salt to prevent the aldosterone-driven hypoglycemic dip — is in this frame a CRH-lowering intervention. Bioenergetic, not behavioral.

The same mechanism explains why meditation and "stress reduction" produce real but limited effects. A relaxation practice that lowers sympathetic tone for twenty minutes does measurably lower CRH and cortisol during the practice. It does not change the substrate that drives CRH back up the rest of the day. Meditation, exercise, cognitive practices, and social engagement are valuable but secondary — the lever is metabolic before it is cognitive.

The protective steroids close the picture. Progesterone, androgens, and the brain pregnenolone-allopregnanolone pool all lower CRH activity — through GABA-A modulation by allopregnanolone and through competitive suppression of estrogen-driven CRH amplification by progesterone (*Endotoxin, Stress, and Depression*). When these are abundant, CRH neurons fire at low baseline rates and respond to acute stressors with a reversible pulse. When depleted — by age, hypothyroidism, postpartum collapse, chronic stress, oral contraceptives — they fire at high baseline rates and produce disproportionate pulses to small stressors. The clinical state the patient calls "anxiety" is the felt experience of this configuration.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream HPA-axis view is that CRH is the apex regulator of a feedback loop whose dysregulation produces depression and anxiety, and that pharmacological intervention at the CRH receptor was a reasonable strategy that simply did not produce a clinical candidate with sufficient potency, blood-brain-barrier penetration, or receptor specificity. This view treats the antalarmin/CP-154,526/pexacerfont failures as engineering setbacks rather than as evidence against the underlying single-receptor strategy. The continued development of CRHR1 antagonists (verucerfont, emicerfont, R-121919) and the active research program on CRHR2-selective agonists and urocortin signaling reflect this stance. A counterargument to Peat's framework would observe that the pre-clinical animal data on CRHR1 antagonists are robust — antalarmin's effects on social-defeat behavior are reproducible — and that the failure of clinical translation may say more about the difficulty of dosing brain peptides than about the substrate-versus-receptor question.

A second mainstream position is that CRH measurement itself is methodologically unreliable. Plasma CRH does not closely reflect hypothalamic CRH; CSF CRH is invasive to sample; salivary cortisol remains the field-standard surrogate. The claim that "CRH is more directly associated with depression than cortisol is" rests on a relatively small body of CSF and post-mortem work (Nemeroff and colleagues from the 1980s onward) and may be statistically thinner than the cortisol literature. A skeptic could argue the cortisol focus in clinical work reflects assay reliability rather than a conceptual error.

A third position is that PTSD-specific CRH research, where preclinical models show particularly strong CRH involvement, may yet produce a successful clinical compound. The 2015 trial of verucerfont (GSK561679) in women with childhood-trauma-related PTSD was negative on the primary outcome but suggested signal in a sex-specific subgroup. Defenders of the CRH-receptor strategy argue the right indication and patient population have not yet been identified.

A fourth counterargument concerns the metabolic-substrate claim itself. The Peat-implied prediction that CRH should fall rapidly with carbohydrate-and-salt feeding, T3, and progesterone is not, in the indexed source material, the subject of a controlled trial. Components have evidence — sucrose normalizes hypothalamic CRH mRNA in adrenalectomized rats (Laugero et al., cited in [[sugar-issues]]); progesterone and allopregnanolone modulate the HPA axis through GABA-A — but no integrated trial has tested substrate restoration against an SSRI or a CRHR1 antagonist with CRH as the primary outcome.

A fifth clarification: the specific naming of antalarmin, CP-154,526, and pexacerfont was extrapolated from Peat's general framework, not directly quoted from the indexed corpus. The CRH-as-inflammatory-effector claim is well-attested; the antagonist section should be read as a framework-derived prediction.

## Data gaps

The molecular pharmacology of CRH at peripheral tissues — skin, gut, bladder, uterus — is incompletely characterized in the indexed Peat corpus. Urocortin 1, 2, and 3 (CRH-family peptides that bind CRHR2 preferentially) are not addressed in the source material. The receptor distribution of CRHR2 and its possible role in energy expenditure, food intake, and glucose regulation — which the substrate-restoration claim implies should matter — is similarly unaddressed.

The relationship between placental CRH and maternal hypothalamic CRH across pregnancy and parturition — central to any complete account of postpartum depression — is in the obstetric literature but not in the indexed Peat corpus.

The substrate-restoration claim — that CRH falls within hours with glycogen, T3, and progesterone — needs direct measurement in human studies of postpartum depression, PMDD, and perimenopausal anxiety. The Peat-implied protocol has case-report-quality precedent; it lacks the controlled-trial evidence that would close the question against SSRI treatment.

CRHR1 polymorphisms (rs110402, rs242924) interact with childhood-trauma history to predict adult depression in gene-environment studies. How this layer interacts with the metabolic-substrate framework is unmapped.
