# Estrogen

Few hormones reveal the gap between commercial medicine and biological reality more starkly than estrogen. While the pharmaceutical industry spent decades positioning it as the "female hormone"—essential for youth, vitality, and protection against disease—Ray Peat's dissertation research in 1972 reached a disturbing conclusion: the effects of estrogen are "indistinguishable from the effects of X-rays, [[aging]], and oxygen deprivation." This was not a rhetorical flourish but a precise biochemical observation about how estrogen damages the cell's respiratory machinery.

## The Primary Metabolic Antagonist

Estrogen functions as a stress hormone. It creates immediate cellular excitement at the cost of long-term structural integrity and energy production. In a [[2015-10-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-current-trends-on-nitric-oxide|2015 KMUD interview]], Peat explained the mechanism he had identified decades earlier:

> "My dissertation in 1972 focused on estrogen, but I saw that the effects of estrogen were indistinguishable from the effects of X-rays, aging, and oxygen deprivation. It works by changing the oxidative enzyme function, cytochrome oxidase."

This comparison to radiation was not hyperbole. Peat cited evidence showing that estrogen and ionizing radiation "synergize; each one produces the same sort of damage to the organism," with both converging on the [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] stored in tissues. The damage compounds rather than merely adding up.

The picture becomes clearer when examining what happens inside the cell. As Peat described in a [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 discussion on dementia and progesterone]]: "Estrogen damages them in at least six or eight different ways. It shifts from the ability to oxidize glucose and impairs the very enzyme that uses oxygen in several different places in the mitochondria. Besides poisoning the energy, it also blocks the ability to turn cholesterol into steroid hormones."

This creates a vicious cycle. The same damage that prevents glucose oxidation also prevents the body from producing the protective steroids ([[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], [[DHEA]]) that would normally oppose estrogen's effects.

## How Estrogen Creates Hypoxia

At the cellular level, estrogen mimics the effects of oxygen deprivation. In a [[2018-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-2|2018 episode comparing progesterone and estrogen]], Peat laid out the mechanism:

> "When the brain is oversupplied with estrogen or lacks its antagonist, progesterone, the whole system wastes oxygen. It is unable to keep the energy level up to the point where cells are stable. A high-energy state is when a cell is ready to work, but not acting. When forced to work more than it is ready for, the energy level falls, and it enters a constantly active state trying to restore energy."

This "constantly active state" is the metabolic signature of estrogen dominance: cells become excited, take on water, and shift from efficient [[oxidative metabolism]] to emergency [[glycolysis|glycolytic metabolism]]—producing [[lactic acid]] instead of carbon dioxide.

The parallel to hypoglycemia is instructive. "Hypoglycemia works the same as a lack of oxygen," Peat continued. "Estrogen interferes with the supply of glucose to cells. It activates insulin but also activates the release of fatty acids, which block the ability to oxidize glucose." Through the [[Randle cycle]], these free fatty acids prevent cells from using glucose even when it is available.

> "Estrogen activates cells by increasing the function of glutamic acid (glutamate), a brain excitant. By increasing glutamate, it decreases GABA, the inhibiting signal. So, estrogen excites brain cells while interfering with their use of oxygen and glucose."

The net effect is a tissue that cannot rest. This explains why estrogen exposure is linked to seizures (catamenial epilepsy typically begins when estrogen rises at puberty before [[progesterone]] catches up), anxiety, insomnia, and the diffuse "nervous" quality that characterizes estrogen dominance.

## The Cancer Connection

The relationship between estrogen and [[cancer]] was understood before commercial interests obscured it. In a [[2015-03-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-breast-cancer|2015 episode on breast cancer]], Peat described how researchers in the 1930s and 40s had documented this clearly:

> "The animal studies in the 30s and 40s were very clear that it isn't just the normally responsive tissues like the uterus and the breast that will cancerize under the influence of estrogen and similar things—those are just the most sensitive. With increased, uninterrupted exposure to estrogen, the sequence tends to go: first cancer of the uterus, then cancer of the breast, then of the lungs, kidneys, intestine, liver, and brain."

The mechanism follows directly from estrogen's effects on cellular respiration. When oxidative metabolism is impaired, cells shift to the fermentative metabolism described by [[Otto Warburg]]—the metabolic pattern that defines cancer cells. As Peat explained in a [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|2016 episode on cancer metabolism]]:

> "When something overstimulates or irritates an area—and the irritating effect of things like estrogen was already known—that excitation exhausts the tissue oxygen. This was at the center of the idea of what cancer is."

This also explains why carcinogens often have estrogenic properties. "The people doing the real science were trying to explain why estrogen had these toxic effects," Peat noted in a [[2011-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-inflammation-ii|2011 discussion of inflammation]]. "They saw that natural estrogen was imitated by these polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in soot, which were recognized carcinogens. They were studying the properties of soot and estrogen and finding that the substance tended to produce inflammation and cancer."

The suppression of this research represents one of the pharmaceutical industry's most consequential distortions of science. Twelve major drug companies formed consortiums to promote estrogen as beneficial, funding research that buried the dangers while aggressive marketing created the myth of estrogen as the hormone of feminine health.

## The Injury Response: Aromatase and Local Estrogen Production

A critical insight that emerged from Peat's framework is that estrogen production is not limited to the ovaries. The enzyme aromatase, which converts androgens to estrogen, gets activated by any tissue injury or [[inflammation]]:

> "Any injury to any tissue, anywhere in the body, happens to activate the enzyme which creates estrogen. It's like when you injure a tree, it causes sprouting by turning on hormones in response to the injury. That's how estrogen works. Many enzymes get turned on, but especially aromatase, which is the basic former of estrogen itself. So in stress or any injury, aromatase appears and begins making estrogen right inside the tissue."

This has profound implications. After menopause, when ovarian production declines, estrogen does not simply disappear—it begins forming in fat cells, skin cells, brain tissue, and throughout the body wherever inflammation exists. In a [[2019-04-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-particles|2019 episode]], Peat elaborated: "After menopause, estrogen begins forming inside cells all through the body—fat cells, skin cells, breast, and uterine cells. It can be formed from adrenal precursors."

This explains the paradox that blood estrogen levels may drop after menopause while tissue estrogen levels actually rise. As Peat noted in a [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|2013 discussion on aging]]:

> "Almost everyone around the age of 40 has the highest ratio of estrogen to progesterone. The trouble is, the estrogen seems to drop when you measure it in the serum, even though it was at its very highest lifetime level around age 40. When progesterone falls, the estrogen can't be released from the cell, so it doesn't appear in the serum until progesterone affects the cells to release it."

The measurement of serum estrogen thus provides a misleading picture. What matters is tissue-level estrogen exposure, which tends to increase with age even as blood levels fall.

## Environmental Estrogens: The Ubiquitous Pollutant

Beyond endogenous production, modern life presents extensive sources of estrogenic exposure. In a [[2021-12-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-hormones-behind-inflammation|2021 episode on hormones and inflammation]], Peat addressed the scope of the problem:

> "Birth control pills getting into the sewage end up in the rivers and recycle. Just the tremendous flow of estrogen down the sewers is a tremendous polluting source. But regarding plastics, the agents used to make stiff plastics more flexible are effective estrogens in very small amounts. All of the water supply of the world is contaminated with those."

The plasticizers (phthalates, bisphenol A and its replacements) that leach from food packaging, water bottles, and countless other plastic products act as estrogens in the body. Soy products contain genistein, which activates the aromatase enzyme that produces estrogen—the opposite of what protective phytoestrogens should do.

Even foods marketed as healthful present problems. In a [[2020-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vibrations-frequencies-part-2|2020 episode]], Peat contrasted the estrogens in milk (mostly in detoxified, sulfated forms ready for excretion) with those in soy: "One of the reasons milk tends to have an overall protective effect against estrogen is that the estrogens in milk are in a detoxified form. Estrone sulfate, for example, is the least harmful form of estrogen. It is not at all comparable to the estrogens that are in the soy milk substitutes."

## The Progesterone Opposition

[[Progesterone]] provides the primary physiological defense against estrogen. The opposition operates at multiple levels: progesterone opposes estrogen's excitatory effects on cells, activates enzymes that detoxify and excrete estrogen, and supports the production of other protective steroids.

Peat described this relationship throughout his work. In a [[2019-03-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-viruses|2019 episode]]: "Any irritation increases the reductive balance of the cell and the activation of estrogen production. Estrogen tends to lock it into that reductive and dysplastic condition. Vitamin A opposes the structural changes on epithelial cells as an anti-estrogen differentiating effect. Progesterone knocks out the estrogen and its products. All of the metabolic changes shift the cell away from estrogen and inflammation towards the oxidative, energy-producing condition."

This makes the ratio of progesterone to estrogen more significant than the absolute level of either hormone. A woman with low estrogen but even lower progesterone is functionally estrogen-dominant.

## Strategies for Reducing Estrogen

The body clears estrogen primarily through the [[liver]], which conjugates it with glucuronic acid or sulfate for excretion through bile and urine. Supporting this process is central to reducing estrogen burden.

**[[Thyroid]] function** is essential because low thyroid impairs liver metabolism. In a [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|2013 episode]]: "Women have 5 or 10 times as much thyroid disease as men. The reason is that estrogen interferes with the liver's ability to convert thyroxine (T4) to the active T3 hormone."

**Intestinal transit** matters because estrogen excreted in bile can be reabsorbed if it sits in the colon too long. In a [[2017-07-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-language-and-criticism-estrogen-part-2|2017 episode on estrogen]], Peat recommended: "Increasing fiber content—raw carrot, cooked bamboo shoots, or cooked mushrooms—can lower stress hormones by cleaning out your intestine and preventing the reabsorption of estrogen and cortisol."

The famous "raw carrot salad" works through multiple mechanisms. In a [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-hormones-behind-inflammation|recent episode]]: "When it's raw carrot and moderately shredded, we absorb very little of the irritating chemicals, but the fiber helps prevent the reabsorption of estrogen from the bowel."

**[[Aspirin]]** inhibits aromatase and reduces the inflammatory processes that activate local estrogen production. **[[Vitamin E]]** functions as "an anti-inflammatory, estrogen-regulating substance," as Peat noted in a [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-lipofuscin|discussion of lipofuscin and aging]].

**Avoiding [[PUFA]]** reduces the substrate for lipid peroxidation and inflammation that drives estrogen production. As Peat explained: "Aging and excess estrogen, in particular, correspond to the deterioration of polyunsaturated fats by random oxidation. A basic thing to protect against aging and stress is to simply reduce the amount of polyunsaturated fats taken in the diet."

**Aromatase inhibitors** from food—naringenin from oranges, apigenin from guavas—can reduce local estrogen production. Peat noted in a [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|2013 interview]] that "two fruits that contain effective aromatase inhibitors are oranges (naringenin) and guavas (apigenin)."

## The Forms of Estrogen

The body produces three main estrogens: estradiol (the most potent), estrone, and estriol (the weakest). The relationship between them matters for understanding toxicity:

> "There are probably a dozen important types of estrogen, but the three main types—estriol, estrone, and estradiol—have pretty much the same effects, just at different potencies. If you can turn your most potent estradiol into the others, you're protected against some of the most toxic effects. During pregnancy, the index of a healthy pregnancy is when estriol is high, because that means you're destroying your estradiol very quickly."

This principle—that the body converts potent estrogens to weaker forms as a protective mechanism—explains why healthy pregnancies show high estriol despite overall elevated estrogen. The protective hormones (progesterone, thyroid) accelerate this conversion.
