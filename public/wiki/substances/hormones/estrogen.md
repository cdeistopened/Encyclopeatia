# Estrogen

Estrogen is the most dangerous substance in Ray Peat's framework, and its danger lies precisely in the fact that it has been mislabeled for a century. Mainstream medicine classifies estrogen as a protective female hormone whose decline at menopause causes aging, bone loss, cognitive deterioration, and cardiovascular disease. Peat spent fifty years arguing the opposite: estrogen is a stress hormone whose accumulation with age is not a sign of health fading but a cause of health fading. Its fundamental action, shared with suffocation, radiation, soot exposure, and [[PUFA]] accumulation, is interference with the cell's ability to use oxygen. "I believe that estrogen's 'principle,' in all of its actions, is to interfere with the respiratory mode of energy production," Peat wrote in his article [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women|"Estrogen and Brain Aging in Men and Women"]]. That single sentence reorganizes the entire landscape of degenerative disease.

Hans Selye demonstrated in the 1940s that a large dose of estrogen creates a shock condition in animals. [[Progesterone]], by contrast, imitated all the features of the stress-protecting hormones so completely that animals could survive without their adrenal glands if given a steady supply of it. These findings were not obscure footnotes; they were central results from one of the twentieth century's most celebrated physiologists. But the pharmaceutical industry had already committed itself to selling estrogen as a fountain of youth, and a series of business decisions, regulatory corruptions, and academic distortions progressively buried the evidence that estrogen causes the very conditions it was marketed to prevent: cancer, heart disease, stroke, dementia, osteoporosis, and premature aging in both sexes.

## The Oxygen Principle

Estrogen's effects are diverse in their clinical presentation but unified in their mechanism. Every estrogenic stimulus produces the same cascade: interference with mitochondrial respiration, a shift from oxidative to glycolytic energy production, oxygen deprivation at the cellular level, cell swelling, calcium influx, [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] accumulation, and, if the exposure is sustained, [[fibrosis]] and eventually [[cancer]]. In his [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone" (2006)]] newsletter, Peat described the sequence with characteristic precision:

> In good health, an animal's systems are designed so that certain tissues will be intensely but briefly stimulated by estrogen. This stimulation by estrogen doesn't produce the normal amount of carbon dioxide, so the tissue experiences oxygen deprivation, leading to swelling and cell division. Any similar stimulation, whether it's produced by soot, or suffocation, or irradiation, will produce the broad range of estrogen's effects, beginning with inflammation but ending with atrophy or cancer if it is too prolonged.

The insight that soot, suffocation, and X-irradiation produce estrogenic effects is not a metaphor in Peat's framework; it is the key to understanding why estrogen is classified as a carcinogen by researchers who study it without financial conflicts of interest. As Peat asked in [[the-dire-effects-of-estrogen-pollution|"The Dire Effects of Estrogen Pollution" (2006)]]: "Otherwise, how could suffocation and X-irradiation have estrogenic effects?" The answer is that these stimuli share estrogen's fundamental action on cellular respiration. They all push cells away from the efficient production of [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] through oxidative phosphorylation and toward the wasteful, injury-associated production of lactic acid through glycolysis.

Peat noted that estrogen visibly demonstrates this oxygen-wasting effect. It increases blood flow to particular organs, but apparently less than it increases their oxygen demand, "as can be seen from the color change of estrogenized tissues, toward purple, rather than pink." Healthy, well-oxygenated tissue is pink because hemoglobin is saturated with oxygen. Estrogenized tissue turns purple because the cells are consuming oxygen faster than it can be delivered, a visible sign of the metabolic suffocation that defines estrogen's action.

The energy failure that results from this interference has been, in Peat's words, "remarkably well characterized (but the meaning of this for the cell hasn't been explored)." The electron transfer process of the mitochondria is interrupted by the futile redox cycling catalyzed by estrogens. Where [[aspirin]] activates the glycolytic enzyme GAPDH to support glucose oxidation, estrogen acts on the same enzyme in the opposite direction, pushing cells toward fermentation. This opposition between estrogen and aspirin is not coincidental; it reflects their positions on opposite sides of Peat's central metabolic divide.

## Estrogen and Shock

The acute effects of estrogen are indistinguishable from the effects of traumatic shock. Selye's experiments in the 1940s demonstrated that a single large injection of estrogen could produce the full syndrome: blood volume decreases as water moves from the bloodstream into the tissues, blood pressure drops, veins relax, cardiac output falls, and animals die unless rescued. Peat connected this to his broader understanding of stress physiology in a [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on estrogen and progesterone]]:

> I started seeing how relevant Hans Selye's work was, showing the stress-shock imitating effect of estrogen. In fact, any kind of shock or stress has estrogenic effects. You can make an animal go into estrus by irradiating any part of the body; something is produced that has an estrogenic effect. If you put a plastic bag over a rat's head, it goes into estrus. Suffocation activates the so-called estrogen receptors and will actually increase the production of estrogen.

The relationship between estrogen and shock runs in both directions. Estrogen mimics shock, and shock produces estrogen. This bidirectional connection is central to understanding why critical illness, surgery, burns, and severe infections all involve surges in estrogen and why they produce the same pattern of edema, organ failure, and inflammatory tissue destruction. When estrogen's effects overlap with endotoxin, as Peat noted, "multiple organ failure is the result."

Estrogen accomplishes its acute effects partly through the inflammatory mediators [[serotonin]] and [[histamine]]. It increases both, which together make blood vessels leaky while simultaneously causing cells to take up water and calcium. The combination of vascular leakage and cellular swelling is the physical basis of edema, and edema is the tissue-level expression of estrogen's energy-disrupting principle. A cell that cannot produce enough energy to maintain its ion gradients swells; a blood vessel whose endothelial cells are energy-depleted leaks.

The excitatory dimension of estrogen connects it, in Peat's analysis, to cocaine. "Since it is excitatory, it can temporarily increase alertness," he explained. "It acts on the same enzymes that cocaine acts on to increase adrenaline action in the brain." This explains why estrogen replacement therapy sometimes produces a temporary sense of wellbeing that patients and their doctors mistake for evidence of benefit. The alertness is real but short-lived, and "carried on for a long time, it is excitotoxic to the nerves." The analogy to cocaine is precise: an initial stimulation followed by dependence, tolerance, and progressive neurological damage.

In his article [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women|"Estrogen and Brain Aging in Men and Women"]], Peat cataloged the mechanisms by which this excitotoxic process destroys brain tissue: estrogen "activates the excitotoxic glutamic acid pathway, and interferes with protective adenosine inhibition of nerves." It promotes the formation of [[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]] and carbon monoxide, both of which "quickly and seriously interfere with mitochondrial respiration." It increases intracellular calcium and free radical production, "contributing to both the excitatory excess and the energy deficit." And it activates the entire adrenal stress reaction through the hypothalamus and pituitary, by direct actions on the adrenal glands, and through indirect effects such as the increase of free fatty acids. Marion Diamond demonstrated that prenatal progesterone enlarges the cortex of the brain, while estrogen makes it smaller. A single injection of estrogen can, as Peat noted, kill a large portion of brain cells, a fact difficult to reconcile with the "estrogen receptor" doctrine but entirely consistent with the principle of respiratory interference.

## The Tissue Trap

One of the most consequential discoveries in estrogen biology, and one of the most systematically ignored, is that tissue-bound estrogen far exceeds the levels measured in blood plasma. Standard hormone panels measure serum estrogen and report apparently low levels in postmenopausal women, which physicians then interpret as evidence that these women need supplemental estrogen. Peat argued that this interpretation is catastrophically wrong.

Citing the work of S. Batra, he wrote in his [[tissue-bound-estrogen-in-aging|"Tissue-Bound Estrogen in Aging" (2008)]] newsletter that "the tissue/plasma ratio of E2 (estradiol) ranged from 1.45 to 20.36 with very high values in early follicular phase and the lowest in mid-luteal phase." The mid-luteal phase is when progesterone is highest. When progesterone is low, "the tissues may contain 20 to 30 times more estrogen than the plasma." This means that progesterone actively prevents tissues from concentrating estrogen, and when progesterone declines with age, the tissues become estrogen sponges even as serum levels appear to drop.

The finding that demolishes the case for postmenopausal estrogen replacement is this: "In postmenopausal women, the tissue concentration of E2 was not significantly lower than in menstruating women in follicular phase." Postmenopausal women are not estrogen-deficient. Their tissues contain as much estrogen as the tissues of menstruating women during the low-progesterone phase of the cycle. What they lack is not estrogen but progesterone, the hormone that would prevent their tissues from hoarding estrogen and that would activate the enzymes needed to eliminate it.

The mechanism by which tissues accumulate estrogen involves [[aromatase]], the enzyme that converts androgens to estrogen locally in fat, skin, bone, brain, and virtually every other tissue under stress (see [[aromatase]] for the full mechanism). It also involves beta-glucuronidase, an enzyme that reverses the liver's inactivation of estrogen. The healthy liver attaches glucuronic acid to estrogen, preparing it for excretion. But inflamed tissue contains large amounts of beta-glucuronidase, which strips the glucuronic acid away and releases active estrogen to accumulate locally. This is why inflammation and estrogen are functionally synonymous in Peat's framework: inflamed tissue both produces and reactivates estrogen through these two enzymatic pathways.

Alexander Lipshuts demonstrated in the 1940s that the uninterrupted effect of a small amount of estrogen is far more harmful than larger but intermittent exposures. Before menopause, progesterone cyclically displaces estrogen from the tissues during the luteal phase. After menopause, when progesterone production ceases, "the tissues retain large amounts of estrogen continuously," producing exactly the kind of uninterrupted exposure that Lipshuts showed to be maximally carcinogenic.

## The Recirculation Loop

Estrogen's persistence in the body depends on a vicious cycle involving the liver, the intestine, and the thyroid. Under healthy conditions, the liver inactivates virtually all the estrogen that reaches it, conjugating it with glucuronic acid and excreting it into the bile for elimination. But this system fails under stress.

The Biskinds, Morton and Gerson, demonstrated in the 1940s that malnutrition and other stresses would increase estrogen by impairing the liver's ability to destroy it. Peat summarized their work in the [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 *EastWest Healing* interview]]: "Normally, 100% of the estrogen that gets to the liver is prepared for excretion. If the liver is underactive because of a protein, B-vitamin, or thyroid deficiency, 100% of the estrogen reaching the liver can pass right through it back into the bloodstream." The liver's capacity to clear estrogen depends entirely on adequate thyroid function, which in turn is suppressed by estrogen itself. [[Hypothyroidism]] prevents the liver from conjugating estrogen, and the resulting estrogen excess further impairs the thyroid gland's ability to secrete [[thyroid]] hormone. This self-reinforcing loop explains why hypothyroid and hyperestrogenic states are so difficult to separate clinically: they are the same condition viewed from two angles.

The intestine provides a second opportunity for estrogen to escape elimination. Even when the liver successfully conjugates estrogen and excretes it into the bile, the conjugated estrogen passes into the intestine, where bacterial enzymes can deconjugate it, allowing it to be reabsorbed through the gut wall and recirculated to the liver for another attempt at clearance. If the bowel is sluggish, as it often is in hypothyroidism, this reabsorption intensifies. Peat noted that "sometimes just an antibiotic will drastically lower your estrogen and stress hormones by limiting that reabsorption."

This intestinal route is what led Peat to one of his most distinctive practical recommendations. "That is what got me interested in the raw carrot effect," he explained. "You see lower estrogen and cortisol, and higher progesterone, within about three days of eating a raw carrot every day." The raw carrot's fiber binds estrogen in the gut, preventing its reabsorption and breaking the recirculation loop without pharmaceutical intervention. In his email exchanges, Peat confirmed that "a daily raw carrot often helps to balance progesterone, cortisol, and estrogen, by improving intestine-liver functions."

The recirculating estrogen does not merely persist passively. It actively worsens the conditions that caused its accumulation. "That recirculating estrogen then turns on the adrenal stress hormones directly and blocks the thyroid gland," Peat stated. It also promotes the release of [[prolactin]] and [[cortisol]], both of which further suppress thyroid function and increase [[aromatase]] activity, generating still more estrogen. The result is a cascading failure in which stress produces estrogen, estrogen amplifies stress, and the body's clearance mechanisms are progressively disabled.

## Estrogen, PUFA, and the Warburg Effect

The dietary factor most responsible for amplifying estrogen's effects is the consumption of [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]]. Peat identified the relationship between PUFA and estrogen as one of the most important and least recognized connections in nutritional science. "Increasingly, one of the environmental factors that impairs liver function, increases estrogen activity, and inhibits thyroid function is the polyunsaturated oils," he explained. "These are all anti-thyroid, pro-estrogen, and anti-progesterone."

The interaction operates at multiple levels. PUFA stored in tissues increases the production of estrogen through the prostaglandin pathway, which activates aromatase. PUFA free fatty acids intensify the actions of estrogen at the cellular level, making each molecule of estrogen more damaging. And PUFA interferes directly with glucose oxidation, compounding the respiratory failure that estrogen initiates.

Peat described the metabolic convergence in precise terms: "The instantaneous effect of either estrogen or unsaturated fatty acids is to imitate the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]], lacking the Pasteur effect, which is for oxygen to turn off lactic acid production. In the presence of high estrogen and PUFA, we make lactic acid and cannot use the energy oxidatively." The Warburg effect, the observation that cancer cells preferentially ferment glucose rather than oxidizing it even when oxygen is available, is not merely associated with estrogen and PUFA; in Peat's framework, it is caused by them. Cancer is the terminal expression of a metabolic shift that estrogen and PUFA initiate long before any tumor appears.

"The pro-estrogenic nature of the unsaturated fatty acids is probably the biggest barrier to the radical elimination of degenerative diseases," Peat wrote in [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women|"Estrogen and Brain Aging in Men and Women"]]. This statement captures why dietary PUFA avoidance is not a peripheral recommendation in Peat's system but the central dietary intervention: PUFA is the substance that makes every estrogenic insult worse, every clearance mechanism weaker, and every protective hormone less effective. The environmental dimension of estrogenic chemical exposure, from plastics to pesticides to pharmaceutical residues, is covered in detail in [[xenoestrogens]].

## The Estrogen Industry

Estrogen was identified as a carcinogen over a hundred years ago. In a [[2017-06-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-language-and-criticism-estrogen-part-1|2017 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on estrogen]], Peat placed the timeline: "Estrogen was clearly identified as a cause of cancer over 100 years ago, around 1905." By the 1930s, the chemical identity of estrogen was established, and "a series of papers came out warning against the use of estrogen for treatment because the chemical work made clear that the soot carcinogenesis known from the 18th century was essentially estrogenic, creating inflammation, fibrosis, and cancer."

Also in the 1930s, progesterone was identified as the protective agent against estrogen's carcinogenic effects. For twenty years, from 1935 to 1955, research on progesterone's protective role continued and accumulated. Then the pharmaceutical industry intervened. As Peat described, "The drug companies intervened to create their own storms. First, they claimed estrogen was a 'female hormone' that would support pregnancy and retard aging." This was a marketing invention, not a scientific conclusion.

The corruption deepened when the industry realized that progesterone, the actual protective hormone, was expensive to manufacture and could not be patented. Each company developed synthetic chemicals with some of progesterone's actions but critical differences. Progesterone was named for its ability to sustain gestation (pro-gestational), but the synthetics were given the same name even though they blocked or destroyed gestation. As Peat explained in [[estrogen-progesterone-and-cancer-conflicts-of-interest|"Estrogen, Progesterone, and Cancer: Conflicts of Interest"]], "The corruption of the term 'progestin' or 'progestogen' by the industry and the drug regulators has been terribly consequential. The synthetic chemicals classified as progestins often have anti-progesterone actions, and shouldn't be called progestins at all, because they don't support gestation." It is precisely their anti-progesterone action that led to their use as contraceptives, yet the FDA required that natural progesterone be labeled with the warnings applicable to these toxic synthetics.

This linguistic corruption allowed the estrogen industry to run a remarkably effective shell game for sixty years. When studies showed that hormone replacement therapy caused cancer, the industry could blame the "progestins" (actually anti-progestins) and claim that estrogen alone was safe. When studies showed that estrogen alone caused strokes and heart disease, the industry could blame the type of estrogen and promote "bioidentical" alternatives. "The estrogen industry responds to the evidence that estrogen causes breast cancer, strokes, heart attacks, blood clots and Alzheimer's disease by saying 'it's progesterone that is responsible,'" Peat wrote, describing a pattern of blame-shifting that relied on decades of deliberate terminological confusion.

DES (diethylstilbestrol) became the most notorious example of estrogen's documented harm. Studies in humans revealed that its use during pregnancy caused cancer, miscarriages, and blood clots in the women who took it, and also caused cancer, infertility, and deformities in their children and even in their grandchildren. The transgenerational damage from DES demonstrated that estrogen's effects are not limited to the person exposed but extend to the developing organisms whose tissues are permanently altered by prenatal exposure.

The Women's Health Initiative in 2002 provided large-scale confirmation of what Peat and others had been arguing for decades: estrogen replacement therapy causes strokes, heart attacks, dementia, and cancer. Consumption of menopausal hormones declined sharply afterward, but the industry adapted rather than retreated, promoting lower doses, different delivery methods, and the "bioidentical" distinction that Peat considered meaningless at the mechanistic level.

## Estrogen and Aging

Peat claimed priority for the hypothesis that aging itself involves increasing estrogen dominance. "As far as I know, I was the first one to suggest that aging itself involves increased estrogen dominance," he wrote in [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone" (2006)]]. The idea emerged from his doctoral research in the late 1960s and early 1970s, when he studied oxidative changes in the uterus during aging. As he described in the [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 *EastWest Healing* interview]], "My thesis advisor had demonstrated that estrogen causes miscarriage. My project was studying the oxidative changes in the uterus during aging. I found that the changes in the uterus with aging were the same as those produced by estrogen treatment, a deficiency of Vitamin E, and radiation treatment."

This convergence between aging, estrogen exposure, [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]] deficiency, and radiation damage became the foundation of Peat's entire biological framework. In his earliest newsletter, [[energy-entropy-and-estrogens-in-aging|"Energy, Entropy and Estrogens in Aging" (1985)]], he wrote: "A variety of biophysical methods (NMR and ESR of living tissue, whole-cell electrophoresis, the influence of electromagnetic fields on nerve cell latent periods, etc.) led me to interpret the biochemical changes occurring in various tissues in aging, hypoxia, and estrogen dominance as a coherent response of the cells to disorder resulting from an excess expenditure of energy."

The parallels between aging and estrogen dominance are extensive. Both involve fibrotic changes in connective tissue, with [[collagen]] accumulating at the expense of functional cells. Both involve the accelerated deposition of age pigment ([[lipofuscin|age pigment]]). Both involve the progressive loss of cellular magnesium and the retention of calcium, a shift that impairs every energy-dependent cellular function. Both involve a decline in sodium-potassium ATPase activity, with proteolysis increasingly dominating relative to protein synthesis.

On the systemic level, Peat cataloged the estrogenic features of aging: "a tendency toward prolactinoma, toxic effects of [[prolactin]] on kidneys and other organs affecting water balance, disturbance of thyroid function, adrenal compensation or exhaustion, atrophy of muscle, skin, and other tissues." Prostate hypertrophy, long attributed to testosterone, "now appears to involve estrogen dominance, and to be reversible with progesterone treatment." The same pattern appears across species: "The same pattern of estrogen dominance is seen in both sexes with aging, and in all species studied."

The behavioral correlates of estrogen dominance further support the connection to aging. Estrogen's effects "resemble those of aging" in domains beyond the purely biochemical: "social subordination in animals often involves high estrogen and reduced fertility." The constellation of high estrogen, low progesterone, high cortisol, high [[prolactin]], and suppressed thyroid function characterizes not only biological aging but also the physiological profile of social defeat, learned helplessness, and depression. In Peat's framework, these are not separate conditions with separate causes but variations on a single theme: the dominance of stress physiology over the protective, energy-producing systems that sustain health.

Estrogen in his email exchanges was described as "a stress and crisis hormone, so there's a steady upward tendency in its effect with age, which is interrupted during the fertile years by progesterone." During the reproductive years, women's cyclic production of large amounts of progesterone "probably retards their aging enough to account for their greater longevity." The female advantage in lifespan is not a gift of estrogen but a gift of progesterone's periodic suppression of estrogen.

## Estrogen's Systemic Effects

Beyond the mechanisms covered in the spoke articles on [[aromatase]], [[prolactin]], and [[xenoestrogens]], estrogen produces a constellation of tissue-specific effects that Peat documented across decades of writing.

Estrogen is cardiotoxic, causing increased calcium deposition in cardiac tissue, increased hypoxia, and increased blood clotting. It promotes the production of prolactin (see [[prolactin]] for the full mechanism), and through prolactin, contributes to osteoporosis. This contradiction between estrogen's marketed role as a bone-protector and its actual role as a promoter of bone-dissolving prolactin was one of Peat's most pointed critiques of pharmaceutical medicine: "It has been known for many years that prolactin is a major factor in bone loss. Estrogen increases prolactin, yet people are told that estrogen prevents osteoporosis."

Estrogen causes water retention as its first action on the cell, a direct consequence of the energy failure it produces. The excitation it imposes moves the cell's energy requirements to a level that cannot be sustained, creating an oxygen deficit and forcing water into the cell. This cellular edema scales up to clinical edema, contributing to the bloating, excessive bleeding, and general misery of premenstrual syndrome. Excess estrogen and decreased thyroid function together cause PMS.

Estrogen directly lowers body temperature while progesterone raises it. This is not merely a thermostat adjustment; estrogen "sets the brain's temperature regulator lower, but, acting through serotonin and other mediators, it can actually lower the metabolic rate, too." The basal temperature measurement that Broda Barnes popularized as a thyroid indicator is, in this sense, also an estrogen indicator: a low morning temperature may reflect hypothyroidism, estrogen dominance, or both (since they are mechanistically intertwined).

Estrogen causes relative hyperventilation, reinforcing the cellular changes it produces. By lowering carbon dioxide through excessive breathing, it compounds the intracellular carbon dioxide deficit that estrogen's respiratory interference creates directly. Estrogen shifts cells from oxidative production of carbon dioxide to the glycolytic formation of lactic acid, and the leakage of the lactic acid-forming enzyme LDH into the serum, along with the increase of copper in the serum, are measurable markers of estrogen's metabolic disruption.

In bone marrow, where oxygen tension is normally low and cell proliferation is already stimulated, estrogen's oxygen-wasting effect changes the balance of cell growth by stimulating fibroblasts and collagen production, displacing the red and white blood cells. This mechanism connects estrogen to anemia and immune suppression through a pathway that has nothing to do with the bone marrow's reproductive significance and everything to do with estrogen's universal action on oxidative metabolism.

Estrogen causes aging of skin, making it thinner through atrophy that extends to pigment cells, decreasing cell size and eliminating dendritic branches. This is the opposite of what cosmetic marketing claims. Cortisol produces the same skin-thinning effect, which is consistent with Peat's framing of both substances as stress hormones that sacrifice tissue integrity to mobilize resources for short-term survival.

## Protection: The Progesterone Axis

The body's primary defense against estrogen is [[progesterone]], and the mechanism of that defense is more absolute than a simple hormonal counterbalance. Peat described it in the [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 *EastWest Healing* interview]]:

> Stress activates the estrogen receptor. Once estrogen is acting, that turns on the cell's production of the progesterone receptor. When progesterone binds to that receptor, it destroys the estrogen receptor. Progesterone is an anti-estrogen in a very absolute sense. It knocks out the protein that keeps estrogen in the cell and activates enzymes that eliminate estrogen.

This is not a competitive inhibition where progesterone merely blocks estrogen from binding. Progesterone causes the physical destruction of the estrogen receptor protein. It also activates the enzymes that detoxify estrogen (sulfotransferases and glucuronosyltransferases) and inhibits the enzymes that reactivate it (beta-glucuronidase and sulfatase). Through its antagonism to cortisol, progesterone further reduces estrogen by lowering the cortisol-driven activation of aromatase. The net effect is that adequate progesterone removes estrogen from cells, prevents its local production, and accelerates its systemic elimination.

Progesterone is not the only protective substance. [[Thyroid]] hormone supports the liver's conjugation of estrogen and opposes estrogen's metabolic effects at every level. [[Aspirin]] inhibits aromatase (reducing estrogen production), blocks prostaglandins (reducing the pro-estrogenic effects of PUFA), and activates mitochondrial respiration in direct opposition to estrogen's glycolytic shift. Vitamin E delays age-related fertility decline by opposing the same oxidative changes that estrogen produces. High altitude reduces aromatase activity, which may contribute to the longevity advantages observed in high-altitude populations.

The raw carrot, as discussed above, breaks the intestinal recirculation of estrogen. Orange juice contains naringenin, a natural aromatase inhibitor. These are not minor lifestyle adjustments in Peat's system; they are mechanistically targeted interventions that address specific nodes in the estrogen-accumulation cascade.

Peat's practical framework for reducing estrogen burden can be summarized as a multi-layered strategy: support thyroid function (which enables the liver to clear estrogen); consume adequate protein and B vitamins (which the liver requires for conjugation); avoid PUFA (which amplifies estrogen at every level); eat a daily raw carrot (which prevents intestinal reabsorption); and, when needed, use progesterone, aspirin, and thyroid supplementation to oppose estrogen's effects directly. "Progesterone won't turn into estrogen," he confirmed in his email exchanges, "but along with thyroid and aspirin it will tend to reduce the amount of estrogen in the body."

## The Estrogen Receptor Problem

Peat's framework challenges the orthodox model of estrogen action, which centers on the estrogen receptor as the mediator of all estrogenic effects. The receptor model cannot explain estrogen's most dramatic and immediate actions. As Peat wrote in [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women|"Estrogen and Brain Aging in Men and Women"]], the respiratory interference principle "is an integrating principle that explains estrogen's immediate, direct effects on cells and organisms, which aren't explained by the idea that it acts on the genes through a specific 'estrogen receptor.' (It's hard to imagine, for example, how the 'estrogen receptor' doctrine could explain the fact that a single injection of estrogen can kill a large portion of brain cells.)"

The receptor model is a genomic model: estrogen binds to the receptor, the complex travels to the nucleus, and gene transcription changes. This process takes hours. But estrogen produces measurable metabolic changes within seconds, including water uptake, calcium influx, and shifts in mitochondrial respiration, none of which can be explained by genomic mechanisms. The receptor is real, and it matters, but it is downstream of estrogen's primary action, which is physical and bioenergetic rather than informational and genetic.

This distinction matters practically because it explains why substances that do not bind the estrogen receptor can still produce estrogenic effects. Suffocation does not bind the estrogen receptor. Radiation does not bind the estrogen receptor. Yet both produce the full spectrum of estrogenic changes, because they share estrogen's primary action: interference with oxidative energy production. The receptor is activated by the metabolic stress, not the other way around. Stress activates the receptor, and progesterone destroys it.

## In PCOS, endometriosis, and fibroids — the local-aromatase reading

Gynecology can do as much surgery as it does for [[endometriosis]], [[fibroids]], and the cysts and androgens of [[pcos|PCOS]] and still see the conditions recur because the lesion is not a passive recipient of circulating estrogen. The lesion makes its own. The Bulun group at Northwestern in the 1990s established that endometriotic implants and uterine leiomyomata express [[aromatase|aromatase (CYP19)]] at concentrations well above adjacent normal tissue — converging with the conclusion Peat had been arguing since his doctoral work: estrogen lives in the tissues, not in the blood draw. Standard serum estradiol can read normal, even low, while the lesion bathes itself in concentrations many-fold higher, locked into a positive-feedback loop the systemic endocrine system no longer regulates.

The mechanism is developed at [[aromatase]] and [[endometriosis]] in detail. Briefly: lesion aromatase is upregulated by [[prostaglandins]] (especially PGE2), [[cortisol]], and the cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α from chronically inflamed connective tissue; the lesion stores [[PUFA]] and turns it via cyclooxygenase into prostaglandins; prostaglandins drive more aromatase; aromatase makes more local estradiol; estradiol increases cyclooxygenase expression. The loop closes on itself. A laparoscopic ablation leaves the metabolic conditions in place; a Lupron course suppresses ovarian output but leaves local synthesis untouched; an oral contraceptive adds exogenous estrogen to a tissue already amplifying its own. Recurrence is the predicted, not the unexpected, outcome.

[[PCOS]] sits one organ over in the same picture. Peat read it as the downstream picture of [[hypothyroidism]], gut [[endotoxin]], and chronic estrogen excess starving the corpus luteum of progesterone — a stalled ovulation, not a primary ovarian disease. "PCOS can be produced in animals by removing the thyroid gland," he wrote in his email exchanges. "The inability of ovaries to make progesterone without thyroid causes the adrenals to be overstimulated, and they are the source of increased DHEA and other androgens and estrogen." The local-aromatase reading applies: adipose tissue in insulin-resistant PCOS is one of the most aromatase-active compartments in the body, making its own estrogen out of the very androgens the hyperandrogenism diagnosis rests on.

[[Fibroids]] are the same disease read at the myometrium. The 2001 [[fibrosis|*Fibrosis*]] newsletter makes the cross-tissue link explicit: "Retroperitoneal fibrosis, which can seriously damage the kidneys and other abdominal organs, has been compared to uterine fibroid tumors, and it is commonly treated successfully with the antiestrogenic drug, tamoxifen." A fibrotic lesion outside the uterus and a uterine fibroid both respond to the same antiestrogen because they run the same metabolic state — chronic local estrogen synthesis on a hypothyroid, PUFA-loaded substrate.

The protocol consequence: ovarian suppression, lesion ablation, and hysterectomy will not resolve any of these three conditions in their general form; only correcting the metabolic substrate does. Peat framed it the same way across the three: opposed [[progesterone]] cyclically replacing what the corpus luteum is failing to produce; [[thyroid]] sufficient to restore the liver's clearance capacity and drop the local aromatase threshold; [[aspirin]] to inhibit aromatase and block the prostaglandin amplifier; [[PUFA]] depletion to drain the cyclooxygenase substrate; daily raw carrot to break the intestinal recirculation loop above. The condition pages — [[pcos]], [[endometriosis]], [[fibroids]] — develop the tissue-specific dosing in detail.

## Xenoestrogen sources — concrete inventory

The mechanistic argument is developed at [[xenoestrogens]]; what follows is the source-by-source inventory — read it as a household audit, not a catastrophe list. A few categories carry most of the dose.

### Food

- **Soy in all forms.** Soy protein isolate (sneaks into protein bars, meal-replacement shakes, "plant-based" meat substitutes, infant formula), soy lecithin (chocolate, baked goods, salad dressings), tofu, tempeh, edamame, soy milk, soy sauce except small amounts of long-fermented traditional varieties, textured vegetable protein. Multiple-pathway: isoflavones (genistein, daidzein) bind estrogen receptors; soy oil is an estrogenic PUFA; the antithyroid action raises endogenous estrogen indirectly.
- **Legumes more broadly.** Clover-grade phytoestrogens are present in all beans, lentils, and peas. Not equivalent to soy on a per-gram basis, but cumulative.
- **Flax and flaxseed oil.** The single highest source of lignans in the Western diet. Often marketed as a hormone balancer; in Peat's reading the lignans act as weak estrogens themselves.
- **Hops and beer.** Hops contain 8-prenylnaringenin, among the most potent phytoestrogens characterized. Brewer's yeast also produces actual estradiol. Beer is the highest dietary estrogen exposure most Western men accumulate.
- **Conventional dairy from late-pregnancy cows.** Estrogen levels in commercial milk track the pregnancy stage of the cow. Higher estrogen content in milk from cows in mid-to-late pregnancy than in milk from non-pregnant cows.
- **Conventional meat from animals fed soy or treated with growth implants.** Estradiol implants are still permitted in U.S. cattle production. Soy in feed concentrates estrogenic compounds up the food chain.
- **Large, long-lived fish.** Orange roughy, halibut, swordfish, tuna. Bioconcentration of estrogenic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and microplastic-bound contaminants up the food chain. Peat stopped eating the largest fish for this reason.
- **Polyphenolic antioxidant supplements at concentrated doses.** Green tea extract, resveratrol, quercetin capsules, grape-seed extract. Often estrogenic at supplement doses despite being marketed as protective.

### Plastics and packaging

- **[[BPA|Bisphenol A]] in polycarbonate plastic and epoxy can-linings.** Hardest, clearest plastics; the inside coating of nearly every canned food and most aluminum beverage cans.
- **[[BPS|Bisphenol S]] and bisphenol F.** The "BPA-free" replacement chemicals, equally or more estrogenic in vitro than BPA itself.
- **[[phthalate|Phthalates]] (DEHP, DBP, DEP).** The plasticizers that make rigid PVC flexible. Concentrated in cling film, soft food containers, vinyl shower curtains, food-service gloves, the soft plastic of squeeze bottles, IV tubing and medical bags, garden hoses.
- **Thermal receipt paper.** Cash-register receipts are coated with free (unpolymerized) BPA or BPS as the heat-developable layer. Absorbed transdermally through the fingertips within seconds of handling. Worse with hand sanitizer, lotion, or wet hands.
- **Polystyrene takeaway containers, coffee-cup lids, and disposable cutlery.** Leaches styrene and oligomers, particularly when hot food or drink is in contact.
- **Microwaved plastic, frozen-then-thawed plastic, scratched plastic.** Heating, freezing, and abrasion all accelerate leaching. The 2017 episode noted that cold or frozen plastic water bottles "become brittle and leach much more plastic compounds into the water" than room-temperature bottles.

### Personal care

- **Parabens** (methylparaben, propylparaben, butylparaben). Preservatives in nearly every conventional shampoo, conditioner, body lotion, deodorant, face cream, and cosmetic. Detected intact in breast tumor tissue.
- **Oxybenzone (benzophenone-3) and other PABA-derived chemical sunscreens.** Penetrate the skin readily; estrogenic in vitro and in animal studies. Peat called out PABA derivatives by name in [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|*Using Sunlight to Sustain Life*]]: "other chemicals used in the sun screen lotions, such as PABA derivatives, also react dangerously with light, and are easily absorbed in significant quantities into the deeper layers of the skin, where they can cause mutations." His recommended alternative was opaque zinc oxide or titanium oxide, which stay on the surface.
- **Alkylphenols and nonylphenol ethoxylates** in surfactants, foaming agents, and cleaning products. Research Peat cited in [[radiation-and-growth|*Radiation and Growth*]] documented that prenatal or neonatal exposure to extremely small amounts of nonylphenol and bisphenol A greatly increases adult sensitivity to estrogen.
- **Synthetic fragrance** in perfumes, colognes, body sprays, scented lotions. The "fragrance" designation on a label legally covers thousands of undisclosed compounds, many of them phthalate-class plasticizers used as fixatives.
- **Conventional nail polish.** Dibutyl phthalate (DBP) was the original plasticizer; phthalate-free formulations exist but the category remains exposure-prone.
- **Hair dye and chemical relaxers.** Multiple estrogenic and endocrine-disrupting compounds; epidemiologic association with breast cancer is most consistent in the heaviest-exposure salon workers.

### Cleaning and laundry

- **Fabric softeners and dryer sheets.** Quaternary ammonium compounds, synthetic fragrance, and benzyl acetate. The estrogenic load comes mostly through skin contact with treated fabric (sheets, underwear, towels) plus inhalation of vented dryer exhaust.
- **Scented laundry detergents.** Same fragrance category as personal-care products; clothing is then worn against skin for hours.
- **Air fresheners, scented candles, plug-ins.** Phthalate-based fragrance dispersion straight into the air column of the room.
- **Antibacterial soaps with triclosan or triclocarban.** Endocrine-disrupting, weak-estrogenic, and largely banned from over-the-counter soaps in the U.S. as of 2017 but still present in legacy product and many industrial settings.

### Cookware and food contact

- **Non-stick coatings** (PTFE/Teflon, ceramic-marketed coatings that often contain PFAS). The PFAS chemicals associated with non-stick manufacture are not estrogenic per se but are potent endocrine disruptors and immunotoxins, and they degrade thyroid function, which loops back to estrogen excess through impaired hepatic clearance.
- **Aluminum cookware with damaged or no anodization, especially with acidic food.** Aluminum has its own neurotoxicity profile; mentioned here for completeness.
- **Plastic spatulas, plastic ladles, plastic mixing bowls** used with hot food. Same plasticizer leaching as food containers, accelerated by heat.
- **Plastic kettles and plastic coffee makers.** Hot water through plastic for ten minutes at a time, every day. The internal reservoir and the tubing carrying heated water are the highest-leach surfaces in most kitchens.
- **Single-use coffee pods.** Hot water through plastic-and-aluminum capsules under pressure.

### Water

- **Municipal tap water carrying pharmaceutical estrogen residue.** Synthetic estradiol and ethinyl estradiol from oral contraceptives are excreted in urine, pass through sewage treatment unchanged, and enter the rivers and reservoirs that feed municipal supply. Asked in [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|the 2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] whether any filter could remove these compounds at the treatment-plant level, Peat answered: "No, nothing practical that's in use." Point-of-use carbon block filtration is the practical residential intervention.
- **Agricultural runoff** (atrazine, glyphosate, organophosphates, chlorinated hydrocarbons). Atrazine is documented to feminize male frogs at parts-per-billion concentrations. Concentrated downstream of corn-belt agriculture.
- **Plastic-bottled water.** All the plasticizer leaching above, applied to drinking water specifically. Worse for bottles that have been heat-cycled in cars or warehouses.

## Clean-up checklist — what to swap this week, this month, this year

The protocol-grade version is at [[estrogen-detox]]; what follows is the prioritized, do-it-tomorrow sequence. Most of the dose comes from a few exposures with the longest daily contact time — remove those first.

### This week (zero-cost or near-zero-cost)

- **Stop heating food in plastic.** No microwaving plastic containers. Transfer leftovers to a ceramic plate or glass bowl before reheating.
- **Stop drinking hot liquid out of plastic.** This is the biggest single change. Replace a plastic kettle with a stainless or glass kettle; replace a drip coffee maker with a French press or stainless moka pot or a Chemex with a metal filter holder; carry a stainless or glass travel mug instead of using paper-and-wax coffee cups (the lid is the worst part).
- **Throw away the cling film.** Switch to wax paper, parchment paper, beeswax wraps, or a glass container with a lid.
- **Decline thermal receipts at the register.** Or take them with a tissue, fold them inside out, put them straight into a wallet. Wash hands before eating after handling any receipts.
- **Switch to a fragrance-free, paraben-free body lotion, shampoo, conditioner, deodorant.** This is the most concentrated daily skin contact most people have. Brands change; the labels to read are: no parabens, no phthalates, no "fragrance" (look for "fragrance-free" rather than "unscented"), no triclosan.
- **Stop using fabric softener and dryer sheets.** Wool dryer balls work for static. Vinegar in the rinse cycle softens cotton.
- **Stop drinking out of soft plastic water bottles.** Glass or stainless steel for water. The Nalgene-class hard plastics are second-best at room temperature only.
- **Eliminate soy lecithin, soy protein isolate, and soy oil from the pantry.** Read the back of every chocolate bar, salad dressing, and protein bar in the cupboard.
- **Pour beer down the drain.** This is the highest single dietary estrogen exposure for most Western men. Replace with wine in small amounts or, ideally, with spirits and citrus or with carbonated mineral water.

### This month (small purchases)

- **Replace the worst non-stick pan with cast iron or stainless steel.** Start with whatever pan you use daily. Cast iron for searing and eggs; stainless for everything else; enameled cast iron for braising.
- **Replace plastic spatulas and ladles with wood or stainless.** They are cheap. The plastic ones go in hot food daily.
- **Install a carbon-block water filter on the kitchen tap or under the sink.** Brita-class pitcher filters are the minimum; a Berkey, an AquaTru reverse-osmosis countertop, or a plumbed-in carbon block is much better. Either way: filter the water you drink and the water you cook with.
- **Switch sunscreens to zinc oxide.** Specifically non-nano zinc oxide if available. Avoid oxybenzone, octinoxate, octocrylene, homosalate, avobenzone. The non-chemical "mineral" sunscreens are the right answer.
- **Audit cleaning products.** Replace scented all-purpose cleaner with vinegar-and-water or a fragrance-free brand. Replace scented dish soap with a fragrance-free one.
- **Remove plug-in air fresheners and scented candles.** Open a window. Soy-free beeswax candles are an alternative if you want flame.
- **Switch from canned tomatoes to glass-jarred tomatoes.** Tomatoes are acidic enough to leach the can lining faster than other canned foods. Glass-jarred passata or fresh tomatoes solve it.

### This year (larger swaps and habits)

- **Replace plastic food storage containers with glass.** Pyrex with snap lids, weck jars, mason jars. Plastic lids are tolerable because they are not in contact with hot food; plastic walls are not.
- **Buy a stainless or glass kettle and a stainless French press if you haven't already.** The kitchen-electric-with-plastic-water-reservoir category is the slowest to replace and the most consequential.
- **Source meat and dairy from local, grass-finished sources.** Estradiol implants are not used on cattle finished on pasture, and feed-grade soy concentration drops substantially.
- **Replace conventional cosmetics with cleaner alternatives over time.** This is not a one-shot purchase; replace each product as it runs out. Foundation, mascara, lipstick are the highest daily skin contact.
- **Reconsider hair dye and chemical hair treatments.** If hair color is non-negotiable, henna and the plant-based dyes are the cleaner option. Salon visits with semi-annual rather than monthly cadence.
- **Reduce or eliminate scented personal care entirely.** Cologne, perfume, scented body wash, scented hand soap. The "fragrance" loophole is the largest single uncharacterized exposure in the personal-care category.
- **Plant intent around long-lived fish.** Smaller, shorter-lived fish (sardines, sole, shellfish) are below the bioconcentration threshold; the largest predatory fish are above it.

The phasing matters because the household audit can otherwise feel infinite. The clean-water filter, the non-plastic kettle, the fragrance-free personal care, and the dietary soy eliminations together cut most of the daily dose. The rest is incremental — items replaced as they wear out.

## Cross-link the symptom map — when to suspect estrogen versus another hormone

Serum estradiol is a poor proxy for tissue estrogen (see the tissue-trap section above), so the practical clinical question is whether the symptom pattern matches estrogen excess. Peat described a recognizable cluster; what follows differentiates it from the four hormones it most often overlaps with.

**Suspect estrogen excess when the pattern includes:** cyclical or constant water retention, swollen ankles in the afternoon, premenstrual breast tenderness, fibrocystic breast tissue; heavy or clotting periods, mid-cycle spotting, perimenopausal flooding; uterine [[fibroids]], [[endometriosis]] pain, ovarian cysts ([[pcos]]); cycle-clustered migraine; persistent low body temperature combined with the above; varicose and spider veins; gynecomastia or soft, watery muscle tone in men; loss of morning erections, gynoid (hip-and-thigh) fat patterning in men; emotional fragility that lifts in the second half of the luteal phase when progesterone is briefly higher.

**Versus low thyroid:** thyroid emphasizes cold extremities, dry skin, constipation, diffuse hair loss, trunk weight gain, slow pulse and reflexes, deep fatigue *without* water retention. The two coexist and reinforce each other (low thyroid impairs estrogen clearance — see [[hypothyroidism]]); the differentiator is whether bloating, edema, and breast tenderness are present. They are estrogen signs, not thyroid signs.

**Versus high cortisol:** cortisol emphasizes central abdominal gain, 2–3 a.m. insomnia, thin skin with easy bruising, high fasting glucose, limb muscle wasting with trunk/face fat, hypertension. Cortisol activates aromatase and the two reinforce, but the picture differs — cortisol is breakdown, estrogen is swelling and leakage.

**Versus low progesterone:** practically the same condition viewed from the opposite side. The Peat-style framing reads PMS, perimenopausal complaints, and luteal-phase symptoms as low progesterone first and high estrogen second, because progesterone destroys the estrogen receptor and clears tissue estrogen. The dosing differentiator: supplemental [[progesterone]] resolves the picture; antiestrogens like [[aspirin]] and naringenin help; tamoxifen helps in fibroid and breast-fibrocystic states. See [[pms]] and [[menopause]].

**Versus high prolactin:** prolactin emphasizes galactorrhea (milk discharge in non-lactating women, occasionally in men), bone loss, libido collapse, infertility with regular cycles, and visual-field changes if a pituitary adenoma is the source. Prolactin is downstream of estrogen (see [[prolactin]]), so the two coexist, but galactorrhea and visual-field changes narrow it.

**Versus low DHEA / low pregnenolone:** the adrenal-precursor pattern is fatigue, loss of mental drive, loss of stress resilience — a "running on fumes" picture without the swelling, bleeding, and reproductive specifics of estrogen excess. The two are linked because adrenal depletion forces more estrogen out of less androgen, but the symptom maps separate.

The clinical use of the map is that correction can begin before the labs catch up. Serum estradiol lags tissue estradiol by weeks; the symptom cluster moves first.
