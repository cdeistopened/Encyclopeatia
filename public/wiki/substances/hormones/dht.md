# DHT (Dihydrotestosterone)

DHT, the 5α-reduced metabolite of [[testosterone]], occupies a peculiar position in mainstream endocrinology: it is the androgen blamed for almost everything bad that happens to an aging man — prostate enlargement, prostate cancer, baldness, acne, aggression — and the receptor target of finasteride, dutasteride, and the herbal saw-palmetto industry built around them. Peat's reading inverts the framing at the root. DHT is one of the body's *stabilizing* steroids, more closely related to [[progesterone]] and the [[allopregnanolone|GABA-active neurosteroids]] than to the stress hormones it is mistaken for. The 5α-reductase enzyme that produces it is not a vestigial pathway for making mischief; it is the gateway to a whole class of morphogen-stabilizing molecules that hold the organism in its shape and protect the brain. The diseases blamed on DHT are diseases of the conditions that DHT is *defending against*. Finasteride, the orthodox intervention, is — in Peat's bluntest published phrasing — "an insane decision."

Four claims structure the inversion: (1) DHT **cannot be aromatized into estrogen**, which makes it the safe androgen in a body whose [[aromatase]] is already converting [[testosterone]] into the stress hormone it was supposed to oppose; (2) 5α-reductase is a **morphogen-stabilizing** enzyme — Dinkov's snail experiment shows 5α-reduced steroids hold biological form, not merely produce "manliness"; (3) the same enzyme producing DHT also produces [[allopregnanolone]] from [[progesterone]] and 5α-THDOC from deoxycorticosterone — two of the most powerful endogenous GABA agonists known — so blocking it starves the brain of allopregnanolone, which is why post-finasteride syndrome is a *neurosteroid* injury; (4) pattern baldness and BPH are driven not by DHT but by the **stress-prolactin-estrogen-PTH axis** the [[hair-loss]] and [[prostate-cancer|prostate cancer]] articles document. Elevated DHT in a balding scalp or hyperplastic prostate is *compensation* — the bodyguard arriving too late.

For androgen-synthesis basics and the aging-as-feminization framework, see [[testosterone]] and [[estrogen]]. The unique signal of this page is DHT itself: what 5α-reduction does, why the orthodox villain narrative is the wrong end of the stick, and why 5α-reductase inhibitors are the most dangerous prescriptions in modern endocrinology.

## Biochemistry: 5α-Reductase and the Stabilizing-Steroid Family

DHT is the product of a single enzymatic step. 5α-reductase reduces the Δ4 double bond on the A-ring of testosterone, producing 5α-dihydrotestosterone, consuming NADPH. Two main isoforms operate in mammals: Type 1 (skin, sebaceous glands, scalp, liver) and Type 2 (prostate, genital skin, hair follicle, brain). Finasteride selectively inhibits Type 2; dutasteride blocks both.

What matters for Peat's framework is the *substrate promiscuity* of the enzyme. 5α-reductase metabolizes essentially every Δ4 steroid: [[progesterone]] becomes 5α-dihydroprogesterone (and from there, allopregnanolone), deoxycorticosterone becomes 5α-tetrahydrodeoxycorticosterone (5α-THDOC), cortisol becomes 5α-dihydrocortisol. The 5α-reduced steroids share a common feature: they are GABA-active, anti-excitatory, neurosteroid-class molecules. This is the family DHT belongs to.

Peat in the 2022 [[#27： Thyroid Function & Pulse Rate ｜ Weaponized Culture ｜ Finasteride as an ＂Insane Decision＂|*Generative Energy* #27]]: "Progesterone, dihydrotestosterone (DHT), and all the stabilizing steroids are essential to holding our organism in its shape and determining its future shape — the way it coils and uncoils." DHT is in the *progesterone family*, not the testosterone-as-stress-hormone family bodybuilding culture has assembled around it. Its closest endocrine relatives are allopregnanolone and 5α-THDOC, not nandrolone and trenbolone.

Georgi Dinkov's snail observation in the same episode is the cleanest demonstration that 5α-reductase is involved in biological form itself: "If you give snails a 5-alpha reductase inhibitor (like finasteride), they no longer coil. It completely disrupts their morphogenetic field." A snail's helical shell is built by the same enzymatic chemistry the brain uses to make neurosteroids and the prostate uses to make DHT. The morphogen-stabilizing function is conserved across phyla.

For Peat, the upstream substrate hierarchy was the practical implication. From the [[#51： Heat Shock Proteins ｜ Antibiotic Resistance ｜ DHT Safety ｜ Energy and Aging with Ray Peat, PhD|*Generative Energy* #51]]: "Correcting thyroid, vitamin D, DHEA, and pregnenolone will usually bring your DHT up to where it should be." In most cases of apparent androgen deficiency, the missing piece is upstream — at [[pregnenolone]], [[thyroid]], or [[dhea|DHEA]] — not at testosterone or DHT themselves.

## The Non-Aromatizable Androgen

The defining biochemical fact about DHT, and the basis for almost everything Peat said about its clinical use, is that DHT cannot be converted to [[estrogen]]. From the [[dht|DHT email]]: "The special difference between testosterone and DHT is that testosterone is easily aromatized into estrogen, and DHT isn't. There are several ways that the body can dispose of estrogen, but I haven't heard of that way of inactivating it; I don't think it happens in the body."

This single asymmetry restructures the clinical picture. [[Testosterone]] is one enzymatic step from estrogen, and [[aromatase]] is *upregulated* by everything that defines aging in men: fat accumulation, hypothyroidism, cortisol elevation, [[pufa|PUFA]] saturation, inflammation, [[insulin]] resistance. A 60-year-old man whose serum testosterone has fallen is typically a man whose aromatase has risen, converting a larger fraction of available testosterone into estradiol. Standard TRT, by adding more substrate to the same hyperactive enzyme, often raises his estrogen more than his testosterone — which is why so many men "fail" TRT.

DHT bypasses the leak. The 5α-reduced molecule is enzymatically off the aromatase pathway. From the [[testosterone|testosterone email]] passage on a man with shrunken testes from exogenous androgens: "Testosterone and DHT aren't toxic, so the testes probably haven't been damaged... it would be good to have your LH and estrogen checked. **If the LH is very high, using a little DHT for a while might be protective until it's more normal.**" The recommendation inverts the bodybuilding-clinic instinct: bridge a stressed system on a small DHT dose precisely because DHT cannot fuel further estrogenization while the upstream cascade normalizes. The aromatase trap stays closed.

The clinical dose, when indicated, is small. From [[#51： Heat Shock Proteins ｜ Antibiotic Resistance ｜ DHT Safety ｜ Energy and Aging with Ray Peat, PhD|*Generative Energy* #51]]: "If you take 1 milligram of DHT backed by DHEA, progesterone, aspirin, and sugar, the DHT is more likely to have a constructive repairing role." The supporting cast — [[dhea|DHEA]], [[progesterone]], [[aspirin]], sugar — does the heavy lifting; the DHT is finishing the job.

## The Allopregnanolone Connection — Why Finasteride Hits Mood

The single most important fact about 5α-reductase that the dermatology textbook does not teach is that the enzyme producing DHT also produces [[allopregnanolone]]. Block it to reduce one and you reduce both.

Allopregnanolone is the 5α,3α-reduced metabolite of [[progesterone]] and one of the most potent endogenous positive allosteric modulators of the GABA-A receptor known. It is the chemistry behind progesterone's anti-anxiety, anti-seizure, sleep-promoting effects in both sexes — the same molecule behind brexanolone (Zulresso), the FDA-approved treatment for postpartum depression. Its sibling 5α-THDOC, made from deoxycorticosterone by the same enzyme, has similar GABA-positive activity and rises adaptively at altitude alongside [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]]. Peat in [[#27： Thyroid Function & Pulse Rate ｜ Weaponized Culture ｜ Finasteride as an ＂Insane Decision＂|*Generative Energy* #27]]: "When you adapt to altitude, you keep your carbon dioxide very high, which goes with allopregnanolone and progesterone metabolites being elevated."

The clinical implication is direct: a man who takes finasteride to block scalp DHT is also blocking the brain's most powerful endogenous tranquilizer. In [[Safe Supplements with Ray Peat [Generative Energy #31]|*Generative Energy* #31]], Peat was explicit. Asked about the FDA's old claim "that dihydrotestosterone (DHT) apparently wasn't used in the organism or had no function," he replied: "It works on the progesterone system too. They said progesterone is only a pregnancy-related chemical, neglecting that the brain absolutely depends on making it. It's a major brain hormone that you're messing with when you try to stop the production of DHT."

Same theme in [[#27： Thyroid Function & Pulse Rate ｜ Weaponized Culture ｜ Finasteride as an ＂Insane Decision＂|*Generative Energy* #27]]: "Brain chemistry depends on it. The metabolism of progesterone as well as testosterone in your brain — if you block any of the enzymes used in making it, you're going to have a weird brain."

This is the mechanistic key to post-finasteride syndrome — the cluster of persistent sexual, cognitive, emotional, and somatic dysfunction reported by patients after stopping the drug. The orthodoxy treated PFS as psychogenic or curious despite a growing literature documenting depressed CSF allopregnanolone, altered neurosteroid metabolism, and persistent symptoms long after the drug has cleared. In Peat's framework this is the predicted outcome of chronic blockade of a morphogen-stabilizing enzyme.

Dinkov added a less-appreciated angle in the same episode: "Apparently the 5-alpha reductase enzyme is important for the synthesis of bile acids. If you inhibit it, you won't produce bile acids crucial for digestion." Peat traced PFS to "a chronic malfunction of your liver and intestine." The enterohepatic dimension explains why PFS often presents with gut, lipid, and energy symptoms that look nothing like a hair-loss drug side effect.

Peat's recovery protocol for PFS is correspondingly radical, not pharmacological: "You have to back up and radically redo your life. Go to high altitude for six weeks, eat a totally different diet, make sure calcium intake is high compared to phosphate." Altitude raises CO2, which raises allopregnanolone; the calcium-to-phosphate fix lowers [[parathyroid-hormone|PTH]]. The point is to coax the morphogen system back into production from upstream.

## Sites of Action: Prostate, Hair Follicle, Brain, Skin

DHT's tissue-level effects depend on its concentration relative to other 5α-reduced steroids, the local activity of 5α-reductase, and the dominant context — which is to say, what else is happening in the tissue when DHT shows up.

**Prostate.** The textbook account: DHT drives BPH and prostate cancer; finasteride shrinks the prostate by depleting DHT; androgen deprivation is therefore rational cancer treatment. Peat's reading, developed in [[prostate-cancer|"Prostate Cancer"]]: "It has seemed odd to many people that enlargement of the prostate should occur mainly in older men, if testosterone is the hormone that causes its growth, and estrogen is antagonistic to its growth. The nature of the growth of the old man's prostate is very different from its natural growth in youth." The first (youth, testosterone-dominant) is healthy hypertrophy. The second (age, estrogen-dominant) is the pathological enlargement clinicians call BPH. The hormone rising in the second case is estrogen, not testosterone.

The survival data inverts the orthodoxy: "Prostate cancer patients who had higher levels of LH and lower testosterone died most quickly... patients with higher testosterone levels had a better prognosis than those with lower levels" (Wilson 1985, Harper 1984). The Umekita et al. (1996) study cited in [[prostate-cancer|"Prostate Cancer"]] is the smoking gun: in androgen-independent LNCaP-104R2 human prostate cancer cells implanted in nude mice, **testosterone propionate suppressed tumor growth and caused regression, while implantation of finasteride caused the tumors to regrow.** 5α-DHT itself inhibited the tumor; 5β-DHT did not. The original paper's conclusion, suppressed in modern urological practice: "the use of 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors, such as finasteride or anti-androgens, in the general treatment of metastatic prostate cancer may require careful assessment."

BPH in Peat's framework is an estrogen-prolactin disease. From [[origins-of-progesterone-therapy|*Origins of Progesterone Therapy*]]: "In cases of specific progesterone deficiency in men, small doses can cure impotence. It has been used effectively to treat benign prostatic enlargement." The treatment is not androgen blockade; it is [[progesterone]], [[thyroid]], [[pregnenolone]], the ratio-fix described under [[testosterone]].

**Hair follicle.** Covered in [[hair-loss]] and [[hair-loss-protocol]]. The summary: DHT correlates with scalp [[5-alpha-reductase|5α-reductase]] activity in pattern baldness, but in Peat's reading this is the bodyguard appearing late, not the cause. The drivers of follicular failure are [[prolactin]], [[cortisol]], [[estrogen]], and [[parathyroid-hormone]] elevation riding on suppressed [[thyroid]] function. From the 2011 [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|*Ask the Herb Doctor*]]: "Testosterone actually makes the hair shaft grow thicker and faster. Prolactin tends to cause it to fall out."

**Brain.** 5α-reductase operates locally in neurons and glia, generating allopregnanolone and 5α-THDOC from progesterone and deoxycorticosterone, and DHT from testosterone. Local concentrations can be far higher than serum suggests. Systemic blockade depletes neuronal allopregnanolone — the mechanism implicated in finasteride-associated depression, anxiety, anhedonia, and PFS brain fog. Dinkov in [[#51： Heat Shock Proteins ｜ Antibiotic Resistance ｜ DHT Safety ｜ Energy and Aging with Ray Peat, PhD|*Generative Energy* #51]]: "DHT... seems to have a neutralizing effect on the nervous system. Nothing terminates a severe stress reaction for me like a few milligrams of DHT." Peat located the effect in anti-inflammatory action with a caveat: "just because it makes you feel good doesn't mean you should get stuck on it without fixing the energy system that started the process."

**Skin.** DHT acts on sebaceous glands, beard, body hair, and genital skin (Type 1 dominant). In aging or hypothyroid men, beard and body hair often persist even as the scalp recedes — consistent with peripheral androgen-dependent skin retaining trophic support while the scalp is attacked by estrogen and prolactin.

## The Orthodox Villain Narrative

The DHT-as-villain framing did not emerge from balanced observation. From the [[dht|DHT email]]: "I think it was the drug industry, thinking of villains [DHT=BAD] to justify their otherwise crazy treatments." Three commercial logics converged. Finasteride (Merck's Proscar 1992 for BPH, Propecia 1997 for AGA) needed a target that sounded clinically responsible — DHT, measurable and correlated with the conditions of interest, fit the slot. Androgen deprivation therapy for prostate cancer extended a century of urological practice from John Hunter's 1786 castration observation through Huggins's 1941 Nobel-winning trials, without revisiting the premise. The herbal saw-palmetto industry needed a "natural DHT blocker" mechanism story, and the marketing premise — *less DHT is better DHT* — became consumer common sense. The cost of accepting the frame is twofold: it produces drugs that block a morphogen-stabilizing enzyme and reliably injure a subset of users in ways the labeling does not disclose, and it suppresses the androgen-restorative strategies that the survival data (Wilson, Harper, Rannikko, Umekita) would support.

## The Peat Reframe: DHT as Bodyguard

The bodyguard metaphor compresses the framework. DHT shows up in tissues under attack — the aging prostate, the receding hairline, the inflamed brain — because 5α-reductase is upregulated in stressed tissue as part of the local response. Elevated DHT signals that the tissue is mounting a defense against a more dangerous upstream signal: estrogen, prolactin, cortisol, inflammatory prostaglandins. Treating the bodyguard as the assailant — removing him because he is at the scene of the crime — leaves the tissue undefended.

The reframe explains the observations the orthodox model has trouble integrating: pattern baldness correlates with scalp DHT but also with [[prolactin]]/[[cortisol]] elevation, low [[thyroid]] function, [[parathyroid-hormone|PTH]] excess, heart disease, and low bone density; BPH and prostate cancer occur in aging men whose total androgen pool is falling while estrogen and prolactin rise; finasteride works short-term but produces persistent neuropsychiatric injury in a non-trivial fraction of users — exactly what blocking allopregnanolone production should predict; rare 5α-reductase Type 2 deficiency populations don't develop the conditions, but they also don't develop the systemic stress profile that drives them. The bodyguard reading does not deny every observation the orthodoxy claims; it reorders the causal chain.

## Practical Use

Peat's recommendations for DHT supplementation were narrow and conditional. First line for low androgens is not DHT; it is the upstream cascade — [[thyroid]], [[vitamin-d|vitamin D]], [[dhea|DHEA]], [[pregnenolone]] — which "will usually bring your DHT up to where it should be" ([[#51： Heat Shock Proteins ｜ Antibiotic Resistance ｜ DHT Safety ｜ Energy and Aging with Ray Peat, PhD|*Generative Energy* #51]]). Narrow indications for exogenous DHT include high-LH states, certain cancers ("In something like breast cancer or prostate cancer, it can give a big push to the system" — same episode), and severe androgen deficiency where the upstream cascade cannot be quickly restored.

When used, the dose is small: "One milligram daily dissolved in tocopherol or fat. But you have to have everything else in place first." The supporting matrix matters — "If you take 1 milligram of DHT backed by DHEA, progesterone, aspirin, and sugar, the DHT is more likely to have a constructive repairing role." The unmodified molecule in oil for topical or oral use was the form Peat was open to; methylated analogues like mesterolone (Proviron) were rejected categorically. From the [[dht|DHT email]]: "A little DHT should be safe, but I don't think mesterolone is safe in any quantity." Methyl groups added to steroids "send it off on unknown pathways. The liver never had to deal with such things" ([[#61： Bodybuilding and Steroids ｜ Progesterone for Men？ ｜ Basic Bioenergetic Therapies with Ray Peat|*Generative Energy* #61]]).

Daily endogenous production sets the dose: "4 milligrams of testosterone and 15 mg of DHEA is a normal daily production for young men, and half of that amount is effective for middle aged men" ([[dht|DHT email]]). The 50-100 mg testosterone shots typical of modern TRT clinics are 10-25× physiological. A 1 mg DHT dose is roughly physiological.

The categorical exclusion is 5α-reductase inhibitors — finasteride, dutasteride, and herbal analogues (saw palmetto, pygeum, β-sitosterol DHT-blockers). All derange the same morphogen-stabilizing system. From [[#27： Thyroid Function & Pulse Rate ｜ Weaponized Culture ｜ Finasteride as an ＂Insane Decision＂|*Generative Energy* #27]]: "First, no one *has* to take it. It's an irrational decision and probably means you're already abjectly obedient to authority."

## Counter-arguments

The DHT-as-villain orthodoxy has real empirical support, and a fair account of Peat's position has to engage it.

**5α-reductase Type 2 deficiency.** Men with congenital homozygous deficiency of Type 2 5α-reductase (the Imperato-McGinley "guevedoces" of the Dominican Republic) develop with ambiguous external genitalia, virilize partially at puberty, and **do not develop pattern baldness, prostate enlargement, or prostate cancer at typical rates**. This is the cleanest natural experiment available and is the orthodox model's strongest card. A Peat-consistent reading is that the entire steroid profile of these individuals — chronically high testosterone relative to DHT, altered allopregnanolone metabolism, altered tissue substrate ratios across the board — protects them from the *systemic* stress signature, not specifically from DHT-as-cause. But this reading is asserted, not empirically demonstrated, and is the single most consequential gap in the framework.

**Finasteride trial efficacy.** Propecia trials showed ~65% visible hair retention versus 7% on placebo over two years. Proscar trials showed BPH symptom reductions and ~25% prostate-volume shrinkage. The drug works on its named endpoints. Peat's counter is mechanistic: finasteride "creates an early false puberty," producing a hair-cycle perturbation that incidentally favors retention short-term ([[#27： Thyroid Function & Pulse Rate ｜ Weaponized Culture ｜ Finasteride as an ＂Insane Decision＂|*Generative Energy* #27]]). Trial efficacy does not validate the proposed mechanism — many drugs work for reasons different from those advertised — but the short-term clinical effect is real.

**The Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial.** The 2003 PCPT trial of finasteride showed a 25% reduction in prostate cancer incidence over seven years — but a *higher* rate of high-grade tumors in the finasteride arm. A Peat-consistent reading: finasteride suppressed indolent androgen-dependent tumors while permitting the aggressive androgen-independent tumors that Umekita et al. showed are *suppressed* by androgens. The standard reading still treats finasteride as net protective, contested by oncologists who weight the high-grade signal more heavily.

**Androgen deprivation therapy in metastatic prostate cancer.** The Huggins observations — castration produces dramatic remission in metastatic prostate cancer — remain part of standard care. Modern ADT extends survival in advanced disease. Peat's reading is that ADT trades short-term tumor regression for the selection of "castration-resistant" prostate cancer — exactly the androgen-independent phenotype Umekita showed is *promoted* by androgen withdrawal — a tradeoff the standard literature does not adequately price.

**Post-finasteride syndrome skepticism.** Mainstream endocrinology has been skeptical of PFS, citing variable biomarkers and placebo-prone subjective complaints. The pushback is real. The counter-pushback is that the growing literature documenting persistently depressed CSF allopregnanolone and altered neurosteroid metabolism is consistent with the Peat-predicted mechanism, and that the burden should sit with the drug, not the patient.

**Hamilton's castrate study (1942).** Pre-pubertal castrates do not develop pattern baldness, and some develop it when given exogenous testosterone — the foundational human evidence for the androgen theory. Peat would note that pre-pubertal castration removes far more than testosterone; the eunuch's altered metabolic, neurosteroid, and inflammatory profile is the relevant variable.

Honest summary: Peat's framework integrates the broader set of findings (the [[prolactin]]/[[cortisol]] elevation in balders, the thyroid signature, the heart-disease/baldness/bone-loss clustering, the allopregnanolone-PFS connection, the BPH-as-estrogen-disease story) better than the orthodoxy does. The orthodoxy explains the specific anatomical pattern of male pattern hair loss, the natural-experiment data from 5α-reductase deficiency, and short-term finasteride efficacy more parsimoniously. A fair synthesis is that DHT may act as a final-step effector on follicles already weakened by systemic metabolic stress — but this is not what the orthodoxy claims, and the appropriate intervention point is the upstream stress, not the downstream enzyme.

## Data gaps

- **Direct measurement of scalp and prostate neurosteroid metabolism after finasteride.** Published PFS data is suggestive but limited. A controlled study with serial CSF allopregnanolone, 5α-THDOC, and DHT measurements before/during/after finasteride would be definitive in either direction. Not done.
- **5α-reductase Type 2 deficiency natural-experiment data.** The orthodoxy's strongest card. A Peat-consistent reading (whole-profile protection, not anti-DHT specificity) is asserted but not empirically tested.
- **PCPT high-grade tumor signal.** Whether higher high-grade rates in the finasteride arm reflect detection bias (smaller prostates → easier-to-find tumors) or a true selection effect (suppression of indolent + permission of aggressive). Peat's framework predicts the latter; the standard reading favors the former.
- **Low-dose DHT supplementation outcomes.** Outside Andractim and similar formulations, no controlled data on the 1 mg/day supplementation Peat described. Anecdotal only.
- **The "DHT-as-bodyguard" reading.** The most contested claim. Peat asserted but did not develop mechanistically. The work to test whether elevated scalp/prostate DHT is compensatory or causal — measuring scalp prolactin, estradiol, and prostaglandin alongside DHT in matched balders and non-balders — has not been done at scale.
- **Bile-acid metabolism and finasteride.** Dinkov's claim about 5α-reductase essentiality for bile-acid synthesis is plausible but not well documented clinically. Direct bile-acid profiling in PFS patients is needed.
- **Topical vs systemic DHT.** Tissue distribution after topical application is not well characterized.
