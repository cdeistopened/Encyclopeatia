# Insulin

Insulin is the hormone modern endocrinology built an entire disease category around, and the one Ray Peat spent forty years trying to pull out of that category. His counter-thesis begins with the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]: the real obstacle to glucose oxidation is not insufficient insulin but an abundance of [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] jamming the machinery insulin is trying to activate. Insulin can't do its job when [[linoleic-acid|PUFA]] is blocking the lock. Fix the fat, and the insulin question largely dissolves. The sugar hypothesis of diabetes, the framing of insulin as the metabolic master switch, the therapeutic use of insulin itself, the claim that sugar causes insulin resistance--Peat regarded all of it as commercial mythology sustained by the insulin industry and the seed oil industry working in tandem.

That reframing has sharp implications. Diabetes, in Peat's view, is not a disease of excess sugar but of blocked sugar oxidation, and insulin injections for type 2 diabetics don't address the block; they drive fat synthesis, accelerate atherosclerosis, and produce the very complications attributed to the disease. Hyperinsulinemia, far from being a protective response, is itself a stress state whose systemic effects rhyme disturbingly with those of [[estrogen]]. "Several years ago, medical people started talking about the harmful effects of insulin, such as stimulating fat production," Peat wrote in his [[diabetes|email exchanges on diabetes]], "so 'insulin resistance' which keeps a high level of insulin from producing obesity would seem to be a good thing, but the medical obesity culture really isn't thinking very straight." Insulin resistance, in that light, looks like damage control against chronic hyperinsulinemia. The real target is whatever drove the insulin up in the first place.

## The Randle Block, Not the Insulin Gap

The central fact of diabetes, Peat argued, is visible in a lab finding mainstream diabetology rarely discusses: diabetics have elevated lactate. If glucose could not enter their cells, there would be no lactate to measure. "Diabetics typically have elevated lactate, which shows that glucose doesn't have a problem getting into their cells, just getting oxidized," he explained in his [[diabetes|emails on diabetes]]. The glucose arrives. The cell absorbs it. What fails is the oxidative pathway from pyruvate into the mitochondria--the step that [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] block at [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]], and the step that fructose, thyroid, and moderate insulin all normally activate.

This is why Peat found the standard diabetic regimen--low sugar, high fat, metformin, insulin injections--to be an inversion of the real mechanism. "If diabetes means that cells can't absorb or metabolize glucose, then any cellular function that requires glucose will be impaired, despite the presence of glucose in the blood," he wrote in [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|"Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes"]]. "It is the intracellular absence of glucose which is problematic, rather than its extracellular excess." Adding insulin forces more glucose into cells that still can't oxidize it, raising intracellular lactate, suppressing mitochondrial respiration further, and driving fat synthesis from the unoxidized carbon. The blood sugar number improves. The actual disease worsens.

Bernardo Houssay's experiments from the 1940s were the neglected foundation for a different approach. "Houssay found that coconut oil protected animals from poison-induced diabetes, while a lard-based diet failed to protect them. Later, glucose itself was found to protect the pancreatic beta-cells from poisons." Saturated fat didn't block glucose oxidation the way unsaturated fat did; glucose nourished the beta cells rather than destroying them. The insulin industry, which emerged in the same decades, preferred the opposite story, and the opposite story won.

## PUFA, Not Sugar, Causes Insulin Resistance

The orthodox narrative holds that sugar causes insulin resistance. Peat held that polyunsaturated fat causes insulin resistance and that sugar is the remedy wrongly blamed for the crime. The biochemistry runs through three overlapping mechanisms.

First, polyunsaturated fatty acids directly inhibit mitochondrial respiration. "Ephraim Racker observed that free unsaturated fatty acids inhibit mitochondrial respiration," Peat noted in [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|"Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones"]], "and recent studies are finding that free linoleic and linolenic acids act as intracellular regulators, stimulating the protein kinase C (PKC) system, which is also stimulated by estrogen and the (cancer promoting) phorbol esters. They stimulate the cell while blocking the energy it needs to respond." The cell gets a growth signal it cannot execute. The result is metabolic dysfunction masquerading as "insulin resistance."

Second, PUFA damages pancreatic beta cells directly. "Starches and polyunsaturated fats keep stressing beta cells as they regenerate," Peat wrote on type 1 diabetes. "Endotoxin and nitric oxide cause insulin resistance, besides being toxic to the beta cells." The beta cells are regenerable--"the pancreas beta cells can regenerate quickly"--but they cannot regenerate faster than ongoing PUFA and [[endotoxin]] exposure destroys them.

Third, PUFA drives insulin resistance indirectly through estrogen. "One factor in the 'insulin resistance' created by PUFA involves estrogen," Peat wrote. "Chronic accumulation of PUFA in the tissues increases the production of estrogen, and the polyunsaturated free fatty acids intensify the actions of estrogen, which acts in several ways to interfere with glucose oxidation." The pathway is not ornamental. It explains why women develop diabetes at higher rates than men, why oral contraceptives produce signs of diabetes, and why hormone replacement therapy worsens glucose tolerance.

Spellacy and Carlson's 1966 observation on Enovid, the first birth control pill, was the lost clue Peat tried to recover. The researchers found that oral contraceptives produced signs of diabetes and suggested elevated free fatty acids were responsible, writing that "free fatty acids can block the Krebs cycle, with relative insulin action resistance resulting." Their warning was ignored. The epidemiology completes the picture: "The government figures I cited show that vegetable fat consumption has increased, sugar hasn't." The obesity and diabetes epidemics tracked the rise of seed oils, not sugar.

## Insulin as an Estrogen-Like Stressor

One of Peat's more radical moves was classifying chronic hyperinsulinemia as a stress state whose systemic effects rhyme with those of estrogen. Both stimulate cell proliferation. Both promote fat synthesis. Both interfere with glucose oxidation. Both drive the HPA axis toward cortisol. Both are associated with atherosclerosis, obesity, and certain cancers. Insulin in pulsatile physiological doses is a nutrient-handling signal. Insulin in continuous therapeutic doses is a growth factor that drives weight gain and pushes cells toward the proliferative, undifferentiated state Peat associated with cancer and degeneration.

The iatrogenic damage from therapeutic insulin was something Peat pointed at directly: "When non-diabetic apes were given insulin treatments, they developed some of the same 'complications of diabetes' that are seen in humans, and antibodies to insulin were found in their retinas, suggesting that some 'complications of diabetes' were complications of insulin treatment." The retinal damage, neuropathy, and kidney failure attributed to diabetes were partly produced by its treatment. "The use of insulin should require a greater diagnostic justification than the use of aspirin for a headache does," Peat wrote, "because insulin use itself constitutes a serious health problem."

## Fructose, Which Insulin Doesn't Need To Move

One of the cleanest refutations of the insulin-centric model of metabolism is that [[fructose]] enters cells without insulin. The GLUT5 transporter is insulin-independent, which means fructose bypasses the entire insulin-resistance apparatus and feeds cells directly. For a diabetic whose glucose oxidation is blocked at [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]], fructose is not the villain of the sugar hypothesis but the missing substrate that restarts oxidation below the block.

Peat pushed this further. Fructose not only enters without insulin; it mimics insulin's activating effect on the same oxidative enzymes. "It stimulates the cells to take up glucose very much the way insulin does," he explained. "You can't distinguish the effects on some of the crucial enzymes. Fructose, and the pyruvic acid that it turns into, stimulate the enzymes that oxidize pyruvic acid just the same way insulin stimulates that same enzyme. One replaces the other perfectly." Fruit and honey are, for the metabolic purpose insulin is supposed to serve, better than insulin. They activate the same targets without producing the hyperinsulinemic side effects, and they don't require a functional insulin-receptor pathway to do it.

Fructose also moderates insulin secretion. Pure glucose triggers a sharp insulin spike; fructose "besides not itself stimulating insulin, actually slightly inhibits the release of insulin." This is why sucrose produces a gentler insulin curve than starch despite delivering the same total sugar. The advice to replace sugar with whole grains is, by this mechanism, backwards.

## Hyperinsulinemia as Stress

The things that provoke unnecessary insulin secretion indict the standard American health-food diet as much as the junk-food diet. Starch was singled out: "Starch stimulates the appetite, promotes fat synthesis by stimulating insulin secretion, and sometimes increases the growth of bacteria that produce toxins." The starch-based diet popularized as a low-fat alternative to sugar produced exactly the insulin spikes it claimed to prevent, plus the endotoxin load that compounds insulin resistance through a separate mechanism.

Eating protein without carbohydrate was another trap. "Amino acids, especially in eggs, stimulate insulin secretion, and this can cause hypoglycemia, which in turn causes cortisol secretion. Eating fruit (or other carbohydrate), coconut oil, and salt at the same meal will decrease this effect of the protein." Protein raises insulin without providing matching glucose; blood sugar falls; the body responds with [[cortisol]] and adrenaline, the catabolic hormones whose job is to break tissue protein back down into glucose. A high-protein meal eaten alone triggers the cortisol cascade despite containing plenty of calories. The solution is not less protein but more fruit or juice alongside it. The overnight period is when this swing does its deepest damage: when glycogen runs out during sleep, insulin falls, cortisol rises, muscle and skin get converted to glucose. Peat's recommendation to drink orange juice or milk on waking in the night addressed exactly this window.

## Magnesium and the Cell's Actual Problem

Peat repeatedly linked [[magnesium]] status to insulin sensitivity. Chronic stress depletes intracellular magnesium, and magnesium is required for the enzymes that let glucose enter the oxidative pathway. A magnesium-depleted cell responds sluggishly to insulin regardless of how much is present. "Insulin resistance" in a magnesium-deficient, thyroid-deficient, PUFA-loaded person is not a single disease with a single drug target; it is the observable output of a cell that cannot burn glucose for any of several reasons, each with its own correction. Metformin and injected insulin address none of them.

What does help the cell use glucose bears little resemblance to standard protocol. "The T3 component of the thyroid hormone makes muscles and other tissues oxidize sugar. Calcium, sodium, and aspirin are other things that increase the ability to use glucose." [[Thyroid]] hormone activates the enzymes of oxidative metabolism. [[Calcium]] and sodium support the membrane potential the cell needs to handle glucose. Aspirin lowers the inflammatory prostaglandins that stimulate lipolysis. None of them raise insulin. Peat also recommended thiamine for its direct role in pyruvate dehydrogenase activity: "Vitamin B1 helps to oxidize glucose, so if you try 50 or 100 mg with a meal you should watch for possible hypoglycemia from the insulin." The fact that restoring a B vitamin can produce hypoglycemia in a diabetic is itself evidence that the issue was never insulin insufficiency--the insulin was always there, waiting for a functioning oxidation pathway to hand glucose to.

For beta cell regeneration in type 1 diabetics, Peat suggested an unorthodox toolkit: "Sugar and brewer's yeast help with the regeneration. Keeping free fatty acids low is important, and niacinamide could help with that. Glucose stimulates regeneration, and pregnenolone, progesterone, and DHEA help to keep them alive. Thyroid and good nutrition gradually help to detoxify the stored fats that are responsible for killing them." The cells can come back. What they need is the absence of the things killing them, plus the substrate to rebuild.

## The Sugar Hypothesis, Inverted

The commercial stakes behind the sugar hypothesis of diabetes help explain why it persists against the biochemistry. "Starting with the insulin industry, a culture of diabetes and sugar has been fabulized and expanded and modified as new commercial industries found ways to profit from it," Peat wrote in [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context"]]. "Seed oils, fish oils, breakfast cereals, soybean products, and other things that were never eaten by any animal in millions of years of evolution have become commonplace as foods, even as health foods." Sustaining the doctrine required ignoring Houssay's coconut oil experiments, Randle's glucose-fatty acid antagonism, the Kulz finding from 1874 that diabetics metabolize fructose better than glucose, and the decades of work showing that PUFA damages the pancreas. The diversion was not accidental; it was the foundation of several large industries.

In Peat's inverted framing, the diabetic does not have too much sugar but too little usable sugar. The hyperinsulinemic patient does not need less food but food their cells can actually handle--fruit, milk, honey, coconut oil, gelatin, salt--while the polyunsaturated fats already stored in their tissues slowly detoxify under sustained thyroid function. Insulin, in this picture, is a minor supporting actor whose prominence on the endocrinology stage is an artifact of marketing and historical accident.
