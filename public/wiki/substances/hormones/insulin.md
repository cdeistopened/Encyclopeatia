# Insulin

Insulin is the hormone that mainstream medicine blames for [[diabetes]] and metabolic syndrome—the villain of low-carbohydrate ideology. Ray Peat's framework tells a different story. Insulin is a necessary and generally beneficial hormone whose pathological elevation reflects upstream problems—primarily the [[PUFA]]-driven [[Randle cycle]] blocking glucose oxidation—rather than dietary sugar excess. The clinical fixation on insulin has created a paradox: patients are prescribed insulin or insulin-sensitizing drugs while consuming the polyunsaturated fats that caused their insulin resistance in the first place.

## The Glucose Disposal Hormone

Insulin's primary function is facilitating glucose uptake into cells and promoting its storage as glycogen and, when glycogen stores are full, as fat. In a healthy metabolism, insulin rises modestly after eating, directs glucose into cells where it is oxidized for energy, and falls back to baseline. The process is efficient and brief.

Problems arise not from insulin itself but from the conditions that force it higher and higher to accomplish the same task. When [[free fatty acids]] block glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]], cells resist insulin's signal—not because the insulin receptor is broken but because the cell cannot use the glucose being offered. More insulin is produced to overcome the resistance, but the underlying problem—fat blocking sugar oxidation—remains unaddressed.

## The Potassium Connection

Peat identified a largely overlooked aspect of glucose metabolism: potassium's role in glucose disposal. In a [[2014-01-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-questions-and-answers|2014 episode]], he noted that "potassium was more involved in handling glucose effectively than insulin." Fruit juice, which contains both sugar and potassium, "requires far less insulin than bread with similar calories"—because the potassium supports glucose uptake independent of insulin's action.

This observation reframes the "glycemic index" approach to diabetes. Starchy foods (bread, pasta, potatoes) consist almost entirely of glucose without the potassium, magnesium, and fructose that fruits provide to support its metabolism. The higher insulin response to starch reflects not the danger of carbohydrates but the inadequacy of refined starch as a glucose delivery vehicle.

## Fructose: Insulin's Bypass

[[Fructose]]—the sugar in fruit and half of table sugar (sucrose)—enters cells and is metabolized without requiring insulin. As Peat explained in his email correspondence, "Fructose limits insulin secretion but intensifies metabolic activity." This makes fruit and sucrose fundamentally different from pure glucose or starch in their effects on insulin.

The research Peat cited showed that "starch-fed rats gained far more weight than sugar-eating rats"—contradicting the popular assumption that sugar is uniquely fattening. Fructose catalyzes glucose oxidation, maintains cells in a state of efficient energy production, and chelates iron (a major oxidative damage factor). The combination of glucose and fructose in sucrose and fruit is metabolically superior to glucose alone.

## Historical Sugar Therapy for Diabetes

Before insulin was commercially available, physicians treated diabetics with sugar—the opposite of modern dietary advice. In a [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|2014 episode]], Peat described how "historical doctors fed diabetic patients about three-quarters of a pound of sugar daily; patients recovered from wasting disease in weeks." In an [[2012-10-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidants|episode on antioxidants]], he added: "1860s Paris doctors cured diabetic patients by giving them sugar equal to what they lost in urine—10 to 12 ounces per day."

These cases involved Type 1 or late-stage Type 2 diabetics who were wasting away because their cells could not use glucose. Rather than restricting the glucose they desperately needed, physicians provided it in a form (sucrose, containing fructose) that could partially bypass the insulin-dependent pathway. The patients recovered not despite the sugar but because of it.

## The Commercial Creation of Diabetes

Peat viewed the modern diabetes epidemic partly as a consequence of medical and pharmaceutical decisions. In a [[2020-02-19-butter-living-podcast-butter-living-podcast-fertility-and-pregnancy|2020 interview]], he stated: "They created Type 2 diabetes to sell insulin, and then gestational diabetes." The progressive lowering of diagnostic thresholds has expanded the diabetic population far beyond those with genuine metabolic disease, creating a market for insulin and insulin-sensitizing drugs.

Children diagnosed as diabetic after an illness—when blood sugar is transiently elevated as part of the stress response—may be placed on insulin permanently, institutionalizing a condition that might have resolved. Peat recommended that any diabetes diagnosis should include assessment of TSH, [[cortisol]], and vitamin D, and that "T3 is always appropriate with diabetes" because thyroid support addresses the underlying metabolic failure.

## Niacinamide and Practical Approaches

Rather than managing insulin levels directly, Peat's approach addresses the free fatty acids that cause insulin resistance. In a [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-nerve-protection|2014 episode on diabetes]], he identified "niacinamide and [[aspirin]] as the most practical tools for lowering free fatty acids and stopping the stress reaction that elevates blood sugar." Niacinamide inhibits the lipase that liberates fats from stores; aspirin blocks the inflammatory prostaglandins from those fats.

The strategy is indirect but addresses the cause: lower the free fatty acids, remove the Randle cycle block, allow cells to oxidize glucose, let insulin work normally, and blood sugar normalizes. This approach treats insulin resistance as a symptom of fat metabolism disorder rather than a primary disease requiring hormonal manipulation.
