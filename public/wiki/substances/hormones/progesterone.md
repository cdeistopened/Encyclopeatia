# Progesterone

If there is one hormone that epitomizes Ray Peat's bioenergetic framework, it is progesterone. While mainstream medicine typically relegates progesterone to a narrow role in reproduction, Peat spent decades demonstrating that this molecule is nothing less than the body's master protective substance—what he called the "anti-stress, anti-aging" hormone. His academic career began with research on progesterone's role in reproductive aging, and his 1972 dissertation outlined the core thesis that would define his work: progesterone and related hormones serve as protectors of the body's structure and energy against the harmful effects of [[estrogen]], radiation, stress, and lack of oxygen.

Progesterone is the first steroid made from [[cholesterol]], the foundational molecule from which all other steroid hormones derive. This positioning is not merely biochemical trivia—it reflects progesterone's primacy in the body's defensive hierarchy. As Peat explained in a [[2012-01-22-politics-and-science-politics-science-progesterone-part-1|2012 interview]], before he had even intentionally studied it, he noticed that handling progesterone in the lab cured his chronic hangnails: "Being careless in the lab and getting some of the progesterone on my hands, I noticed that my hangnails disappeared and have never come back."

## The Brain's Highest-Concentration Steroid

One of the most revealing facts about progesterone is where the body concentrates it most: the brain. As Peat noted in a [[2020-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vibrations-frequencies-part-2|2020 discussion on brain injury]], "The brain has the highest concentration of progesterone of any tissue in the body. It's an essential brain-stabilizing molecule." This is not incidental to reproduction—the brain actively manufactures progesterone because it requires this molecule for stable, efficient function.

The brain's need for progesterone explains its powerful effects on cognition, mood, and neurological conditions. Peat described treating a 52-year-old woman who had become "hopelessly demented" from epilepsy and anti-seizure drugs. After she absorbed progesterone through her arthritic fingers, her seizures stopped completely. "Four or five days later, her fingers were not arthritic anymore, she felt fine, and she could find her way home," Peat recounted in his [[2012-01-22-politics-and-science-politics-science-progesterone-part-1|progesterone lecture]]. "She enrolled back in the university and got her master's degree with straight A's."

The neurosteroid effects operate through multiple mechanisms. Progesterone and its metabolite allopregnanolone activate the GABA-A receptor, producing what Peat described as calming, anxiolytic effects. In a [[2018-01-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-female-hormones-progesterone|2018 episode on female hormones]], he explained that "besides activating GABA to reduce stress, progesterone also inhibits excitatory processes, including the glutamatergic and NMDA receptor systems." This dual action—enhancing calming signals while suppressing excitatory ones—makes progesterone fundamentally anti-excitotoxic.

The implications for brain trauma are profound. Research by Donald Stein demonstrated that progesterone dramatically improves survival and recovery from traumatic brain injury. As Peat explained in a [[2013-03-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-palpitations-and-cardiac-events|2013 episode]], "if you can get a big dose of progesterone to someone who has had a potentially fatal head injury, they will have a high survival and recovery rate because progesterone stops the progression of brain damage. It does the same thing to any tissue that's injured."

## The Synthesis Pathway: Cholesterol to Steroid Cascade

Understanding progesterone requires understanding its place in steroid synthesis. The pathway begins with [[cholesterol]], which the mitochondria convert into [[pregnenolone]], then into progesterone. From progesterone, the body can produce all other steroid hormones—[[cortisol]], [[DHEA]], [[testosterone]], and even [[estrogen]].

This cascade depends critically on [[thyroid]] function. As Peat explained in a [[2008-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-and-polyunsaturated-fatty-acids|2008 interview]], "Cholesterol is very good for a person. If you take an adrenal gland or an ovary and run blood into it, the amount of progesterone coming out the other side is directly proportional to the amount of cholesterol in the blood. That depends on Vitamin A and thyroid to be converted."

This explains why artificially lowering cholesterol is so problematic in Peat's framework. "When you force your cholesterol down," he warned, "you're unable to make progesterone, pregnenolone, DHEA, and the whole range of protective steroids." The brain, which requires the highest concentrations of these steroids, suffers most acutely from this deficiency.

The practical implication is clear: if cholesterol rises with age while protective steroids decline, the problem is not too much cholesterol but inadequate conversion. Peat explained this in a [[2018-06-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-positive-thinking-sleep-and-repair|2018 episode]]: "Cholesterol rises with age because low thyroid and low Vitamin A interfere with the conversion of cholesterol into pregnenolone and progesterone. The body compensates by increasing cholesterol."

## The Anti-Estrogen

Progesterone's most important function may be its opposition to [[estrogen]]. While estrogen creates inflammation, water retention, and cellular excitation, progesterone systematically reverses these effects. As Peat stated emphatically in a [[2018-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-1|2018 episode]], "the evidence clearly shows that progesterone is the protective hormone, while estrogen is the most inflammatory hormone continually secreted throughout a woman's life."

The opposition operates through at least ten distinct mechanisms. As Peat explained in a [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-hormones-behind-inflammation|2025 episode on inflammation]], "If you can produce progesterone, one of its first effects is to knock out the estrogen in roughly ten different ways. It turns off the production of estrogen, knocks out the estrogen receptor so the tissue is less sensitive, and changes the metabolism."

The relationship between these hormones explains much about aging. Around age 40, most people reach their lifetime peak of estrogen relative to progesterone. As Peat explained in a [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|2013 episode]], "almost everyone around the age of 40 has the highest ratio of estrogen to progesterone." When progesterone declines and can no longer force estrogen out of cells, serum estrogen appears to drop—but tissue estrogen actually increases. "The fact that you don't find it in the blood is simply because estrogen sticks inside the cells in proportion to the deficiency of progesterone."

This insight inverts the conventional understanding of menopause. The estrogen industry promoted the idea that menopausal women are estrogen-deficient, but Peat argued the opposite: "In rat studies, when ovaries are removed, within a week the rats were right back to their normal estrogen level but without progesterone. My dissertation was based on the fact that sterility in the middle of the lifespan is caused by unopposed estrogen."

## Protective Effects Beyond Reproduction

Progesterone's protective effects extend to virtually every tissue and condition. Peat described it as a "catatoxic steroid"—a term from Hans Selye meaning a substance that protects by blocking the effects of toxins.

### Heart Protection

Progesterone stabilizes heart rhythm through mechanisms similar to digitalis. In a [[2013-05-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-heart-i|2013 episode on heart health]], Peat explained that "progesterone not only has anti-seizure effects, but it has heart-protective effects—steadying the heart rhythm the way digitalis does and blocking aldosterone, preventing fibrosis of the heart."

The mechanism involves accelerating the heart's ability to return to its resting, energized state. "Estrogen shows up as a prolonged QT interval," Peat explained in a [[2018-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-1|2018 episode]]. "Progesterone shortens that interval and makes the heart less likely to have a rhythm problem because it accelerates the ability to return to the resting, energized state." Research concluded that "estrogen causes sudden cardiac death, and progesterone prevents it."

### Cancer Protection

The relationship between progesterone and cancer reflects its fundamental anti-proliferative properties. Alexander Lipschutz's research demonstrated that continuous estrogen exposure is carcinogenic, but "if you interrupt that with progesterone periodically, you don't get cancer." Lipschutz called pregnenolone and progesterone the "anti-fibromatogenic steroids"—substances that "start out inhibiting fibrous overproduction and end up protecting against tumors."

Peat personally witnessed progesterone's anti-cancer effects. In a [[2019-04-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-particles|2019 episode]], he recalled: "The first one I saw was a man about 80 years old, 45 years ago. He put progesterone on his diagnosed lip cancer, and in a week or so, it was gone."

### Radiation Protection

Because radiation's effects parallel estrogen's effects on cellular metabolism, progesterone protects against radiation injury. As Peat explained in a [[2011-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-fukujima-i|2011 episode during the Fukushima crisis]], "The radiation effect itself imitates estrogen in the body. Everything estrogen does can be imitated by radiation. If you are in a high-estrogen state, you are more susceptible to radiation injury. That is why progesterone, thyroid, and caffeine—by helping to lower estrogen and inflammation—are protective."

### Inflammatory Conditions

The anti-inflammatory properties make progesterone useful for arthritis, allergies, and autoimmune conditions. In a [[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|2016 episode on allergies]], Peat explained: "Progesterone has a broad variety of antihistamine effects. Estrogen turns on both the multiplication of mast cells and their tendency to release histamine and serotonin."

## Pregnancy: The Ultimate Demonstration

Pregnancy provides the most dramatic demonstration of progesterone's protective effects. By the end of a healthy pregnancy, the placenta produces 800-900 milligrams of progesterone daily—hundreds of times more than a cycling woman produces. As Peat noted in a [[2018-01-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-female-hormones-progesterone|2018 episode]], "That doesn't do anything harmful; it is very protective. Women generally feel great when their progesterone is at a maximum."

This explains why pregnancy protects against certain cancers. Women who have multiple pregnancies accumulate residual corpus luteum cells in their ovaries, maintaining higher progesterone levels throughout life. As Peat explained, "a woman who has been pregnant eight times will have higher residual progesterone levels and will live much longer on average than a woman who hasn't had so many pregnancies."

Progesterone also protects the developing brain. In a [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 episode]], Peat described research showing that "progesterone helps to stabilize and make more efficient use of glucose. Glucose and progesterone work together. Women with low progesterone tend to have unstable low blood sugar and many more problems through the pregnancy."

Katharina Dalton's research from the 1940s and 50s demonstrated this dramatically. She treated premenstrual syndrome with progesterone injections and continued treatment through pregnancy. Peat described the results in his [[2012-01-22-politics-and-science-politics-science-progesterone-part-1|2012 lecture]]: "Her patients' babies, who usually came from working-class backgrounds with average IQs of 90, tended to average 130 IQ after progesterone treatment. The intelligence of the babies corresponded to the amount of progesterone she gave the mothers."

## Practical Considerations

### Dosing and Safety

The massive doses produced during pregnancy demonstrate progesterone's safety margin. When trials on traumatic brain injury used inadequate doses—a few hundred milligrams—they predictably failed. Peat observed that "they could easily have given 2,000 milligrams of progesterone per day instead of a few hundred."

For general use, Peat typically suggested modest amounts. In a [[2015-10-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-current-trends-on-nitric-oxide|2015 episode]], he recommended "10 to 30 milligrams" as capable of making "a big difference" for therapeutic effects. For migraines, he reported personal experience with "about 100 milligrams" producing "complete reversal of symptoms in one to two minutes."

The main caution involves continuous high-dose use. As Peat explained in a [[2017-01-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-precautionary-principle-part-1|2017 episode]], "The liver is always regulating the hormones. When you use progesterone all month, the liver develops the ability to excrete it quickly over the first two weeks. So in the following weeks, you're excreting it almost as fast as you can put it in. The natural cycle has a rest of two weeks where the liver resets itself so that a small amount will have a full effect."

### Men and Progesterone

While often considered a "female hormone," progesterone serves important functions in men. Peat noted that men who had vasectomies often experienced symptoms related to falling progesterone: "They found that these men had very low progesterone. Replacing their progesterone levels up to normal cured their symptoms."

For men, Peat generally recommended starting with [[pregnenolone]] rather than progesterone directly. As he explained in a [[2013-03-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-palpitations-and-cardiac-events|2013 episode]], "Pregnenolone can produce testosterone and DHEA as well as progesterone, so it tends to keep your hormones more in balance than applying big doses of progesterone."

### Synthetic Progestins vs. Natural Progesterone

The pharmaceutical industry created synthetic progestins that can be patented, but these molecules do not replicate progesterone's protective effects. As Peat emphasized in a [[2015-03-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-breast-cancer|2015 episode]], "The additives attached to the progesterone molecule specifically block its conversion into that natural range of other steroids. Since the brain is a major source and user of progesterone, probably the worst effect of the synthetic progestins is that they interfere with brain function."

The naming convention itself causes confusion. "The term 'progestins' or 'progestogens' implies that these chemicals act to preserve pregnancy the way progesterone does," Peat explained. "But in fact, the synthetic so-called progestins are used in birth control pills exactly because they *don't* support pregnancy."
