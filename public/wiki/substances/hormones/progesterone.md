# Progesterone

This article covers progesterone; the estrogen mechanisms it opposes are covered in [[estrogen]]. If estrogen is the most dangerous substance in Ray Peat's framework, progesterone is the most protective. It is the first steroid the body makes from [[cholesterol]], the upstream precursor of [[cortisol]], aldosterone, testosterone, and estrogen itself. It is also the single substance that Hans Selye, working in the 1940s, found could imitate "the full spectrum of regulatory and anti-stress functions of the adrenal steroids," so completely that animals given a steady supply could survive the removal of their adrenal glands altogether. Mainstream medicine has spent the last sixty years treating progesterone as the "other female hormone." Peat spent the same sixty years arguing that framing is inverted. "Progesterone is not just the hormone of pregnancy," he wrote in his [[progesterone-summaries|"Progesterone Summaries" (2012)]] newsletter. "To use it only 'to protect the uterus' would be like telling a man he doesn't need testosterone if he doesn't plan to father children, except that progesterone is of far greater and more basic physiological significance than testosterone."

Progesterone is synthesized in the brain, by the adrenal glands, by the corpus luteum, by the placenta in enormous amounts during pregnancy, and by peripheral tissues under metabolic pressure. Its list of protective actions is unusually long: anti-inflammatory, anti-edema, anti-excitotoxic, anti-catabolic, anti-carcinogenic, anti-clotting, pro-GABAergic, pro-thyroid, pro-fertility, and, in cases Peat documented personally, capable of reversing seizure disorders, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and dementia within days of administration. These are not lucky coincidences. They are downstream consequences of a single cellular action: the restoration of efficient, oxidative energy production in tissues that stress, estrogen, and [[PUFA]] have pushed toward fermentation.

## The Selye Inheritance

Peat traced his understanding of progesterone directly to Hans Selye's work on the general adaptation syndrome. In his newsletter [[origins-of-progesterone-therapy|"Origins of Progesterone Therapy" (1992)]], Peat summarized the finding that reorganized his view of the endocrine system: "By 1945, Hans Selye had demonstrated that progesterone in itself has the full spectrum of regulatory and anti-stress functions of the adrenal steroids." Adrenalectomized animals, which normally die when challenged with stress, would survive if given sufficient progesterone. The hormone was functioning as an adrenal substitute because it was, chemically and functionally, the parent molecule from which all the adrenal steroids are made.

The adrenal cortex starts with cholesterol, converts it to [[pregnenolone]], and then converts pregnenolone to progesterone. Everything downstream (cortisol, aldosterone, DHEA, the androgens, and the estrogens) begins with progesterone. When the adrenals are under stress, they use progesterone as the substrate for cortisol production. When progesterone is abundant, the adrenals meet the demand without running short. When it is deficient, as Peat noted in the [[progesterone-summaries|2012 newsletter]], "we produce too much cortisol, and excessive cortisol causes osteoporosis, aging of the skin, damage to brain cells, and the accumulation of fat, especially on the back and abdomen." The constellation of cortisol-driven aging is, in this framing, a progesterone-deficiency syndrome. The adrenals are not overproducing cortisol because their regulation has failed. They are overproducing it because they have been forced to compensate for the loss of the upstream hormone that would have met the stress demand directly.

One immediate practical consequence follows. Supplemental progesterone reduces cortisol not by suppressing it pharmacologically but by making its excessive production unnecessary. "Supplemental progesterone can reduce excessive cortisol production," Peat confirmed in an email exchange. The body treats progesterone as a signal that resources are available and stops demanding the catabolic intervention that cortisol represents.

Albert Szent-Györgyi, discoverer of vitamin C and the catalysts of the citric acid cycle, demonstrated that progesterone regulates the heart "in a manner similar to digitalis." In the 1970s, Peat extended this work to vascular smooth muscle, showing that progesterone regulates its tone, preventing venous pooling, maintaining normal filling of the heart, and opposing shock. "The immediate improvement in circulation can have dramatic effects, which include restoration of kidney function, elimination of fluid from the lungs, restoration of sensation in the feet, and healing of gangrenous toes." These are the properties of a fundamental anti-shock agent, not a minor reproductive hormone.

## The Brain's Hormone

Peat emphasized repeatedly that "the brain contains a very high concentration of progesterone, reflecting its protective function for that most important organ." Progesterone is not merely present in the brain, it is concentrated there more densely than in any other organ, in both sexes and particularly in the young. This is the anatomical signature of a hormone whose primary function is neurological protection.

Progesterone's metabolite allopregnanolone is one of the most potent positive modulators of the GABA-A receptor known to pharmacology, producing the anti-anxiety, anti-seizure, and sedative effects that conventional tranquilizers attempt to imitate with progressively worse side-effect profiles. "Experiments have shown that progesterone relieves anxiety, improves memory, protects brain cells, and even prevents epileptic seizures," Peat wrote. In his article [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women|"Estrogen and Brain Aging in Men and Women"]], he described progesterone as "the basic brain-protective antiestrogen," operating against the excitotoxic cascade at every step: "preventing lipid peroxidation, excitotoxicity, nitric oxide damage, energy deficit, edema, etc."

The opposition to excitotoxicity is central, not incidental. In his [[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|"Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone" (2007)]] newsletter, Peat laid out the relationship: "In cultured breast cancer cells, GABA inhibits growth, NMDA increases growth. As in the brain, progesterone supports the actions of GABA, and opposes those of NMDA or the excitatory amino acids, while estrogen in general promotes the effects of the excitatory amino acids." Cancer cell proliferation and seizure activity share the same underlying biology: unrestrained excitation driven by calcium influx, energy depletion, and the failure of the inhibitory systems that progesterone supports. This is why the same hormone that stops epileptic seizures also stops tumor growth.

Peat's most striking clinical stories involved the brain. In a [[2012-01-22-politics-and-science-politics-science-progesterone-part-1|2012 *Politics & Science* episode on progesterone]], he described the patient who first pulled him into recommending the hormone directly:

> What got me started recommending it directly to people was a 52-year-old woman who had been epileptic from the age of 35. A neurologist had given her anti-seizure drugs for migraines, which made her unable to teach school. When she stopped the drugs, she had actual seizures. By the time I saw her, she was considered hopelessly demented. I told her about the transdermal use of progesterone. Four or five days later, her fingers were not arthritic anymore, she felt fine, and she could find her way home. She enrolled back in the university and got her master's degree with straight A's. Her seizures stopped completely.

Another woman with multiple sclerosis and optic neuritis "lectured to my endocrine class about how progesterone injections helped her." About thirty patients, Peat estimated, had "essentially immediate recovery from various types of arthritis" once they began transdermal progesterone. The pattern was consistent enough, and the mechanism coherent enough, that Peat was willing to stake his professional reputation on the recommendation.

The application to head trauma became one of his most prescient positions. "It was known in the 50s as a brain-preserving hormone. It's a growth factor for nerves and an anti-inflammatory, anti-edema agent. Edema is one of the factors that kills brain cells after injury. Progesterone stops the damage and promotes remyelinization of the fibers. In the 70s, I advocated that everyone should carry a bottle with them in case they were in an accident." Decades later, emergency medicine researchers rediscovered the same mechanism in trials of progesterone for traumatic brain injury.

## The Destruction of the Estrogen Receptor

The relationship between progesterone and estrogen is not a simple competitive balance. It is an absolute antagonism at the molecular level. In a [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode]], Peat described the sequence:

> Stress activates the estrogen receptor. Once estrogen is acting, that turns on the cell's production of the progesterone receptor. When progesterone binds to that receptor, it destroys the estrogen receptor. Progesterone is an anti-estrogen in a very absolute sense. It knocks out the protein that keeps estrogen in the cell and activates enzymes that eliminate estrogen.

The estrogen receptor is a physical protein. Progesterone, acting through its own receptor, causes that protein to be degraded. This is not competitive inhibition. It is the physical destruction of the molecular machinery through which estrogen exerts its effects. Peat returned to this point because it demolishes the conventional framing in which progesterone and estrogen "balance" each other like opposing forces on a scale. They do not balance. Progesterone abolishes.

Receptor destruction is only the beginning. Progesterone also activates the enzymes (sulfotransferases and glucuronosyltransferases) that conjugate estrogen for excretion, and inhibits the enzymes (beta-glucuronidase and sulfatase) that reactivate conjugated estrogen back into its active form. It reduces the activity of [[aromatase]], the stress-responsive enzyme that generates estrogen locally in virtually every tissue. "Progesterone, by many mechanisms, including its antagonism to cortisol, lowers the amount of estrogen in cells," Peat wrote in his email exchanges, "causing the estrogen-binding proteins to be degraded, inhibiting the enzymes that release estrogen from the sulfates and glucuronides, and activating the enzymes that detoxify estrogen."

The systemic consequence is that progesterone pulls estrogen out of the tissues where it has accumulated. Normal tissue can contain twenty to thirty times more estrogen than the bloodstream. Under progesterone's influence, that ratio collapses, and the estrogen released from the tissues enters the circulation where the liver can eliminate it. This has one counterintuitive clinical implication Peat emphasized whenever women reported "worse symptoms" after starting progesterone: "Progesterone causes the estrogen to be released from inside cells into the bloodstream where it can be eliminated." A temporary rise in serum estrogen is not a sign that progesterone is "turning into estrogen" (it cannot; the biosynthetic pathway runs in the opposite direction) but a sign that the hormone is working as expected.

Progesterone's effect scales inversely with the severity of the estrogen problem. "Since an essential mechanism of progesterone's action involves its opposition to estrogen, smaller amounts are effective when estrogen production is low, and if estrogen is extremely high, even large supplements of progesterone will have no clear effect; in that case, it is essential to regulate estrogen metabolism, by improving the diet, correcting a thyroid deficiency, etc. (Unsaturated fat is antithyroid and synergizes with estrogen.)" Progesterone supplementation works best as part of a broader framework that addresses the upstream causes of estrogen excess.

## The Thyroid Partnership

Progesterone and [[thyroid]] hormone form a mutually reinforcing partnership. Thyroid's role in enabling progesterone synthesis is covered in detail in the thyroid article; the relationship runs through the vitamin A conversion pathway, with T3 required to turn carotene into retinol and retinol required for the conversion of cholesterol into pregnenolone and then progesterone. When thyroid is low, the progesterone cascade stalls, carotene accumulates unconverted (visible as the yellow-orange calluses of hypothyroid palms and soles), and estrogen rises unopposed.

The relationship runs in the opposite direction as well. "Progesterone helps the thyroid to secrete, and helps the liver to regulate glucose and convert T4 to T3," Peat wrote. "Women who are low in progesterone usually have hypothyroid symptoms (because of insufficient T3), including high cortisol, which promotes the synthesis of estrogen." The loop closes: hypothyroidism produces progesterone deficiency, progesterone deficiency produces hypothyroid symptoms, and both produce elevated cortisol and estrogen. This is why Peat recommended thyroid and progesterone together. "If you adjust the thyroid dose every two weeks according to how you feel, and according to your temperatures and pulse rate, there should be a point where your cycle is right, without needing progesterone." Adequate thyroid treatment, by restoring the synthetic capacity of the ovaries and adrenals, could often normalize endogenous progesterone production without direct supplementation.

## Pregnancy, Fertility, and the Corpus Luteum

Progesterone's name comes from "pro-gestational," and its role in sustaining pregnancy is the use conventional medicine acknowledges. But Peat's reading of why pregnancy requires so much progesterone differs sharply from the standard account. The enormous quantities produced during pregnancy are not merely supporting the uterine lining. They are protecting a developing organism from the stress of the intrauterine environment. "The large amounts that are produced during pregnancy result from the developing baby's need for protection from the stressful environment," he wrote.

Peat described the mechanism in the 2012 *Politics & Science* transcript: "Progesterone turns off the histamine and estrogen and makes it possible to deliver oxygen and sugar to the implanted embryo. It's the shift towards oxygen and sugar delivery that makes progesterone the preserver of pregnancy." The hormone actively creates the metabolic conditions under which a new organism can grow: suppressing inflammation, reducing histamine, opposing vasoconstriction, and ensuring that fuel and oxygen reach the embryo.

One of Peat's most striking observations involved cumulative ovarian progesterone. "With each pregnancy, some of those [corpus luteum] cells remain in the ovary. So a woman who has been pregnant eight times will have higher residual progesterone levels and will live much longer on average than a woman who hasn't had so many pregnancies." The longevity advantage public health data associates with higher parity is not a statistical curiosity but a direct consequence of the protective hormone each pregnancy leaves behind.

Peat drew on the work of Katharina Dalton, a British physician who treated women with severe premenstrual syndrome and toxemia of pregnancy with progesterone injections starting in the 1950s. Dalton followed the children of her treated patients and found something unexpected. "Her patients' babies, who usually came from working-class backgrounds with average IQs of 90, tended to average 130 IQ after progesterone treatment. The intelligence of the babies corresponded to the amount of progesterone she gave the mothers." The forty-point IQ difference could not be explained by socioeconomic factors; it corresponded directly to the dose administered during gestation. Peat took this as evidence that progesterone's brain-building effects extended from the adult nervous system all the way back to prenatal brain development.

## The Progestin Problem

The story of how progesterone was replaced in medical practice by synthetic chemicals that actively block progesterone's effects is told in detail in [[estrogen]]. The short version is that natural progesterone was cheap, unpatentable, and public domain by the 1940s, while the "progestins" pharmaceutical companies developed in the following decades were patentable, profitable, and marketed under names designed to imply equivalence to the natural hormone they opposed. Medroxyprogesterone acetate, the most widely prescribed of them, is, as Peat put it in [[the-progesterone-deceptions|"The Progesterone Deceptions" (1987)]], "luteolytic, and lowers the ovaries' production of progesterone while progesterone itself has a positive effect on the corpus luteum." The synthetic substance prescribed to women "as progesterone" actively destroys the ovarian cells that produce the real hormone. The FDA, compounding the deception, required that natural progesterone carry warnings derived from studies of the synthetic progestins, so that a woman reading the label of a bioidentical product would encounter cancer and birth defect warnings that applied only to the drugs her physician was likely prescribing in its place. "Thus, a false message attached to progesterone creates precisely the harm it claims to want to prevent."

## Progest-E: The Vitamin E Delivery System

Peat's practical contribution was a delivery system as much as a theoretical framework. He discovered in 1971 that "vitamin E and progesterone work together to sustain efficient production and use of biological energy," a finding that led him to dissolve natural USP progesterone in [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]] oil for his own use and eventually for commercial distribution as [[progest-e|Progest-E]]. Vitamin E is itself an anti-estrogen and a progesterone-sparing agent, and the two substances amplify each other at the cellular level. "Both prevent oxygen waste and appear to improve mitochondrial coupling of phosphorylation with respiration."

The delivery route matters as much as the molecular pairing. Pharmaceutical progesterone in oral capsules is heavily metabolized by the liver on first pass, with bioavailability typically below ten percent. Progesterone dissolved in vitamin E and absorbed through the oral mucosa or skin bypasses first-pass metabolism entirely, entering the bloodstream via the chylomicron system in a form that retains its biological activity. This is why Peat recommended applying drops to the gums or skin rather than swallowing capsules, and why small amounts of the Progest-E formulation often produced effects that much larger doses of oral progesterone would not.

Peat was emphatic that natural progesterone, unlike the synthetic progestins, has no known toxic level in humans. "Animals are generally more sensitive to progesterone than humans are, and in animals no toxic level has been found, except that in the highest doses it is anesthetic. In humans, even this effect has never been reported in the medical literature, and it is clearly anti-toxic in nature."

## Systemic Effects

The inventory of progesterone's tissue-specific actions reads like a catalog of the systems that fail with aging, because aging, in Peat's framework, is largely an expression of progesterone deficiency and estrogen dominance.

In the circulatory system, progesterone "prevents bulging veins by increasing the tone of blood vessels, and improves the efficiency of the heart." Szent-Györgyi's digitalis-like effect on cardiac muscle was extended by Peat's work on vascular smooth muscle, with the consequence that progesterone increases stroke volume, opposes venous pooling, and restores filling of the heart.

In the skin, progesterone reverses the atrophy cortisol and estrogen produce. It "makes aged, atrophied skin thicker, more regular." The mechanism is the opposite of cortisol's catabolic action on collagen and connective tissue.

In bone, progesterone "promotes healthy bone growth" and "helps prevent and reverse bone loss, even in women on ERT." The contrast with estrogen is instructive. Estrogen is marketed as a bone-preserving hormone, but it increases [[prolactin]], and prolactin is one of the major drivers of bone dissolution. Progesterone acts directly on the osteoblasts while opposing the prolactin-driven bone loss estrogen produces.

In the lungs, progesterone "promotes respiration, and has been used to correct emphysema." It "sensitizes nerves that regulate respiration, and has been used to treat infant apnea, sleep apnea in adults, and polycythemia vera in men." The respiratory effect reflects the same CO2-preserving, oxidative-efficiency-promoting action progesterone has in every other tissue.

In the immune system, the thymus gland (the key organ of immunity) is "profoundly dependent on progesterone," and Peat identified deficiency as a driver of autoimmune conditions. The arthritis cases that responded to progesterone within days were examples of immune dysregulation resolving once the signal that restrains inflammatory excess was restored.

In men, progesterone is not a female hormone at all. "In cases of specific progesterone deficiency in men, small doses can cure impotence. It has been used effectively to treat benign prostatic enlargement." The prostate hypertrophy conventionally attributed to testosterone involves estrogen dominance, and progesterone opposes it through the same receptor-destroying, aromatase-inhibiting actions it uses against estrogen in female tissues.

In cancer, the protective relationship had been established by the 1950s. "By 1950, the results of animal studies of progesterone's anticancer effects were so clear that the National Cancer Institute got involved." The anti-NF-kappaB action, the suppression of TNF, the support of GABA over glutamate, the destruction of the estrogen receptor, and the restoration of oxidative metabolism converge on the same outcome: cells stop dividing inappropriately and resume their differentiated function.

## Raw Materials and Practical Use

Adequate progesterone production depends on adequate raw materials. "If your body has thyroid, Vitamin A, and cholesterol, it will make a massive amount of progesterone in response to estrogen stimulation to neutralize it," Peat explained. Thyroid hormone is required to activate the synthetic cascade and to convert carotene to retinol. [[Vitamin A]] is the cofactor for the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone. Cholesterol is the starting material. When any of these is deficient, progesterone production fails regardless of how much stress signaling reaches the adrenals and ovaries.

This is why Peat so often framed progesterone deficiency as a downstream symptom rather than an independent condition. A woman with low progesterone is usually a woman with inadequate thyroid function, insufficient vitamin A intake, low cholesterol (often iatrogenic, from statin therapy), or all three. Correcting the upstream deficiencies often restores endogenous production without supplementation. "Progesterone synthesis will be increased by the higher metabolic rate, and will tend to keep the temperature higher." A basal temperature measurement is simultaneously a thyroid indicator, a progesterone indicator, and a general measure of metabolic sufficiency.

Peat's practical recommendations converge on a consistent framework. First, address upstream causes: correct hypothyroidism, ensure adequate vitamin A and cholesterol, eliminate PUFA from the diet, and support liver function with protein and B vitamins. Second, if direct supplementation is indicated, use natural USP progesterone dissolved in vitamin E, applied to the skin, gums, or sublingual tissue rather than swallowed. Third, use the minimum dose that produces the desired effect. Fourth, expect a transient period during which tissue-bound estrogen is released into the bloodstream as progesterone displaces it, and do not mistake this for the treatment failing.

For acute applications (head injury, stroke, severe PMS, threatened miscarriage, seizure), Peat recommended keeping progesterone on hand as first aid. The substance Selye identified as the basic hormone of adaptation, that the brain concentrates above all other organs, and that Dalton's patients used to produce unusually intelligent children is also the most generally protective substance a person can carry for everyday emergencies. It has no acute toxicity, no contraindications beyond the marketing-driven warnings applied to its synthetic namesakes, and a mechanism of action that addresses almost every tissue failure characteristic of stress, injury, and aging.

## Cycle-phase dosing

Progesterone is a cyclic hormone, and the framing that organizes every dosing recommendation Peat made is the natural luteal rhythm. The corpus luteum produces progesterone for the second half of the cycle — day 14 through day 28 — and almost none for the first half. Supplementing in the wrong half disrupts ovulation and provokes the symptoms it is supposed to suppress.

The luteal-only protocol is the default. From [[progesterone-summaries|"Progesterone Summaries" (2012)]]: "The normal pattern of progesterone secretion during the month is for the ovaries to produce a large amount in the 2nd two weeks of the menstrual cycle, (i.e., day 14 through day 28)... An average person produces about 30 milligrams daily during the 2nd two weeks." Supplementation imitates the rhythm: two weeks on, two weeks off.

Peat's reference solution is the 10% Progest-E formulation, ~3 mg progesterone per small drop. Standard luteal dose: **3 to 4 drops (10–15 mg) daily, days 14 through 28** — enough to bring blood levels into the normal luteal range. The dose "may be repeated once or twice during the day as needed to alleviate symptoms" — a woman with significant [[pms]] symptoms might take 10–15 mg three times across the day, 30–45 mg total.

For acute PMS rescue the dose ramp follows a different logic. The 1986 [[oral-absorption-of-progesterone|JAMA letter on oral absorption]] documented that 50–100 mg oral doses in Progest-E reach blood levels of 6–16 ng/mL within 4–8 hours and stay in the normal luteal range for at least 12 hours. PMS rescue therefore: **30–50 mg as a single dose** at symptom onset, repeatable. Peat's titration rule: "the correct dose should be determined by taking about 10 mg. at a time allowing it to spread into the membranes of the mouth, and repeating the dose after 10 minutes until the symptoms are controlled." The endpoint is symptomatic, not numerical.

For anovulatory or irregular cycles, the day-14 start is a guess; Peat's [[progesterone|email exchange]] calendar fallback applies: "just starting the progesterone could renew the rhythm." Approximate a two-weeks-on, two-weeks-off schedule on the typical 28-day calendar at the same 10–15 mg/day baseline. **Perimenopausal dosing** holds the cyclic pattern (see [[menopause]]); **postmenopausal dosing** can be cyclic or continuous at the low floor of 10 mg/day in slender women.

The dose ceiling is set by symptom, not by toxicity. "Occasionally, a woman won't feel any effect even from 100 mg. of progesterone; I think this indicates that they need to use thyroid and diet, to normalize their estrogen, prolactin, and cortisol" ([[progesterone-summaries|"Progesterone Summaries"]]). When progesterone has no clear effect even at large doses, the bottleneck is upstream — usually [[hypothyroidism]] and tissue estrogen loading.

## Routes: oral vs. topical vs. sublingual/gum

The route determines the pharmacokinetics, and the formulation determines whether a given route works at all. Peat's framework has three parts: vitamin-E-dissolved progesterone works by *any* route (oral, sublingual, gum, transdermal, vaginal, rectal); cream-based and oil-based products do *not* work transdermally because progesterone crystallizes out of those vehicles; and the conventional textbook claim that oral progesterone is destroyed by first-pass hepatic metabolism is empirically wrong when the carrier is right.

The first-pass dogma is the key inversion. Conventional pharmacology textbooks describe oral progesterone as essentially inactive because the liver clears 90%+ on first pass. Peat's [[oral-progesterone-is-not-inactivated-by-stomach-acids-pancreatic-enzymes-or-liver-detoxification|1995 *Townsend Letter*]] dismantled this directly: "Dentali invokes the 'first pass' dogma of liver inactivation, but he neglects the whole point of what I said, namely, that chylomicrons are not consumed on the first pass though the liver. Progesterone equilibrates from the chylomicrons into the other fractions of the blood, and a large part of it is carried in the red blood cells."

The mechanism is lipid trafficking. Progesterone dissolved in vitamin E enters the intestinal lymphatics packaged in chylomicrons — fat droplets smaller than red blood cells — and passes through the liver intact. Standard cellulose-based progesterone capsules (the "micronized progesterone" of conventional pharmacy practice) deliver crystalline progesterone that the liver clears almost completely on first pass. Same molecule, different vehicle, different fate.

Sublingual and buccal (gum) administration are the fastest routes. The oral mucosa is thin, vascular, and drains directly into systemic circulation, bypassing the gut and liver entirely. From [[progesterone-summaries|"Progesterone Summaries"]]: "When dissolved in vitamin E, progesterone begins entering the blood stream almost as soon as it contacts any membrane, such as the lips, tongue, gums, or palate." Peat's preferred application: **a few drops rubbed into the gums** — "It is good for the general health of the gums." Clinical onset is often within 20–30 minutes for acute symptoms. The [[oral-absorption-of-progesterone|1986 JAMA letter]] showed a fasting man taking 100 mg sublingually went from 0.2 ng/mL baseline to 7 ng/mL at hour one, peaking at 14 ng/mL by hour six.

Swallowed-with-food administration gives a slower, longer release: "Some will enter your blood stream very quickly from the mouth membranes, but taking it with food the effect will be more gradual and prolonged" ([[progesterone|email exchange]]). The progesterone partitions into the dietary fat and is absorbed via the chylomicron pathway — appropriate for steady-state luteal support.

Transdermal application requires the vitamin-E vehicle. From [[concerns-about-progesterone-cream-and-yam-extracts|"Concerns About Progesterone Cream and Yam Extracts" (1996)]]: "Crystalline progesterone isn't available transdermally, and when it is ingested, it is quickly metabolized by the bowel and the liver, into a derivative of progesterone... chemically and biologically distinct from progesterone." Most commercial creams sold as "progesterone cream" contain crystalline progesterone suspended in a cold-cream base, visible "in a thin film... under a microscope" and biologically unavailable through skin. For topical use, vitamin-E-dissolved progesterone is applied to thin-skinned areas (inner forearms, throat, abdomen), preceded by olive oil to ease spreading.

Oral cellulose capsules — including most "compounded bioidentical progesterone" prescriptions — sit in the worst category: crystalline progesterone, no lipid carrier, near-complete first-pass clearance, serum effect dominated by the metabolite 5α-pregnanedione rather than progesterone itself. A woman swallowing a 100 mg cellulose capsule and a woman applying 5 mg in vitamin E to her gums are doing fundamentally different things, and the smaller buccal dose often produces a larger physiological effect.

The route hierarchy in practice: **gum/sublingual first** for acute symptoms and standard luteal dosing; **with food** for steady-state luteal support; **transdermal** for skin and local applications (sunburn, arthritis, mastalgia); **rectal or vaginal** for situations where oral routes are impractical. All four require the vitamin-E carrier.

## Men's dosing

From [[some-effects-of-progesterone-in-men|"Some Effects of Progesterone in Men" (2021)]]: "Classifying progesterone as a 'pregnancy hormone' and a 'female sex hormone' has seriously limited understanding of its function in both men and women. It is a stabilizer of cellular structure and function in all organs, similarly in men and women." Progesterone deficiency in men produces erectile dysfunction, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and the inflammatory/prostatic cluster of midlife.

The dose range is small. Peat's clinical reference: **5–6 mg as a single dose, "a drop of Progest-E the size of an unpopped kernel of popcorn."** Action noticeable within half an hour; effects from one dose can last "a few days or more." For chronic inflammation, stress, or sleep: **1–3 drops Progest-E (3–10 mg) daily or as needed**.

The mechanism in men runs partly through aldosterone antagonism: "Increased aldosterone is associated with erectile dysfunction... and it interacts locally as a testosterone antagonist. In this, and other situations involving testosterone antagonists (estrogen, cortisol, aldosterone), progesterone can have a directly testosterone-supporting effect." Peat's clinical anecdote: a man who became impotent after vasectomy recovered erectile function from a single dose; "other young men with erectile dysfunction, from other causes, had similarly good results with small amounts of progesterone."

The feminization concern is dose-dependent, not categorical. Peat distinguished between the small physiological doses (1–3 drops, 3–10 mg) that *support* testosterone by antagonizing aldosterone, cortisol, and estrogen, and the larger doses that compete with testosterone directly: "At a certain level, progesterone can antagonize the effects of testosterone; in younger men, 2 or 3 drops can have that effect. That effect passes within a day of stopping the progesterone." Younger men with intact testosterone production sit at the lower end; men past 40 use the 5–6 mg dose without feminization risk because they are replacing a deficiency rather than competing with abundant endogenous testosterone.

Transdermal application in young men carries one specific warning: "If a younger man uses progesterone topically, for example for sunburn, a little olive oil should be applied to the skin first to make it spread easily; if it's used on a large area, the same anti-testosterone effect is likely to be noticed." Large-area transdermal absorption delivers a higher systemic dose than the same volume in the gums.

For post-finasteride syndrome and other 5α-reductase-blockade syndromes, progesterone supplementation alone does not bypass the block — the GABA-A action runs through [[allopregnanolone]]. Men with PFS often need pregnenolone and progesterone together, plus inhibitor removal, to restore neurosteroid synthesis.

The male analog of the cyclic luteal dose, without the cycle: a drop in the gums in the evening on a stressful day, restoring the buffer that endogenous production used to supply.

## The vitamin-E carrier

The dissolution of progesterone in vitamin E is not a marketing flourish. It is the chemical step that allows the molecule to do what it is supposed to do, and it is the patent Peat held from the 1970s. From [[oral-progesterone-is-not-inactivated-by-stomach-acids-pancreatic-enzymes-or-liver-detoxification|"Oral Progesterone is Not Inactivated"]]: "My patent is based on the fact that vitamin E stably holds progesterone in solution, so that it can be assimilated effectively by any route, transdermally, vaginally, or orally."

Progesterone is a small lipophilic steroid: essentially insoluble in water and only sparingly soluble in common vegetable oils. In olive or safflower oil, dissolved progesterone slowly crystallizes out at room temperature — the "bizarre crystallization" Peat documented in commercial products. Once crystallized, the molecule cannot cross epithelial membranes; it is biologically unavailable. Vitamin E (tocopherols, mostly d-alpha and mixed) holds progesterone in stable solution indefinitely.

The biological synergy is independent of the solubility benefit. Vitamin E is itself an anti-estrogen — Peat called it the "antisterility vitamin" — and it spares progesterone from oxidative degradation during storage and after absorption. Both molecules "prevent oxygen waste and appear to improve mitochondrial coupling of phosphorylation with respiration" ([[progesterone-summaries|"Progesterone Summaries"]]). The combination is more than additive: vitamin E protects tissue from estrogen-driven peroxidation while progesterone destroys the estrogen receptor that drives the peroxidation. See [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]] for the full case.

The cream critique is specific. From [[concerns-about-progesterone-cream-and-yam-extracts|"Concerns About Progesterone Cream and Yam Extracts" (1996)]]: "the studies of topical progesterone, by Papa and Kligman, showed that progesterone had a local effect, but no detectable systemic effect. This is because of its low solubility in ordinary oils." A cold-cream base does not hold progesterone in solution; the progesterone crystallizes out and sits on the skin surface. Local effect real, systemic effect absent. "Wild yam" creams contain no progesterone at all: "If progesterone is 'a yam extract,' then so is estrogen, and so are the anabolic steroids... and the contraceptive steroids." Diosgenin is a chemical starting point for industrial steroid synthesis; the human body cannot convert dietary or topical diosgenin into progesterone.

The implication: read the label, look for vitamin E listed in proportion to progesterone (the 10% Progest-E formulation is ~1 g progesterone per 9 g vitamin E), and reject opaque containers that hide crystallization.

## Fertility and early-pregnancy dosing

Luteal-phase progesterone is the dose that bridges conception and the placental hand-off. The corpus luteum produces progesterone for the first 10 weeks of pregnancy; the placenta takes over at 10–12 weeks; the most common iatrogenic miscarriage cause is tapering progesterone during that hand-off window. See [[fertility]] and [[pregnancy-nutrition]] for the full both-partners framework.

The dose ladder runs from prophylactic to therapeutic:

- **Luteal-phase support (BBT thermal shift, trying to conceive):** 20–40 mg/day, divided morning and evening, on gums, from thermal shift through menstruation. If pregnancy is confirmed, continue through week 12 minimum.
- **Short luteal phase (<10 days), prior early miscarriage, threatened miscarriage:** 100–200 mg/day under physician supervision. The [[oral-absorption-of-progesterone|1986 JAMA letter]] documented this range as the dose that brings serum progesterone in luteal-deficient women up to the normal luteal range and holds it there for at least 12 hours.
- **Anovulatory or irregular cycles:** the calendar fallback — "if there's no cycle you can detect, just starting the progesterone could renew the rhythm" ([[progesterone|email]]). Approximate a 14-on, 14-off schedule on the 28-day calendar.

Timing matters as much as dose. From the same email: "I think the symptoms of increased estrogen and cortisol are the result of... using it at the wrong time, for example, too early, triggering premature ovulation." Progesterone before ovulation suppresses the LH surge and can prevent the cycle's own ovulation. The rule is **not before the thermal shift**. For women without reliable BBT, use the conservative calendar rule (start day 14, or 14 days before expected next period).

The off-week in the cyclic pattern lets brain progesterone receptors resensitize and permits the next ovulation to proceed normally. Continuous use through the cycle sometimes produces suppressed ovulation, irregular periods, and paradoxical symptom return.

For threatened miscarriage with active bleeding or cramping: 100–200 mg/day, divided 4–6 times across the day, on gums and with food, until bleeding stops; then taper toward 20–40 mg/day; continue through week 12. From [[aging-ovaries-not-the-eggs|"Aging Ovaries, Not the Eggs"]]: "Even in young individuals, when stress occurs around the time of ovulation, interference with progesterone production will prevent implantation. If progesterone becomes deficient after the embryo has become implanted, miscarriage occurs."

## Dalton prenatal protocols

Katharina Dalton, the British physician who built the postwar evidence base for progesterone therapy, treated severe PMS, threatened miscarriage, toxemia of pregnancy, and postpartum depression with high-dose progesterone injections from the 1950s through the 1990s. Peat drew on her work throughout his corpus, both for the clinical doses and for the longitudinal outcomes data that no controlled trial has duplicated. See [[katharina-dalton]] for the biographical entry; this section covers what Peat extracted clinically.

Dalton's pregnancy-protection protocol had three phases.

**First, prevention through pregnancy** for women with prior toxemia, prior miscarriage, or severe non-pregnant PMS: 100–400 mg/day from before conception through delivery, by injection or suppository (and, after Peat's formulation became available, by buccal Progest-E). From [[eclampsia-in-the-real-organism-a-paradigm-of-general-distress-applicable-in-infants-adults-etc|"Eclampsia in the Real Organism" (1997)]]: "Katherina Dalton observed that her patients who suffered from PMS... were likely to develop 'toxemia' when they became pregnant... a single injection of progesterone could sometimes eliminate the signs of toxemia for the remainder of the pregnancy."

**Second, cyclic-bleeding response** — what Dalton called "menstruation continuing through pregnancy" — treated as a progesterone-deficiency emergency. From the same newsletter: "This cyclic bleeding seems to represent times of an increased ratio of estrogen to progesterone, and during such periods of cyclic bleeding the risk of miscarriage is high." Dose: upper-end threatened-miscarriage range, 200–400 mg/day, continued until cyclic bleeding stopped, then through week 20.

**Third, postpartum depression prevention**. Estrogen falls catastrophically after delivery; progesterone falls with it; allopregnanolone — the GABA-A modulator that protects against anxiety and depression — collapses within hours. Dalton's protocol: **100–400 mg/day for the first 8–12 weeks postpartum** in women with prior postpartum depression. See [[allopregnanolone]] — the postpartum allopregnanolone collapse is the proximate cause of postpartum depression, and progesterone restores the substrate. Peat extended this with the observation that "postpartum psychosis" sometimes reflects micro-strokes from the prothrombotic late-pregnancy state.

Dalton's IQ outcome was the most striking finding. From [[2012-01-22-politics-and-science-politics-science-progesterone-part-1|the 2012 *Politics & Science* episode]]: "Her patients' babies, who usually came from working-class backgrounds with average IQs of 90, tended to average 130 IQ after progesterone treatment. The intelligence of the babies corresponded to the amount of progesterone she gave the mothers." A forty-point dose-responsive shift is not attributable to socioeconomic confounding.

The mechanism runs through fetal brain protection: estrogen during gestation reduces cortical size in rats (Marian Diamond's work), progesterone increases it. Clinical translation: **progesterone is the prenatal supplement no clinic recommends and the one most likely to be needed** in any pregnancy with prior toxemia, prior miscarriage, severe PMS history, or hypothyroidism. Coordinate with an obstetric provider; the dose ranges here are above the 200 mg/day typical in mainstream high-risk-pregnancy protocols. The Dalton ranges scale directly into [[pregnancy-nutrition]] and [[fertility]] via the buccal Progest-E route.
