# Prolactin

Prolactin is known as the milk-producing hormone, but in Ray Peat's framework it functions primarily as a stress marker—a hormone whose chronic elevation signals metabolic dysfunction, [[estrogen]] dominance, and progressive degeneration. Prolactin rises steadily with [[aging]], amplifies estrogen's harmful effects, and promotes the tissue changes associated with [[cancer]], [[inflammation]], and immune suppression. Its quiet, persistent elevation is so common in modern populations that the "normal" reference range has been raised to accommodate what is actually a widespread pathology.

## A Stress Hormone That Increases With Age

Prolactin's rise with aging parallels the decline in protective hormones. As Peat explained in a [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 episode on estrogen versus progesterone]], "Prolactin is a stress-reacting hormone that increases steadily with aging." This increase is not benign—it reflects and reinforces the metabolic decline that characterizes the aging process.

The mechanism is tied to estrogen. As [[estrogen]] rises relative to [[progesterone]]—which it does progressively from the late 30s onward—prolactin follows. In a [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 episode on endocrinology]], Peat described how "old men get low testosterone because stressed tissue converts it to estrogen. High estrogen brings up prolactin, growth hormone, and adaptive secondary hormones." The estrogen-prolactin axis operates in both sexes, driving the same pattern of tissue degeneration.

## The Pituitary Tumor Epidemic

One of the most dramatic consequences of chronic prolactin elevation is pituitary tumor formation. As Peat noted, there has been an "epidemic of prolactin-secreting pituitary tumors from contraceptive estrogen." Oral contraceptives, by maintaining chronic estrogen elevation, stimulate the pituitary's prolactin-producing cells to the point of tumor formation.

These prolactinomas can press on the optic nerve, causing visual disturbances that are sometimes mistaken for neurological disease. In Peat's clinical experience, some of these cases responded to [[thyroid]] supplementation—which lowered estrogen and prolactin—rather than requiring surgery or dopamine agonist drugs.

The reference range tells a revealing story. As Peat described, "The normal range was lowered 30 years ago, then raised again because the average person is now hyperprolactinemic." Rather than recognizing widespread pathological elevation, the medical system adjusted its definition of normal upward to match the increasingly sick population.

## Synergy With Estrogen

Prolactin and estrogen operate synergistically. Estrogen stimulates prolactin release from the pituitary, and prolactin amplifies estrogen's effects in tissues. In his articles, Peat cited research showing that "prolactin and estrogen have synergistic effects in causing tissue degeneration, including cancerization, and that their effects tend to operate with fewer protective restraining influences in old age."

This synergy explains why cancer risk increases so dramatically after middle age. With protective hormones (progesterone, thyroid, DHEA) declining and stress hormones (estrogen, prolactin, cortisol) rising, the balance shifts decisively toward tissue degeneration. The prolactin-estrogen axis goes unopposed precisely when the body is least equipped to handle its effects.

## TSH and Prolactin: The Thyroid Connection

Prolactin and TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) tend to rise together, reflecting a common cause: inadequate thyroid function. As Peat noted in his emails, "Prolactin and TSH tend to increase together." When [[thyroid]] output is insufficient, the pituitary increases both TSH (trying to stimulate more thyroid production) and prolactin (a general stress response).

This connection means that elevated prolactin often responds to thyroid correction. Improving thyroid function reduces the pituitary's stress response, lowering both TSH and prolactin simultaneously. The conventional treatment—dopamine agonists like bromocriptine or cabergoline—addresses the prolactin elevation without correcting its cause.

## Lowering Prolactin

Peat's approach targets the upstream causes:

**[[Thyroid]] support** is primary. Adequate thyroid function reduces the pituitary stress that drives prolactin elevation. Since thyroid also lowers estrogen (by supporting liver metabolism), it addresses both sides of the estrogen-prolactin axis.

**Salt** lowers prolactin through mechanisms Peat discussed repeatedly. Adequate sodium supports blood volume, reduces stress hormones, and directly suppresses prolactin secretion.

**[[Vitamin E]]** may help lower prolactin. Its anti-estrogenic effects reduce the estrogen stimulation that drives prolactin production.

**[[Progesterone]]** opposes estrogen's stimulation of prolactin. In women, adequate progesterone during the luteal phase and after menopause keeps the estrogen-prolactin axis in check.

**Reducing [[PUFA]]** lowers the inflammatory signals that stimulate both estrogen (via aromatase) and prolactin. As Peat noted in a [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 episode]], "Cutting out PUFA is key to decreasing estrogen via aromatase inhibition."

**[[Aspirin]]** blocks aromatase—the enzyme producing estrogen in tissues—reducing the estrogen stimulation of prolactin.
