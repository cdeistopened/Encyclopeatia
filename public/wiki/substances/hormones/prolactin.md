# Prolactin

Prolactin is almost universally described as the "milk hormone," a benign substance whose only meaningful role is enabling lactation. Ray Peat saw it very differently. In his framework, prolactin is a stress hormone with an ancient evolutionary lineage, one that rises with [[estrogen]], [[serotonin]], darkness, and [[hypothyroidism]], and whose tissue-destructive effects touch nearly every system in the body. It dissolves bone, thins hair, enlarges prostates, shrinks fertility, and promotes cancer. Its role in lactation is real but incidental to its deeper function, which Peat traced all the way back to fish physiology: the regulation of salt, water, and the body's response to environmental shock. The reason prolactin's damaging effects have been obscured for decades, in Peat's telling, is straightforward. Estrogen increases prolactin. Acknowledging prolactin's harms would mean acknowledging estrogen's harms, and the estrogen industry could not afford that.

In a [[2013-04-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hormone-replacement-therapy|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hormone replacement therapy]], Peat stated the problem plainly: "Prolactin had many toxic effects that were well recognized. But since estrogen increases prolactin, there was great pressure not to think about the fact that prolactin causes bone loss and contributes to osteoporosis." That pressure shaped decades of medical research, redirecting attention away from the hormones that actually cause degenerative disease and toward the very substances that promote them.

## The Stress Hormone in Disguise

Prolactin's identity as a stress hormone becomes obvious once you step outside the mammalian obsession with milk. In fish, prolactin regulates salt and water metabolism. When Pacific salmon swim from the ocean into freshwater rivers to spawn, the sudden absence of salt triggers a massive prolactin surge. As Peat described in his [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|"Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis" (1985)]] newsletter, "the fresh water, which is very sodium deficient compared to the ocean, does stimulate the secretion of prolactin, and the prolactin is involved in their adaptation to the fresh water." The adaptation is catastrophic: "These fish undergo very rapid aging. Their bones are transformed in ways very similar to the bones of other animals in extreme old age; for example, the back humps and the jaw juts forward."

Peat returned to the salmon example throughout his career because it illustrated prolactin's fundamental nature so vividly. On the *Generative Energy* podcast, he described the sequence in metabolic terms: "When a salmon comes from the ocean and confronts the absence of salt in river water, they have a shock reaction, an estrogenic reaction, and put out a burst of prolactin. Like [[parathyroid hormone]], this dissolves their bones and gives them an immediate old person humpback." The parallel to human aging was not metaphorical. Estrogen increases water retention while losing sodium, producing the same hypotonic internal environment that river water imposes on salmon. "Estrogen increases our water retention and imitates the condition of a salmon hitting fresh water. The shock of that hypotonic condition turns on prolactin."

In mammals, the components of lactation represent a specialized application of this ancient fluid-regulation system. As Peat explained in a [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on endocrinology]], "In fish, prolactin regulates salt and water metabolism. Mammals have the various components of milk formation involving the flow of water, salts, proteins, and sugar. Prolactin in all animals has a range of anti-stress functions, and lactation is just a useful component for handling the stress of pregnancy."

The stress-responsive nature of prolactin puts it in the same category as [[cortisol]], [[growth hormone]], and [[adrenaline]]. All of these pituitary-driven hormones rise during the night, during illness, during emotional distress, and during any condition that depresses [[thyroid]] function. Peat noted in his [[growth-hormone|"Growth Hormone" (2013)]] newsletter that "the increase of GH during sleep might seem to be utterly incompatible with the idea that it is a stress hormone, but in fact the other stress hormones, adrenalin, cortisol, and prolactin also tend to increase during night-time sleep. Thyroid function and progesterone function decrease at night." Darkness itself is a stressor in Peat's framework, and prolactin is one of the hormones that proves it.

## The Estrogen-Serotonin-Prolactin Loop

One of the most clinically important features of prolactin is that it participates in a self-amplifying stress cascade with estrogen and serotonin. Each of these substances stimulates the production of the others, creating a vicious cycle that can be difficult to break once it is established.

The loop works like this. Estrogen stimulates serotonin production and increases the body's exposure to serotonin. Serotonin, in turn, stimulates prolactin secretion from the pituitary. And prolactin, along with estrogen, promotes tissue degeneration that further impairs thyroid function, which would otherwise keep all three substances in check. As Peat explained in the same 2013 HRT episode: "Serotonin is, in a way, a co-factor for estrogen. Estrogen increases prolactin or serotonin systemically, and serotonin in turn increases the production of estrogen. So when one gets out of control, they both tend to become dominant."

The neurochemical dimension involves the balance between serotonin and [[dopamine]]. In a [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 endocrinology discussion]], Peat described the axis: "Estrogen shifts the balance toward serotonin and away from dopamine. This shift is one of the things that activates the prolactin system." Dopamine is prolactin's natural antagonist. When estrogen pushes the serotonin-dopamine ratio toward serotonin, it simultaneously removes the brake on prolactin while pressing the accelerator. [[Progesterone]] opposes this shift: "Progesterone and its derivatives in the brain, like allopregnenolone, are important for keeping the balance in the right direction. Estrogen increases serotonin and stress; progesterone tends to reverse that and increase the balance toward dopamine."

The loop has a direct mechanism through the body's fluid chemistry. Peat proposed in his "Growth Hormone" newsletter that "since estrogen causes a slight tendency to retain water while losing sodium, producing hypotonic body fluids, and since hypotonicity is a sufficient stimulus to cause prolactin secretion, I have proposed that it is estrogen's effect on the body fluids which causes it to stimulate prolactin." This connects the hormonal cascade to the basic physics of cell hydration: estrogen dilutes the internal environment, and the dilution itself triggers prolactin, just as freshwater triggers it in salmon.

## Bone Loss and Osteoporosis

The relationship between prolactin and bone loss may be the most consequential fact in endocrinology that mainstream medicine has chosen to ignore. Prolactin dissolves bone. Estrogen increases prolactin. Yet for forty years, estrogen has been sold as the treatment for osteoporosis.

Peat documented this contradiction meticulously. In his [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|"Osteoporosis, Aging, Tissue Renewal and Product Science"]] newsletter, he traced the history: "In the 1960s and '70s the estrogen industry was looking for ways to build on the knowledge that in puberty estrogen is responsible for accelerating the calcification of the growth plate at the ends of the long bones, and to find a rationale for selling estrogen to all women concerned with the problems of aging." The industry found that estrogen slows osteoclasts, the cells that break down bone, and built an entire marketing campaign around this single mechanism. "Although estrogen was known to increase prolactin, and prolactin was known to accelerate bone loss, nearly all publications began to focus on substances in the blood or urine that corresponded to the rate of bone turnover, with the implication that increasing bone turnover would correspond to a net loss of bone."

The logic is circular and self-serving. Slowing bone turnover looks good on a lab test, but living bone requires constant remodeling. An agent that slows both breakdown and rebuilding does not strengthen bone; it creates old, brittle bone that cannot repair microdamage. Meanwhile, the prolactin that estrogen stimulates is actively pulling calcium out of the skeleton.

Peat quoted the editor of the *Yearbook of Endocrinology* (1984) in his "Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis" newsletter, who had stated the obvious: "Consider, if hyperprolactinemia leads to osteoporosis, and the administration of estrogens to postmenopausal women leads to hyperprolactinemia..." The ellipsis was the editor's, not Peat's. The implication needed no further spelling out.

The mechanism is not limited to estrogen. Serotonin also increases prolactin, and serotonin has its own bone-destroying effects. In the osteoporosis newsletter, Peat noted that "a few years ago, the serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressants, already known to increase prolactin by increasing the effects of serotonin, were found to be causing osteoporosis after prolonged use." The SSRIs cause bone loss through serotonin, through prolactin, and through their suppression of thyroid function. Peat cited researchers who said they "rediscovered whole animal physiology" when they realized the intestine was the source of the serotonin causing the osteoporosis.

The dietary connection runs through [[calcium]]. In a [[2011-08-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-milk|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on milk]], Peat explained: "One of the factors in osteoporosis is high cortisol or high prolactin. Both of those are caused by high serotonin. Serotonin, prolactin, and cortisol are all anti-bone formation. Calcium in your diet suppresses prolactin, cortisol, and serotonin, allowing the bone-building factors to be dominant." This is why Peat recommended high-calcium diets, centered on [[milk]] and [[cheese]], as the foundation of bone health rather than estrogen supplementation.

Prolactin's bone-dissolving function makes biological sense in the context of lactation. A nursing mother needs to mobilize calcium from her skeleton to produce calcium-rich milk. But outside of that narrow context, elevated prolactin simply destroys bone tissue. As Peat noted in a [[2019-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-tryptophan|2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on tryptophan]]: "Prolactin has a bone-weakening effect. It normally takes calcium out of the bone to put into milk, but under stress, it causes calcium loss."

Some researchers have noticed that estrogen's prolactin-mediated bone destruction extends to unexpected tissues. Peat mentioned in the osteoporosis newsletter that "some people have noticed that oral contraceptives and menopausal hormone treatments have damaged the bones of the inner ear, causing otosclerosis and impaired hearing, and have suggested that prolactin mediates the effect."

## Hair Loss: The "Molting Hormone"

Mainstream medicine attributes male pattern baldness to testosterone, or more precisely to dihydrotestosterone (DHT). Peat considered this attribution to be almost entirely wrong, analogous to the discredited claim that testosterone causes prostate cancer. The actual hormonal driver of hair loss, in his view, is prolactin.

In a [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hair loss]], Peat dismantled the testosterone theory: "I think the belief in testosterone as the cause is similar to the old belief that males got prostate cancer because of testosterone. Since males get a particular kind of baldness, it's easy to blame it on testosterone. But in fact, there is no evidence showing that excess testosterone is responsible for it, any more than excess testosterone causes prostate cancer." The concept of "increased sensitivity" to testosterone was invented, Peat argued, because the straightforward evidence for excess testosterone did not exist. "That fills in for a lack of evidence."

What the evidence actually shows is a different hormonal picture entirely: "When you look at the actual hormone situation of people with lots of hair versus those with not much, you see an excess of prolactin and cortisol in the people losing their hair, both men and women. Testosterone actually makes the hair shaft grow thicker and faster. Prolactin tends to cause it to fall out."

Peat drew on comparative biology to make the point. "In birds, prolactin is known as the molting hormone. In humans, it is the milk-producing hormone, but it also regulates practically every other cell in the body. It has the analogous function of terminating the growth cycle of the hair shaft." Just as prolactin mobilizes calcium from bone for milk production, it terminates hair growth cycles as part of its broader tissue-remodeling stress response.

The connection extends to both sexes and to seemingly opposite hair problems. In the same episode, Peat noted that "both hirsutism and hair loss are associated with high prolactin." The paradox resolves when you understand that prolactin disrupts the normal cycling of hair follicles in multiple ways, sometimes accelerating growth in the wrong places while terminating it in others.

## Gynecomastia and Male Fertility

For men, prolactin levels provide one of the clearest windows into overall hormonal health. Peat was specific about the numbers. In the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 hair loss episode]], he stated: "Back in the 70s, they found the healthy range for women was around 12 to 15, and for men, it was about 4 to 7. They've now raised the upper limit for men sometimes as high as 20."

The expansion of the "normal" range is itself a diagnostic problem. Peat observed that lab reference ranges track population averages, and when the population becomes sicker, the ranges expand to accommodate the sickness. "All the men I've talked to who had levels close to 20, even though they were told they were normal, had developing breasts, or gynecomastia. Fertility is best when a man has a level of 4 to 7."

The mechanism connecting high prolactin to male breast development and infertility runs through the same stress cascade that affects every other tissue. As Peat explained in the 2017 endocrinology episode: "Old men get low testosterone because their stressed tissue is turning it quickly into estrogen. A younger man who is very sick or has an accident will have very high estrogen. That will usually bring up prolactin, growth hormone, and a lot of adaptive secondary hormones." The sequence is: stress raises estrogen, estrogen raises prolactin, and elevated prolactin further suppresses the pituitary's production of luteinizing hormone (LH), which is needed to produce testosterone. The result is a man with high estrogen, high prolactin, low testosterone, developing breasts, and declining fertility, who is told by his doctor that all his hormone levels are "normal."

In his email exchanges, Peat recommended specific prolactin targets for men: the level should be between 4 and 7. In cases where a woman's high prolactin was causing infertility, as described in a [[2018-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-1|2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]], Peat recommended watching the TSH first: "You should first see the TSH go down close to zero, because the same serotonin which rises in hypothyroidism activates the production of prolactin. The TSH descent will be followed by a slower lowering of the prolactin."

## Pituitary Tumors and Prolactinoma

The most dramatic evidence for prolactin's connection to estrogen came from the birth control pill. In a 2019 *One Radio Network* episode, Peat described what happened: "Starting in about 1965, when the birth control pills came on the market, there was suddenly about a hundredfold increase in the incidence of pituitary tumors that secrete prolactin." A hundredfold increase is not a subtle epidemiological signal. The synthetic estrogens in oral contraceptives were stimulating the prolactin-secreting cells of the pituitary to divide uncontrollably, forming prolactinomas.

Peat explained in the [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 *EastWest Healing* interview on estrogen vs. progesterone]] that the reference ranges had to be adjusted: "The normal range for prolactin was lowered about 30 years ago because they saw that the top number was increased by women using birth control pills. There was an epidemic of prolactin-secreting pituitary tumors resulting from contraceptive estrogen exposure." Then, as the general population became more stressed and hyperprolactinemic, the range crept back up. "Just a few years ago, the top number was raised again in some labs because the average person is now in a stressed, hyperprolactinemic condition. Don't feel reassured if your prolactin is 'normal' by today's standards; everything is under stress when it's that high."

The mechanism connects estrogen to pituitary cell division. As Peat wrote in his [[menopause-and-its-causes|"Menopause and Its Causes"]] newsletter: "Other researchers have pointed out that estrogen dominance promotes mitosis of the prolactin-secreting cells of the pituitary, and that prolactin causes osteoporosis; by age 50, most people have some degree of tumefaction of the prolactin-secreting part of the pituitary."

[[Bromocriptine]], a dopamine agonist, is effective at shrinking prolactinomas precisely because dopamine is the natural inhibitor of prolactin. Peat noted in a [[2011-05-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-misconceptions-relating-to-serotonin-and-melatonin|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on serotonin and melatonin]]: "Bromocriptine is another one used to treat pituitary tumors because prolactin is promoted by stress, serotonin, or radiation."

## The CO2 and Hyperventilation Connection

One of Peat's most original contributions to understanding prolactin was his explanation of how breathing patterns affect its secretion. The connection runs through [[carbon dioxide]], blood pH, and platelet serotonin.

In the 2019 *One Radio Network* episode, Peat described the discovery process: "I realized that this has been known as an effect of strenuous exercising. When you breathe very intensely, your blood can become very over-oxygenated. They know that increases prolactin as a stress reaction hormone." Researchers had tested pure oxygen administration and found it dramatically raised prolactin. But the mechanism was not the oxygen itself.

"I realized that what is happening is the lack of carbon dioxide in the blood when you are being treated with oxygen, or just hyperventilating, raises the pH of your blood. This causes the platelets to release their serotonin, and serotonin drives up prolactin." The chain is: hyperventilation blows off CO2, alkaline blood triggers platelet serotonin release, serotonin stimulates the pituitary, and prolactin rises.

This connects to estrogen through thyroid function. "Estrogen causes you to hyperventilate and interferes with your thyroid gland. The thyroid makes you produce carbon dioxide, which is an anti-serotonin agent. So if you are high in estrogen or low in thyroid function, you're going to have effectively a shortage of carbon dioxide and an excess of prolactin and serotonin." The practical implication is that anything that supports CO2 retention (adequate thyroid function, proper breathing, avoiding extreme exercise) will tend to suppress prolactin.

Peat also addressed the common objection that platelet serotonin should not be able to affect the brain. "People have rejected that idea because they believed there was a blood-brain barrier that would keep the platelet serotonin out of the brain. But in fact, I've been going over the evidence that there is no blood-brain barrier. When you have too much estrogen, for example, the enzymes in the capillaries of the brain lose their ability to detoxify serotonin, and so it goes right from your blood into your brain."

## Cancer and Prostate Growth

Prolactin's role in cancer follows from its general tissue-destructive properties. In his [[growth-hormone|"Growth Hormone" (2013)]] newsletter, Peat wrote: "Many experiments show that prolactin and estrogen have synergistic effects in causing tissue degeneration, including cancerization, and that their effects tend to operate with fewer protective restraining influences in old age."

The prostate is a particularly clear example. In his [[prostate-cancer|"Prostate Cancer"]] newsletter, Peat documented that "prolactin, which is increased by estrogen, stimulates growth of the rat's lateral prostate, and stimulates the growth of human prostate epithelial cells *in vitro*." He noted that the standard medical narrative blaming testosterone for prostate cancer is contradicted by the data: "In human prostate slices, several hormones (including insulin, and probably prolactin) stimulated cell division; testosterone did not, under these experimental conditions." And survival data pointed in the same direction: "A high ratio of testosterone to estradiol or of testosterone to prolactin corresponded to better survival."

The age-related convergence of rising prolactin and falling testosterone explains why prostate problems emerge in middle age: "By the age of 50, men often show an excess of both prolactin and estrogen, and a deficiency of thyroid and testosterone. This is the age at which enlargement of the prostate often becomes noticeable."

In his [[energy-entropy-and-estrogens-in-aging|"Energy, Entropy and Estrogens in Aging"]] newsletter, Peat placed prolactin in the broader aging cascade: "On the systemic level in aging, estrogenic effects are evident: a tendency toward prolactinoma, toxic effects of prolactin on kidneys and other organs affecting water balance, disturbance of thyroid function, adrenal compensation or exhaustion, atrophy of muscle, skin, and other tissues."

## Prolactin and Metabolic Energy

At the cellular level, prolactin shifts metabolism away from efficient oxidative energy production toward the inefficient glycolytic pathway. On the *Generative Energy* podcast, Peat explained: "Like parathyroid hormone, it turns off oxidative energy production and shifts over to lactic acid production. You get a substitution of protective carbon dioxide, which hardens the bone tissue and keeps soft tissue soft, with lactate, which softens the bone tissue and tends to calcify your arteries."

This metabolic shift connects prolactin to the broader Peat framework in which [[lactic-acid]] production signals cellular stress and CO2 production signals cellular health. Prolactin does not merely cause isolated problems in specific tissues; it shifts the entire organism toward a stress metabolism that accelerates aging across every system.

The [[arachidonic acid]] connection adds another layer. Peat noted in his osteoporosis newsletter that "arachidonic acid stimulates prolactin secretion, and prolactin acts on the thyroid gland to decrease its activity, and on other tissues to increase their glycolysis (with lactate production), while decreasing oxidative metabolism." [[PUFA]] consumption thus feeds directly into the prolactin problem through arachidonic acid, creating yet another self-reinforcing loop.

## Spontaneous Lactation and Hypothyroidism

One of the most reliable clinical indicators of elevated prolactin is milk production in women who are not breastfeeding. Peat reported encountering this frequently. In the [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 endocrinology episode]], he said: "I have talked to probably 100 women where hypothyroidism was their basic problem, but unwelcome lactation was one of their symptoms. Usually, there were menstrual irregularities at the same time, either too much or too little menstruation. Just correcting their thyroid function took care of the lactation and menstruation both."

The mechanism involves [[TRH]] (thyrotropin-releasing hormone), which stimulates both TSH and prolactin from the anterior pituitary. When the thyroid gland cannot produce enough hormone to satisfy the body's needs, the hypothalamus produces more TRH, which drives up both TSH and prolactin simultaneously. Peat described it in the [[2013-04-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hormone-replacement-therapy|2013 HRT episode]]: "It is not uncommon for a middle-aged woman to have chronic milk formation associated with the overactivity of the thyroid system because their thyroid gland itself isn't able to produce the active hormones to turn off the pituitary."

The practical lesson is that spontaneous lactation, menstrual irregularities, and elevated prolactin are all symptoms of the same underlying problem: insufficient thyroid function. Treating the prolactin in isolation misses the point.

## The Overactive Pituitary and Lifespan

Peat placed prolactin within a broader argument about the pituitary gland and aging. The pituitary hormones are adaptive in acute stress but destructive when chronically elevated. As he stated in the 2013 HRT episode: "Almost everything the pituitary does has a constructive, adaptive effect to overcome stress. However, in itself, the substances produced in the pituitary are all harmful if they are continued. You want to have such a responsive, high metabolic system that the tissue can respond fully to a little bit of pituitary hormone and turn it off quickly."

The evidence for this comes from hypophysectomy experiments. "Animals that have their pituitaries removed and are given a supplement of cortisol and thyroid will often live two or three times longer than animals with normal pituitary function. This shows that the pituitary, to the extent that it is overactive, is shortening your life and shrinking your potential." Prolactin, along with growth hormone, ACTH, and the other pituitary hormones, belongs to a class of substances that are useful in emergencies but toxic as a chronic condition.

In the "Growth Hormone" newsletter, Peat cited experiments showing that "removal of the pituitary caused retardation of aging similar to food restriction. These pituitary hormones, especially prolactin, are very responsive to food intake." The connection to caloric restriction is significant: much of the benefit attributed to eating less may come simply from suppressing chronically elevated pituitary hormones, particularly prolactin and growth hormone.

## How to Lower Prolactin

Peat's approach to lowering prolactin always began with thyroid function. The logic follows directly from the TRH mechanism: if hypothyroidism drives prolactin up through excess TRH and serotonin, then restoring thyroid function addresses the root cause.

In his email exchanges, Peat wrote: "Either vitex or bromocriptine would probably stop it, but I think it's probably caused by mild hypothyroidism, and that the best way to handle it would be with a thyroid supplement, and that would probably help your libido too." And in the 2018 fertility case: "If there is a tumor secreting it, bromocriptine is almost always successful at shrinking it. But if the tumor isn't very large, you can do it just as reliably by keeping thyroid function a little above normal for two or three weeks."

The monitoring protocol is specific: "Watch the prolactin; you should first see the TSH go down close to zero, because the same serotonin which rises in hypothyroidism activates the production of prolactin. The TSH descent will be followed by a slower lowering of the prolactin." TSH drops first; prolactin follows.

Beyond thyroid, Peat identified several other interventions:

**Salt.** Sodium suppresses prolactin secretion directly, as demonstrated in experiments Peat cited in his "Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis" newsletter. The hypotonic (sodium-deficient) condition that stimulates prolactin is corrected by adequate salt intake.

**Calcium.** As Peat stated in the milk episode: "Calcium in your diet suppresses prolactin, cortisol, and serotonin." In his osteoporosis newsletter, he explained the mechanism: "Calcium activates mitochondrial respiration, and lowers adrenaline, parathyroid hormone, and prolactin."

**[[Vitamin B6]].** In his email correspondence, Peat recommended small doses: "You might want to try a little vitamin B6; even a small amount, about 10 mg per day, can lower prolactin."

**[[Vitamin A]].** In the 2011 hair loss episode, Peat listed vitamin A alongside thyroid and calcium as prolactin-lowering interventions for women with hair loss and hirsutism.

**Bromocriptine.** A dopamine agonist that directly suppresses prolactin secretion. Peat considered it appropriate for prolactinomas and for cases where thyroid correction alone is insufficient.

**High altitude.** Peat noted in his osteoporosis newsletter that "living at high altitude, which strengthens bones, increases thyroid activity and decreases prolactin and parathyroid hormone." The increased CO2 retention and thyroid stimulation at altitude naturally suppress the prolactin-serotonin axis.

**Progesterone.** Listed in the [[comparison-of-progesterone-and-estrogen|"Comparison of Progesterone and Estrogen"]] newsletter as a prolactin inhibitor: "Inhibits prolactin (which stimulates breast milk production)." Progesterone opposes estrogen's prolactin-stimulating effects while also shifting the brain's neurotransmitter balance toward dopamine and away from serotonin.

For breastfeeding women concerned about prolactin's negative effects, Peat offered reassurance with a condition. On the [[2020-02-19-butter-living-podcast-butter-living-podcast-fertility-pregnancy-and-development|2020 *Butter Living Podcast*]], he said: "If you keep your thyroid function up, you won't experience bad effects. Thyroid supports lactation and keeps the pituitary from overproducing stress-induced prolactin."
