# Dopamine

Dopamine is the antagonistic pole to [[serotonin]] in Ray Peat's framework, the neurotransmitter of creative reach and bodily relaxation, and the physiological brake on [[prolactin]]. Almost every conclusion Peat drew about dopamine runs through that antagonism. Where serotonin is the injury signal that shifts metabolism toward glycolysis and locks an animal into harm avoidance, dopamine is the signal of adequate energy and successful activity. The clinical conditions attributed to dopamine deficiency turn out, on closer inspection, to be serotonin excess conditions; the drugs marketed as dopamine agonists turn out to work primarily by blocking serotonin; and attempts to raise dopamine directly tend to damage the very system they are trying to restore. Understanding dopamine, in Peat's reading, means learning not to push it.

## The Antagonistic Pair

Peat treated the monoamines as a dynamic balance maintained against the pull of stress rather than as independent systems with their own diseases. "Dopamine is similar to the adrenaline or sympathetic nervous opposition to the shock tendency," he told Patrick Timpone in a [[2019-09-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fascinating-insights-into-mr-thyroid|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on thyroid]]. "It's the body's balancing factor for serotonin. You want to keep your serotonin down and under control, and then your dopamine will take care of keeping things in balance."

That last phrase captures the practical orientation. Dopamine is not something you push upward; it is the automatic consequence of a system in which serotonin is kept down. In a [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 episode of *Ask the Herb Doctor*]], the reciprocal logic came into view: "Serotonin increases CRH and prolactin; dopamine tends to inhibit them. Nitric oxide, endotoxin, and serotonin all shift your body toward distress. Progesterone and its derivatives in the brain, like allopregnenolone, are important for keeping the balance in the right direction. Estrogen increases serotonin and stress; progesterone tends to reverse that and increase the balance toward dopamine."

The antagonism extends down into personality. "About 35 or 40 years ago, a psychiatrist named Cloninger was defining personality types and relating people to animal studies," Peat recalled in a [[2019-09-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-education-and-reeducation|2019 discussion on education]]. "He proposed that higher serotonin promoted a personality type of 'harm avoidance.' Rather than creative activity, the animals figured out different ways to avoid being hurt. They were fearful and anxious." A dominant, curious, exploratory animal has low serotonin and high dopamine; a defeated, subordinate, fearful animal has the reverse. The distinction between creative reach and harm avoidance is not psychological but chemical. See [[learned-helplessness]] for the full behavioral model.

## Parkinson's: The Serotonin Excess Reframe

Parkinson's disease is the clinical territory where the reframing becomes most consequential. The standard account identifies Parkinson's as a dopamine deficiency caused by the death of dopamine-secreting cells in the substantia nigra, with L-DOPA as the gold standard of treatment since the late 1960s. Peat considered almost every element of that account mistaken.

"Traditionally, they have thought of Parkinson's disease as a deficiency of dopamine," he explained in a [[2021-06-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-june-2021-kmud|June 2021 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]]. "But dopamine and serotonin are an antagonistic pair. The evidence more strongly indicates that Parkinson's is related to a serotonin excess rather than just a dopamine deficiency. A few people have argued that serotonin can't get from your intestines to your brain, but there is evidence that if you have an undetoxified amount of serotonin in the bloodstream, it can get into the brain and shift the balance away from dopamine."

The pharmacology supports the reframe. The [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|2012 newsletter on brain energy]] laid it out bluntly: "In Parkinson's disease, the benefits seen from increasing the concentration of dopamine could result from dopamine's antagonism to serotonin; anti-serotonin drugs can alleviate the symptoms, and 5-hydroxytryptophan can worsen the symptoms. Other movement disorders, including akathisia and chorea, can be produced by serotonin." Giving a Parkinson's patient the direct serotonin precursor makes the tremor worse; giving an anti-serotonin drug makes it better. If dopamine deficiency were the primary problem, neither result should hold.

L-DOPA itself turned out to be a trap. In [[2017-03-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-1-parkinson-s|the first of a 2017 endocrinology trilogy]], Andrew Murray noted that within five years on the drug, most patients develop dyskinesia and end up worse than they would have been untreated. Peat's response was direct: "I think the problem is that the whole theory of what L-DOPA and dopamine are doing is mechanistic and mistaken." The mechanistic framing treats the brain as a machine with a broken part; Peat's alternative treats the substantia nigra as a living tissue with a metabolic problem, in which dopamine is the output of a healthy cell rather than the target to push when the cell is dying.

## What Actually Damages the Substantia Nigra

The dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra are unusually sensitive to oxidative damage. Peat identified several converging sources: polyunsaturated fats and their prostaglandin derivatives, nitric oxide, excess iron, copper deficiency, and excitotoxic amino acids. The pattern is consistent: metabolic failure of the mitochondrial respiration that these cells depend on.

The 2017 trilogy placed PUFA at the center of the damage cascade: "One of the prostaglandins produced in the substantia nigra is very toxic to the brain. Simply having a regular use of aspirin will protect against that prostaglandin made from polyunsaturated fats. The breakdown of polyunsaturated fats produces acrolein, a very toxic material. That is one of the things that turns synuclein into a toxic fibrous material, sort of like the prions in mad cow disease." The alpha-synuclein aggregates in Lewy bodies are, in this reading, a downstream consequence of lipid peroxidation rather than a primary disease process. The [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|2006 newsletter on aspirin]] confirmed that aspirin protects against "excitotoxicity, dopamine toxicity, and oxidative free radical toxicity" through the same prostaglandin mechanism.

Nitric oxide adds a second vector. "In Parkinson's disease," Peat noted in a [[2014-11-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-nitric-oxide|2014 episode on nitric oxide]], "there are signs that nitric oxide is one of the central damaging components of the dopamine system." Excitotoxic amino acids attack the same cells: the [[multiple-sclerosis-and-other-hormone-related-brain-syndromes|1993 newsletter on brain syndromes]] argued that excitotoxins "destroy certain types of nerve, especially the dopaminergic and cholinergic types, leaving the noradrenergic types, paralleling the changes that occur in aging." Iron and copper complete the picture. The [[irons-dangers|2006 newsletter on iron]] connected the mineral directly to Parkinson's and noted that copper is required for "the normal functioning of certain nerve cells (substantia nigra) whose degeneration is involved in Parkinson's disease." Each source reinforces the others, and none are corrected by giving L-DOPA.

## Thyroid and the Relaxed State

The thyroid connection runs through a cell's capacity to relax. In Peat's bioenergetic framework, a truly rested cell is not inert but loaded with energy, ready to act. Without adequate thyroid hormone, cells cannot reach that high-energy resting state and remain in a semi-contracted, excited condition that looks, from the outside, exactly like Parkinsonian rigidity.

"Nothing in the body can relax fully when your thyroid function is low," Peat explained in the 2017 series. "If you don't have enough thyroid, glucose, and oxygen to produce the energy, you stay in a semi-contracted, excited state. In Parkinson's, this reaches a chronically deadly, cell-killing condition. In chronic hypothyroidism, it's simply a nuisance that your sleep isn't as restful as it should be." The hypothyroid muscle cannot let go; the Parkinsonian muscle cannot let go more dramatically. The difference is degree, not kind.

The [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|2007 newsletter on TSH]] made the diagnostic observation explicit: Parkinson's disease can conceal hypothyroidism, because L-DOPA and related treatments lower TSH and produce normal-looking lab values in patients with real thyroid deficiency. "In the same way hypothyroidism can simulate Parkinson's disease, the latter can also conceal hypothyroidism." The consequence is that a significant fraction of Parkinson's patients are undertreated hypothyroid patients, and a significant fraction of early cases might be prevented by treating the underlying metabolic failure before the dopaminergic neurons die. Active T3 directly opposes serotonin at the level of synthesis and retention, supporting dopamine by removing its primary antagonist. The full metabolic picture lives at [[thyroid]] and [[hypothyroidism]].

## Bromocriptine and the Anti-Serotonin Rebranding

The pharmaceutical industry's relationship with the dopamine hypothesis produced Peat's favorite example of pharmacology driven by marketing rather than mechanism. In the 1970s, drug companies developed lysergic acid derivatives with strong anti-serotonin activity. The parent compound was LSD, which had acquired a terrible reputation during the government's anti-psychedelic campaign, so the new drugs were rebranded as pro-dopamine agents rather than anti-serotonin agents.

"Research on LSD and its derivatives led to drugs such as bromocriptine, which oppose the effects of histamine and estrogen," the 2012 newsletter argued. "Some of bromocriptine's effects are clearly antagonistic to serotonin, though bromocriptine is usually called a 'dopamine agonist'; dopamine is pretty generally a serotonin antagonist." Peat was blunter in a [[2011-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-endotoxins-stress|2011 episode on serotonin and endotoxins]]: "The drug companies came out with modified forms of lysergic acid such as bromocriptine and lisuride, and some anti-serotonin drugs that were known to lower serotonin. However, they advertised them as pro-dopamine drugs rather than anti-serotonin." A substantial portion of the "dopamine agonist" literature is really an anti-serotonin literature told in the wrong vocabulary. See [[serotonin]] for the parallel Prozac rebranding story.

## Prolactin Suppression

Dopamine's most clinically consequential direct action is its inhibition of prolactin release from the anterior pituitary. Prolactin and dopamine sit in a reciprocal relationship: when dopamine rises, prolactin falls; when dopamine falls, prolactin rises. This is the mechanism bromocriptine exploits to treat prolactinomas, suppress lactation, and restore fertility in women whose high prolactin has suppressed ovulation.

"Serotonin increases CRH and prolactin; dopamine tends to inhibit them," Peat summarized in the 2017 series. "Estrogen activates the pituitary, the hypothalamus, and the adrenal glands directly to increase the tissue breakdown effect." The estrogen-serotonin axis drives prolactin upward; the progesterone-dopamine axis pulls it back down. Because serotonin drives prolactin release via CRH, any successful anti-serotonin intervention is also anti-prolactin, and this explains why bromocriptine is effective across conditions as different as Parkinson's and infertility. The full estrogen-serotonin-prolactin stress loop is covered in [[prolactin]].

## The Dietary Approach: Gelatin and Tryptophan Restriction

If pushing the dopamine end product damages the system that produces it, what remains is the indirect approach: lower serotonin by lowering its precursor, and supply the precursors of dopamine in a form the body can use. Peat's answer was gelatin.

"A listener asks about restoring dopamine and reducing serotonin," Patrick Timpone read in a 2019 *One Radio Network* episode. The response was characteristically specific: "The important thing is to limit your tryptophan intake as far as you can without becoming protein deficient. That's why I advocate gelatin, because gelatin contains no tryptophan, which is the source for serotonin. It also has the precursor for dopamine." Muscle meat contains roughly 1% tryptophan by weight; gelatin contains essentially none. Balancing beef or chicken with bone broth or gelatin dramatically lowers total tryptophan intake without reducing protein intake, shifting brain amino acid uptake away from serotonin synthesis.

Gelatin also contains tyrosine, the direct amino acid precursor to L-DOPA and dopamine. Supplementing isolated tyrosine was something Peat viewed with modest caution. "Tyrosine is okay in moderation; it leads to adrenaline," he noted in a [[2019-07-18-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-milk|2019 episode on milk]]. "But gelatin has a generous amount of the precursors to adrenaline and dopamine." The same episode dismissed the widely recommended 5-HTP: "I don't think 5-HTP is good at all; it can lead to increased inflammation." Taking 5-HTP to treat depression is, in Peat's reading, the pharmacological equivalent of giving a Parkinson's patient the substance that worsens tremor. The [[tryptophan]] article covers the amino acid's broader metabolic role.

## The Estrogen-Progesterone Axis

If dopamine and serotonin are antagonists, estrogen and progesterone are the hormones that tip the balance between them. Estrogen shifts the monoamine balance toward serotonin; progesterone and its derivatives shift it toward dopamine. The 2017 series made this explicit: "Estrogen shifts the balance toward serotonin and away from dopamine. This shift is one of the things that activates the prolactin system." The reproductive-age sex difference in depression, Parkinson's, MS, and IBS follows from this pattern. See [[estrogen]] for the full account.

[[Progesterone]] occupies the opposite role. "I think you can correct it most safely with progesterone and testosterone, or the precursor of both, pregnenolone," Peat recommended. "When you try to push the dopamine system directly, you tend to increase oxidative damage. It's safer to work at the anti-stress level rather than pushing the end product of dopamine." A 2017 follow-up added that "in Parkinson's disease, simply giving testosterone has a very powerful therapeutic effect, but it is outside of the mainline thinking regarding dopamine deficiency."

## Caffeine and Coffee

Among the few substances Peat recommended for direct dopaminergic support, [[coffee]] occupied a special place. "Caffeine is extremely protective against all of the defects in Parkinson's disease," he stated in the 2017 series. "It activates while protecting the mitochondria." Caffeine lowers nitric oxide production locally, protects against the excitotoxic and oxidative stress that damages dopaminergic neurons, and mildly elevates the oxidative metabolism dopamine synthesis depends on. Epidemiological data have long shown that regular coffee drinkers develop Parkinson's at dramatically reduced rates, and Peat took this as confirmation of what the metabolic logic predicted.

The [[2015-08-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-longevity-and-nootropics|2015 episode on longevity and nootropics]] captured the experiential dimension. Asked about Ginkgo biloba, which decreases monoamine oxidase and raises dopamine, Peat confirmed the effect: "It definitely makes you feel good; it's sort of an 'upper.' Anything that increases your dopamine tends to increase adrenaline usually. Brain circulation is decreased by serotonin. It tightens up the blood vessels, especially the veins leaving the brain. So if you have too much serotonin, you can get brain congestion and a migraine." The upper feeling of dopamine elevation corresponds to real improvements in cerebral circulation, alertness, and mood.

## Why You Cannot Push Dopamine Directly

The thread running through every section of this material is a warning against the direct approach. Attempting to raise dopamine through L-DOPA, tyrosine supplementation, selegiline, or any intervention that targets the end product rather than the metabolic context tends to accelerate the very damage it was meant to reverse. Dopamine itself is a reactive molecule. When its synthesis outpaces the cell's capacity to sequester it in vesicles or metabolize it safely, the excess oxidizes into quinones and free radicals that attack mitochondria and membrane lipids. The substantia nigra is especially vulnerable because its neurons produce dopamine as their principal product.

"When you try to push the dopamine system directly, you tend to increase oxidative damage," Peat reiterated. "It's safer to work at the anti-stress level rather than pushing the end product of dopamine." The anti-stress level means thyroid, sugar, gelatin, progesterone, calcium, coffee, aspirin, environmental enrichment, and the rest of the bioenergetic toolkit. None of these raise dopamine directly; all remove the stressors that suppress it.

The [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|2009 newsletter on thyroid and insomnia]] placed the whole framework in its final context. "Looking for general physiological problems behind the various symptoms is very different from the practice of classifying the insanities according to their symptoms and the hypothetical 'brain chemicals' that are believed to 'cause the symptoms.' The dopamine excess (or serotonin deficiency) theories developed at a time when only a few 'transmitter substances' were known, and when they were thought to act as very specific on/off nerve switches, rather than as links in metabolic networks. The drug industry helps to keep those ideas alive." Dopamine is not a switch. It is a metabolic output. Treating it as an output, rather than as a target, is the whole of Peat's practical teaching on the subject.
