# Thyroid

The thyroid gland produces what may be the single most important regulatory substance in human physiology. When thyroid function is optimal, cells consume oxygen efficiently, produce abundant carbon dioxide, maintain proper temperature, and resist the degenerative processes of [[aging]]. When thyroid function fails, every system in the body deteriorates. The widespread epidemic of [[hypothyroidism]] represents one of the most significant but poorly recognized health crises of modern times.

## The Master Metabolic Regulator

Thyroid hormone orchestrates cellular energy production. It determines how much oxygen cells consume, how much carbon dioxide they produce, and how efficiently they convert fuel into usable energy. As Peat explained in a [[2013-09-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-learned-helplessness-nervous-system-and-thyroid-questionaire|2013 discussion]], thyroid's effect extends far beyond simple "metabolism" in the calorie-burning sense---it shapes the entire character of cellular function.

The hormone works by entering cells and activating the machinery of [[oxidative metabolism]]. When thyroid is adequate, cells preferentially burn glucose through complete oxidation, producing [[carbon dioxide]] as a byproduct. This CO2 is not merely waste; it actively promotes tissue oxygenation through the Bohr effect and has direct anti-stress properties. Peat considers carbon dioxide one of the body's most important protective substances, and adequate thyroid function is the primary way to ensure its production.

The relationship between thyroid and oxygen consumption was the basis for the original diagnostic test. In the 1930s, the Basal Metabolic Rate test measured how much oxygen a person breathed in and how much carbon dioxide they exhaled. As Peat noted, "The best single test would be the Basal Metabolic Rate from the 1930s---measuring how much oxygen you breathe in and how much carbon dioxide you exhale. Temperature is just a rough approximation of how much fuel you're burning."

## The Hypothyroidism Epidemic

Most people with inadequate thyroid function remain undiagnosed. The modern reliance on TSH testing misses vast numbers of hypothyroid individuals, and even those who are diagnosed often receive inadequate treatment.

The problem begins with how hypothyroidism is defined. Peat argues that functional hypothyroidism---where tissues fail to receive adequate thyroid hormone despite normal-appearing blood tests---is extraordinarily common. Women experience thyroid disease at five to ten times the rate of men, largely because [[estrogen]] interferes with thyroid function at multiple levels. As Peat explained in a [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|discussion on Hashimoto's]], "women have 5 or 10 times as much thyroid disease as men. The reason is that estrogen interferes with the liver's ability to convert thyroxine (T4) to the active T3 hormone."

The thyroid gland produces primarily T4 (thyroxine), with a smaller amount of [[T3]] (the active hormone). The body must convert T4 to T3, primarily in the liver, for the hormone to have its full effect. Estrogen impairs this conversion, creating a situation where standard treatment with T4 alone often fails. "If the liver isn't producing T3, and you give that person big doses of thyroxine, you suppress the TSH. This turns off the little bit of active hormone coming out of their thyroid gland, and they go into an increasingly hypothyroid state."

This dynamic explains the paradox that mystifies many doctors: patients on T4 supplementation who show "normal" lab values yet remain symptomatic. They are simultaneously hypothyroid at the tissue level while their pituitary has been suppressed.

## TSH: The Problematic Messenger

TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone) serves as the pituitary's signal to the thyroid gland. When thyroid hormones are low, TSH rises to drive more production. Conventional medicine uses elevated TSH as the primary marker for hypothyroidism. But Peat argues that TSH itself is part of the problem.

"It has been well established in the last several years that TSH itself causes many of the symptoms blamed on hypothyroidism," Peat noted. "It acts on the bone marrow and many other tissues, including the liver, increasing inflammatory cytokine chemicals. So, TSH in itself is a basic problem of hypothyroidism."

This insight inverts the conventional approach. Rather than viewing high TSH merely as a marker of inadequate thyroid, it should be understood as an active contributor to the disease state. The goal of thyroid replacement, in Peat's framework, is not just to achieve normal tissue function but to suppress TSH to minimal levels.

Animals that have their pituitaries removed and are given adequate thyroid and cortisol supplementation often live significantly longer than animals with normal pituitary function. As Peat stated in a [[2017-04-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-2|2017 discussion on endocrinology]], "Animals that have their pituitaries removed and are given a supplement of cortisol and thyroid will often live two or three times longer than animals with normal pituitary function. This shows that the pituitary, to the extent that it is overactive, is shortening your life."

## Temperature and Pulse: The Practical Markers

Because laboratory testing often fails to identify functional hypothyroidism, Peat recommends assessing thyroid status through temperature and pulse measurements. The logic is simple: if thyroid hormone is doing its job, cells will be producing heat and the heart will beat at an appropriate rate.

The interpretation requires nuance. "Low thyroid people typically compensate with stress hormones increasing---adrenaline, cortisol, serotonin, histamine," Peat explained. "These make your tissues metabolize in an energy-wasting way. Adrenaline makes your skin cold. Cortisol breaks down muscle and glandular tissue to turn it into sugar, which heats your body up."

This creates a characteristic pattern: some hypothyroid people wake with high temperatures driven by stress hormones, then see their temperature crash after eating breakfast. The food reduces stress hormones, revealing the underlying metabolic deficit. "I first noticed this in a friend who cycled between depression and mania. During manic periods where she slept very little, her morning temperatures were extremely high. After eating, they would fall several degrees."

The healthy pattern is the reverse: temperature should rise after eating as the liver begins producing T3 in response to fuel availability. A pulse between 75 and 85 during the daytime indicates adequate metabolic rate. Cold extremities---fingers, toes, the tip of the nose---signal that adrenaline is shunting blood away from the periphery, a classic stress response to inadequate energy production.

Peat offered a practical diagnostic insight: "Checking your pulse rate and the temperature of your fingers and toes in a cool room are more sensitive indicators" than basal temperature alone. The amount of water a person evaporates also reveals metabolic rate. "A person should take in two quarts more fluid than they lose per day in urine. That two quarts would be average evaporation if your thyroid is good. Many hypothyroid people evaporate less than a quart a day, meaning they are waterlogged."

## What Suppresses Thyroid Function

The modern epidemic of hypothyroidism has identifiable causes. [[PUFA|Polyunsaturated fatty acids]] interfere with thyroid at every level---gland production, transport in blood, cellular uptake, and conversion to active hormone. As Peat noted in discussing seizure disorders, "The degree of unsaturation of the fat corresponds to the degree of interference with thyroid hormone. So fish oil is more anti-thyroid than the seed oils. Olive oil is very weakly anti-thyroid. Butter, coconut oil, cream, beef, and lamb fat are not anti-thyroid."

Estrogen, as discussed, blocks hepatic conversion of T4 to T3. It also increases the proteins that bind thyroid hormone in blood, reducing the free hormone available to tissues. The estrogen-thyroid antagonism is bidirectional: low thyroid allows estrogen to accumulate (by slowing liver clearance), and elevated estrogen further suppresses thyroid function.

[[Endotoxin]] from intestinal bacteria triggers [[inflammation]] that suppresses thyroid. Slow digestion allows bacterial overgrowth; bacterial overgrowth produces endotoxin; endotoxin suppresses thyroid; low thyroid slows digestion further. Breaking this cycle often requires addressing gut function alongside thyroid supplementation.

Peat pointed to an often-overlooked historical factor: "Traditional diets before 1940, even in the industrial countries, included quite a bit of thyroid in the food. If you stewed a chicken or a fish, the thyroid would break up and be consumed. With that traditional diet, a person probably averaged the equivalent of maybe 30 milligrams a day of Armour Thyroid. The removal of natural thyroid from the food supply has contributed to hypothyroidism."

## Thyroid and the Brain

The brain is exquisitely sensitive to thyroid status. Cognitive function, mood, and even the basic capacity for learning depend on adequate thyroid hormone reaching neural tissue.

Peat described his own experience with insomnia: "My own experience when I took thyroid was being able to relax and sleep soundly. I once met a doctor who hadn't slept for three nights. I gave him 10 micrograms of Cytomel [T3]. The next night he told me, 'That stuff is better than morphine.'"

The connection extends to more severe conditions. In discussing [[learned helplessness]], Peat explained that thyroid (along with [[progesterone]]) can block the physiological changes that lead to depression and even premature death in trapped animals. "In situations where they were studying learned helplessness, which produces increased acetylcholine and nitric oxide, they found that either progesterone or thyroid (T3) would block the formation of that behavior and keep them from dying prematurely."

Hair loss, often attributed to excess androgens, frequently reflects inadequate thyroid function. The scalp of balding individuals tends to be cooler than normal, reflecting reduced metabolic activity. As Peat observed, raising overall energy production addresses the syndrome at its root rather than attempting to block specific hormones.

## Thyroid and Cholesterol

One of the clearest demonstrations of thyroid's metabolic effects involves cholesterol. In the 1930s, researchers documented that removing the thyroid gland caused cholesterol to rise, while thyroid supplementation caused it to fall. The relationship was precise enough that elevated cholesterol served as a diagnostic marker for hypothyroidism.

"When a person's thyroid gland was removed, as their metabolic rate declined, the blood cholesterol increased. When they were given a supplement of thyroid, it was a mirror image: as the metabolic rate increased, the cholesterol declined," Peat explained. "This allowed many doctors to diagnose hypothyroidism simply by looking at elevated cholesterol."

The mechanism involves steroid synthesis. Thyroid activates the conversion of cholesterol into [[pregnenolone]] and [[progesterone]]. Without adequate thyroid, cholesterol accumulates because it cannot be converted into downstream hormones. This insight reveals the futility of cholesterol-lowering drugs in hypothyroid patients: they reduce the substrate for protective hormones without addressing the underlying metabolic defect.

## Thyroid and Blood Pressure

Contrary to common assumptions, hypothyroidism causes high blood pressure, not low. The mechanism involves how the body produces vasodilation.

"Hypothyroid people have increased nitric oxide, but at the same time, they have a tendency of too much contraction of the blood vessels and high blood pressure," Peat explained. "The basic thing that relaxes the blood vessels produced by thyroid hormone is carbon dioxide."

[[Nitric oxide]], often promoted as beneficial for vasodilation, actually works by blocking cellular energy production. It relaxes blood vessels by preventing them from contracting---a form of energetic failure rather than healthy relaxation. Carbon dioxide, produced by adequate thyroid function, relaxes blood vessels while maintaining high cellular energy.

"Carbon dioxide relaxes blood vessels in a very different way than nitric oxide does. Nitric oxide interferes with energy supply. Carbon dioxide retains a high energy level while relaxing, partly by changing the electrical pH behavior of the cell."

## Thyroid and Radiation Protection

Peat's research on radiation defense, dating from his earliest newsletters, identified thyroid as crucial for repair of radiation damage. "Research has confirmed that keeping your metabolic rate up accelerates the repair process faster than the injury," he explained. "Progesterone decreases the injury, and thyroid increases the rate of repair."

After radiation exposure, [[magnesium]] becomes the key element for cellular repair. But magnesium uptake requires thyroid activation. "After your thyroid is activated, cells take up magnesium, and enzymes are activated to do the repair."

This protective effect extends beyond radiation to general tissue repair. Thyroid supports the anabolic processes that maintain and rebuild tissue, counteracting the catabolic effects of [[cortisol]] and other stress hormones.

## Practical Considerations

For detailed information on thyroid supplementation, including forms, dosing, and timing, see [[thyroid-supplement]]. For the specific properties of the active hormone, see [[T3]].

The fundamental principle is that thyroid status should be assessed by function, not just laboratory values. Temperature, pulse, cholesterol, energy level, cognitive clarity, and emotional stability all reflect thyroid adequacy. When these indicators suggest deficiency, supplementation may be warranted even with "normal" lab results.

Dietary factors strongly influence thyroid function. Avoiding PUFA, ensuring adequate [[protein]] and [[carbohydrate]], supporting liver function, and minimizing intestinal irritation all promote natural thyroid production and peripheral conversion.
