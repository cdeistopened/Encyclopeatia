# Thyroid

Thyroid hormone is the master conductor of oxidative metabolism. Every cell in the body depends on it to produce energy efficiently, and when it is absent or insufficient, the entire organism slides toward the inefficient, inflammatory, cancer-promoting mode of energy production that Ray Peat spent fifty years identifying as the root of degenerative disease. "The basic function of thyroid is to energize cells and to give them enough energy and efficiency to allow them to differentiate so that they don't just concentrate on eating and growing the way plants, amoebas, and fungi do," Peat explained in a [[2011-02-22-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-thyroid|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on the thyroid]]. This single function, the energizing of cellular differentiation, is what separates organized animal life from mere growth. Without thyroid, you get tumors and malformations. With it, you get structure, intelligence, and repair.

Peat called thyroid "our basic anabolic hormone," a description that places it above growth hormone, above testosterone, above insulin in the hierarchy of substances the body requires to build and maintain itself. If you remove an animal's pituitary gland, the supposed "master gland," and replace only thyroid hormone, the animal lives far longer than normal. "In some cases the animals live ten times as long as normal, usually about twice as long as normal for the lack of the pituitary gland, as long as they had adequate thyroid," Peat said in the same interview. This extraordinary result inverts the conventional hierarchy of endocrinology. The pituitary is not the master. Thyroid is.

The diagnostic challenge created by fifty years of fraudulent blood testing is covered in [[tsh]] and [[hypothyroidism]]. The clinical pioneer who first demonstrated that temperature reveals what blood tests conceal is covered in [[broda-barnes]]. This article addresses the hormone itself: what T3 does at the cellular level, why T4 is not the same thing, what suppresses thyroid function, and what restores it.

## What T3 Does

The active thyroid hormone is triiodothyronine, T3. Its fundamental action is to increase the rate at which cells consume oxygen and produce carbon dioxide through oxidative phosphorylation. This is not a marginal effect. It is the difference between a cell that functions and one that ferments.

When T3 reaches a cell, respiration increases. The cell takes up oxygen, burns glucose completely, and produces carbon dioxide, water, and a large quantity of ATP. The carbon dioxide is not merely a waste product. It performs essential protective functions: it binds to proteins in their native, youthful configuration, it displaces calcium from excited cells, allowing them to relax, and it stabilizes the intracellular environment against the swelling and excitotoxicity that characterize stress. "Carbon dioxide produced under the influence of the thyroid is needed to carry the exciting calcium out of the cells and allow the cell to relax," Peat explained in the 2011 *EastWest Healing* interview.

When T3 is deficient, the opposite occurs. Cells shift toward glycolysis, producing lactic acid instead of carbon dioxide. This shift is not benign. "Even at rest, a person will produce lactic acid as if they were under strenuous exercise," Peat said. "Lactic acid turns on inflammatory mediators and acts as a toxin. Over time, it leads to fibrosis, arthritis, connective tissue diseases, and calcification of soft tissues." The lactic acid accumulation is the same metabolic defect that [[otto-warburg|Otto Warburg]] identified in cancer cells in the 1920s. [[Broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]], working at the University of Chicago just a few years later, recognized that hypothyroid patients showed the same deficiency of oxygen consumption involving the same enzyme system. The connection between low cellular respiration, hypothyroidism, and cancer was visible to anyone willing to look at it.

The tadpole experiment illustrates the principle with particular clarity. If you add an anti-thyroid chemical to an aquarium, the tadpole never metamorphoses into a frog. It simply grows into a huge tadpole. If you add thyroid hormone, even a tiny hatchling turns into a miniature frog. "This shows that the thyroid energizes the cells so that they use oxygen and produce a huge amount of energy. This energy allows the cells to realize their function," Peat said. "Without thyroid, all you have is growth. That's fine for amoebas and trees, but in humans, it can lead to tumors and malformations." Growth without differentiation is the definition of cancer. Thyroid hormone is what tips the balance toward organized structure.

The muscle relaxation test captures this principle in clinical practice. When a tendon is tapped and the muscle contracts, the speed at which it relaxes afterward reveals the rate of energy regeneration. In a hypothyroid person, as Peat described in his [[thyroid-misconceptions|"Thyroid: Misconceptions" (1993)]] newsletter, "the foot returns as if controlled by a pneumatic door-closer." The muscle cannot regenerate its ATP quickly enough to release. The same delayed relaxation appears in the heart, where the T-wave on the electrocardiogram flattens and stretches, and in the brain, where nerves slow to relax and sleep becomes unrestorative.

## T4 and T3: The Conversion Problem

The thyroid gland in the neck secretes two hormones: thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), in a ratio of approximately three to one. T4 is not the active hormone. It is a precursor, analogous to the way proinsulin is the precursor to insulin. The distinction between them is not subtle. "When thyroxine is added to brain tissue in vitro, it suppresses respiration," Peat wrote in his 1993 newsletter. "But when triiodothyronine is added to any tissue, respiration is increased." The two molecules have opposite effects on the very process that defines thyroid's function.

Approximately two-thirds of the body's active T3 is produced not in the thyroid gland but in the [[liver]], through the enzymatic conversion of T4. This conversion requires adequate glucose, selenium, and overall liver health. The liver's dual role as the primary site of T3 production and the primary site of [[estrogen]] clearance makes it the most critical organ in thyroid physiology. "The liver is the source of the most active thyroid hormone," Peat explained. "About two-thirds of the thyroid used by the body is produced in the liver. If your liver isn't getting enough glucose or selenium, it is unable to convert thyroxine into T3."

This arrangement creates a fragile dependency. Anything that damages the liver compromises T3 production, and the resulting hypothyroidism further damages the liver. Estrogen impairs the liver's conjugation enzymes. [[PUFA]] accumulation in liver tissue disrupts its metabolic functions. [[Cortisol]] promotes gluconeogenesis at the expense of glycogen storage, depleting the glucose the liver needs for conversion. Starvation and caloric restriction empty the liver's glycogen reserves within twelve to twenty-four hours, shutting down T4-to-T3 conversion as a survival mechanism.

The clinical consequences of this dependency are severe. When a doctor prescribes T4 alone (the standard modern practice), the patient's liver must convert it to T3. In healthy young men, this works adequately. In women, whose higher [[estrogen]] levels impair liver function, the conversion often fails. "A doctor prescribes only T4, it works fine in healthy young men," Peat said. "But women, because of higher estrogen levels, have more liver issues. A little problem with thyroid or estrogen means the liver allows estrogen to increase, which decreases the production of active thyroid hormone, creating a vicious circle."

When T4 accumulates because the liver cannot convert it, the excess T4 feeds back to suppress whatever T3 the thyroid gland was still producing on its own. The patient becomes more hypothyroid on the treatment than they were before it. Peat recounted the case of a woman whose doctor kept increasing her T4 dose as her symptoms worsened: "Her symptoms got worse. The doctor increased the dose until she reached 400 or 500 micrograms of T4, at which point she went into a myxedema coma. They injected pure T3, and she came right out of the coma." The coma was caused by the treatment. The cure was the hormone the treatment failed to deliver.

The stress hormones add another layer of interference. "All of the stress hormones interfere with the conversion of T4 to T3," Peat stated in a 2019 *Jodellefit* episode on cortisol and testosterone. "Instead of making the effective, respiration-promoting T3, they convert thyroxine into Reverse T3. They dispose of the precursor and turn it into an interfering, inactive suppressor of respiration." Reverse T3 is not merely an inactive byproduct. It actively opposes the action of T3 at the cellular level.

## The Thyroid-Cholesterol Mirror

One of the oldest and most reliable relationships in endocrinology is the inverse correlation between thyroid function and blood cholesterol. By the mid-1930s, it was universally recognized that hypothyroidism causes cholesterol to rise and that thyroid supplementation brings it down. As Peat wrote in his [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud|"Thyroid" (2007)]] newsletter: "Barnes experimented on rabbits, and found that when their thyroid glands were removed, they developed atherosclerosis, just as hypothyroid people did. Administering a thyroid supplement, blood cholesterol came down to normal exactly as the basal metabolic rate came up to the normal rate."

The mechanism is straightforward. T3 is required for the rapid conversion of cholesterol into steroid hormones: [[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], [[DHEA]], and the other protective substances that the body manufactures from cholesterol as its starting material. When T3 is deficient, this conversion slows, and cholesterol accumulates in the blood. The cholesterol is not a disease. It is a symptom of the metabolic failure that prevents its conversion into useful substances. "If you chart the metabolic rate, it's a mirror image of the cholesterol," Peat said.

This relationship means that elevated cholesterol, rather than requiring statin therapy, may indicate a need for thyroid evaluation. Peat considered the pre-war understanding of this connection one of the great lost insights in medicine. "The biology of atherosclerotic heart disease was basically solved before the second world war," he wrote. Barnes demonstrated over thirty years of clinical practice that thyroid-supplemented patients did not develop heart disease, regardless of their cholesterol levels at the start. The cholesterol fell because the thyroid restored the metabolic capacity to use it.

Artificially lowering cholesterol with statins does not restore that metabolic capacity. Because cholesterol is the raw material for progesterone, pregnenolone, and other protective hormones, suppressing it pharmacologically reduces the body's ability to produce these substances. Early studies of cholesterol-lowering interventions showed, as Peat noted, that the reduction "tremendously increased cancer mortality, total mortality, and increased the death rate from accidents, homicide, and even heart disease and strokes."

## The Suppression Story

Thyroid function is under constant assault from substances and conditions that modern life has made ubiquitous. The four major suppressors form an interlocking system in which each one amplifies the effects of the others.

### PUFA: The Three-Level Block

[[PUFA|Polyunsaturated fatty acids]] interfere with thyroid at every step between the gland and the cell. Peat described the sequence in his 1993 newsletter: "Unsaturated oils interfere with thyroid function in several ways, including blocking the 'digestive' proteolytic enzymes involved in the release of hormone from the globulin." This is the first block: PUFA prevents the thyroid gland from secreting its stored hormone. The second block occurs in the bloodstream: "Unsaturated fatty acids interfere with binding of the hormone to a transport protein." The third occurs at the tissue level: "The tissue response to the hormone is inhibited by unsaturated fats in proportion to the number of double bonds in the fat."

The toxicity scales with the degree of unsaturation. Linoleic and linolenic acids are roughly twice as toxic to these systems as oleic acid. Fish oil, with five and six double bonds per molecule, is "an almost total inhibitor of thyroid transport." Linoleic acid inhibits transport by approximately 30%. This means that a person eating a diet high in vegetable oils, nuts, seeds, and fish oil can have blood tests showing adequate thyroid levels while remaining functionally hypothyroid at the tissue level. The hormones circulate but never reach their targets.

The PUFA already stored in body fat has a half-life of approximately four years, which means that even after eliminating vegetable oils from the diet, the accumulated stores continue to suppress thyroid function for years. This is one reason Peat considered thyroid supplementation necessary for many people who have been eating a standard modern diet, even when they are also improving their food choices.

### Estrogen: The Vicious Circle

[[Estrogen]] and thyroid exist in a relationship of mutual antagonism mediated by the liver, and this antagonism is the single most important feedback loop in Peat's entire framework. The thyroid enables the liver to clear estrogen from the body. "The thyroid works partly by energizing the liver so it can detoxify everything, including removing 100% of the estrogen that reaches it," Peat said. "If your thyroid is low, the liver loses the ability to detoxify estrogen."

When estrogen rises because the liver cannot clear it, the estrogen itself further suppresses thyroid function through multiple pathways. It inhibits the proteolytic enzyme that releases thyroid hormone from the gland, so the gland accumulates stored hormone it cannot secrete. It drives the pituitary to produce more [[tsh|TSH]], but simultaneously blocks the gland's ability to respond, causing enlargement. This is the pattern frequently diagnosed as [[hashimotos|Hashimoto's thyroiditis]]. It impairs the liver's conversion of T4 to T3, deepening the very hypothyroidism that caused the estrogen accumulation in the first place. Each pass through this loop worsens the condition.

This loop explains why women develop thyroid disease at five to ten times the rate of men. Their higher baseline estrogen levels make them more vulnerable to the initial entry into the cycle. It also explains why Peat considered estrogen the primary hormonal enemy of thyroid function, more consequential even than cortisol, because estrogen attacks both the gland's ability to secrete and the liver's ability to activate.

### Cortisol and Serotonin

[[Cortisol]] suppresses thyroid through several mechanisms. It depletes the liver's glycogen stores, removing the glucose needed for T4-to-T3 conversion. It promotes the conversion of T4 to reverse T3 rather than active T3. And it inhibits TSH secretion from the pituitary, masking the hypothyroidism on blood tests even as it worsens it at the tissue level.

[[Serotonin]] drives the release of TRH from the hypothalamus, which in turn drives both TSH and [[prolactin]] from the pituitary. Elevated serotonin therefore produces a chronically stimulated thyroid axis in which the gland is being driven harder but cannot keep up, especially when estrogen is simultaneously blocking secretion. The combination of high serotonin, high estrogen, and high cortisol creates the classic picture of chronic stress: the thyroid axis is activated but the hormone never reaches the cells, and the pituitary hormones that result from the overstimulation (TSH, prolactin) themselves become sources of tissue damage.

## The Anti-Aging Hormone

W.D. Denckla, a researcher at the National Institute on Aging, conducted experiments in the 1970s that Peat considered among the most important in the history of aging research. Denckla removed the pituitary gland from aging animals and replaced only thyroid hormone. The animals lived dramatically longer than normal, in some cases twice their expected lifespan. From this result, Denckla proposed the existence of a "Decreasing Oxygen Consumption" hormone (DECO), which the pituitary secretes in increasing amounts with age and which progressively blocks the tissues' ability to use thyroid hormone.

Peat endorsed Denckla's finding as consistent with everything else in his framework. The pituitary hormones that increase with age, particularly TSH, prolactin, and growth hormone, all promote inflammation, tissue degeneration, and metabolic inefficiency. Naturally pituitary-deficient dwarf mice, which lack TSH, prolactin, and growth hormone, live roughly a year longer than their normal littermates. The implication is that aging is not the failure of protective hormones but the accumulation of pituitary-driven interference with thyroid function at the tissue level.

Thyroid's anti-aging action operates through the same mechanism as its basic cellular function. Cells that produce energy efficiently through oxidative metabolism maintain their structure, repair their components, and resist the degeneration that accumulates over a lifetime. Cells that produce energy through glycolysis accumulate lactic acid, age pigment ([[lipofuscin]]), calcium deposits, and cross-linked proteins. "Many other diseases are now known to be caused by respiratory defects," Peat wrote. "Inflammation, stress, immunodeficiency, autoimmunity, developmental and degenerative diseases, and aging, all involve significantly abnormal oxidative processes." Thyroid is the hormone that keeps those oxidative processes normal.

## Thyroid and Carbon Dioxide

The relationship between thyroid and [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] is one of the most important and least appreciated connections in Peat's framework. Adequate T3 ensures that glucose is oxidized completely, producing CO2 as the final product. This CO2 is not merely exhaled. A substantial portion remains dissolved in the blood and tissues, where it maintains the Bohr effect (governing how readily hemoglobin releases oxygen), stabilizes proteins by binding to exposed amine groups, and facilitates calcium's removal from excited cells. "Higher CO2 means you are oxidizing things completely," Peat explained. "If you oxidize fats completely, you stop producing lactic acid. Carbon dioxide binds to our proteins, protecting them."

The hypothyroid person, by failing to produce adequate CO2, loses all of these protections simultaneously. Their tissues become hypoxic despite adequate blood oxygen, because hemoglobin holds its oxygen too tightly in the absence of CO2. Their proteins lose their native conformation. Calcium accumulates in their cells, producing chronic excitation. Lactic acid builds up, driving inflammation and fibrosis. The connection between hypothyroidism and the dozens of diseases it produces runs through this single pathway: the failure to produce enough carbon dioxide.

## Thyroid, Fertility, and Pregnancy

Thyroid's role in reproduction is so central that fertility itself can be read as a marker of thyroid adequacy. Hypothyroid animals develop cystic ovaries. Hypothyroid women cannot convert [[cholesterol]] to [[progesterone]] at the rate needed to sustain pregnancy. Hypothyroid men show decreased testosterone and increased estrogen, a pattern that correlates with low sperm count and poor sperm motility.

The mechanism connecting thyroid to progesterone production runs through [[vitamin-a|vitamin A]]. The liver requires thyroid hormone to convert carotene into retinol (active vitamin A). Retinol is in turn required for the conversion of cholesterol into pregnenolone and then progesterone. When thyroid is low, carotene accumulates unconverted, progesterone production falls, and estrogen rises unopposed. The yellow-orange calluses that appear on the palms and soles of hypothyroid people are accumulated carotene, a visible marker of this broken conversion chain.

Peat wrote in his 1993 newsletter that "thyroid promotes the formation of progesterone, which in turn promotes the secretion of thyroid hormones. Estrogen blocks their release from the thyroid gland, causing the gland to enlarge." The progesterone-thyroid axis is a mutually reinforcing partnership: supporting one supports the other, and deficiency in one tends to produce deficiency in the other. This is why pregnancy often reveals latent hypothyroidism. The estrogen surge of pregnancy demands increased thyroid output and increased progesterone, and if either is insufficient, the pregnancy is at risk.

The connection extends to the next generation. Thyroid hormone is essential for fetal brain development. The fetal brain requires T3 during the period of rapid neuronal differentiation, and maternal hypothyroidism during pregnancy is associated with measurably lower IQ in offspring. The geographic epidemiology of iodine deficiency, which produces hypothyroidism, has been mapped against regional variations in cognitive ability for over a century. The relationship between thyroid and intelligence is not metaphorical. It is the same relationship between thyroid and differentiation that the tadpole experiment demonstrates: without adequate thyroid, cells grow but do not specialize.

## Thyroid and Immunity

Barnes demonstrated that hypothyroid populations show elevated rates of tuberculosis, and that thyroid supplementation dramatically reduced susceptibility to infection. The mechanism is the same one that governs all of thyroid's effects: efficient energy production. Immune cells require ATP to engulf pathogens and proliferate in response to challenge. When thyroid is deficient, immune cells shift toward glycolysis, produce lactic acid, and lose their capacity for the oxidative burst that kills intracellular pathogens.

Peat connected thyroid's immune function to its suppression of estrogen. "Supplemental thyroid so easily normalizes estrogen and the immune system that it, with progesterone and pregnenolone and DHEA, should be considered as the cure for autoimmune diseases, including thyroiditis," he wrote in his 1993 newsletter. The chain from low thyroid to high estrogen to high [[prolactin]] to tissue proliferation also explains the concentration of breast cancer in hypothyroid regions. "Excess prolactin and excess estrogen are associated with each other, with breast cancer, and with hypothyroidism," Peat noted.

## Supplementation

The practical question of how to restore thyroid function when it is inadequate occupied Peat throughout his career. His hierarchy of supplementation options reflected his understanding of T3 as the active hormone and the liver as the site of conversion failure.

### Desiccated Thyroid (Armour)

Peat's preferred preparation for most patients, especially women, was desiccated thyroid glandular, historically sold as Armour Thyroid USP. Desiccated thyroid contains both T4 and T3 in approximately the ratio secreted by a normally functioning human gland, along with traces of T2, T1, and calcitonin. "Every batch is biologically standardized, and studies have shown it to be reliably within 1% of the labeled potency," Peat wrote. "The best known brand of the supposedly chemically precise levothyroxine, however, was for a long time 30% below the labeled potency." The pharmaceutical narrative that Armour was poorly standardized and that synthetic T4 was precise was factually reversed.

The advantage of desiccated thyroid is that it provides T3 directly, bypassing the liver conversion step that fails in so many patients. For someone whose liver is compromised by estrogen, PUFA, or nutritional deficiency, receiving both T4 and T3 means the active hormone arrives regardless of liver function.

### Cytomel and T3 Preparations

For patients whose liver conversion is severely impaired, or who need rapid relief from specific symptoms, synthetic T3 (liothyronine, sold as Cytomel) provides the active hormone directly. Peat noted that T3 "acts quickly, within a few minutes, and leaves the body quickly, with half of it gone in a day." This rapid action makes it particularly useful for acute symptoms. He recommended T3 for insomnia with notable frequency: "Insomnia is the most commonly troublesome symptom of menopause, and it usually responds immediately to a small bedtime dose of T3, just as T3 rapidly restores the normal quick relaxation rate to the calf muscles and heart muscle."

The Mexican product Cynoplus, which contains both T4 and T3 in a fixed ratio, and Cynomel, which contains T3 alone, were preparations Peat mentioned as available alternatives when Armour became difficult to obtain or when its formulation changed.

Because T3 clears the body quickly, Peat recommended taking it in small divided doses throughout the day, with meals. The body produces approximately 4 micrograms of T3 per hour under normal conditions, and exceeding this rate can produce transient symptoms of excess. Taking T3 with food, particularly carbohydrate-containing food, helps smooth its absorption and matches the physiological pattern in which liver T3 production is gated by glucose availability.

### What Pure T4 (Levothyroxine) Gets Wrong

Synthetic levothyroxine (T4 alone, sold as Synthroid) depends entirely on the liver's ability to convert T4 to T3. When T4 is given to a patient whose liver cannot convert it, the accumulating T4 feeds back to suppress the thyroid gland's own T3 secretion. The patient becomes more hypothyroid on the treatment than before. Blood tests show a suppressed TSH and high T4, leading the doctor to conclude the patient is adequately treated while the patient is functionally worse. "I think biology should occupy a world in which things are not simultaneously themselves and their opposites," Peat observed of the patients diagnosed as "both hypothyroid and hyperthyroid." The T4-only problem is covered in greater detail in [[hypothyroidism]].

## The Progesterone Partnership

[[Progesterone]] and thyroid exist in a relationship of mutual reinforcement that stands in direct contrast to the mutual antagonism between estrogen and thyroid. Progesterone facilitates the proteolytic enzymes in the thyroid gland that release stored hormone into the bloodstream, the same enzymes that estrogen inhibits. It supports liver function, enabling T4-to-T3 conversion. It antagonizes estrogen at the cellular level, reducing the primary hormonal suppressor of thyroid activity.

Thyroid, in return, promotes progesterone formation through the vitamin A conversion pathway, supports the liver's estrogen clearance, and provides the energy the ovaries and adrenals need for synthesis. The result is a positive feedback loop that is the metabolic mirror image of the estrogen-thyroid vicious circle: supporting either hormone supports both. A single dose of progesterone can sometimes break a woman out of the estrogen-thyroid downward spiral by opposing estrogen at every relevant site, allowing the liver to recover and thyroid function to improve. Peat recommended both substances together for conditions ranging from infertility to autoimmune disease.

## Temperature as the Ultimate Readout

If blood tests are unreliable for the reasons detailed in [[tsh]], then the most practical measure of thyroid adequacy is its effect on energy production, visible as heat. Peat recommended measuring temperature and pulse at waking, after breakfast, and in the afternoon. The post-meal reading is the most revealing, because hypothyroid people often compensate with high [[adrenaline]] and [[cortisol]] that mask their deficit. "If you see a person's temperature and pulse rate fall after a good meal, that reveals a low metabolic rate that was being held up by emergency stimulation from adrenaline and cortisol," Peat explained. "Food reduces stress, so when the stress hormones drop, the true low metabolism is revealed."

The numbers Peat considered healthy are notably higher than what most doctors would accept as normal. A midday temperature of 98 to 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit and a resting pulse of 80 to 85 beats per minute indicate adequate thyroid function. "A low resting heart rate tends to go with fertility problems, hormonal problems, low testosterone, increased estrogen, and degenerative problems," he said. The detailed diagnostic protocol, including the interpretation of various temperature-pulse patterns, is covered in [[hypothyroidism]] and [[broda-barnes]].

## The Fraud Timeline

The full history of how thyroid function came to be so broadly misdiagnosed is detailed in [[hypothyroidism]] and [[tsh]]. The essential facts: before the Second World War, roughly 30 to 40 percent of Americans showed evidence of inadequate thyroid function based on symptoms and oxygen consumption testing. The introduction of the Protein Bound Iodine test in the late 1940s reduced this estimate to 5% overnight. The PBI test was later proven irrelevant to thyroid function, but the 5% doctrine survived and was built into the TSH reference ranges that replaced it. Simultaneously, the pharmaceutical industry promoted synthetic thyroxine as chemically precise while falsely claiming Armour thyroid was poorly standardized, and the FDA allowed iodinated casein to be sold as "thyroid USP" on the basis of iodine content alone.

"In the last 50 years, a culture, a mythology, related to thyroid hormone has been manufactured in this country," Peat wrote in his 1993 newsletter. "The articles and advertisements in medical journals, and the more direct contacts between drug companies and physicians, have covered most aspects of thyroid physiology with a thick layer of misinformation." The practical consequence is that millions of people whose symptoms match every pre-war diagnostic criterion for hypothyroidism are told their blood work is normal, and the unifying cause of their fatigue, weight gain, high cholesterol, infertility, and infections goes unrecognized.

## Supporting Thyroid Without Supplementation

Before reaching for a prescription, several dietary and environmental factors can meaningfully improve thyroid function.

**Glucose and the liver.** The liver requires glucose to convert T4 to T3. Fruit, fruit juice, honey, and sugar provide the fructose and glucose that support this conversion. [[Oranges|Orange juice]] occupies a special position because its naringenin content inhibits [[aromatase]], reducing estrogen production while simultaneously providing the sugar the liver needs. "You need orange juice first to get your thyroid going," Peat told listeners.

**Coconut oil.** The saturated medium-chain fatty acids in [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]] support thyroid function by helping the liver store glycogen and by displacing the polyunsaturated fats that inhibit thyroid at every level. G.W. Crile found that the basal metabolism of people in Yucatan, where coconuts are a staple food, was 125% of that of Americans. The saturated fats do not interfere with any step of thyroid production, transport, or cellular response.

**The raw carrot.** A daily raw carrot binds estrogen and bacterial endotoxin in the intestine, preventing their reabsorption and breaking the estrogen-thyroid vicious circle at the gut level. "I had women measure their hormones, then eat a raw carrot daily," Peat said. "After just a few days, their hormones were back in a favorable state: reduced estrogen and cortisol, increased progesterone and thyroid."

**PUFA avoidance.** Because stored PUFA suppresses thyroid at every level and has a four-year half-life in tissue, reducing dietary PUFA is the single most important long-term dietary intervention for thyroid support. Replacing vegetable oils with coconut oil, butter, and olive oil removes the ongoing source while the body slowly clears its stored reserves.

**Avoiding goitrogens.** Raw cruciferous vegetables (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower), raw beans and lentils, and nuts contain substances that directly inhibit the thyroid gland. Cooking reduces but does not eliminate their goitrogenic activity.

## Seasonal and Individual Variation

Thyroid requirements are not fixed. Peat described summer requirements as sometimes one-quarter of winter requirements, and altitude increases T3 by roughly 18%, which may contribute to the lower disease rates Barnes documented in mountain populations. The modern combination of sea-level living, indoor climate control, PUFA-rich diets, and fluoridated water creates an environment that suppresses thyroid function from multiple angles simultaneously. Individual responses vary enormously: Peat described patients for whom 15 milligrams of Armour was sufficient and others who temporarily needed 500 milligrams. The key is not a fixed dose but consistent monitoring through temperature, pulse, and symptom resolution.

## The Recovery Question

A common fear about thyroid supplementation is that it will permanently suppress the gland's own production. Peat addressed this directly in his 1993 newsletter: "It is sometimes true that 'if you take thyroid it will suppress your own gland.' By overdosing people until the output of their own glands had gone to zero, it was possible to see what happened when the overdose stopped. Everyone's gland was functioning perfectly again within two or three days."

Peat went further, reporting that some patients recovered their own thyroid function through supplementation. "I've seen several people who were hypothyroid who, when they took the amount that brought their oxygen consumption up to the right level, had recovery of their own thyroid function and could stop taking it." The supplementation, by breaking the estrogen-thyroid vicious circle and restoring liver function, allowed the gland to resume its normal output. The supplements, in these cases, were therapeutic rather than replacement. They provided the push the system needed to escape the downward spiral.

Similarly, some patients with apparently absent pituitary function recovered completely through nutritional improvement alone. "A few people who had extremely low levels of pituitary hormones, and were told that they must take several hormone supplements for the rest of their life, began producing normal amounts of those hormones within a few days of eating more protein and fruit," Peat reported. "Their endocrinologist described them as, effectively, having no pituitary gland." The "permanent" endocrine failure was, in these cases, nutritional suppression masquerading as organic disease.

## NDT Brand Comparison

Peat's hierarchy of products is a hierarchy of trust, not of pharmacology. He distinguished between three things that go by the name "desiccated thyroid": the original Armour USP formula from before 1990, the various reformulations sold under the same name afterward, and the foreign and compounded products that filled the vacuum the reformulations created. His clinical writing returned constantly to the question of *which tablets actually contain thyroid*, because his answer for the U.S. market was: most of them don't, or don't reliably.

### Armour (pre-1990 vs. post-1990)

Armour Thyroid USP was the standard preparation for roughly eighty years. The original was made from beef and pork glands and contained T4, T3, T2, T1, calcitonin, and trace porcine components in something close to the physiological ratio of a working gland. "Armour thyroid, USP, was the standard thyroid used widely for about 80 years," Peat wrote in the [[thyroid|Ray Peat email exchanges on thyroid]]. "Since ownership of the product name was bought by Revlon and then a series of other companies, I'm not sure anything of the simple original formula remains; maybe magnesium stearate, I haven't looked lately."

The reformulation timeline matters because it explains why patients who did well on Armour in the 1980s reported the product "stopped working" through the 1990s and 2000s. Peat had a direct exchange with the manufacturer. "A few years ago I had some communication from a pharmacist at Forest Pharmaceutical, and he said that over ten years ago they began having thyrocalcitonin extracted from the pig thyroid powder to sell separately as a new drug. I think that left stearic acid as the only ingredient the current product might have in common with traditional Armour thyroid, USP" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The calcitonin was pulled out and sold separately. Excipients changed. Formulations were further altered in 2009 under Allergan ownership, with patients reporting reduced potency.

His verdict on the modern product, when asked directly for an NDT recommendation: "I haven't seen anything that compares well with the original Armour" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). This is the load-bearing claim against assuming current Armour will behave like the literature suggests.

### NP Thyroid (Acella)

NP Thyroid from Acella Pharmaceuticals became the most common U.S. alternative through the 2010s, with the same nominal grain-equivalent dosing as Armour (1 grain = 65 mg containing ~38 mcg T4 + 9 mcg T3, in the canonical 4:1 ratio). Peat did not write extensively about NP Thyroid specifically — it post-dates most of his commentary on the U.S. NDT market — but his general filter applies: trust depends on actual lot-to-lot potency, not the label. The 2020 Acella recall of multiple NP Thyroid lots for sub-potency (87–113% labeled range violations) is exactly the failure mode he warned about in his 1970s drug-store survey: "someone bought 10 products sold in US drugstores as 'thyroid, USP,' and found (by immuno-assay) that only 2 of them contained any thyroid glandular tissue" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

### Thyroid-S (Sriprasit, Thailand)

Thai NDT under the Sriprasit "Thyroid-S" label became one of the most widely-sourced over-the-counter options for self-experimenters in the 2010s, available without prescription through online vendors. Peat mentioned the adjacent Thai product Thiroyd from Greater Pharma with characteristic caution: "I have heard from a few people using it, one thinks it doesn't work, but I haven't heard enough details to form an opinion yet" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The clinical lesson he drew across all of these was the same: lot variability in foreign NDT is the rule, not the exception, and the only way to validate a tablet is by metabolic response — temperature, pulse, symptom resolution — over two weeks.

### Erfa Thyroid (Canada)

Erfa Thyroid is the Canadian-manufactured porcine NDT that became the European fallback when Armour quality declined. Peat treated it with the same agnostic suspicion: "I don't know how reliable the Erfa is in composition" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). When asked about European sourcing more broadly, he routed people toward T3 plus separately-sourced synthetic T4: "Is ERFA the only one available? A synthetic thyroxine could be combined with the Cynomel."

### NatureThroid and WP Thyroid (discontinued)

NatureThroid and WP Thyroid from RLC Labs were the U.S. NDT options most often recommended by integrative practitioners as "cleaner" alternatives to Armour, until repeated FDA recalls for sub-potency culminated in RLC discontinuing both products in 2020. Their disappearance is itself confirmation of Peat's broader claim: "I think it's fairly customary for thyroid supplements to be compounded by crooks, morons, and pharmacists, so it's good to be very watchful when trying out any specific product" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

### Cynomel and Cynoplus (Mexico — Peat's preferred)

The two products Peat used himself, and the two he recommended most frequently when asked, were Mexican: Cynomel (pure T3, 25 mcg liothyronine) and Cynoplus (combination, 100 mcg T4 + 25 mcg T3 — a 4:1 ratio matching a healthy gland). "I use Cynoplus (contains T4 and T3) and Cynomel (T3 only) that I usually get from www.mymexicandrugstore.mx. There is only one size tablet, and a fourth of a tablet is a typical starting dose" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). And again: "I use Cynomel and Cynoplus mostly, but they come in only one size, so I cut the tablets into about ten parts."

These were trusted not because they were exotic but because their formulation was simple and their potency tracked the label. "Cytomel and Cynomel, so far, have been very well made, and there isn't any other T3 product that I trust," he wrote in the same exchange. He flagged a subsequent change: "Cytomel's formulation has changed recently, so I'm not sure of its present potency." Cynomel remained his anchor.

His night-dose practice with Cynoplus is a small but distinctive detail: "I always take my cynoplus at night, to go to sleep faster; it has an antiinflammatory effect" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

## Dosing Schedule

Peat's dosing protocol is built on three physiological facts: the healthy human gland produces roughly 4 mcg of T3 per hour, T3 has a half-life of about a day while T4 has a half-life of about two weeks, and the body's response to thyroid is cumulative over roughly two weeks before stabilizing. Every dosing recommendation he gave traces back to these constraints.

### Start low

The starting dose Peat returned to most often was modest by mainstream prescription standards. "A starting dose of about 1 mcg can produce a noticeable effect, and can be repeated at intervals according to the effect. 5 mcg with a meal is another way to start it" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). For NDT preparations, he wrote: "Half a grain of Armour, or about 30 mcg of T4 and 7.5 mcg of T3, is traditionally a common starting dose; it should be taken with a meal, so that it absorbs slowly. Taking a very small amount at bedtime usually helps with insomnia." For Cynomel or Cynoplus the practical instruction was the same: "An eighth of a tablet of either is a good starting dose" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

Compare this to a typical prescribed levothyroxine starting dose of 50–100 mcg taken once in the morning and the physiological mismatch becomes obvious: a 100 mcg T4 bolus exceeds the gland's normal hourly output by twenty-five fold, hits a fasting liver with no glucose to support conversion, and reaches blood T4 levels that suppress the patient's residual gland activity before any therapeutic benefit appears.

### Divide and take with food

"The most important things about supplementing thyroid, that most doctors ignore, are that it should be taken in small divided amounts with meals, and that the temperature and pulse rate should be measured at waking, midday, and afternoon, to watch the effects" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). Food slows absorption, which matters most for T3, where a sudden spike triggers a counter-regulatory liver response: "When you take T3 without food, it enters the blood stream very suddenly, and the liver is likely to detect an excessive amount, causing it to produce enzymes to eliminate it. The result can be a decrease in T3 for the rest of the day."

The split-dose default is a quarter of a Cynoplus or Cynomel tablet (roughly 25 mcg T4 + 6 mcg T3, or 6 mcg T3 alone) taken three or four times across the day with meals, with a small bedtime dose for insomnia. "Usually, a 5 mcg dose of T3 with each meal is effective" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

### Titrate by temperature and pulse, not by TSH

Because TSH suppression and metabolic restoration are different things — and because TSH itself is pathogenic ([[tsh]]) — Peat's titration signal was the [[broda-barnes|Barnes]] protocol: morning temperature on waking, midday temperature, afternoon temperature, resting pulse, and the temperature differential between fingers/toes and core. Healthy targets: midday oral 98.0–98.6°F, resting pulse 80–85 bpm, hands warm.

The post-meal reading is diagnostic for masked hypothyroidism: "If you see a person's temperature and pulse rate fall after a good meal, that reveals a low metabolic rate that was being held up by emergency stimulation from adrenaline and cortisol" (the existing [[#temperature-as-the-ultimate-readout|Temperature as the Ultimate Readout]] section in this article). The Achilles relaxation rate is the cross-check: "Checking the relaxation rate of the Achilles reflex is a quick way to check the effect of the thyroid on your nerves and muscles; the relaxation should be instantaneous, loose and floppy" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

### Two-week increment cycle

Because T4 has a two-week half-life, dose changes for any combination product must be evaluated over two weeks at minimum. "Thyroxine's half-life in the body is two weeks, so the effect is cumulative, and if you get the desired effects in less than two weeks the dose should probably be reduced" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The pattern Peat watched for: gradual lowering of morning temperature (stress hormones falling) plus rising midday temperature (oxidative metabolism rising). A flat or worsening curve at two weeks is the signal to reassess, not to increase further.

### The Barnes ramp

[[broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]]'s original protocol — used on tens of thousands of patients across thirty years of clinical practice — was to start at 1 grain (60 mg NDT) and titrate by basal axillary temperature taken before getting out of bed. Peat's version is lower and slower, reflecting his observation that hypothyroid patients on a Standard American Diet often have such elevated stress hormones that even a small T3 dose unmasks the deficit and feels like overshoot: "T3, by lowering stress, sometimes reveals a low basal metabolic rate, that was hidden by high stress hormones" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

### Magnesium-load before starting

Magnesium retention is itself a thyroid-dependent function. "Magnesium retention by cells is dependent on thyroid hormone, and is essential for cell relaxation," Peat wrote in his [[thyroid-misconceptions|1993 newsletter]]. The implication runs both ways: hypothyroid patients tend to be magnesium-depleted before they start supplementing, and the increased cellular activity from rising T3 will deplete them further unless intake increases. Two quarts of milk daily, supplemented with magnesium glycinate or topical magnesium chloride if needed, is the load-in. Without it, patients commonly report cardiac symptoms (palpitations, racing pulse) that they misattribute to "too much thyroid" when the actual issue is magnesium running short of the metabolic demand the thyroid is now placing on tissue.

### Seasonal adjustment

Thyroid requirements vary by season and latitude. "Sensitivities and requirements vary widely. I've known people who temporarily needed 500 mg of Armour even in the summer, but usually the summer requirement is a fourth of the winter requirement. For some people, 15 mg of Armour was enough, and for some 1 mcg of Cytomel was an effective dose" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). Peat's own dose ranged from half a grain in summer to two grains in winter. Patients who hold a fixed dose year-round commonly overshoot in summer and undershoot in winter.

## T3 Alone vs T4 Alone vs Combination

The choice among preparations is not merely about preference. It reflects whether the patient's liver can convert T4 to T3 reliably, how stable their stress-hormone background is, and whether they need rapid acute relief or long-run metabolic restoration. Peat's framework rank-orders the options.

### Combination (NDT or Cynoplus): the default

For most patients, especially women, the preferred preparation is a combination containing both T4 and T3 in roughly the 4:1 ratio a healthy gland secretes. "I think a combination or natural thyroid such as Thyrolar, Cynoplus, or Armour is usually best" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The advantage is two-fold: the T3 component delivers active hormone immediately, bypassing the liver conversion step that fails in estrogen-loaded or PUFA-loaded patients; the T4 component suppresses TSH and provides a slow background pool from which the body can convert at its own pace.

The combination also stabilizes the response. "When I used only Cytomel, any little stress would make me suddenly hypothyroid, and my heart would stop several times in a minute; when I started using some thyroid, USP, that contained both T4 and T3 it stopped happening" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). T3-only protocols are vulnerable to stress-induced dips because the half-life is too short to buffer a missed dose; combinations carry through.

### Pure T4 (Synthroid, Levothyroxine): the most common, the least effective

The dominant U.S. prescription — synthetic T4 monotherapy — fails in exactly the patient population most likely to be hypothyroid. The full failure mode is covered in the existing [[#what-pure-t4-levothyroxine-gets-wrong|What Pure T4 Gets Wrong]] section, but the cancer-and-conversion summary belongs here: "US doctors don't correct hypothyroidism, because they don't prescribe the active hormone T3, only the precursor T4, which fails to be converted because hypothyroid women's livers aren't efficient. T3 is needed for the storage of glycogen and the efficient use of glucose, and glucose is needed to form T3. Therefore, women in the US who 'are treated for hypothyroidism' are still hypothyroid, and hypothyroid women are much more likely to get cancer" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

T4-only treatment is appropriate for one situation: a young, healthy patient with a well-functioning liver, adequate selenium, low estrogen burden, and good glycogen reserves — in which case the gland's own residual T3 production plus liver conversion will keep up. For the typical hypothyroid woman with elevated estrogen, depleted glycogen, and a fatty liver, T4 monotherapy fails predictably.

### Pure T3 (Cytomel, Cynomel): the acute-relief tool

T3 alone is Peat's choice for two specific situations. First, when symptoms are severe enough to require rapid intervention — insomnia, postpartum collapse, post-MI cardiac protection, dementia in the elderly: "Insomnia is the most commonly troublesome symptom of menopause, and it usually responds immediately to a small bedtime dose of T3" ([[thyroid-misconceptions|"Thyroid: Misconceptions" 1993]]). Second, when the diagnostic question is whether thyroid resistance is present — pure T3 bypasses every step where the resistance could be operating.

The pulsatile-dosing logic comes from T3's pharmacokinetics. Because half-life is roughly 24 hours and the body produces 4 mcg per hour endogenously, the supplementation strategy is to mimic continuous production with small frequent doses rather than to load. "If you use some T3 (such as Cytomel or Cynomel) it's important to keep each dose small, while watching for changes in your pulse and temperature. Usually 4 or 5 mcg at a time is o.k. (the body makes about 4 mcg per hour)" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). A 25 mcg Cynomel tablet cut into six pieces yields roughly 4 mcg doses; taking one with each meal and one at bedtime gives a four-dose schedule that approximates physiological output.

T3-only protocols suit one further population: diabetics with severe conversion impairment. "In diabetes, when cells aren't getting enough glucose, T4 can't be converted to the active T3, and so it can build up in the body to levels that interfere with metabolism, but the advantage of a combination is that T4 inhibits TSH" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). Either pure T3 or a combination works; pure T4 in a diabetic accumulates as inactive precursor.

### Heart-protective small-dose T3

A particularly striking application is acute cardiac use. "For example, a man in the hospital right after a heart attack started taking one mcg per hour; the doctors had said that at the rate his enzymes were rising they would be expected to keep rising for another day, but they started decreasing exactly when he started the small doses, and they had decreased the next day when he left the hospital, without symptoms. T3, sugar, and aspirin are the most heart-protective things" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). See [[heart-disease]] and [[heart-disease-protocol]] for the broader cardiac context.

## The Hair-Loss Connection

Hair follicles are among the most metabolically expensive tissues in the body and among the first to fail when systemic respiration falters. The thyroid-hair connection is one of the cleanest causal chains in Peat's framework, and it explains why the dominant orthodox treatment — finasteride — attacks a stabilizing steroid rather than the underlying metabolic failure.

The chain runs through prolactin. Low thyroid raises serotonin and TRH, which drives both TSH and prolactin from the pituitary. Prolactin is the molting hormone: "In birds, prolactin is known as the molting hormone. In humans, it is the milk-producing hormone, but it also regulates practically every other cell in the body. It has the analogous function of terminating the growth cycle of the hair shaft" ([[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]]). Restoring thyroid lowers prolactin, restores the growth phase of the follicle, and improves copper assimilation, which the [[hair-loss]] page identifies as the melanin cofactor: "Thyroid regulates copper assimilation, and also the hormones that regulate pigment."

The clinical implication: pattern hair loss is not a finasteride problem. It is a thyroid problem with prolactin and estrogen amplification, and the appropriate intervention runs through restoring oxidative metabolism — combination NDT or Cynoplus, divided doses with meals, temperature and pulse titration — not through suppressing 5-alpha-reductase. The full protocol lives in [[hair-loss-protocol]]; the framework page is [[hair-loss]]. The same thyroid-restoration logic underlies the cardiac protective effects covered in [[heart-disease]] and [[heart-disease-protocol]], and the [[broda-barnes|Barnes]] temperature-based dosing protocol referenced throughout this section.

## No-Prescription Workaround

The legal and prescription environment for thyroid hormone varies sharply by country, and Peat's writing reflects a clinical reality in which patients who cannot get adequate prescribed treatment route around their own healthcare systems. This section describes what he documented; it is not a recommendation to bypass medical supervision.

### Why patients route around the system

The U.S. clinical default is levothyroxine monotherapy dosed by TSH suppression, with the reference range (0.3–3.0 mIU/L per AACE, or 0.5–5.0 by older labs) treating the population mean of a chronically hypothyroid society as healthy. A patient who is symptomatic at TSH 2.5, who would have been diagnosed pre-1948 by basal metabolic rate, and who would respond to combination therapy, will typically be told by their prescriber that their thyroid is fine. The mismatch between Peat's diagnostic standard (temperature, pulse, Achilles reflex, symptoms) and the prescribing standard (TSH) creates the patient population that sources hormone abroad.

### Mexican pharmacies

Cynomel and Cynoplus are Mexican prescription products available over-the-counter at many border-town pharmacies and through mail-order vendors who ship internationally. The vendor Peat cited explicitly was mymexicandrugstore.mx; he also referenced farmaciadelnino.com for Cynoplus pricing: "Cynoplus (www.farmaciadelnino.com has a good price) is cheaper than Armour, and an eighth to a fourth of a tablet would be a reasonable amount to start with" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). These vendors fluctuate; the substance, when sourced from a legitimate Mexican pharmacy filling actual Sanofi-manufactured tablets, is identical to U.S. prescription Cytomel by molecular composition. The variable is supply-chain trust, not pharmacology.

### Thai and European sources

Thyroid-S (Sriprasit), Thiroyd (Greater Pharma), and Erfa (Canada) have all been sourced via online vendors. Peat's tone was consistently cautious about the Thai products and agnostic about Erfa. Anti-Aging Systems in England was the European clearinghouse he named: "Anti-aging Systems in England has a large variety of thyroid products" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

### Legal and quality caveats

Three things matter for any patient considering this route. First, U.S. customs treats personal importation of prescription medications in a gray zone — small quantities for personal use typically pass, larger quantities are subject to seizure, and FDA policy has tightened over time. Second, the quality control problem Peat documented in the 1970s ("only 2 of 10 products contained any thyroid glandular tissue") applies *more* strongly to unregulated international supply chains. Third, the absence of medical supervision means no one is watching for the cardiac, bone-density, and gland-suppression effects that prolonged misdosing can produce.

The Peat-aligned alternative for U.S. patients is to find a prescriber willing to write for Cytomel, NP Thyroid, or compounded T4/T3 — the network of physicians compiled historically by Mary Shomon was Peat's referral path ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). For patients with no other option, his protocol of small divided doses with food, two-week temperature-pulse titration, and conservative starting dose is the same regardless of the tablet's origin. The hardest constraint is not the source but the discipline of charting.

The diagnostic and pharmacological foundations for everything in this section live in the existing body of this article, in [[hypothyroidism]], in [[tsh]], and in [[broda-barnes]]. The point of describing the workaround is honest documentation of what Peat said, not endorsement of self-medication outside clinical care.
