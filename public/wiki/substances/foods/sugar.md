# Sugar

No position in Ray Peat's framework provokes more disbelief than his defense of sugar. In an era where sugar is blamed for [[obesity]], [[diabetes]], heart disease, and cancer, Peat argued the opposite: adequate sugar intake prevents the stress-hormone cascade that drives degenerative disease, supports [[thyroid]] function, and provides the substrate for efficient oxidative [[metabolism]]. "Ordinary sugars—sucrose—have a tremendously protective effect against inflammation-producing factors," he explained in a [[2011-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-endotoxins-stress|2011 episode on serotonin and stress]]. "Fructose catalyzes the ability to use glucose efficiently. Sucrose is better than even well-cooked starch."

## The Anti-Stress Mechanism

Sugar's protective role operates through a simple metabolic principle: when blood glucose is adequate, the body does not need to activate stress hormones to maintain it. Every time blood sugar drops—from skipping a meal, exercising intensely, or sleeping through the night—[[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]] rise to mobilize stored fuel. If that stored fuel contains [[PUFA]], the released fatty acids suppress thyroid function, block glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]], and generate inflammatory products.

Adequate dietary sugar prevents this entire cascade. By maintaining blood glucose directly, sugar renders unnecessary the cortisol-driven tissue catabolism, the adrenaline-driven fat mobilization, and the PUFA-mediated thyroid suppression that follow every blood sugar drop. As Peat stated: "A dose of sugar is the first best antidote to starvation poisoning."

## Sucrose Over Starch

Peat consistently distinguished between sugar (sucrose, fruit sugars) and starch (glucose polymers from grains, potatoes, and root vegetables). The distinction centers on fructose—the sugar that makes sucrose and fruit metabolically superior to starch.

Sucrose is one molecule of glucose bonded to one molecule of fructose. Table sugar, honey, and most fruits provide this combination. Starchy foods provide only glucose. As Peat explained in a [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|2011 episode on sugar myths]], "Fruits have approximately the same ratio of fructose and glucose as white sugar. Grains, beans, starches, bread, pasta, potatoes are almost all glucose—devoid of fructose."

This matters because fructose provides unique metabolic benefits. It enters cells without requiring [[insulin]], maintaining cellular energy even when insulin signaling is impaired. It chelates iron—reducing the oxidative damage that excess iron catalyzes in every tissue. And it "catalyzes the ability to use glucose efficiently," as Peat described—meaning cells that have access to fructose oxidize glucose better than cells that have glucose alone.

In a [[2012-10-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidants|2012 episode on antioxidants]], Peat elaborated: "Fructose maintains cells in low phosphate, intense oxidative energy, and chelates iron—a major oxidative damage factor." The combination of glucose and fructose in sucrose activates oxidative metabolism more efficiently than glucose alone can.

## Historical Evidence

The historical medical use of sugar for diabetics—documented before the insulin era—provides some of the most striking evidence for Peat's position. Physicians in 1860s Paris treated diabetic patients by adding 10-12 ounces of sugar daily to their diets, matching the amount they were losing in urine. Patients who were wasting away recovered within weeks.

Late 19th-century doctors "added about 12 ounces per day of sugar to diets of terminal diabetics; people came back from near death in weeks," as Peat described in a [[2015-03-11-politics-and-science-politics-science-biochemical-health|2015 episode]]. The logic was metabolic rather than paradoxical: these patients' cells were starving for glucose despite being awash in it (because fat was blocking its oxidation). Providing fructose alongside glucose allowed cells to bypass the block and resume energy production.

## Cancer and Sugar

The claim that "sugar feeds cancer" is one of the most common objections to Peat's sugar advocacy. He addressed it directly. For cancer patients, Peat recommended "a very low-fat, very high sucrose/fructose diet." In a [[2021-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-managing-hormones-and-cancer-treatment-with-nutrition|2021 episode on cancer nutrition]], he discussed the "Grape Diet"—a 1940s-50s protocol combining high glucose/sucrose with anti-inflammatory flavonoids as anti-cancer agents.

The reasoning: cancer cells rely on fermentative metabolism (producing lactic acid from glucose) because their oxidative machinery is damaged. Restricting sugar does not selectively starve cancer—it triggers the stress-hormone cascade that feeds cancer more effectively than dietary sugar ever could. Cortisol breaks down tissue, mobilizes PUFA, suppresses immunity, and provides cancer with exactly the catabolic environment it thrives in. Adequate sugar prevents this cascade, keeping the immune system functional and the metabolic environment hostile to cancer.

## Fruit as the Optimal Sugar Source

While refined sugar is "extremely pure but lacks essential nutrients," as Peat noted, fruit provides sugar with the minerals needed to metabolize it—particularly potassium and magnesium. Orange juice was Peat's most frequently recommended sugar source, combining fructose, glucose, potassium, magnesium, and anti-inflammatory flavonoids (including the aromatase inhibitor naringenin).

"Two quarts of milk plus one quart of orange juice daily provides enough fructose and sugars for general stress resistance," Peat wrote. "Larger amounts can protect against increased stress and reverse degenerative conditions."

His preferred fruits included oranges, watermelons, grapes, and guavas—all providing high sugar content with protective phytochemicals. Berries were "preferable to starches if eaten without too many seeds." Refined sugar, while not harmful per se, "should be considered temporary therapeutic material or occasional substitute" because it lacks the minerals that fruits provide.

## High Fructose Corn Syrup

Peat distinguished HFCS from fruit sugar and sucrose. In a [[2011-07-12-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-qa-i|2011 episode]], he noted that "high fructose corn syrup may contain harmful intermediates between sugar and starch." The processing can leave residual compounds that are biologically active in ways that pure fructose and sucrose are not. His recommendation: "White sugar is safe regarding allergens. Fruit is the best way to get sugar because it comes with minerals."

## Practical Application

Peat personally consumed substantial amounts of sugar. As he noted in his emails, he "often had a gallon of orange juice in a day plus 100 grams of other sugar with no problem. If metabolic rate is high, even a pound of sugar still leaves appetite for fat and protein."

The principles for practical application:

- **Fruit juice** (especially orange juice) as the primary sugar source, providing minerals alongside carbohydrate
- **Sugar with meals** rather than between meals, to prevent the insulin spike-crash cycle
- **Adequate protein** alongside sugar, to prevent the cortisol rise that occurs when protein intake is insufficient
- **Avoid starch as a sugar substitute**—starch provides glucose without fructose, lacks the minerals, and may cause more intestinal irritation
- **No fear of quantity** when metabolic rate is adequate—the body converts excess sugar to saturated fat through de novo lipogenesis, which is protective
