# Cherries

Cherries earned an explicit place on Ray Peat's short list of recommended fruits--a distinction that carries more weight than it might appear, given how selective Peat was about which fruits he endorsed by name. In email correspondence listing "good sources of sugar to have regularly," Peat included cherries alongside orange juice, guavas, watermelons, cherimoyas, cooked apples, and ripe papayas. This is the inner circle of Peat's fruit recommendations: foods that deliver readily available sugar with minimal digestive burden, supporting the high [[metabolic rate]] he associated with health, youth, and resilience against disease.

## A Named Recommendation

Peat's fruit endorsements followed a consistent logic: the best fruits are those that provide simple sugars (glucose and fructose) in easily absorbed form, carry protective micronutrients, and impose minimal stress on the intestinal tract. Cherries satisfy each criterion. Their flesh is soft and juicy when ripe, their sugar content is substantial (roughly 12-14g per 100g), and their fiber content is low compared to temperate fruits like [[apple|apples]] and pears, whose [[pectin]] content Peat considered "very good bacterial food and very poor human food."

The small pit at the center of each cherry is swallowed or discarded--not chewed--which means cherries avoid the seed-consumption concerns Peat raised with other fruits. [[Berries|Berry]] seeds, for instance, carry small amounts of [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] and pass through the gut largely intact, feeding [[bacteria]] along the way. Cherry pits are too large and hard to eat accidentally, making the fruit functionally seedless from a digestive standpoint.

## Anthocyanins and Anti-Inflammatory Properties

The deep red and purple pigments in cherries are anthocyanins--a class of [[bioflavonoids]] that Peat valued for their anti-[[estrogen|estrogenic]], anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. As discussed in a [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|2013 episode on aging and energy reversal]], the bioflavonoid compounds in fruits serve protective roles that extend beyond simple antioxidant chemistry: they modulate enzyme activity, support [[progesterone]] pathways, and help maintain the oxidative balance Peat considered essential for cellular health.

Dark-colored cherries--Bing, black, and other deeply pigmented sweet varieties--contain the highest anthocyanin concentrations. These same compounds have been documented in research literature as potent inhibitors of COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes, the same targets as [[aspirin]] and ibuprofen. While Peat did not cite cherry-specific research extensively, his broader framework valued exactly these properties: natural compounds that reduce inflammation without the side effects of pharmaceutical anti-inflammatories, delivered in a food matrix that simultaneously provides metabolic fuel.

## The Melatonin Irony

Cherries contain measurable quantities of [[melatonin]]--a fact widely promoted in health media as a reason to eat cherries before bed. This creates a small irony within Peat's framework, since he was one of melatonin's most persistent critics, arguing that it functions as an immunosuppressant, promotes cellular reversion toward primitive states, and accelerates aspects of aging rather than opposing them.

The resolution is quantitative rather than philosophical. The melatonin content of cherries--typically measured at 1-13 nanograms per gram of fruit--is trivial compared to supplemental doses, which range from 0.5 to 10 milligrams. A serving of cherries delivers roughly 0.001% of a standard melatonin supplement dose. At these levels, the melatonin is physiologically insignificant, overwhelmed by the fruit's sugar, bioflavonoids, and other protective compounds. Peat's objection to melatonin was directed at the pharmacological doses used in supplements and the inflated claims surrounding them, not at trace amounts naturally present in recommended foods.

## Sweet vs. Tart Varieties

The distinction between sweet cherries (Bing, Rainier, Stella) and tart cherries (Montmorency, Morello) matters within Peat's framework. Sweet cherries are higher in sugar and lower in organic acids, making them more aligned with his preference for easily metabolized, non-irritating fruit. Tart cherries contain higher concentrations of anthocyanins and have generated more research interest for anti-inflammatory applications, but their astringency and lower sugar content make them less ideal as a regular sugar source.

Tart cherry juice, often marketed as a recovery drink for athletes, concentrates both the beneficial anthocyanins and the organic acids. Within Peat's approach, sweetening tart cherry juice with [[sugar]] or [[honey]] would offset the acidity while preserving the bioflavonoid content--a preparation that extracts the protective compounds without the astringent drawback of eating the tart fruit whole.

Sweet cherry juice, while less studied, provides a more balanced ratio of sugar to acid and would align more naturally with Peat's recommendations for regular fruit juice consumption alongside [[orange-juice|orange juice]] and [[watermelon]] juice.

## Seasonal Availability and Practical Use

Cherries are among the most seasonal of temperate fruits, with fresh availability typically limited to a few weeks in early summer. This narrow harvest window means that for most of the year, cherries are available only frozen, dried, or as juice. Frozen cherries retain their nutritional profile well and can be thawed for consumption or used in cooked preparations. Dried cherries, like all dried fruit, concentrate [[iron]] and sugar while losing water and vitamin content--the same concern that places [[dates]] below fresh fruit in Peat's hierarchy.

Fresh cherries during their brief season represent one of the better temperate fruit options available. Their combination of endorsement by name from Peat, favorable sugar content, protective anthocyanins, and practical seedlessness makes them worth prioritizing when available--a seasonal complement to the year-round staples of orange juice and tropical fruits that formed the backbone of Peat's fruit recommendations.
