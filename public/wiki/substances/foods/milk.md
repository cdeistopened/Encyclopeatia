# Milk

Milk stands beside [[oranges|orange juice]] and [[coffee]] as one of the three foundational beverages of Ray Peat's dietary framework, and of the three, it is the one he recommended in the largest daily quantity. Where orange juice supplies sugar and flavonoids, milk provides the nutrient Peat considered most underappreciated in modern nutrition: an abundant, readily absorbed source of [[calcium]] in a ratio to phosphorus no other common food matches. "The high ratio of calcium to phosphorus is one of the extremely beneficial qualities of milk," he told the KMUD hosts on a [[2011-08-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-milk|2011 episode of *Ask the Herb Doctor*]] devoted to the subject. For a quick therapeutic diet, his prescription was unambiguous. On a [[2008-12-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cholesterol-is-an-important-molecule|2008 discussion of cholesterol]]: "I recommend at least two quarts of milk and two quarts of orange juice every day. This will stimulate the [[thyroid]], suppress inflammation, and help the liver regulate things."

Peat's framework inverts the mainstream reasoning. The saturated fat that cardiology condemns, he saw as protective. The lactose that functional medicine treats as inflammatory, he identified as a metabolic stimulant. The calcium that orthopedic specialists pack into pills, he insisted could only work properly when delivered in the biological package nature built around it. Milk was a concentrated preparation of the anti-stress machinery the body uses to keep [[cortisol]], [[prolactin]], and serotonin in check.

## The Calcium-Phosphorus Ratio

Most protein-rich foods, especially muscle meats, deliver large quantities of phosphorus alongside relatively little calcium. The phosphorus load drives up [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]] (PTH), which mobilizes calcium from bone to buffer the blood. Peat associated the resulting chronically elevated PTH with nearly every degenerative disease he studied.

Milk's roughly 2:1 calcium-to-phosphorus balance makes it one of the few whole foods that actively suppresses PTH. In [[milk-in-context-allergies-ecology-and-some-myths|"Milk in context: allergies, ecology, and some myths"]], Peat laid out the cascade: "When there is adequate calcium, vitamin D, and magnesium in the diet, PTH is kept to a minimum. When PTH is kept low, cells increase their formation of the uncoupling proteins, that cause mitochondria to use energy at a higher rate, and this is associated with decreased activity of the fatty acid synthase enzymes." The question of dietary calcium opens onto the broader question of metabolic rate itself.

The disease associations stretch across domains mainstream medicine rarely connects. "The metabolic syndrome, that involves diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, is associated with high PTH. Multiple sclerosis relapses consistently occur at times of high PTH, and remissions consistently occur at times of low PTH. Alzheimer's disease involves decreased mitochondrial activity and low levels of the uncoupling proteins. There is evidence that milk drinkers are protected against dementia." On a [[2011-09-29-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-cholesterol-and-saturated-fats|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode]], he distilled the pharmacology: "PTH is a stress hormone that, when continued too long, contributes to degenerative diseases. Having a chronically high calcium intake gives you a chronically low parathyroid level, which protects your arteries, kidneys, and bones."

The clinical implication cuts against intuition. On a [[2011-06-03-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-milk-calcium-and-hormones|2011 conversation with Josh Rubin]], Peat described treating patients with elevated serum calcium by giving them *more* dietary calcium: "I often see people with a blood calcium of 11, 12, or 13 (when it should be 9 or 10). By getting them to eat *more* calcium, and maybe take Vitamin D and K, they can very quickly get their calcium down to normal. The reason it is high is generally because they are eating too much phosphate and overdriving their parathyroid hormone." The high number was not evidence of excess; it was evidence of bones being dismantled to compensate for dietary inadequacy.

## The Anti-Inflammatory Mineral

The same paradox shows up in the domain of calcification. People who fear milk because of arterial plaque or kidney stones are pulling the wrong lever. On the 2011 KMUD episode, Peat cited Adelle Davis's 1950s observation: "People who have the least calcium in their diet have the most kidney stones and the most calcified arteries. That is because of the anti-inflammatory effect of calcium." The calcium deposited in arteries was being pulled from bone in response to deficiency. "The calcium in your diet, by having an anti-inflammatory and pro-thyroid effect, tends to direct the deposition of calcium into your bones. It keeps the production of carbon dioxide up by stimulating cell metabolism. The same carbon dioxide that deposits calcium into the bones prevents calcium from depositing in arteries and kidneys by keeping it in a soluble state." Calcium and CO2 are partners in the same traffic system.

The hormonal layer compounds the mineral one. "One of the factors in osteoporosis is high cortisol or high prolactin. Both of those are caused by high serotonin. Calcium in your diet suppresses prolactin, cortisol, and serotonin, allowing the bone-building factors to be dominant." For a woman worried about bone density, the answer was not a calcium tablet and a bisphosphonate but enough milk, every day, to shut down the stress cascade eating her skeleton.

## Lactose: The Absorption Key

If calcium is the load milk delivers, lactose is the key that unlocks it. "The absorption of calcium in milk is greatly improved by the presence of lactose," Peat told the KMUD audience. "When you compare different sugars, [[fructose]] is not as good as lactose, but it is still an important factor in helping to absorb calcium. Starch has a very poor effect on calcium absorption." Vitamin D-deficient rats fed sucrose developed strong bones while starch-fed controls did not. The sugar itself was the variable that determined skeletal outcome.

The implication for lactose-free milk was unambiguous. Asked whether its drinkers would absorb as much calcium, Peat answered with a single word: "Right." The category marketed as a gentler alternative had discarded the component that made the mineral work. Beyond absorption, lactose fits the broader pattern of metabolism-stimulating sugars. "It actually works parallel to thyroid and Vitamin D for stimulating metabolism. Calcium, the T3 component of the thyroid hormone, and fructose all have overlapping effects in activating respiratory metabolism." The intersection of calcium, lactose, and saturated fat places milk at the crossing point of three of Peat's metabolic levers simultaneously.

## Lactose Intolerance: Usually a Symptom

Peat treated the modern anxiety about lactose intolerance with skepticism. The diagnosis confuses two things: true genetic lactase deficiency, which is relatively rare, and the acquired loss that follows from not drinking milk. Because the enzyme is inducible by its substrate, the absence of lactase in a lifelong non-drinker is a consequence of the avoidance, not a reason to continue it.

On the KMUD episode, Peat described research from San Francisco's Chinatown: "Studies compared people who hadn't grown up drinking milk to people who had, same family, same genes. It is a matter of what you grow up exposed to. When you take someone who has grown up without it and have them drink a total of a glass and a half of milk a day, about half a glass at a time, in just two or three weeks, they induce the enzymes in their intestine." The same pattern held for biopsy-confirmed cases: the lactase could be restored in about two weeks. Blinded studies closed the question. Peat wrote in "Milk in context" that intolerant people given milk with or without lactose showed no difference and "consistently tolerate having a glass with each meal." Later research confirmed measurable adaptation (Pribila, et al., 2000).

The more interesting question was what caused the loss of lactase in the first place. "A deficiency of [[progesterone]] and thyroid, or a bacterial infection of the small intestine, will cause the loss of those enzymes." Lactose intolerance, in this reading, is often a symptom of [[hypothyroidism]] or bacterial overgrowth, both themselves symptoms of the metabolic collapse adequate milk helps reverse. In his emails Peat noted the epidemiological irony: "The people who drink the most milk, in the Samburu and Masai cultures, and the cultures of northern India, don't seem to have the problem. I doubt that this is a matter of genetic differences."

## Raw vs. Pasteurized: A Non-Issue

On the question that preoccupies the alternative-health world, Peat was uninterested in the romanticism of raw milk. Asked on the KMUD episode whether he considered raw milk nutritionally superior, he acknowledged the marginal difference without endorsing the crusade: "Definitely. It is nutritionally better. But that doesn't mean you should eat soybeans or bread if you can't get raw milk." In his email correspondence, he was blunter: "[RAW OR PASTEURIZED MILK] The difference isn't enough to worry about. [HOMOGENIZED MILK] It's ok." Raw milk carries genuine infection risks that pasteurization was developed to eliminate, and Peat, who spent his career writing about the dangers of [[endotoxin]] and bacterial inflammation, was not going to recommend a preparation that increased infection probability for marginal gains.

Homogenization received similar treatment. The mainstream alternative argument holds that mechanical breaking of fat globules causes the fat to penetrate arterial walls. Peat dismissed it directly: "When we secrete bile, the soapy material in the bile breaks up all the fats we eat into particles of about that same size, which do get directly into the bloodstream. That is the chylomicron system." The body routinely handles fat particles at homogenization size. He conceded one minor concern: "The pressure breaks the particle of fat up into such a small size that it doesn't separate. Presumably, that makes unstable fats oxidize faster, so it has a small harmful effect. But considering the alternatives in a supermarket, other foods are going to be much more dangerous as oxidants."

## Estrogen, Grass, and the 1% Recommendation

The fear that milk delivers dangerous estrogen is another one Peat addressed with arithmetic. "The amount in milk is maybe up to 1 microgram per quart. Estrogen is really as much a male hormone as a female one. Natural estrogen is processed in both men and women and detoxified fairly quickly. About 85% of the estrogen in milk is attached to either sulfate or glucuronic acid, which prepares it to be excreted by the kidneys. A birth control pill has 30 micrograms. No one drinks 30 liters of milk in a day." The progesterone content further moderated the picture: "Most milk has at least 20 times as much progesterone as [[estrogen]]. If you did get some active estrogen, the progesterone is more than enough to counteract its effect."

Grass-fed milk drew Peat's specific approval for reasons that differed from the mainstream narrative. "Fresh grass has a high ratio of Vitamin E to fatty acids. In grains, especially if stored, Vitamin E is depleted. Cows need Vitamin E in proportion to fatty acids because the bacteria in the cow's rumen destroy the polyunsaturated fats (PUFA) in plant material. The bacteria hydrogenate those PUFAs so that 98% of the polyunsaturated fat is destroyed, *if* the cow has enough Vitamin E." A properly fed cow is a bacterial refinery that converts plant material, PUFA and all, into saturated milk fat. The same filtering logic explained milk's general safety: the cow's rumen, liver, and udder strip allergens and toxins before nutrients reach the milk.

On fat content, Peat's recommendation contradicts a common assumption in the Peat-adjacent community. In his emails: "I think 1%-fat milk is best for most people." The reasoning was pharmacological. The estradiol in milk, he noted, "is concentrated in the cream, so low-fat milk has very little estrogen." Low-fat milk delivers the therapeutic calcium, protein, and lactose with a smaller fat load and a lower hormonal footprint. Whole milk was not forbidden; 1% was the default.

## The Strontium Filter

One of Peat's more striking observations concerned milk's behavior as a biological filter for environmental contaminants. On the 2011 *EastWest Healing* episode, he described the Strontium 90 pattern: "Plants extracted the calcium, since that was what their cells needed, but left behind a good proportion of the strontium. When cows ate the vegetation, they again extracted a high proportion of calcium and filtered out a lot of strontium. When you add up the essential nutrients, if you get them from vegetable matter rather than milk, you are getting extremely high concentrations of the fallout isotopes. Milk is a good filtering process to eliminate all kinds of contaminants." The same logic extends to hormones. Speaking with Josh Rubin, Peat noted that "babies whose thyroids were destroyed by living near Three Mile Island during the accident didn't suffer symptoms of hypothyroidism as long as they were being breastfed," because the mother's thyroid hormone passed through milk.

## Iron, Fruit, and the Anti-Stress Package

The comparatively low iron content of milk, often cited as a weakness, is a second design feature. In "Milk in context," Peat explained: "Since the fetus stores a large amount of iron during gestation, the iron content of milk is low. However, for an adult, the low iron content of milk and cheese makes these foods useful for preventing the iron overload that often contributes to the degenerative diseases." For an adult with decades of iron accumulation, the property is straightforwardly protective.

Combined with fruit, milk moves from anti-inflammatory to actively therapeutic. "Combining milk and cheese with fruits adds to the antistress effect. The additional sugar and potassium and other minerals allow the milk protein to be used more efficiently, by moderating the secretion of cortisol, and helping to inhibit the secretion of PTH." The two-quarts-of-milk plus two-quarts-of-orange-juice prescription was not arbitrary. Milk suppressed PTH and calmed the nervous system; orange juice's potassium, [[magnesium]], and fructose kept cortisol down and supported the mitochondrial energy production milk's calcium was priming. The combination delivered a more comprehensive intervention than either alone.

## Carrageenan and Powdered Milk

Peat's relaxed attitude toward pasteurization and homogenization ended abruptly at additives. Carrageenan, the thickening agent common in chocolate milk, cream, and yogurt, was a food he warned against unambiguously. On the 2011 *EastWest Healing* episode: "It's a sulfated polysaccharide. It can be interpreted as a sign of damage to our own connective tissue, causing acute immune reactions. It is used experimentally to cause inflammation in research." The industry defense relied on a distinction that failed in vivo. "The use of it in food is justified by experiments showing that native carrageenan doesn't induce cancer. But if you allow bacteria to break down the carrageenan into smaller fragments, that *will* cause cancer. Our intestine contains bacteria able to do that breakdown." Carrageenan fights the very calcium regulation milk is supposed to supply. Organic milk, chocolate milk, whipping cream, cottage cheese, and many yogurts regularly contain it. Plain fluid milk and most cheeses do not. The label check takes seconds.

Powdered milk got a more nuanced treatment. In his emails: "It's not as good as fresh milk, or cheese, but when they aren't available, 100 grams (or more) would be a good addition to the diet, because of the high ratio of calcium to phosphate." The calcium-phosphorus ratio survives processing. What does not survive is the milk fat. Spray-drying exposes unsaturated fats to high heat and oxygen, producing oxidized PUFA that Peat spent his career warning against. For someone without access to fresh milk, powdered is a reasonable substitute. For anyone with a refrigerator, it is not the default.

## The Daily Protocol

The practical implementation was straightforward. Two quarts per day for a quick therapeutic result, 1% milk for most people, paired with orange juice and fruit for the full anti-stress effect, cheese as backup when fresh milk is unavailable, carrageenan off the shopping list. The quantity is the part most people resist. Two quarts is eight cups, roughly double what most Americans drink in a week. The high dose was metabolic rather than nutritional in the ordinary sense: the therapeutic effect on PTH, prolactin, and cortisol requires a calcium load large enough to shift the hormonal set point. Below a threshold, milk is a pleasant beverage. Above it, milk is a pharmacological intervention.

Peat addressed the caloric concern on the 2008 KMUD episode: "As it starts working, it will increase caloric needs." The protocol activates the thyroid axis that burns the fuel it delivers. The mathematics of chronic dieting, which assume a fixed metabolic rate, do not apply to a body pulled out of the low-energy trap by its calcium intake.

Milk was one food among several in a framework that also emphasized sugar, saturated fat, and the anti-stress function of [[gelatin]]. What distinguished it was the density of metabolic effects per gram and per dollar. A carton of pasteurized milk delivered calcium, balanced protein, naturally occurring progesterone, [[vitamin-a|vitamin A]], lactose as an absorption cofactor, and a pharmacologically meaningful dose of [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]]2 in the fat fraction. No other food in the refrigerated case could make the same claim. The case for drinking it daily was the case for using the foundation the body was built to use.

## Low-fat vs whole vs raw — the Peat ranking

A common misreading of Peat in his online afterlife is that he ranked whole or raw milk above the 1% carton on the supermarket shelf. He didn't. His ranking was situational, and for the median adult he named the version most people would discard. In the milk email file: "I think 1%-fat milk is best for most people." The rationale ran through estradiol partitioning: estrogen "is concentrated in the cream, so low-fat milk has very little estrogen." Pulling out two-thirds of the fat removed most of the hormonal residue while preserving the calcium, protein, lactose, and water-soluble vitamins that did the therapeutic work. From his diet-general correspondence: "I use 1% milk, some butter and hydrogenated coconut oil, to keep polyunsaturated fats to a minimum." Decoupling the milk-fat decision from the saturated-fat decision lets you load saturated fat from cleaner sources — [[butter]], [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], cheese — without raising the daily PUFA floor every gallon of whole milk carries.

For Peat himself it was practical: "Over the years I averaged a gallon a day, and I liked to eat butter, fat meat, ice cream, and thick cream in my coffee, so 1% milk had enough fat. I didn't like the taste of skimmed milk, and the available 1% happens to be pasteurized." 1% was the residual after the other fat decisions had been made.

Whole milk wasn't banished. It worked better for thin or growing people who needed the calorie density, for children, and for adults rebuilding from severe weight loss. He used 2% and 3% himself during higher-activity years: "I used to drink at least a gallon of 2% or 3% milk daily, and often ate more than 5000 calories." The fat fraction is a calorie and estradiol dial, not a moral one. Raw is a separate axis again, and ranks above pasteurized only when tolerated and uncontaminated.

The ordering, in practice:

1. **1% pasteurized** — default for most adults. Concentrates [[calcium]] without the cream's estradiol or PUFA load.
2. **Whole pasteurized** — thin adults, growing children, athletes, rebuilders. Calorie density and [[vitamin-a|vitamin A]] / [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]]2 from the fat fraction.
3. **Raw, from a known clean herd** — marginal nutritional edge, real infection risk; worth it if you have it.
4. **Skim** — almost never. Peat disliked the taste and the missing fat-soluble vitamins.
5. **Lactose-free, ultra-pasteurized** — last resort for the small population that genuinely can't tolerate anything else.

## The 2-quart floor

The two-quart prescription is the part of the protocol most people quietly ignore. Two quarts is eight cups, roughly 2,400 mg of calcium plus a complete amino-acid profile and ~50 g of [[lactose]]. The number isn't arbitrary: it's the [[calcium]] load required to push [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]] into the lower end of its range and keep it there.

The mainstream RDA — 1,000-1,200 mg per day for adults — Peat treated as enough to prevent acute deficiency disease, not enough to produce a therapeutic effect on PTH, [[prolactin]], serotonin, or [[cortisol]]. In [[calcium-and-disease-hypertension-organ-calcification-shock-vs-respiratory-energy|"Calcium and disease"]] he framed the problem as population-wide subclinical insufficiency: "Simply eating an adequate amount of calcium and magnesium can alleviate many problems related to stress and aging that are considered serious, such as heart arrhythmia, pancreatitis, and tissue calcification." Two quarts is the lowest reliable food-only intake that hits the dose-response threshold without leaning on supplements he didn't trust to deliver the same biological effect.

The framework is hierarchical: the [[parathyroid-hormone|Ca:P ratio]] determines whether PTH rises or falls regardless of the absolute calcium number. Phosphorus from muscle meats, grains, and soft drinks can swamp the calcium even when intake is technically adequate. Two quarts of milk delivers both a high absolute dose *and* a calcium-favorable ratio that displaces phosphorus-heavy foods from the rest of the plate.

In the diet-general email Peat tied the floor directly to low-carbohydrate patterns: "Usually the low carbohydrate diets have a high ratio of phosphate to calcium, and I suspect that your present diet does, too. If you powder some eggshells, that's the best way to supplement it, but two quarts of milk per day would be best, providing adequate protein and a safe ratio of P to Ca." The floor exists because anything less lets the diet's phosphorus load win.

For working applications, see [[core-diet-protocol|the core diet protocol]] and [[pregnancy-nutrition|pregnancy nutrition]] — both lean on two quarts as the structural anchor. People who can't get there quickly are asked to substitute cheese (~30-40 g per missed cup) plus eggshell powder, not to lower the calcium target.

## A1 vs A2 beta-casein

The A1/A2 question — whether milk from cows carrying the A1 variant of beta-casein releases a digestive peptide (BCM-7, beta-casomorphin-7) that drives inflammation, while A2 milk does not — is a recurring source of anxiety in the Peat-adjacent community and a recurring source of marketing for the small dairies that carry Jersey, Guernsey, Brown Swiss, or Devon herds. The basic biology is real: a single amino-acid swap at position 67 of beta-casein (proline in A2, histidine in A1) does change cleavage during digestion, and A1 milk releases more BCM-7 in vitro. The clinical consequences in humans are much weaker than the marketing suggests.

Peat addressed this through the lens he applied to most allergy claims: the cow's diet, the herd's bacterial load, and the consumer's gut state usually mattered more than casein subtype. In the milk email: "When a woman or a cow eats an allergen, such as peanuts or soybeans, the allergens appear in the milk. Weeds in the pasture are another potential source of tainted milk... most of the milk in commerce has synthetic vitamins (dissolved in corn oil) emulsified into the product, which could account for many of the bad reactions." The diagnostic he kept returning to was what the cow ate and what the consumer's intestine looked like, not which casein gene the herd carried.

This is where Peat's position diverges from the A2-only marketing community. The A2 camp treats the casein variant as the load-bearing variable. Peat treated it as secondary behind feed, herd bacteria, freshness, and the consumer's own [[hypothyroidism|thyroid status]] and gut flora — a matter of secondary importance. His first-line advice was to try a different dairy when one brand caused symptoms; herd-to-herd variability was large enough that switching brands often solved the problem before the casein question had to be answered.

For the practical chooser:

- **US supermarket default** — Holstein-dominant, A1/A1 or A1/A2 mix.
- **Jersey, Guernsey, Brown Swiss, Devon** — mostly A2/A2 or A2-leaning. Small dairies, herdshares, branded products (a2 Milk Company).
- **Human, goat, sheep, buffalo** — naturally A2-type. Relevant for those trying goat as a substitute.

The standard troubleshooting order is: switch brands → try ultra-pasteurized → change fat percentage → try goat → try A2-only. Casein subtype lives in the middle of the list, not the top.

## Lactose-intolerance workarounds

For people already told they can't drink milk, Peat's framework offers several maneuvers before conceding the diagnosis. All stack onto the [[lactase]]-induction observation in the section above: the enzyme is responsive to substrate, and the intestine can usually be coaxed back into producing it in two to three weeks.

The maneuvers, roughly in order of use:

1. **Split into small doses.** From the [[2011-06-03-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-milk-calcium-and-hormones|2011 EastWest Healing]] episode: "Even if a person does have lactose intolerance from a lactase deficiency, tests have found that if they drink a cup per meal or less, they don't have the diarrhea that could be produced by drinking a pint of milk on an empty stomach." A pint on an empty stomach is the worst case. Half a cup with food is the dose that induces. The 2011 KMUD protocol of "a total of a glass and a half of milk a day, about half a glass at a time" re-induced the enzyme in two to three weeks.

2. **Warm milk with a meal.** Cold milk on an empty stomach moves through the small intestine faster, giving residual lactase less contact time. Warm milk with other food slows transit. From the diet-general email: "If you think you might have a real milk allergy, you should start with just a drop or a sip. Adding sugar or honey (if you aren't allergic to honey) will decrease any allergic reaction to the milk."

3. **Add salt and sugar.** Sugar suppresses [[cortisol]] release; salt supports sodium balance and intestinal calcium absorption. The Mexican café con leche pattern — milk, coffee, sugar, sometimes cinnamon — fits this template.

4. **Try ultra-pasteurized.** From the 2011 EastWest Healing episode: "I know a few people who get gas or diarrhea from most supermarket milks, but if they drink only the ultra-pasteurized milk—which has been heated to about 135 degrees Fahrenheit—they tolerate that nicely." UHT removes the residual bacterial-fermentation pathway that triggers symptoms in some sensitive intestines.

5. **Switch brands or breeds.** Different herds, different feed, different bacterial profiles. Peat's first-line move for any milk reaction. A2-only milk fits here, not at the top.

6. **Add lactase enzyme.** From the milk email: "I think the lactase milk is safe." Lactaid or a lactase pill is a reasonable bridge through the induction weeks.

7. **Substitute cheese and yogurt.** Aged cheeses contain almost no lactose; yogurt contains pre-digested lactose plus live lactase-producing bacteria. From the milk email: "I know people who tolerate only the ultrapasteurized milk. What about cheeses?" Cheese was Peat's standard fallback when fluid milk wasn't tolerated.

8. **Address the upstream cause.** Lactase loss often tracks [[hypothyroidism]], low [[progesterone]], or bacterial overgrowth. From [[milk-in-context-allergies-ecology-and-some-myths|"Milk in context"]]: "A deficiency of progesterone and thyroid, or a bacterial infection of the small intestine, will cause the loss of those enzymes." This is the real fix; the other seven are the bridge until it lands.

The combination most people respond to within a month: small doses + warm + with food + sugar/salt + aged cheese as the calcium backstop on hard days.

## Mexican origin and raw-milk legality

Peat lived in Mexico for stretches across the 1960s and again later, and his milk preferences tracked the supply he could actually buy there. From the diet-general email: "I use pasteurized milk because the dairies in this region with raw milk happen to use feed that give the milk a bad taste." When the local raw supply was clean, he preferred it; when it wasn't, he switched. Describing his own intake: "In Mexico when I get it from the farmer, I don't know how much fat it has, but on average it's probably similar." Farmer-direct milk in Mexico functioned as his de facto whole-milk source.

The broader Mexican observation was a population one. In [[milk-in-context-allergies-ecology-and-some-myths|"Milk in context"]]: "When I lived in Mexico in the 1960s, good milk was hard to find in the cities and towns, and most women had fat hips and short legs. Twenty years later, when good milk was available in all the cites, there were many more slender women, and the young people on average had much longer legs." Milk availability tracked the same anti-obesity and growth signals he saw between Slavic Europe and Finland. The Mexican preference was less about Mexican milk being uniquely good than about farmer-direct grass-fed supply staying available in a country where that channel never collapsed the way it did in the US.

For US readers the practical question is raw-milk legality. Federal rule prohibits interstate shipment of raw milk for human consumption (21 CFR 1240.61); state law fills the rest and varies widely:

- **Retail sale legal**: California, Connecticut, Maine, New Hampshire, New Mexico, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Washington, and a handful of others.
- **On-farm sale only**: Texas, Kansas, Tennessee, North Carolina, Utah, and others.
- **Herdshare or cow-share legal**: Colorado, Michigan, Ohio, Virginia, Oregon — fractional ownership in a cow or herd, the most common workaround.
- **Effectively banned**: Hawaii, Iowa, Louisiana, Nevada, Rhode Island, and a few others.

The herdshare model is the most reliable route for someone in a restricted state who wants Peat-grade grass-fed milk. Weston A. Price Foundation chapter leaders, the Real Milk directory (realmilk.com), and local farm cooperatives are the standard discovery channels. The infection risk Peat acknowledged is concentrated in unknown-source raw milk; a known clean herd is a different risk profile.

Operational ranking for daily use: a known clean local raw dairy you trust > grass-fed whole pasteurized > conventional 1% pasteurized > skim or lactose-free. The expensive part of the decision is usually not the milk itself — it's whether the upstream supply is reachable at all.
