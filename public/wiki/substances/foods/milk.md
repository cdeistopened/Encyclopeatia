# Milk

Milk is the cornerstone food in Ray Peat's dietary framework—not because of any mystical quality but because its nutrient profile addresses the specific metabolic needs he identified as most important: high [[calcium]], favorable calcium-to-phosphate ratio, easily digestible protein, and natural hormones in their safest forms. "Milk drinkers tend to be the least obese population," Peat noted, citing the Masai and other traditionally dairy-dependent cultures. In a world of metabolic dysfunction driven by [[PUFA]], [[estrogen]], and inadequate [[thyroid]] function, milk provides a concentrated corrective.

## The Calcium Imperative

Calcium is the anti-[[stress]] mineral in Peat's framework, and milk is its optimal source. In a [[2016-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vitamin-d|2016 episode on vitamin D]], Peat described aiming for "about 2,500 mg of calcium per day"—a level most easily achieved through dairy consumption.

The calcium-to-phosphate ratio matters as much as the absolute amount. Phosphate—abundant in meat, grains, nuts, and seeds—is what Peat described as "an age-accelerating factor." As he explained in a [[2013-09-09-voice-of-america-voice-of-america-water|2013 interview]], "Milk has high calcium and low phosphate-to-calcium ratio. Meat and nuts have excess phosphate. Calcium stimulates metabolic rate and prevents obesity."

When calcium intake is low relative to phosphate, [[parathyroid hormone]] rises to pull calcium from bones. This parathyroid elevation is itself a stress signal that promotes [[inflammation]], [[calcification]] of soft tissues, and the very osteoporosis that people mistakenly try to prevent by avoiding dairy. Adequate calcium from milk keeps parathyroid hormone low, protecting both bones and soft tissues.

## Estrogen in Milk: The Detoxified Form

One of the more nuanced aspects of Peat's dairy advocacy is his distinction between the estrogens in milk and those in other foods. In a [[2020-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vibrations-frequencies-part-2|2020 episode]], he explained: "One of the reasons milk tends to have an overall protective effect against estrogen is that the estrogens in milk are in a detoxified form. Estrone sulfate, for example, is the least harmful form of estrogen. It is not at all comparable to the estrogens that are in the soy milk substitutes."

The cow's liver has already processed the estrogen into its sulfated, water-soluble, excretion-ready form. This means dairy estrogen passes through the human body with minimal biological activity—unlike the genistein in soy, which activates aromatase and increases active estrogen in tissues.

## Protein Quality

Milk protein—a combination of casein and whey—provides a complete amino acid profile with a favorable ratio of anti-inflammatory to pro-inflammatory amino acids. Casein digests slowly, providing sustained amino acid availability that supports stable blood sugar and reduces the need for [[cortisol]]-driven gluconeogenesis.

The protein content supports [[thyroid]] function directly: adequate protein provides the transport proteins (thyroid-binding globulin) that carry thyroid hormone through the blood. Protein-deficient diets often produce functional hypothyroidism even when the thyroid gland itself is working properly.

## Forms of Dairy

Peat discussed various forms with practical recommendations:

**Whole vs. skim milk.** Peat often recommended low-fat or skim milk to minimize PUFA intake, since even dairy fat contains about 2% polyunsaturated fats. The calcium, protein, and lactose are present in all forms; removing the fat simply reduces total PUFA while retaining the metabolically important components.

**Powdered milk** is acceptable when fresh milk is unavailable. As Peat noted in his emails, "Powdered milk is acceptable when fresh milk or cheese is unavailable." He recommended "100+ grams for calcium-to-phosphate ratio."

**Cheese** provides concentrated calcium and protein with very low lactose. Peat's primary caution was about industrial processing: "When cheese labels say 'enzymes,' they're likely using new industrial products causing serious intestinal reactions. Real animal rennet is safe."

**Calcium supplements** as backup. When dairy is unavailable or not tolerated, Peat recommended "powdered eggshells as the best calcium supplement, oyster shells next."

## Lactose Intolerance

Peat viewed most lactose intolerance as a reversible adaptation rather than a fixed genetic trait. In his discussions of dairy, he noted that "the intestine adjusts enzymes within weeks" when dairy is gradually reintroduced. The enzyme lactase, which digests milk sugar, upregulates with consistent dairy consumption and downregulates with abstinence.

Practical approach: start with small amounts of milk (or fermented dairy like yogurt and cheese, which have reduced lactose), and increase gradually. Most adults who believe they are permanently lactose intolerant simply have not consumed dairy consistently enough for the enzyme to upregulate.

## The Anti-Obesity Effect

The relationship between dairy consumption and leanness is consistent across populations and studies. As Peat noted, "Calcium stimulates metabolic rate and prevents obesity. Milk drinkers are a very slim population." The mechanisms are multiple: calcium suppresses parathyroid hormone (which promotes fat storage), the protein provides sustained satiety, and the lactose (like fructose) supports efficient glucose metabolism.

The contrast with plant-based milk alternatives is stark. Soy milk provides estrogenic isoflavones, almond milk provides negligible nutrition in large amounts of water, and oat milk provides starch without the calcium-to-phosphate ratio that makes dairy metabolically protective.
