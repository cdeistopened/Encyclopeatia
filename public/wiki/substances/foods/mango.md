# Mango

Mango occupies a conditional position in Ray Peat's fruit recommendations--genuinely valued when ripe and well-tolerated, but flagged with an allergy warning that most tropical fruits do not carry. Peat noted that "mangos, apples, and pears are allergenic to some people," a caution that distinguishes mango from the unconditionally endorsed fruits like [[orange-juice|orange juice]], [[watermelon]], and [[grapes]] that topped his hierarchy. For those who tolerate it, a ripe mango is an excellent source of the simple sugars and carotenoids Peat considered protective. For those who react to it, the allergy signals something worth understanding about the fruit's botany and the conditions under which it reaches the consumer.

## The Allergy Question

Peat's allergy warning for mango appeared in the same email correspondence where he identified [[banana|bananas]] and jackfruit as "strong allergens, possibly because of their cultivation methods." The mango warning was softer--"allergenic to some people" rather than a blanket caution--but its inclusion alongside apples and pears suggests Peat considered mango reactivity common enough to mention. He followed the observation with a directive that applies to all fruits in his framework: "Poorly ripened fruits of all sorts should be avoided."

The botanical explanation for mango allergy runs deeper than cultivation. Mango (*Mangifera indica*) belongs to the Anacardiaceae family--the same plant family as poison ivy, poison oak, and poison sumac. The mango's skin, sap, and the tissue immediately beneath the peel contain urushiol-like compounds (specifically mangiferin and related resorcinols) that trigger contact dermatitis in sensitized individuals. The reaction can manifest as lip and mouth inflammation from eating unpeeled fruit, or as a skin rash from handling the fruit during preparation.

This botanical kinship likely explains why mango allergy does not follow the usual pattern of food sensitivities in Peat's framework. Where [[banana]] allergenicity may relate to cultivation practices and serotonin content, mango allergy is fundamentally a chemical defense issue--the fruit carries irritant compounds in its outer layers that some immune systems recognize and react to vigorously.

## Ripeness and the Peel Problem

Peat's insistence that "poorly ripened fruits of all sorts should be avoided" applies with particular force to mango. An unripe mango contains higher concentrations of the irritant compounds in its skin and flesh, along with more [[starch]] and less sugar than a fully ripe specimen. The ripening process converts starch to glucose and fructose, softens the flesh, and reduces--though does not eliminate--the concentration of potentially allergenic compounds near the surface.

The practical implication is that peeling mango thoroughly before consumption removes the primary source of urushiol-like irritants. The inner flesh of a ripe mango, well separated from the skin, presents a dramatically different chemical profile than the peel. For individuals with mild mango sensitivity, careful peeling and full ripeness may make the difference between a problematic food and a tolerable one. For those with severe Anacardiaceae sensitivity, the trace compounds remaining in even the ripest flesh may still provoke a reaction.

## Nutritional Value When Tolerated

A ripe mango delivers its calories almost entirely as simple sugars--approximately 14g per 100g of flesh, split between glucose, fructose, and sucrose. This places it squarely in the category of fruits Peat valued as "good sources of sugar," providing the easily oxidized fuel that supports [[thyroid]] function and suppresses the [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]] release triggered by falling blood sugar.

The deep orange flesh signals exceptional beta-carotene content, which the body converts to [[vitamin A]]. Peat viewed vitamin A as essential for thyroid hormone conversion, [[progesterone]] synthesis, and protection against [[estrogen]] dominance--functions that made carotenoid-rich fruits a natural fit within his dietary framework. Mango also provides meaningful [[vitamin C]] and [[potassium]], contributing to the mineral and antioxidant profile Peat associated with protective fruits.

As Peat explained in a [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|2013 discussion on aging and energy]], fruits containing aromatase-inhibiting [[bioflavonoids]] support the hormonal balance central to his anti-aging framework. While mango is not as rich in flavonoids as citrus or dark [[grapes]], its combination of sugar, carotenoids, and vitamin C makes it nutritionally valuable among tropical fruits.

## Where Mango Sits in the Fruit Hierarchy

Mango ranks below the top-tier fruits in Peat's system--[[orange-juice|oranges]], [[watermelon]], and [[grapes]]--primarily because of the allergy caveat. It ranks above [[apple|apples]] and [[banana|bananas]], which carry more serious structural concerns (pectin and resistant starch, respectively). For someone who tolerates mango well, it functions as a good tropical fruit option: rich in sugar, rich in vitamin A precursors, low in fiber when ripe, and free of the [[serotonin]] content that makes bananas problematic.

The key practical guidelines are straightforward: select only fully ripe specimens (soft, fragrant, yielding to gentle pressure), peel thoroughly to avoid urushiol-like compounds, and observe any personal sensitivity before making mango a regular dietary component. When these conditions are met, mango earns its place among the tropical fruits Peat endorsed as protective.
