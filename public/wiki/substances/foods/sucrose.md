# Sucrose

Sucrose is the substance Ray Peat chose to defend more vigorously than any other food in his framework, because it was the substance mainstream nutrition attacked most aggressively and with the least scientific grounding. Ordinary table sugar, the same white crystalline powder sold in five-pound bags for baking, sits at the intersection of nearly every major mechanism Peat identified as health-promoting: it suppresses [[cortisol]], supports the [[thyroid]], bypasses the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] block that defines diabetes, opposes the effects of [[estrogen]], and stimulates the regeneration of pancreatic beta cells that polyunsaturated fats destroy. Sucrose is a disaccharide, one unit of [[glucose]] bound to one unit of [[fructose]]--and for Peat, that 50/50 ratio was not an accident but nearly the ideal composition for a therapeutic carbohydrate. "Sucrose, glucose, fructose, and lactose are very good, well-metabolized sugars," he told listeners in a [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 episode on sugar]]. The [[fructose]] component does the catalytic work; this article focuses on what sucrose as a whole molecule accomplishes that its individual components cannot, and why Peat spent the last forty years of his life recommending the specific product that dietitians warned against.

## The Yudkin Origin Story

Peat's interest in sugar began, paradoxically, with a book that tried to condemn it. "I did that dissertation in 1972," he recalled in a [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|2011 discussion of sugar myths]]. "That was the year that John Yudkin published his book, *Pure, White and Deadly*. I read that and was so impressed by his arguments that sugar increases blood lipids, saturated fats, and [[cholesterol]]--that that was what started me on the idea of recommending increased sugar for people who are under stress. When I would see someone deficient in [[progesterone]], having too much estrogen and [[age-pigment|age pigment]], they would often recover if they could increase their cholesterol production. The simplest way to do that was to have them eat some extra sugar."

Peat read the most famous anti-sugar book of the twentieth century and came away convinced sugar was therapeutic, specifically because its effect on cholesterol made it medically useful. What Yudkin interpreted as pathology--rising cholesterol on a high-sugar diet--Peat interpreted as evidence that sugar was driving the production of protective steroid hormones. [[Cholesterol]] is the precursor of [[pregnenolone]], [[progesterone]], and every other hormone in the anti-stress cascade. A substance that raises cholesterol in a person deficient in those hormones is not poisoning them; it is feeding a starved endocrine system its missing substrate. This reframing is the foundation of everything Peat later said about sugar. Sucrose is therapeutic in exact proportion to how stressed the organism is.

## What the Molecule Does

Sucrose is split by the enzyme sucrase in the small intestine into its two component sugars within minutes of consumption. The glucose is absorbed quickly; the fructose lags slightly behind. "The glucose from the sugar gets absorbed, and then the smaller amount of fructose coming in accelerates the absorption of the glucose by the various tissues--not just the liver, but brain, kidney, heart, everything," Peat explained in a [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|2011 episode on sugar myths]]. "It is fulfilling some of insulin's responsibility without having to call on the [[insulin]]. Under some circumstances, it can actually inhibit the release of insulin."

This is the central metabolic argument for sucrose over pure glucose or starch. Starch converts rapidly and entirely to glucose, stimulating a sharp insulin surge that drives fat deposition and often overshoots into hypoglycemia. Pure glucose does the same. Sucrose, because half of it is [[fructose]], produces a gentler insulin curve while simultaneously accelerating glucose oxidation at the cellular level. The fructose component--the details of which are covered in the [[fructose]] article--bypasses the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] block at [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]], activates the thyroid system at multiple intracellular targets, and catalyzes the disposal of the glucose it arrived with. The two sugars, bound together and released together, function as a self-regulating package.

Peat described fructose's catalytic effect in striking terms. "Burr didn't understand that it was his rats' high sugar diet, freed of the anti-oxidative unsaturated fatty acids, that caused their extremely high metabolic rate," he noted, referring to essential fatty acid researcher George Burr. "His diet was mostly plain sucrose, and his animals respired 50% faster than animals on a normal diet." A 30% to 50% increase in whole-body oxidative metabolism from adding sucrose to the diet is not a trivial effect. It is the kind of change that, in any other context, would be hailed as a major therapeutic discovery.

## The Glycemic Index Myth

One of Peat's most effective rhetorical moves was to turn the glycemic index--the tool dietitians use to warn patients away from sugar--back on starch. In his newsletter [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context"]], Peat published the comparison directly:

| Food | White Bread Index | Glucose Index |
|---|---|---|
| Fructose | 32 | 22 |
| Lactose | 65 | 46 |
| Honey | 83 | 58 |
| Sucrose | 92 | 64 |
| Glucose | 137 | 96 |
| White bread | 101 | 71 |
| French baguette | 136 | 95 |

Sucrose's glycemic index is lower than whole wheat bread and dramatically lower than a French baguette, which dietitians recommend without hesitation to the same diabetic patients they counsel to avoid table sugar. Even by its own metric, the food the glycemic index is deployed against is safer than the foods it recommends. Peat took the critique further. The real problem with starch is not its glycemic peak alone but what happens after the peak. "Rather than the sustained hyperglycemia which is measured for determining the glycemic index, I think the 'diabetogenic' or 'carcinogenic' action of starch has to do with the stress reaction that follows the intense stimulation of insulin release." Starch drives blood sugar up, insulin crashes it down, and the body responds to the crash with a surge of [[adrenaline]] and cortisol. Sucrose, because fructose moderates the insulin response, avoids this entire cycle.

## Cortisol Suppression and the Stress System

Peat identified sugar's effect on [[cortisol]] as perhaps the single most important reason to eat it. The mechanism is simple: sugar reaching the bloodstream tells the stress system to stand down. "Sugar happens to be the best thing for lowering cortisol to normal," he told Josh Rubin in a [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|2011 interview on glycemia]]. "Since high cortisol gives the impression of diabetes by causing high blood sugar, you get the unexpected effect that eating sugar lowers the cortisol, and some of these people had a very quick recovery from their so-called diabetes."

The observational evidence runs through Peat's work. "In monkeys living in the wild, when their diet is mainly fruit, their cortisol is low, and it rises when they eat a diet with less sugar," he wrote in [[sugar-issues|"Sugar Issues"]]. "Sucrose consumption lowers ACTH, the main pituitary stress hormone. If animals' adrenal glands are removed, so that they lack the adrenal steroids, they choose to consume more sucrose. Stress seems to be perceived as a need for sugar." The craving for sweets that mainstream nutrition pathologizes, Peat interpreted as a physiological intelligence: the organism knows that sugar suppresses cortisol and seeks it out accordingly.

Every major trigger of the stress response involves some interference with glucose metabolism. "Hypoglycemia itself is a powerful activator of the stress system," Peat explained to Jodelle Fitzwater in a [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|2019 interview on cortisol and testosterone]]. "Excess of [[free-fatty-acids]], which block the ability to use glucose, will activate the stress hormones. Lactic acid results when you're not using your glucose properly; it's a sign of a deficiency of glucose that activates cortisol production." In the same interview he gave a specific dosage: "Usually, something more than 100 grams per day of carbohydrate--preferably in the form of sucrose--is needed. If you're very active physically, 200 or 300 grams a day of carbohydrate, especially sugar, can be restorative to the brain." The recommendation is specific to sucrose rather than starch because sucrose supplies the fructose catalyst that keeps the whole oxidative chain running under stress.

## Sugar as Medicine: The 1857 Diabetes Cure

The most startling clinical evidence Peat cited for sucrose comes not from modern research but from two Victorian physicians who treated diabetes with it. In [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|"Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes"]], Peat reconstructed the story. "In 1857, M. Piorry in Paris and William Budd in Bristol, England, reasoned that if a patient was losing a pound of sugar every day in 10 liters of urine, and was losing weight very rapidly, and had an intense craving for sugar, it would be reasonable to replace some of the lost sugar. Keeping patients from eating what they craved seemed both cruel and futile."

Budd's results were remarkable. He prescribed "8 ounces of sugar every day, along with a normal diet including beef and beef broth" to a terminally ill diabetic woman, and she recovered progressively over several weeks. A subsequent patient, a young man too weak to work, received "8 ounces of white sugar and 4 ounces of honey every day, and again, instead of increasing the amount of glucose in the urine, the amount decreased quickly as the patient began eating almost as much sugar as was being lost initially, and then as the loss of sugar in the urine decreased, the patient gained weight and recovered his strength." A London physician named Thomas Williams publicly ridiculed Budd's approach as "metaphysical" and "homeopathic," and the medical establishment sided with Williams. The practice was abandoned. Patients continued dying from sugar restriction for another seventy years, until insulin was discovered in 1922.

Peat told the same story to Patrick Timpone in a [[2019-10-15-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-a-plethora-of-wide-randing-questions|2019 One Radio Network interview]]: "150 years ago in France and England, doctors cured terminal diabetic patients--people wasting away--by giving them as much sugar as they wanted. That was about 11 or 12 ounces of white sugar added to their regular meals." The mechanism, as Peat reconstructed it, involves the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] and the beta cell. "It works because it suppresses the breakdown of fat in your body. The free fatty acids are constantly killing the pancreatic beta cells that produce insulin when you are under stress. You have a stressful event, produce free fatty acids, they kill off your insulin-producing cells, and so you're unable to oxidize the fat, which keeps you under stress. You can break the cycle, as those doctors showed, by letting them have as much sugar as they crave."

Modern evidence supports the Victorian clinicians. As Peat noted in [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|Sugar Myths II]], "sugar itself, sucrose, is known to stimulate the regeneration of beta cells. New in vitro studies show sugar stimulates regeneration and [[polyunsaturated-fats|polyunsaturated fats]] kill the beta cells. Sugar often gets the blame for things the polyunsaturated fats do, such as the glycation of hemoglobin (HbA1c). Most of the so-called glycation is really the oxidative breakdown fragments of the polyunsaturated fats." The inversion is complete: the substance blamed for diabetes heals pancreatic beta cells; the substances consumed as "heart-healthy" alternatives destroy them.

## The Randle Cycle and the PUFA Inversion

The deeper claim Peat made about diabetes is that [[polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]] causes it and sugar reverses it. The key piece of machinery is the Randle cycle, the competition between [[free-fatty-acids]] and glucose for oxidative processing that Philip Randle described in 1963. When polyunsaturated fats flood the bloodstream, they block pyruvate dehydrogenase--the gatekeeper enzyme that allows glucose to enter the mitochondria--and glucose backs up in the blood. "This was demonstrated in hospitals when giving nutritional support to patients in the form of a soy oil emulsion," Peat recalled in a [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 sugar episode]]. "About 15 minutes after injecting this emulsified soy oil, people would get hyperglycemic." Fifteen minutes. The diabetogenic effect of polyunsaturated fat is measurable in a quarter of an hour, yet the same polyunsaturated fats remain the backbone of "heart-healthy" dietary advice for diabetics.

The damage from PUFA is not only instantaneous. "When the fatty acids involved in blocking the sugar are polyunsaturated, they produce long-range damage. A group led by Fu demonstrated that the glycated proteins seen in diabetic people--which are usually blamed on glucose--are actually caused more powerfully by polyunsaturated fatty acids." The advanced glycation end products diabetologists attribute to high blood sugar are, in the Peat reading, lipid peroxidation products masquerading as sugar damage. Strip the PUFA from the experiment, and the damage disappears. Add the sugar back, and the beta cells regenerate.

## White Sugar vs. Brown: Purity as a Feature

Peat's preference for plain white sugar over brown sugar, turbinado, or molasses runs counter to every assumption of the natural foods movement, but his reasoning is mechanistically specific. "White sugar is very highly purified--more pure than many reagent chemicals in research labs," he told Timpone in the [[2019-10-15-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-a-plethora-of-wide-randing-questions|2019 interview]]. "It is washed to a crystalline purity, so it is almost perfectly free of allergenic material. Brown sugar can cause intense inflammatory reactions for sensitive people, and molasses is even worse."

The problem with molasses and brown sugar is that they concentrate whatever was in the cane that did not crystallize. "If you taste blackstrap molasses, you can sometimes taste smoke," Peat told Andrew Murray in a [[2013-08-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-environmental-enrichment-bad-science|2013 episode]]. "They used to burn cane fields to harvest them. Molasses collects minerals but also the junk and smoky material. When sugar is browned by cooking, it breaks down and produces irritating, possibly toxic materials." In [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|Sugar Myths II]] he acknowledged the trade-off: crude brown foods matter in situations of poverty, but "when people start eating more fat and protein, they can stand to lose the nutrients in those to gain freedom from toxins and inflammation."

The ideal form of sucrose, in Peat's ranking, was fruit--"which is really ideal because you get nutrients with the sugar." Fresh fruit delivers sucrose in its original biological package: bound up with potassium, magnesium, [[bioflavonoids]], and the water that makes the sugar absorb gently. When fruit was unavailable or allergenic, white sugar was the next best thing, precisely because it carried no hidden cargo. "Refined granulated sugar is extremely pure, but it lacks all of the essential nutrients, so it should be considered as a temporary therapeutic material, or as an occasional substitute when good fruit isn't available, or when available honey is allergenic." The recommendation was calibrated: fruit first, [[honey]] second, white sugar third--and brown sugar, molasses, and "natural" cane products never.

## Sucrose for Stress Recovery: Ice Cream as Medicine

Peat consistently described sucrose as a therapeutic intervention for acute stress, illness, and sleep disturbance. The more severe the metabolic insult, the higher the dose he recommended. In a [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 episode on food]], Sarah Murray described a patient who had undergone extensive surgery: "We had a client who had extensive surgery and needed up to a pound of sugar a day in various forms--marshmallows, sugar in milk--to lower the stress so he could sleep. He went from not sleeping at all to sleeping all through the night just from increasing his sugar." Peat confirmed the pattern: "I've seen similar things in people with cancer or extreme old age insomnia. When they drank a syrup-like concentrate of sugar in milk or ate a bowl of ice cream during the night, they could get back to sleep."

The image of an elderly insomnia patient reaching for ice cream at three in the morning is precisely the behavior mainstream nutrition would pathologize as sugar addiction or emotional eating. Peat saw it as the organism correctly identifying its missing input. Ice cream--sugar, saturated fat from cream, protein and calcium from milk--is in fact a near-optimal delivery vehicle for sucrose in a stressed state, because the fat and protein slow absorption while the calcium supports cellular metabolism and the sucrose itself suppresses cortisol. The recommendation scales with stress, not with body weight. A stressed body needs more sugar than a relaxed one, because the stress itself is an expression of the energy system's failure to oxidize glucose efficiently.

## The Non-Reducing Sugar Argument

A subtler reason Peat preferred sucrose to glucose emerged in a [[38-in-defense-of-mexican-coke-the-authoritarian-metabolism-symbols-of-an-alien-sky-astrology|Generative Energy podcast]] with Danny Roddy and Georgi Dinkov. Sucrose and trehalose are non-reducing sugars; glucose and fructose in isolation are reducing sugars. "Since sucrose and trehalose are non-reducing sugars, wouldn't that be reason enough to use them as fuel?" Dinkov asked. Peat's answer: "That's my reason for preferring fructose and sucrose. One of the reasons for preferring fructose or sucrose over starch and pure glucose is that ketone function has that biasing effect, putting the cell into a more oxidizing state. Fructose, at the right level, favors the oxidizing condition and acts as an antidote to the fatty liver producing effect of ethanol." Non-reducing sugars tilt the cell toward the oxidized state Peat associated with health and differentiation. Reducing metabolic conditions--produced by starch, ketosis, and alcohol--tilt it toward the reduced state he associated with cancer, degeneration, and stress.

## Sucrose, Bones, and Vitamin D

One of the stranger experimental findings Peat cited involved vitamin D deficiency in rats fed different carbohydrates. In the same animals, on the same deficient diet, sucrose and starch produced opposite bone outcomes. "When animals were fed an otherwise balanced diet lacking vitamin D, with the addition of either 68% sucrose or 68% starch, the bones of those on the starch diet failed to develop normally, as would be expected with a vitamin D deficiency, and their serum calcium was low. However, the bones of those on the diet with sucrose developed properly, and didn't show evidence of being calcium deficient." Peat drew the implication directly: "This study suggests that the famous dietetic emphasis on the 'complex carbohydrates,' i.e., starches, has made an important contribution to the prevalence of osteoporosis."

The mechanism Peat proposed runs through [[carbon-dioxide]]. Sugar oxidation produces more CO2 than fat oxidation, and CO2 is essential for the proper deposition of calcium in bone. Lactic acid, which dominates in the reducing metabolism that starch promotes, dissolves bone rather than building it. "The first bone material laid down is calcium carbonate," Peat explained in [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|Sugar Myths II]]. "You can have an acidic condition as long as it's based on carbon dioxide and have strong bones. The same amount of lactic acid pH will cause dissolution of the bones. Starch tends to produce a shift towards lactic acid rather than carbon dioxide." Sucrose shifts the body toward carbon dioxide production and supports bone calcification even in the absence of adequate vitamin D.

## Sucrose Is Less Fattening Than Starch

The most consistent finding in the animal literature Peat reviewed was that sucrose is less fattening than starch. "Many studies have found that sucrose is less fattening than starch or glucose," he wrote in [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]]. "Rats were fed either sucrose or Coca-Cola and Purina chow, and were allowed to eat as much as they wanted. They consumed 50% more calories without gaining extra weight, relative to the standard diet." The rats on the sugar-heavy regimen ate fifty percent more food and failed to gain fifty percent more weight because their metabolic rate rose to burn off the excess.

The broader conclusion Peat drew was that starch, not sugar, is the fattening carbohydrate. "Eating 'complex carbohydrates,' rather than sugars, is a reasonable way to promote obesity. Eating starch, by increasing insulin and lowering the blood sugar, stimulates the appetite, causing a person to eat more." Starch provokes insulin, insulin drives fat storage, the subsequent blood sugar drop triggers appetite, and the cycle repeats. Sucrose, with its built-in fructose moderator, sidesteps the cycle at its first step.

## Practical Guidance

Peat's baseline was roughly "170 to 250 grams of sugar per day" for a person of normal activity consuming mixed foods, with the upper range appropriate for stress recovery, illness, or high physical demand. The sugars should come primarily from fruit and fruit juice, with white sugar, [[honey]], and [[milk]] (for its lactose) filling in the rest. His baseline dietary formula was consistent across decades: "A daily diet that includes two quarts of milk and a quart of [[orange-juice|orange juice]] provides enough fructose and other sugars for general resistance to stress, but larger amounts of fruit juice, honey, or other sugars can protect against increased stress, and can reverse some of the established degenerative conditions."

Sucrose combined with saturated fat works better than either alone. "Saturated fat works with fructose and insulin to handle glucose oxidation, and fat slows absorption so you don't get a surge of glucose," Peat explained in [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|Sugar Myths II]]. This is why ice cream, not gummy candy, was the format Peat most often recommended for therapeutic sugar delivery. Sucrose combined with [[polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]], on the other hand, is a disaster. Sarah Murray captured the distinction in a memorable comparison: "A really bad example of a way to eat sugar is a donut: white flour (glucose) fried in canola, sunflower, or corn oil (PUFA). You have pure glucose and pure polyunsaturated fatty acids blocking your cells from using that glucose. A good example is ice cream with fresh fruit." The same sugar, delivered in two different fatty contexts, produces opposite metabolic effects.

Peat's summary, offered repeatedly in different interviews, was that the sweet tooth is a physiological intelligence worth trusting. The craving intensifies under stress because stress burns through glucose reserves and threatens to force the body into a cortisol-dependent catabolic state. The medical establishment has spent a century teaching patients to override this signal, with results that Peat watched accumulate as rising rates of diabetes, obesity, osteoporosis, and metabolic dysfunction. His counsel was simpler: eat the sugar. Prefer fruit. When fruit fails, reach for the white bag.
