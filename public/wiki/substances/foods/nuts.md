# Nuts

Few foods enjoy a more unshakable reputation in mainstream nutrition than nuts. They are the darling of dietitians, the anchor of "heart-healthy" snack recommendations, and the centerpiece of Mediterranean diet pyramids. In Ray Peat's framework, however, nuts occupy a starkly different position: they are concentrated sources of [[polyunsaturated-fats]], [[phytic acid]], and enzyme inhibitors--a combination of anti-nutritional factors that makes them, with one narrow exception, incompatible with the pro-metabolic diet. When Peat listed his safe foods in a [[2022-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2022-kmud|2022 KMUD interview]]--"milk, cheese without additives, eggs, low-starch fruit, and generally meat and fish"--nuts were conspicuously absent. That omission was not an oversight. It was a conclusion drawn from decades of studying what polyunsaturated fatty acids do to [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]], [[estrogen]] metabolism, and cellular respiration.

## The Seed's Chemical Arsenal

Understanding why Peat rejected nuts requires understanding what a nut actually is: a seed. Every nut is a plant embryo packaged with enough stored energy to sustain germination, and that package comes with an elaborate chemical defense system. As Peat explained in a [[2022-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2022-kmud|November 2022 KMUD episode]], "plants create a storage form of nutrients to support the embryo's development. Those nutrients have to be in an insoluble form so they don't wash away while the seed is waiting to germinate. They have to be mobilized in an organized way. Those preparations for the seed embryo go with specific defenses against those same enzymes."

The logic is elegant and ruthless. A nut must store minerals, fats, and proteins in forms that remain stable through months or years of dormancy in soil, rain, and temperature fluctuation. To accomplish this, it binds its minerals with [[phytic acid]]--an anti-nutrient that forms insoluble complexes with [[calcium]], [[magnesium]], [[iron]], and [[zinc]], rendering them unabsorbable by any digestive system that encounters the seed before germination signals have been received. The nut simultaneously deploys enzyme inhibitors--compounds that specifically block the [[protein]]-digesting enzymes of any animal that eats it, impairing digestion and reducing the nutritional value of whatever meal the nut accompanies.

These are not incidental features. They are the nut's primary survival strategy: make yourself nutritionally unavailable to anything that tries to eat you, so that you pass through the gut intact and arrive in the soil ready to germinate. The health food industry markets these same defense chemicals under friendlier names--"fiber," "phytonutrients," "plant compounds"--but their biological function is anti-nutritional.

## The PUFA Problem: Why Fat Content Matters Most

The most consequential issue with nuts, from Peat's perspective, is not the phytic acid or the enzyme inhibitors but the fatty acid composition of their stored fats. As Sarah Murray summarized during a [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 KMUD episode on food]], "nuts have a lot of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) that are very estrogenic." Murray further clarified in a [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode on dementia]] that "PUFAs, also known as Omega-3 and Omega-6 oils, are found in all nuts and seeds."

The statement "all nuts and seeds" is worth pausing on, because it forecloses the common workaround of simply choosing a "better" nut. Walnuts are approximately 47% [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] by weight of their fat content. Pecans are roughly 30% polyunsaturated. Almonds, often positioned as a moderate choice, still derive about 25% of their fat from PUFA. Cashews, pine nuts, pistachios--all deliver substantial polyunsaturated loads. And these fats, once consumed, do not simply pass through. They incorporate into cell membranes, accumulate in tissues, and persist for months to years, progressively suppressing [[metabolic-rate|metabolic rate]], promoting [[estrogen]] dominance, and interfering with [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]] at the mitochondrial level.

The estrogenic effect Murray identified is a direct consequence of PUFA accumulation. Polyunsaturated fatty acids increase the activity of [[aromatase]]--the enzyme that converts androgens to estrogen--while simultaneously making cells more sensitive to estrogen's effects by altering membrane fluidity. A handful of walnuts daily, over months, creates a hormonal environment tilted toward estrogen dominance: lower [[thyroid]] output, higher [[cortisol]], increased [[inflammation]], and the cascade of degenerative processes Peat spent his career describing.

## Nut by Nut: A Field Guide to the Problem

**Walnuts** represent perhaps the worst case among common nuts. Their fat is nearly half linoleic acid (omega-6), with additional alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3)--both polyunsaturated, both problematic. The health marketing around walnuts--brain-shaped, therefore good for the brain--is a folk association that Peat's framework inverts entirely. The PUFA in walnuts suppresses the [[respiration|cellular respiration]] that the brain depends on more than any other organ.

**Almonds** are marketed as a moderate, protein-rich option, but they combine significant PUFA content with one of the highest phytic acid concentrations of any food. The "soaking and sprouting" practices popular in health food communities reduce but do not eliminate these anti-nutrients, and they do nothing about the polyunsaturated fat.

**Brazil nuts** present a particular dilemma. They are the richest dietary source of [[selenium]]--a mineral Peat valued for its role in thyroid hormone conversion and antioxidant defense. But a single Brazil nut also delivers a substantial dose of polyunsaturated fat. The trade-off is unfavorable: selenium can be obtained from [[shellfish]], [[eggs]], and other foods that do not come packaged with PUFA. Using Brazil nuts as a selenium supplement means accepting the metabolic damage of their fat content as collateral.

**Cashews and pistachios**, while lower in PUFA than walnuts, still carry meaningful polyunsaturated loads alongside other concerns--cashews are high in [[starch]], and pistachios are prone to aflatoxin contamination.

## The Macadamia Exception

One nut stands apart from this analysis: the macadamia. Approximately 80% of macadamia fat is monounsaturated--primarily oleic acid, the same fatty acid that predominates in olive oil and in the fat of healthy human tissues. Its polyunsaturated content is among the lowest of any nut, typically under 3%. Macadamias also have relatively low phytic acid compared to other tree nuts.

Within Peat's framework, this makes the macadamia nut a genuinely different food from its botanical relatives. Oleic acid does not suppress thyroid function, does not promote estrogen conversion, and does not accumulate in cell membranes the way linoleic and linolenic acids do. A person who craves the taste and texture of nuts can consume macadamias without the metabolic consequences that attend every other nut. The limitation is practical: macadamias are expensive and calorically dense, and they provide neither the mineral benefits nor the [[protein]] content that would make them a dietary staple. They are permissible rather than recommended--a snack, not a food group.

## Nut Butters and Trail Mix: Concentrated Harm

The modern health food industry has found ingenious ways to increase nut consumption beyond what anyone would achieve by cracking shells. Almond butter, cashew butter, and peanut butter (peanuts being legumes rather than true nuts, but sharing the same PUFA problem) deliver concentrated doses of polyunsaturated fat in a form that is easy to overconsume. A tablespoon of almond butter contains more PUFA than most people would encounter eating whole almonds in a sitting, and the smooth, palatable texture encourages larger servings.

Trail mix compounds the issue further by combining nuts with dried fruit--the drying process concentrates not only sugars but also any pesticide residues, while the nuts contribute their full anti-nutrient profile. The product is marketed as an energy food for hikers and athletes, but in Peat's framework it represents a concentrated source of the fats that suppress the very [[energy-metabolism|energy metabolism]] the hiker needs.

> Plants create a storage form of nutrients to support the embryo's development. Those nutrients have to be in an insoluble form so they don't wash away while the seed is waiting to germinate. They have to be mobilized in an organized way. Those preparations for the seed embryo go with specific defenses against those same enzymes.

This principle--that the plant's survival strategy and the animal's nutritional needs are fundamentally opposed--is the core of Peat's case against nuts. The marketing of nuts as health food requires ignoring what they are: seeds armored against digestion, loaded with fats that suppress metabolism, and defended by chemicals that block mineral absorption. The exception proves the rule: the one nut that lacks these features, the macadamia, is the one nut Peat's framework permits.
