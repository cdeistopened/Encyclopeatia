# Cheese

Cheese serves a specific function in Ray Peat's dietary framework: it concentrates [[calcium]] while eliminating most of the lactose and fluid that make [[milk]] problematic for some people. For those who need the calcium-to-[[phosphate]] ratio that dairy provides but cannot drink milk in the quantities Peat recommended (a quart or two daily), cheese offers an alternative pathway to the same metabolic goals.

As Peat wrote in email exchanges, "Cheese would be a good way to get enough calcium, if you don't use milk."

## The Calcium Imperative

Cheese's primary value lies in correcting the calcium-phosphate imbalance that characterizes modern diets. High [[meat]] consumption, in particular, creates a phosphate excess that activates [[parathyroid hormone]], mobilizing calcium from bones and depositing it in soft tissues.

Peat consistently emphasized the need for high calcium intake: "It's best to have more calcium than phosphate, and your diet is deficient in calcium, and heavy on phosphate, and that by itself can cause serious stress."

A few ounces of hard cheese can provide the calcium that would require several glasses of milk. For people who find drinking quarts of milk impractical or digestively challenging, cheese makes the calcium recommendation achievable.

## Salt and Variety

Peat mentioned using salty cheeses to satisfy sodium appetite—another underrated dietary need in his framework. In email exchanges, he listed his preferences: "There are some very salty cheeses that help to satisfy salt appetite, for example pecorino or feta; sometimes I add salt to cheeses such as gouda, emmental, mozzarella, or cheddar."

The combination of calcium, [[sodium]], and protein makes cheese a nutrient-dense food that addresses multiple common deficiencies simultaneously.

## Anti-Inflammatory Protein

Like milk and eggs, cheese provides protein in a context that supports [[metabolism]] rather than stressing it. The calcium-to-phosphate ratio in cheese is favorable—unlike [[meat]], which is phosphate-heavy. This means cheese protein can be metabolized without triggering the stress response that unbalanced phosphate intake causes.

Peat's dietary pattern often included cheese alongside other anti-inflammatory foods: "Cooked greens, cheese, milk, and eggs are anti-inflammatory foods."

## Practical Considerations

Not all cheeses are equal. Aged hard cheeses concentrate calcium more than soft cheeses. Fresh cheeses may contain more lactose, which could be problematic for lactose-intolerant individuals, though the fermentation process that creates most cheeses consumes lactose.

The fat content of cheese, predominantly [[saturated fat]], is not a concern in Peat's framework—saturated fat is protective, and the fear of dairy fat reflects the same misguided lipid hypothesis that demonized [[eggs]] and [[butter]].
