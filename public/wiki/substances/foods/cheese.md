# Cheese

Cheese is milk concentrated into the form that addresses the single most important mineral ratio in Ray Peat's dietary framework: the balance of [[calcium]] to phosphate. Where a glass of [[milk]] delivers calcium in a dilute matrix that some adults find difficult to consume at therapeutic volume, a few ounces of aged cheese deliver the same calcium in a dense, shelf-stable package that supplies the mineral load Peat considered foundational to [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]], anti-stress physiology, and protection against the [[estrogen]]-driven changes of aging. "Cheese would be a good way to get enough calcium, if you don't use milk," he wrote in one of his email exchanges, and that practical framing captures the food's essential role. Cheese is milk with the water removed and, in traditional varieties, most of the [[lactose]] and much of the whey removed as well, leaving behind a protein-calcium complex the adult body uses efficiently. For people who cannot tolerate lactose, and for people whose metabolic demands require calcium in concentrated form, cheese accomplishes nearly everything milk does while solving several of milk's practical problems.

## The Calcium-to-Phosphate Ratio

The case for cheese begins with a ratio, not an amount. In his email correspondence, Peat stated the principle directly: "The ratio of calcium to phosphate is more important than the absolute amount of calcium." This single sentence upends most of the mainstream conversation about dairy, which obsesses over total intake and ignores what accompanies the calcium. Calcium in the wrong proportion to phosphate fails to suppress [[PTH|parathyroid hormone]], and chronically elevated PTH drives calcification of soft tissues, loss of bone matrix, and the inflammatory signaling that culminates in fibrosis and cardiovascular degeneration.

Muscle meats deliver phosphate in large excess of calcium. Peat made the comparison in an email on low testosterone: "The problem with chicken is that the fat is highly unsaturated, and the meat provides very little calcium. Milk and cheese have a much better ratio of calcium to phosphate." A diet built around chicken breast, steak, or fish without compensating calcium pushes parathyroid hormone upward on every meal. Four ounces of parmesan contains more calcium than most adults would get from an entire day of vegetables and meat, delivered in a ratio that actively lowers PTH rather than raising it. On variety, Peat noted: "The type of cheese doesn't matter much as far as calcium goes." For calcium delivery itself, any real cheese works. For vitamin K content, lactose burden, additives, and mold cultures, the differences between varieties become important.

## Parmesan as the Ideal

One variety received Peat's recommendation by name. In his correspondence on calcium ratios, he specified the quantity: "The ratio of calcium to phosphate is more important than the absolute amount of calcium. 4 ounces [Parmigiano Reggiano] would usually be enough." In his description of his own daily diet, parmesan appeared alongside the other staples: "Eggs and orange juice, milk and oysters, and a raw carrot. For variety, smoked oysters, crab, cod fried in butter, ox-tail soup, parmigiano reggiano, sapotas, lychees, liver."

Parmigiano Reggiano exemplifies the ideal cheese for several overlapping reasons. It is aged for a minimum of twelve months, which means bacterial fermentation has consumed nearly all the lactose. It is made from milk collected from cows grazing on forage rather than grain-heavy confinement feed. It is produced with traditional rennet and native bacterial cultures rather than the industrial enzyme products Peat considered suspect. And the long aging concentrates the calcium so that a small serving provides what would otherwise require a quart of milk to match. Four ounces of parmesan per day, consumed alongside fruit and eggs or used as a seasoning, approaches the calcium intake Peat considered optimal for adults, and the cheese doubles as one of the most flavorful ingredients in traditional cooking.

## Vitamin K2 and the Hard Cheese Advantage

Beyond calcium, aged hard cheeses deliver something fluid milk cannot match in quantity: menaquinone, the bacterial form of [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] that directs calcium into bones and teeth and away from arterial walls. Cheese-fermenting bacteria produce menaquinone as a metabolic byproduct, concentrating it in the finished product. Hard cheeses from traditional European producers, particularly aged Gouda and the long-aged Italian varieties, contain the highest levels.

The significance relates to Peat's broader framework on calcium metabolism. Calcium without its cofactors migrates to the wrong places. The calcification of arteries and the soft tissue deposits that mainstream nutrition blames on "too much calcium" are in Peat's reading a problem of calcium without vitamin K2 to direct its deposition. Peat included vitamins D and K among the substances that "can sometimes produce quick antistress effects, alleviating insomnia, hypertension, edema, inflammations and allergies" and noted in the same passage that "the regular use of milk and cheese can prevent many chronic stress-related diseases." Aged hard cheese is the dietary vehicle that combines calcium with the vitamin K2 that makes the calcium safe and effective.

Fresh cheeses like mozzarella, ricotta, and cottage cheese contain minimal menaquinone because aging has not occurred. Processed products contain essentially none. For an adult seeking the full calcium-and-K2 synergy, aged hard cheeses are the only reliable source.

## What Cheese Removes from Milk

Cheesemaking retains some components of milk and removes others. Casein, the main milk protein that Peat noted has "direct antistress effects," stays in the curds along with most of the calcium. The whey drains away, taking with it much of the lactose and a disproportionate share of the tryptophan.

This matters in Peat's hierarchy of protein sources. He considered [[tryptophan]] a pro-inflammatory amino acid whose excess should be balanced by [[gelatin]]. Because whey contains more tryptophan relative to other amino acids than the curds do, cheese ends up with a cleaner amino acid profile than whole milk. Peat made this explicit: whey protein "has been promoted as a protein supplement, but it contains a slightly higher proportion of tryptophan than milk does. Cheese, by contrast, has the whey removed and therefore contains less tryptophan." A diet built on aged cheese and eggs with complementary gelatin delivers a better amino acid balance than the same protein obtained from chicken and whey isolate.

The lactose removal has practical consequences for the significant population of adults who cannot tolerate milk sugar. Aged hard cheese sidesteps the problem. A lactose-intolerant adult who cannot drink a glass of milk without digestive distress can often eat four ounces of parmesan with no symptoms at all. Peat noted in his emails that tryptophan content "varies a little with the method of making cheese, and the calcium content varies even more." Ricotta received a specific warning: "The calcium content [of ricotta cheese] can vary greatly, depending on whether the whey is separated by acid or by bacterial proteolysis." Aged hard cheeses are more reliable than fresh varieties whose mineral content depends on processing choices the label does not disclose.

## The Processing Problem

Peat reserved some of his sharpest warnings for industrial cheesemaking. His objection was not to cheese itself but to the substitution of modern processing for traditional methods. In an email on the subject, he wrote:

> When the label says "enzymes," it is likely that they are using one of the new products; lots of people are having serious intestinal reactions to commercial cheeses. Real animal rennet is still safe, as far as I know. The producers of the enzyme products claim they are highly purified, but some people react as though they still contain some antigens from the microorganisms.

Traditional cheese relied on native bacteria in raw milk and on animal rennet from the stomach lining of young ruminants. Industrial production replaces them with fermentation-derived enzymes cultured from genetically modified bacteria and fungi, sold as "microbial rennet" or listed simply as "enzymes." Peat extended the warning to aged varieties: "The same thing has been happening with aged cheeses, many places are no longer letting the native molds infect the cheese curds." The living cultures that give traditional blue cheese, Brie, and Camembert their flavor have been replaced in many industrial products by standardized powdered mold starters. The practical rule: read the label, prefer cheeses made with animal rennet, favor traditional European producers. Parmigiano Reggiano remains one of the few cheeses whose production method is legally protected against industrial shortcuts.

## Mold-Ripened Cheeses and the Histamine Question

The living molds in traditional blue cheese, Brie, and Camembert represent both the flavor and, for some people, the problem. These cheeses produce the widest range of secondary compounds during ripening, including biogenic amines like tyramine and histamine that can trigger symptoms in susceptible individuals. The mechanism involves decarboxylation of amino acids by the cheese cultures: tyrosine becomes tyramine, histidine becomes histamine, and both compounds act on the sympathetic nervous system and on mast cells.

For someone whose system is already primed toward excitation (high estrogen, elevated serotonin, compromised [[liver]] detoxification), the additional amine load from heavily ripened cheese can push them past their tolerance, producing headaches, flushing, or digestive upset that aged hard cheeses do not cause. Aged parmesan contains substantially less of these amines than soft-ripened varieties, though it is not entirely amine-free.

This qualifies rather than contradicts Peat's broader recommendation. For the average adult seeking calcium and anti-stress nutrition, cheese is a foundational food. For the person with mast cell activation or histamine intolerance, the lightest, least-fermented varieties may be better tolerated. Paradoxically, the cheeses that deliver the most vitamin K2 are often the ones with the most tyramine, creating a tradeoff the individual must navigate.

## Cheese in the Anti-Stress Diet

The broader function of cheese connects to Peat's model of chronic stress reversal. In his newsletter on milk in context, he wrote: "Combining milk and cheese with fruits adds to the antistress effect. The additional sugar and potassium and other minerals allow the milk protein to be used more efficiently, by moderating the secretion of cortisol, and helping to inhibit the secretion of PTH."

The formula is not arbitrary. Cheese delivers calcium and protein. Fruit delivers sugar, potassium, and [[bioflavonoids]]. Together they suppress both cortisol (which rises when blood sugar falls) and parathyroid hormone (which rises when serum calcium drops). A slice of parmesan with a piece of fruit addresses the two dominant stress hormones in a single small meal. His anti-aging diet, described in his article on diabetes and scleroderma, placed cheese in the same foundational list: "A major part of the diet should be milk, cheese, eggs, shellfish, fruits and coconut oil, with vitamin E and salt as the safest supplements." Cheese eaten alongside fruit, eggs, and [[coconut-oil]] becomes part of a coherent metabolic strategy.

His 1968 European travel observation captured the population-level pattern. When he crossed from Russia into Finland, he noticed "there were many stores selling a variety of cheeses, and the people were generally slender." The causal inference he drew was specific to calcium intake, and he connected it to similar differences observed in Mexico twenty years apart as good milk and cheese became widely available.

## Processed Cheese: The Exception to the Rule

Not everything sold as cheese qualifies in any meaningful sense. American processed cheese products, sold as individually wrapped slices or in aerosol cans, are made by emulsifying real cheese with milk powder, whey concentrate, stabilizers, colorings, and sometimes vegetable oils. The resulting product retains some calcium but loses most of the benefits of traditional cheese and introduces additives Peat would not have recommended. [[Powdered milk]] received his criticism because of the oxidation that occurs during industrial drying, producing damaged cholesterol and denatured proteins. Processed cheese products containing powdered milk carry the same concerns.

The rule is to eat cheese that a traditional European farmer would recognize as cheese. Hard aged varieties made with animal rennet, whole milk cheeses from grass-fed cows, and traditional blue and bloomy-rind cheeses from small producers all qualify. Processed American singles do not, regardless of what the calcium line on the nutrition label says.
