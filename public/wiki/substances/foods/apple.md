# Apple

Few foods illustrate Ray Peat's inversion of conventional nutrition better than the apple. "An apple a day keeps the doctor away" is perhaps the most familiar dietary proverb in the English language—yet in Peat's framework, a raw apple is closer to bacterial fertilizer than human food. The distinction between raw and cooked apple encapsulates one of Peat's core dietary principles: that the way a food is prepared matters as much as the food itself, and that modern assumptions about "fresh" and "natural" often work against human physiology.

## "Very Good Bacterial Food and Very Poor Human Food"

The problem with raw apples, in Peat's analysis, centers on [[pectin]]—a soluble fiber that gives apples their characteristic firm, crisp texture. As Peat explained in a [[2011-08-25-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-serotonin-and-endotoxin|2011 discussion on serotonin and endotoxin]], "even firm fruits with fibrous material, like crisp apples or pears, are very good bacterial food and very poor human food." The distinction is precise: pectin is indigestible by human enzymes but serves as an ideal substrate for intestinal bacteria.

This matters because bacterial fermentation in the gut produces [[endotoxin]]—lipopolysaccharide from gram-negative bacteria that Peat considers one of the central drivers of [[inflammation]], hormonal disruption, and degenerative disease. When undigested pectin reaches the lower intestine, it feeds exactly the bacterial populations that produce endotoxin. The bacteria thrive; the human host absorbs the toxic byproducts.

In a [[2011-07-12-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-questions-and-answers-i|Q&A session]] from the same period, Peat elaborated on the mechanism: "With fruit, a typical apple is likely unripened and contains pectin. Pectin isn't digestible by humans but feeds bacteria. Eating raw, unripe fruit feeds bacteria." The double problem is clear—not only does the pectin itself feed bacteria, but the commercial apple is typically harvested before full ripeness, compounding the issue with even higher pectin concentrations.

## The Ripeness Problem

Store-bought apples represent a particular failure of the modern food system from Peat's perspective. Most commercial apples are picked well before maturity, stored in controlled-atmosphere warehouses for months, and then exposed to ethylene gas to trigger cosmetic ripening. This process produces a fruit that looks ripe but retains the biochemical profile of an unripe one—high in pectin, defensive chemicals, and potential allergens.

Peat noted in email correspondence that "mangos, apples, and pears are allergenic to some people. Poorly ripened fruits of all sorts should be avoided." The allergenic potential is not incidental; it reflects the presence of defensive compounds that the plant produces precisely to discourage consumption before its seeds are mature. A truly tree-ripened apple, softened by natural enzymatic processes, contains far less pectin than the firm, glossy specimens stacked in supermarket bins. But such apples are nearly impossible to find commercially.

This stands in sharp contrast to tropical fruits like [[orange-juice|oranges]], papayas, and mangoes, which Peat generally recommended eating raw. Tropical fruits tend to be juicier, lower in structural fiber, higher in simple sugars, and more thoroughly ripened before consumption—all qualities that make them better human food and worse bacterial food.

## Cooking Transforms the Apple

The solution to the apple's pectin problem is heat. As Peat explained, "with fruit like an apple, it contains a huge amount of pectin which isn't digestible by humans but feeds bacteria. Cooking breaks this down." Heat denatures the pectin's gel-forming structure, breaking the long polysaccharide chains into shorter fragments that are less available to bacterial fermentation and pass through the intestine more rapidly.

This is why Peat listed "applesauce" among the acceptable forms of cooked and canned fruits. Baked apples, stewed apples, apple butter, and commercially canned apple products all benefit from the same thermal processing. The heat accomplishes what human digestive enzymes cannot—dismantling the pectin matrix and converting a bacterial feast into a reasonably safe source of fruit sugar and minerals.

> Peat cited a revealing experiment from the 1940s in which rats fed canned vegetables thrived while those on raw vegetables did not—a finding that runs directly counter to the modern raw-food ideology but aligns perfectly with his understanding of how cooking neutralizes antinutrients and indigestible fibers.

Apple juice occupies a similar safe category. The pressing and filtration process that produces clear apple juice removes most of the pectin (which is why unfiltered juice has a cloudy appearance and gels more readily). What remains is primarily fructose, glucose, and organic acids—simple compounds that are absorbed high in the digestive tract before reaching the bacterial populations of the lower intestine.

## The Broader Principle: Cooked Over Raw

The apple exemplifies a pattern that runs through Peat's dietary thinking. He consistently argued that cooking is not a nutritional compromise but a metabolic advantage—a form of external pre-digestion that makes nutrients more available to human physiology while neutralizing compounds that feed bacteria, irritate the intestinal lining, or trigger immune responses.

The conventional assumption that raw food retains more "nutrition" ignores the fact that much of what makes raw plant foods difficult is precisely the material that cooking eliminates. The [[fiber]] celebrated by mainstream nutrition—pectin, cellulose, resistant starch—is, in Peat's framework, largely food for [[bacteria]] rather than for the human consuming it. The [[serotonin]] produced by bacterial fermentation of these fibers in the lower gut contributes to a cascade of problems including [[inflammation]], intestinal irritation, [[osteoporosis]], and mood disturbance.

For someone following Peat's dietary principles, the practical hierarchy for apple consumption is clear: apple juice and well-cooked apple preparations are acceptable sources of fruit sugar; raw apples—especially the firm, crisp, commercially ripened varieties—are best avoided.
