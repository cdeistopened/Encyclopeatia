# Ice Cream

Few dietary recommendations capture the spirit of Ray Peat's contrarian framework as vividly as his advocacy for ice cream. Where mainstream nutrition treats it as an indulgence to be rationed, Peat treated it as a legitimate therapeutic food--one he reached for during migraines, recommended for blood sugar emergencies, and formulated from scratch in his own kitchen using [[coconut-oil]], [[eggs]], and [[sugar]]. Ice cream, in Peat's hands, is not a guilty pleasure but a delivery system for the nutrients his framework prizes most: rapidly absorbable sugar, [[calcium]]-rich [[milk]], [[saturated-fat]], and high-quality [[protein]].

## The Migraine Cure: A Quart of Ice Cream

The most striking example of Peat's ice cream philosophy is his use of it to abort migraines. After years of observing the connection between falling [[blood sugar]] and the onset of visual aura, Peat realized he could intervene directly with a massive dose of sugar, fat, and protein. As he described in a [[2013-02-20-politics-and-science-politics-science-questions-and-answers-i|2013 Q&A session]], "After thinking about that for years, I realized I could intervene. It happened that I had a couple of quarts of ice cream in the fridge. Realizing I hadn't been eating enough, I ate a quart of ice cream as fast as I could when the blind spots were already starting to flash. I found that they turned right off as I absorbed the ice cream."

The mechanism is straightforward within Peat's framework. Migraines involve a collapse of cellular energy production--blood vessels in the brain constrict and dilate erratically as cells lose their ability to maintain stable [[metabolism]]. When blood sugar drops, the body compensates with [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]], mobilizing [[free fatty acids]] from storage. If those stored fats contain [[PUFA]], they suppress [[thyroid]] function and block glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]], worsening the energy crisis. A quart of ice cream delivers sugar fast enough to halt this cascade before it becomes self-sustaining.

Peat elaborated on this approach in his email correspondence, noting that "lots of sugar, without coffee, would be quicker for restoring blood sugar. At least a quart of milkshake or ice cream can provide the needed sugar in a form that can be assimilated quickly." He added that "100 mg of [[progesterone]] in oil can usually stop it, by stopping the wastage of glucose"--but for most people, the ice cream is more immediately available than a hormone supplement.

## Why the Combination Matters

Ice cream works as a therapeutic food precisely because it combines multiple nutrients simultaneously. Pure sugar--a spoonful of table sugar, for instance--can restore blood sugar, but it arrives without the fat and protein needed to sustain the effect. The fat in ice cream slows gastric emptying, creating a sustained release of glucose rather than a spike-and-crash. The protein provides amino acids that support stable blood sugar through gluconeogenesis if needed. And the calcium from milk and cream suppresses [[parathyroid hormone]], which would otherwise amplify the stress response.

This is the same principle that makes [[milk]] and [[orange-juice]] central to Peat's dietary recommendations: the combination of macronutrients in a single food creates metabolic stability that isolated nutrients cannot. As Peat explained, "A very large amount of sugar will usually relieve a migraine; ice cream (about a quart) or milkshakes with some fat and protein make it easier to assimilate the sugar without stomach upset."

## Peat's Homemade Recipe

Peat did not rely on commercial ice cream. He developed his own formulation built around coconut oil--a fat he valued for its rapid oxidation, thyroid-supportive properties, and exemption from the [[Randle cycle]]. His recipe, shared in email correspondence, reveals his characteristic precision:

"I blend an egg (warmed to 40 degrees C) with a cup of sugar (also warmed) and a cup of coconut oil until it's smoothly emulsified, and maybe half a cup of powdered milk for extra texture, then add milk to fill the blender (total volume a little over a liter), with strong [[coffee]] or orange juice for flavor, or other fruit or vanilla, etc. The high oil content, and powdered milk, make it freeze without crystallizing, so the ice cream machine isn't necessary."

The recipe is nutritionally deliberate. Coconut oil provides medium-chain fatty acids that bypass the carnitine transport system and oxidize as readily as glucose. The egg contributes [[cholesterol]], fat-soluble vitamins, and lecithin that acts as a natural emulsifier. Powdered milk adds calcium and casein. The sugar provides the metabolic fuel. And the coffee--a frequent Peat recommendation--supports oxidative metabolism through its magnesium content and metabolic-rate-boosting effects.

As Peat noted in a [[2009-09-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-ten-most-toxic-things-in-our-food|2009 episode on toxic foods]], coconut oil lends itself naturally to this kind of preparation: "If you warm the oil and the milk, just a touch of egg yolk will allow it to emulsify. You can make ice cream or cheesecake--all kinds of buttery, creamy consistency foods--using the emulsified coconut oil instead of cream or cream cheese." Ice cream, in other words, was one of Peat's preferred vehicles for incorporating coconut oil into the diet in a palatable, sustained-release form.

## Choosing Commercial Ice Cream

Not all commercial ice cream qualifies as a health food, even within Peat's permissive framework. The critical distinction is between ice cream made from simple, recognizable ingredients and ice cream loaded with industrial additives.

The ideal ingredient list reads like a recipe from a previous century: cream, milk, sugar, egg yolks, vanilla. Haagen-Dazs vanilla became the default recommendation in the bioenergetic community precisely because its ingredient list approximates this standard--no gums, no seed oils, no polysorbate 80.

The additives to avoid include:

- **Carrageenan** -- a seaweed-derived thickener that Peat identified as a potent intestinal irritant and promoter of [[inflammation]]. It has been used experimentally to induce colitis in laboratory animals.
- **Guar gum and locust bean gum** -- thickeners that can promote bacterial overgrowth and intestinal irritation.
- **Polysorbate 80** -- an emulsifier that disrupts the intestinal barrier and promotes [[endotoxin]] absorption.
- **Seed oils** (soybean oil, canola oil) -- sources of [[PUFA]] that undermine the metabolic benefits of the saturated dairy fat.

The principle is simple: ice cream made from cream, sugar, and eggs delivers the nutrients Peat valued. Ice cream made from stabilizers, emulsifiers, and industrial oils delivers the [[toxins]] he warned against. Reading the ingredient label is the entire intervention.

## Ice Cream in the Bioenergetic Diet

Peat's ice cream recommendation became one of the most recognizable--and most misunderstood--elements of his dietary framework. For newcomers encountering his work, "eat a quart of ice cream for migraines" sounds like a joke. For those who understand the underlying [[bioenergetics]], it is a logical application of the same principles that lead Peat to recommend [[sugar]] over starch, [[milk]] over water, and [[saturated-fat]] over polyunsaturated fat.

Ice cream combines every element Peat considered protective: sugar to maintain [[blood sugar]] and suppress stress hormones, calcium to suppress parathyroid hormone, saturated fat to support thyroid function without triggering the Randle cycle, and protein to sustain the metabolic effect. It is calorie-dense in a framework that views adequate caloric intake as anti-stress. It is palatable in a framework that views appetite suppression as a symptom of metabolic dysfunction. And in Peat's homemade formulation, it serves as an elegant delivery system for [[coconut-oil]]--a fat he described in a [[2008-09-18-eluv-eluv-good-fats|2008 episode on good fats]] as "delicious for ice cream or cookies" while noting its weight loss, anti-cancer, and liver-protecting effects.

The cultural resonance of the recommendation--a PhD biochemist telling people to eat ice cream--should not obscure the metabolic reasoning behind it. In Peat's framework, the stress of deprivation is more damaging than the calories of indulgence, and a food that restores blood sugar, provides calcium, and delivers protective fats is not an indulgence at all. It is medicine that happens to taste good.
