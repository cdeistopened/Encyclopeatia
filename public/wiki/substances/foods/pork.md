# Pork

Pork occupies a position in Ray Peat's framework that is nearly as cautionary as the vegetable oils he spent decades opposing. Like [[chicken]], pork comes from a monogastric animal that deposits dietary fats directly into its tissues without conversion. The pig's body fat is a mirror of its feed, and modern pigs eat corn and soy--which means modern lard is loaded with the same [[PUFA]] that Peat identified as a central driver of [[inflammation]], [[thyroid]] suppression, and metabolic disease. The transformation of pork from a traditional staple with relatively saturated fat into an industrial product high in [[linoleic acid]] tracks precisely with the changes in animal feeding that Peat traced to the 1950s.

## The Deliberate Suppression of Pig Metabolism

The story of how pork became problematic is not one of accidental contamination but of deliberate metabolic manipulation. As Peat explained in a [[2009-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-bowel-endotoxin|2009 episode on bowel health and endotoxin]], "they discovered they could economize on food for pigs and chickens by poisoning their thyroid glands so they wouldn't eat as much but would still get fat. They found that polyunsaturated fats suppress the metabolic rate."

The industry applied what Peat had been teaching in reverse--using PUFA not to protect human health but to make animals fattier on less feed. Corn and soy diets became the standard because the polyunsaturated fats in those crops effectively poisoned the pigs' thyroids, reducing energy expenditure while promoting fat deposition. The economics were compelling; the health consequences for anyone eating the resulting pork were not.

This metabolic suppression does not stay in the pig. The same PUFA that lowered the pig's metabolic rate carries through into whoever consumes the fat, suppressing human thyroid function by the same mechanism. As Peat stated plainly in a [[2010-12-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-radiation|2010 episode]]: "Avoid pork and non-ruminants because those reflect whatever they ate and are usually highly unsaturated and anti-thyroid. Beef and lamb are saturated."

## Monogastric Biology and the Ruminant Advantage

The fundamental problem with pork is the pig itself--or more precisely, its digestive system. [[Ruminant]] animals like cattle, sheep, and goats possess a rumen filled with bacteria that biohydrogenate polyunsaturated fats, converting them into saturated fats before they reach the animal's tissues. This is why beef tallow and lamb fat remain predominantly saturated even when the animals are grain-fed. The rumen acts as a biological filter against PUFA.

Pigs have no such mechanism. Their single-chambered stomach processes food much as a human stomach does, and the fatty acids from their diet pass through largely unchanged into their adipose tissue. A pig fed corn and soy produces lard rich in linoleic acid--the same omega-6 fatty acid that dominates soybean oil and corn oil. The pig's lard becomes, in effect, a solid form of the vegetable oils Peat warned against.

This distinction explains why Peat consistently recommended beef and lamb while cautioning against pork. The recommendation is not about the animal species per se but about the fatty acid profile of its fat, which in the case of non-ruminants is a direct function of what the animal ate.

## The Historical Lard That No Longer Exists

Before the 1950s, pigs were fed differently--on scraps, whey, root vegetables, and whatever they foraged. The resulting lard was meaningfully more saturated than what modern industrial pork produces. Traditional cooking with lard was not the metabolic hazard that cooking with modern lard represents, because the fat composition was fundamentally different.

This historical context matters because proponents of traditional diets sometimes argue that since grandparents cooked with lard and remained healthy, modern lard must be acceptable. The argument ignores the transformation Peat described. The lard of 1940 and the lard of today are chemically different products that share only a name. The shift from relatively saturated traditional lard to highly unsaturated industrial lard parallels the broader replacement of [[saturated-fat]] with PUFA in the American diet--a change Peat connected to the rise of obesity, [[diabetes]], [[cancer]], and cardiovascular disease.

## The Pastured Pork Question

"Pastured" pork presents a subtler problem than pastured [[chicken]] or [[eggs]]. Even pigs raised outdoors with access to pasture are typically given supplemental feed, and that feed is almost always corn- or soy-based. A pig foraging acorns in a Spanish dehesa produces the relatively saturated fat found in traditional Iberian ham. A pig rooting in an American pasture but eating corn and soy from a trough produces fat that is not substantially different from conventionally raised pork.

The label "pastured" provides no guarantee about fatty acid composition. Without detailed knowledge of the animal's actual diet--not just its living conditions--there is no reliable way to know whether pastured pork fat is acceptably saturated or unacceptably polyunsaturated. This uncertainty led Peat to recommend avoiding pork fat entirely rather than attempting to source acceptable versions.

## Pork Rinds: Peat's Qualified Exception

Despite his general caution about pork, Peat did consume one pork product in a modified form. As he described in email correspondence, "Sometimes pork rinds, chicharrones, are good for a salty snack; I usually heat them in coconut oil and then drain them, to remove some of the pork fat."

Pork rinds are primarily collagen-rich skin rather than muscle meat, giving them a [[gelatin]]-like amino acid profile rich in [[glycine]] and proline. By heating them in [[coconut-oil]]--a highly saturated fat--and draining them, Peat displaced some of the PUFA-laden pork fat with protective saturated fat. The technique illustrates a broader principle in his dietary framework: problematic foods can sometimes be made acceptable through preparation methods that reduce their harmful components. The goal is not purity but pragmatic reduction of metabolic stress.

## Processed Pork: Additional Concerns

Bacon, ham, sausage, and other processed pork products carry concerns beyond their PUFA content. Nitrates and nitrites used in curing can form nitrosamines--compounds Peat identified as carcinogenic--particularly when exposed to high heat. The combination of high PUFA content and nitrosamine formation makes conventionally processed pork among the least favorable protein sources in Peat's framework.

Smoked and cured meats also tend to be high in [[iron]] from the curing process, adding another variable Peat associated with oxidative damage, bacterial growth, and accelerated [[aging]]. The cumulative burden--PUFA, nitrosamines, excess iron--makes processed pork a convergence of several factors Peat identified as pro-inflammatory and anti-metabolic.
