# Beans

Ray Peat devoted an entire newsletter to beans. Published in 1993, "The Bean Syndrome" argued that common legumes -- kidney beans, lentils, broad beans, chickpeas, and above all [[soy]] -- contain toxins potent enough to mimic viral infection, suppress [[thyroid]] function, and contribute to chronic fatigue. That newsletter was not an outlier. Across decades of interviews, Peat consistently placed beans among the foods most hostile to human metabolism, excluded them from every version of his safe food list, and in a [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 KMUD episode on food]], delivered what may be his most concise verdict: "potatoes are infinitely better than beans as a source of protein." When the best he could say about a food is that a tuber outperforms it, the food has no place in a pro-metabolic diet.

## Lectins and the Intestinal Barrier

The primary toxin in beans is phytohaemagglutinin (PHA), a lectin that binds to the cells lining the intestinal wall and damages them directly. In "The Bean Syndrome," Peat noted that an extract of the common bean "causes the appearance of large mononuclear cells in the blood, the suppression of DNA synthesis in thymic cells, a slight leucopenia, and hepatic necrosis." These are not subtle nutritional shortcomings -- they are toxic effects that overlap with viral infection and autoimmune disease. Extracts of lentils, broad beans, and fenugreek showed comparable activity, while peas, chickpeas, lupinus, and soy beans also demonstrated measurable toxicity.

The intestinal damage lectins cause has downstream consequences that extend far beyond the gut itself. A compromised intestinal barrier allows [[endotoxin]] -- the lipopolysaccharide coating of gram-negative bacteria -- to enter the bloodstream, triggering systemic [[inflammation]], suppressing mitochondrial respiration, and promoting what Peat described as a "shock-physiology." In people already dealing with [[stress]], malnutrition, or aging, the barrier is already weakened, and the lectin assault from beans compounds the problem.

## The Gas Problem Is the Endotoxin Problem

Everyone knows beans cause gas. What Peat's framework clarifies is why that matters beyond social embarrassment. As he explained in a [[2015-01-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-digestion-and-emotion|2015 KMUD episode]], "the bean carbohydrates -- the type of polysaccharide that human enzymes can't work on are ideal for feeding bacteria, hence all the gas." The indigestible oligosaccharides in beans -- raffinose, stachyose, verbascose -- pass through the small intestine intact and arrive in the colon as a feast for bacterial populations.

The gas itself is merely the visible symptom. The real problem is what those bacteria produce alongside it: [[endotoxin]], [[serotonin]], histamine, and other inflammatory mediators. Sarah Murray reinforced this in a [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|2011 KMUD episode]], noting that "starches -- grains, beans, and starchy vegetables" are the foods most likely to feed bacterial fermentation and should be replaced with [[fruit]] when possible. The intestinal ecosystem, in Peat's view, is not neutral territory -- it is a battleground where the wrong fuel supply shifts the bacterial population toward endotoxin-producing species, and beans are among the most effective fuels for that shift.

## Anti-Thyroid Effects

In "The Bean Syndrome," Peat stated plainly that "beans and lentils happen to be powerful anti-thyroid agents." This is a devastating charge in a framework where [[thyroid]] function is the master regulator of metabolic health. Suppressed thyroid output means reduced body temperature, impaired [[protein]] synthesis, poor magnesium retention, increased [[estrogen]] activity, and a general decline in the body's ability to produce energy and maintain structure.

The anti-thyroid activity of beans comes from multiple compounds: goitrogens that interfere with iodine uptake, [[phytic acid]] that blocks the mineral cofactors thyroid function depends on, and the inflammatory cascade triggered by lectins and endotoxin, which itself suppresses thyroid hormone conversion. A person eating beans regularly is attacking their thyroid from several directions simultaneously.

## Protein Quality: The Vegetarian Myth

The vegetarian and vegan movements have elevated beans to the status of a primary [[protein]] source -- the centerpiece of the "beans and rice" combination that supposedly provides "complete protein." Peat's assessment was blunt. In the [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 KMUD food episode]], he said that "potatoes are infinitely better than beans as a source of protein, but that requires your intestine to be in fairly good health." If [[potatoes]] -- a food that rates at only 40% of egg yolk protein quality -- are "infinitely better" than beans, the protein value of beans is effectively negligible in practical terms.

The problem is not just amino acid composition but digestibility. The lectins, enzyme inhibitors, and indigestible carbohydrates in beans all interfere with protein absorption. Whatever amino acids beans theoretically contain, the body extracts only a fraction of them -- and pays a metabolic toll in intestinal damage, endotoxin exposure, and thyroid suppression for the privilege.

## Soaking, Sprouting, and Cooking: Necessary but Insufficient

Traditional food preparation cultures developed soaking, fermenting, and prolonged cooking methods for beans precisely because the raw form is overtly toxic -- raw kidney beans can cause acute poisoning. Peat acknowledged that "boiling it for 40 to 60 minutes" helps break down starch in a [[2015-05-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-sb-277-degradation-of-the-food-supply|2015 KMUD episode]], but he separately noted that beans remain harder to digest than [[potatoes]] even after thorough cooking. Soaking reduces but does not eliminate phytic acid. Cooking reduces but does not eliminate lectins. Nothing commonly done in a home kitchen eliminates the indigestible oligosaccharides that feed bacteria.

The argument that traditional cultures thrived on beans ignores the context: those cultures also had robust gut ecosystems, ate beans as part of diverse diets, and often employed fermentation processes far more extensive than modern quick-soaking. They were also, by Peat's framework, dealing with the chronic metabolic consequences of bean consumption -- they simply did not have the conceptual tools to connect their health problems to their diet.

> Potatoes are infinitely better than beans as a source of protein.
> -- Ray Peat, [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|Food]] (KMUD, 2016)
