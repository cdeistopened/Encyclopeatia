# White Rice

It sounds funny to say that white rice has advantages over brown rice -- but nutritionally, it does. That was Ray Peat's position, and it inverts decades of dietary guidance that treats refined grains as nutritionally gutted versions of their whole counterparts. In Peat's framework, the refining process that strips away the bran and germ is not a loss but a gain: it removes the [[phosphate]], the [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]], and the [[phytic acid]] that make whole grains metabolically problematic. White rice is not a health food in the way Peat understood that term -- it remains a [[starch]], and he was cautious about all starches -- but among the grains available to someone who needs a calorie-dense carbohydrate, polished white rice occupies a uniquely defensible position.

## The Case Against Brown Rice

The health food movement's reverence for brown rice rests on the assumption that the bran and germ contain valuable nutrients. They do -- but Peat argued that those nutrients come packaged with substances that do more harm than good. In a [[2012-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-phosphate-and-calcium-metabolism|2012 episode on phosphate and calcium metabolism]], he explained that "the main sources of phosphate are meat, seeds, beans, nuts, and whole wheat -- much more than refined white flour or white polished rice. Those have the germ removed, and the germ contains most of the phosphate." His co-host Sarah Murray added the other critical point: removing the germ also removes the polyunsaturated fatty acids.

This double removal -- of excess [[phosphate]] and [[PUFA]] -- is the core of Peat's argument. Phosphate in excess disrupts the [[calcium]]-to-phosphate ratio that Peat considered essential for cellular function, promoting [[calcification]] of soft tissues while paradoxically weakening bones. PUFA suppresses [[thyroid]] function, blocks [[glucose]] oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]], and generates inflammatory breakdown products as it oxidizes in storage. Brown rice delivers both of these problematic substances in every serving. White rice, by having its germ polished away, sidesteps them.

The bran also contains [[phytic acid]], which binds minerals like [[calcium]], [[magnesium]], [[iron]], and [[zinc]] in the digestive tract, reducing their absorption. Whole grain advocates argue that soaking or fermenting can reduce phytic acid levels, but Peat's position was simpler: why work to neutralize an anti-nutrient when you can eat a grain that has already had it removed?

## Still a Starch -- and Peat Was Cautious

Defending white rice against brown rice is not the same as recommending it. Peat placed all starches below simple sugars in his metabolic hierarchy, and rice was no exception. In a [[2020-03-16-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-what-is-a-virus-anyway|2020 interview]], he stated plainly: "I am cautious about starches. Any starch, even when well-cooked, can pass directly into your bloodstream." The concern is that incompletely digested starch molecules can enter circulation and provoke immune responses, and that even properly digested starch feeds bacterial populations in the lower intestine, generating [[endotoxin]].

When a caller on a [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 episode]] asked whether Peat was "saying that sugars -- fruit sugars and even white sugar -- are okay, but the bad thing is white flour, white rice, pasta, and simple carbohydrates," Peat confirmed it: "Definitely. The starches and the polyunsaturated fats are the main culprits in fatness." This places white rice squarely in the category of foods Peat tolerated rather than championed. [[Fruit]], [[orange-juice]], [[honey]], and white [[sugar]] -- all providing [[fructose]] alongside [[glucose]] -- were his preferred carbohydrate sources because they are absorbed quickly in the upper intestine, provide the metabolic advantages of fructose, and leave little residue for gut bacteria to ferment.

In a [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|2011 episode on sugar myths]], Sarah Murray reinforced this hierarchy when a caller mentioned preferring brown rice and whole grains: "All the grains, starches, and beans have a higher glycemic index than fruits, honey, and white sugar." The implication is clear -- even the "healthier" version of rice produces a larger and more disruptive blood sugar response than the simple sugars that mainstream nutrition considers more dangerous.

## Low Protein Quality

Rice protein is notably incomplete. In a [[2015-05-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-sb-277-degradation-of-the-food-supply|2015 episode on food quality]], Peat rated rice protein at "about 6% value compared to egg yolk," making it one of the lowest-quality protein sources in the human diet. For comparison, he rated potato protein at roughly 40% of egg yolk's value -- still modest, but nearly seven times more biologically useful than rice.

This means rice should never be relied upon as a protein source. In Peat's framework, it needs to be paired with high-quality [[protein]] from [[eggs]], [[milk]], [[cheese]], [[gelatin]], or [[meat]] to prevent the [[cortisol]] rise that occurs when protein intake falls short. Rice eaten alone -- the basis of many traditional fasting diets and modern "cleanse" protocols -- would represent exactly the kind of protein-deficient, starch-heavy meal Peat associated with metabolic stress.

## Advantages Over Wheat

If white rice sits low in Peat's overall food hierarchy, it nonetheless holds a clear advantage over [[wheat]] and other gluten-containing [[grains]]. Rice contains no [[gluten]], the protein complex in wheat, barley, and rye that Peat associated with intestinal irritation, autoimmune reactions, and increased gut permeability. For someone who needs a starchy staple -- whether for cultural, economic, or practical reasons -- rice causes less intestinal damage than bread or pasta.

Rice is also less likely to contain the [[serotonin]]-promoting peptides found in wheat. Peat identified excess serotonin as one of the four major metabolic disruptors (alongside PUFA, [[estrogen]], and [[endotoxin]]), and wheat's opioid-like peptides (gliadorphins) can increase intestinal serotonin production. White rice, being a simpler grain with fewer bioactive peptides, avoids this problem.

## Practical Guidelines

For those who choose to include white rice in their diet, Peat's framework suggests several principles:

- **Cook it thoroughly.** Well-cooked rice is more completely digested in the upper intestine, leaving less undigested starch to feed bacteria in the colon. Overcooked, soft rice is preferable to al dente preparations.
- **Eat it with fat and protein.** [[Butter]], [[coconut-oil]], or other [[saturated-fat]] sources slow gastric emptying and improve the metabolic context. Pairing with [[eggs]], [[cheese]], or [[meat]] compensates for rice's near-absent protein quality.
- **Prefer it over brown rice, whole wheat, and other whole grains.** The polishing process is a feature, not a bug.
- **Do not treat it as a staple.** In Peat's hierarchy, [[fruit]] and [[fruit juice]] are superior carbohydrate sources, followed by [[honey]] and white [[sugar]]. Rice is an acceptable fallback, not a foundation.
- **Watch for digestive signs.** Bloating, gas, or increased intestinal discomfort after eating rice may indicate bacterial fermentation of undigested starch -- the very process Peat warned against.

> "It sounds funny to say that white rice and white bread have advantages, but nutritionally they do."
> -- Ray Peat, [[2012-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-phosphate-and-calcium-metabolism|Phosphate and Calcium Metabolism]] (KMUD, 2012)
