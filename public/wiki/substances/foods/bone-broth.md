# Bone Broth

Bone broth has become one of the most popular health foods of the last decade, marketed as a cure-all and sold in cartons at premium prices. Ray Peat's perspective on bone broth is characteristically more precise than the enthusiasm surrounding it: the value lies almost entirely in the [[gelatin]] extracted from connective tissue, and the way most people make bone broth--simmering marrow bones for twelve to twenty-four hours--actually undermines the purpose by concentrating [[iron]] and oxidizing nutrients. The right bone broth, made from the right parts for the right amount of time, is an excellent food. The wrong bone broth is a source of problems.

## Not the Bones--the Joints

The central misconception about bone broth, in Peat's view, is that the bones themselves are the point. They are not. As he stated plainly in a [[2014-01-01-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-patrick-timpone-dr-peat-answers-questions-regarding-health-die|2014 interview]], "You don't want to use marrow bones because of the high iron content. You want the joints that have the tendons and cartilage attached." The therapeutic substance is the collagen-rich connective tissue--tendons, ligaments, cartilage, and skin--not the bone or its marrow.

This distinction matters because iron is one of the substances Peat most consistently warned against. Excess iron promotes [[oxidative stress]], feeds pathogenic bacteria, and drives the lipid peroxidation that Peat placed at the center of aging and degenerative disease. Marrow bones are dense repositories of iron and fat, and long simmering extracts both into the broth. The popular image of bone broth--a pot of split femur bones bubbling for a full day--is, in Peat's framework, a recipe for an iron-loaded, oxidized liquid rather than a healing food.

The preferred cuts are those richest in connective tissue: chicken feet, lamb shanks, oxtails with their abundant cartilage, pigs' feet, and any joint where tendons and ligaments attach to bone. These release their [[gelatin]] into the water as the collagen denatures, creating the characteristic gel that a properly made broth forms when cooled. If your broth does not gel, it likely lacked sufficient connective tissue.

## The Three-to-Four-Hour Window

Peat was specific about cooking time. As he explained in his correspondence on the topic, "most of the gelatin is released in 3 or 4 hours. Excess cooking oxidizes nutrients, especially if there's marrow in the bone." This runs counter to the popular bone broth culture of twenty-four- or even forty-eight-hour simmers, which Peat viewed as counterproductive.

The logic is straightforward: once the collagen has denatured into gelatin and dissolved into the liquid--a process that is substantially complete within a few hours--continued cooking does not extract more of the beneficial substance. What it does is progressively oxidize the fats, amino acids, and other nutrients already in the broth, creating the very kind of oxidative damage that gelatin consumption is meant to counteract. The long-simmered broth may taste richer because it has extracted more minerals and broken down more tissue, but Peat would argue that richness comes at a metabolic cost.

## Glycine, Proline, and the Anti-Inflammatory Amino Acids

The reason bone broth matters in Peat's dietary framework has nothing to do with minerals, collagen peptides, or the other selling points of the bone broth industry. It matters because of its [[amino acids|amino acid]] profile--specifically, the abundance of [[glycine]] and proline and the near-total absence of the amino acids Peat identified as problematic.

As Peat explained in a [[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|2019 episode on vitamin D and thyroid]], "Glycine and proline are the main reasons for using gelatin. Gelatin is completely free of the amino acids that are pro-inflammatory, anti-thyroid, and even carcinogenic: tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine. Gelatin is a very protective protein for a mature person who isn't growing."

This is the core of Peat's argument for bone broth. Modern diets are dominated by muscle meat--chicken breast, steak, ground beef--which is rich in [[tryptophan]], [[cysteine]], and [[methionine]]. In Peat's framework, these amino acids, consumed in excess, drive [[serotonin]] production in the gut, promote [[inflammation]], suppress [[thyroid]] function, and accelerate [[aging]]. Glycine and proline do the opposite: glycine is an inhibitory neurotransmitter that calms excitotoxic processes, supports liver [[detoxification]], and has broad anti-inflammatory effects. Proline supports connective tissue maintenance throughout the body.

Bone broth--or more precisely, the gelatin it contains--provides a way to get substantial protein without the metabolic burden of the amino acids that come with muscle meat. The goal is not to replace muscle meat entirely but to shift the overall amino acid ratio in a protective direction.

## The Gelatin-Collagen Terminology Problem

The supplement industry has muddied the distinction between gelatin and collagen, a confusion Peat addressed directly. In a [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 interview]], he noted: "Gelatin is basically the stiffening glue that holds our other cells together. A lot of people are selling gelatin calling it collagen. Collagen is the raw form in your tissues; to buy actual collagen is very expensive."

The practical implication: the "collagen peptides" and "collagen powder" sold by supplement companies are, in most cases, simply hydrolyzed gelatin marketed under a trendier name. True collagen is the intact structural protein in living tissue. What you extract from bones and connective tissue by cooking is gelatin--denatured collagen. What you buy in a canister of "collagen peptides" is gelatin that has been further broken down enzymatically. The distinction matters less for health outcomes than for understanding what you are actually purchasing, though Peat generally favored whole-food gelatin from broth or simple gelatin powder over heavily processed supplement forms.

## The Chicken Bone Broth Caution

Peat raised a specific concern about chicken-based broths in the American context. As he noted in his email correspondence, "In the US, chickens are fed arsenic to make them grow faster, and it concentrates in the bones; you should find out what the chicken feeding practice is in your area." Roxarsone, an organoarsenic compound, was widely used in US poultry feed for decades and was only partially withdrawn from the market. Arsenic accumulates preferentially in bones, making chicken bone broth a potential vector for chronic low-level arsenic exposure.

This does not mean chicken-based broth is universally problematic--chickens raised without arsenic-containing feed would not carry this risk. But Peat's caution underscores the importance of sourcing, particularly for a food that people consume daily as a health practice. Chicken feet from pastured, organically raised chickens remain one of the richest sources of gelatin available. The concern is specifically about conventionally raised US poultry and the compounds concentrated in their bones.

## Gelatin Powder as a Practical Alternative

Peat recognized that making broth from scratch is not always practical. For those who cannot or do not want to cook broth regularly, commercial gelatin powder--the unflavored kind sold by Knox or Great Lakes--provides the same amino acids in a shelf-stable, instantly usable form. Dissolved in warm coffee, juice, or milk, a few tablespoons of gelatin powder delivers the glycine and proline that are the entire point of the exercise.

Peat himself incorporated gelatin into his daily diet alongside other protective proteins like [[milk]], [[eggs]], and [[cheese]]. In his correspondence, he mentioned drinking a gallon of milk daily and eating gelatin-rich foods as part of an overall [[protein]] balance strategy--the goal being to keep the ratio of protective amino acids (glycine, proline) high relative to the anti-metabolic ones (tryptophan, cysteine, methionine) that dominate muscle meat.

> "Gelatin is a very protective protein for a mature person who isn't growing."

For younger people still in growth phases, where tryptophan and methionine play necessary roles in tissue building, the ratio matters less. But for adults--and especially for aging adults--Peat's recommendation was clear: shift protein intake away from muscle meat and toward gelatin, whether through properly made broth or simple supplementation.
