# Kale

Few foods illustrate the gap between mainstream nutrition and Ray Peat's framework as starkly as kale. In popular health culture, kale is a "superfood"--blended into smoothies, baked into chips, tossed raw into salads by people who believe they are doing something profoundly good for their bodies. In Peat's view, they may be suppressing their [[thyroid]] function with every bite. Kale belongs to the Brassica family of cruciferous vegetables, and Peat consistently listed it among the goitrogenic foods that interfere with [[iodine]] uptake and thyroid hormone production. In a [[2008-08-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-metabolism-and-coconut-oil|2008 KMUD episode on thyroid metabolism]], he named "uncooked goitrogenic foods such as Brussels sprouts, broccoli, rutabaga, turnips, radishes, cauliflower, millet, cabbage, or kale" as direct suppressors of thyroid function. The irony is difficult to overstate: people eating kale for "health" may be creating the very [[hypothyroidism]] they are trying to prevent.

## The Goitrogen Problem: Thiocyanates and Thyroid Suppression

Cruciferous vegetables contain glucosinolates--sulfur-containing compounds that, when the plant tissue is crushed or chewed, break down into thiocyanates and isothiocyanates. These compounds compete directly with [[iodine]] for uptake at the sodium-iodide symporter on thyroid cells. When thiocyanates occupy the receptor sites that iodine needs, the thyroid gland cannot produce adequate [[thyroid-hormone|thyroid hormone]]. The result is a functional iodine deficiency even in people whose iodine intake is technically sufficient.

Raw kale delivers these compounds at their highest concentration. Chewing and blending--exactly the preparations most popular with health-conscious consumers--maximize the enzymatic conversion of glucosinolates into their active goitrogenic forms. A kale smoothie is, from the thyroid's perspective, a concentrated anti-thyroid preparation. Kale chips, despite being dried at high temperatures, still retain goitrogenic compounds because the dehydration process does not reach the sustained heat necessary to fully deactivate them.

Cooking reduces goitrogen content, but does not eliminate it. Boiling is the most effective method because the water-soluble thiocyanates leach into the cooking water--which must then be discarded. Steaming and sauteing are less effective because the goitrogenic compounds remain trapped in the plant tissue. Even after thorough boiling, some residual activity persists.

## Beyond Goitrogens: PUFA, Tannins, and Oxalates

The thyroid-suppressing glucosinolates are not kale's only problem in Peat's framework. In email correspondence on the subject of vegetables, Peat acknowledged that "the minerals and [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] are definitely valuable" in leafy greens, but warned that "the high content of [[pufa|PUFA]] and tannins is a problem." Leafy greens, including kale, contain polyunsaturated fatty acids in their cell membranes--a fact that surprises people who think of PUFA as something found only in seed oils. The quantities per serving are small compared to a tablespoon of soybean oil, but for someone eating large daily salads or blending multiple cups of raw kale into smoothies, the cumulative PUFA exposure becomes meaningful.

Tannins compound the issue by binding proteins and inhibiting digestive enzymes, reducing the bioavailability of the nutrients that kale is supposed to provide. Kale also contains oxalates, which bind [[calcium]] in the gut and prevent its absorption--directly opposing Peat's emphasis on high calcium intake as protective against [[inflammation]], [[estrogen]] excess, and degenerative disease. For someone drinking kale smoothies in place of [[milk]] or [[orange-juice|orange juice]], the net mineral effect may be negative: less calcium absorbed, not more.

## If You Must Eat Greens: The Boiling Protocol

Peat did not categorically condemn all greens. In one email exchange on diet, he noted that "chard, collard, and kale are good greens"--but the context was specifically about cooked greens as a source of [[magnesium]]. This was not an endorsement of raw kale salads. The operative word is *cooked*, and Peat's preferred method was aggressive: "Boiling the leaves and discarding all but the water can produce a good magnesium supplement." The recommendation is to boil the greens thoroughly, throw away the leaves--which contain the PUFA, tannins, goitrogens, and oxalates--and drink only the mineral-rich broth. This is the opposite of how kale is marketed and consumed. The "superfood" approach maximizes contact with every problematic compound. The Peat approach extracts what is useful and discards what is harmful.

## The Kale Smoothie as Metabolic Self-Sabotage

The green smoothie trend represents a near-perfect inversion of Peat's dietary principles. Raw kale, blended to maximize cell rupture and goitrogen activation, often combined with raw [[spinach]] (adding oxalates), banana (adding [[serotonin]]-precursor [[tryptophan]]), and almond milk (adding more PUFA)--this is a drink designed, however inadvertently, to suppress [[thyroid]] function, block [[calcium]] absorption, increase [[serotonin]] production, and deliver polyunsaturated fats. That it is consumed by people who consider themselves health-conscious is a testament to how effectively marketing has overridden metabolic logic.

> "The minerals and vitamin K are definitely valuable, but the high content of PUFA and tannins is a problem. Boiling the leaves and discarding all but the water can produce a good magnesium supplement."

In Peat's framework, anyone concerned about their metabolic health would be better served replacing kale with well-cooked root vegetables, [[fruit]], or the [[carrot-salad|raw carrot salad]]--a food that actually supports thyroid function and intestinal health rather than undermining both.
