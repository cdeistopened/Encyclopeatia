# Liver

In a dietary landscape dominated by muscle meat, liver stands apart as the single most nutrient-dense food available. Ray Peat recommended it regularly—typically once a week—as the best natural source of preformed [[vitamin A]], B vitamins (particularly B12 and B2), [[copper]], [[vitamin K]], and a form of vitamin C that nutrition charts have largely stopped listing. As Peat described in a [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 interview]], liver, oysters, and eggs contain "the highest concentration of the healing minerals and vitamins."

## The Vitamin A Anchor

Liver's most important contribution is preformed [[vitamin A]] (retinol). Where plant carotene must be converted to retinol by a thyroid-dependent process that is highly inefficient in many people, liver provides retinol directly—the form the body actually uses for steroid hormone synthesis, [[thyroid]] function, and [[estrogen]] clearance.

As Peat explained in a [[2014-05-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-may-2014|2014 episode]], liver—whether from fish, chicken, or beef—is "a great source" of vitamin A and B12. He also noted in email exchanges that nutrition databases have stopped listing liver's [[vitamin E]] and vitamin K content, but "it's very good for those" as well. The fat-soluble vitamin complex (A, D, E, K) works as a unit, and liver provides meaningful amounts of all four.

For eye health specifically, Peat recommended "having liver once a week" to address B2 deficiency symptoms like bloodshot eyes, and "regular eggs, to keep the vitamin intake high" for conditions like eye floaters. These practical recommendations reflect liver's role as a concentrated source of the nutrients most modern diets are deficient in.

## Copper and Iron Balance

Liver is the body's primary storage organ for both [[copper]] and [[iron]], and the food reflects this concentration. As Peat noted in a [[2012-07-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-blood-pressure-regulation-and-diabetes|2012 episode]], beef liver is a top source of copper—a mineral essential for proper iron metabolism, energy production, and enzyme function.

The iron content of liver requires context in Peat's framework. While Peat generally cautioned against excessive iron intake, the iron in liver comes packaged with the copper, B vitamins, and vitamin A that support its proper utilization. This is qualitatively different from the isolated iron added to fortified flour and cereals, which accumulates without the cofactors needed for safe incorporation into hemoglobin and enzymes. The once-weekly frequency Peat recommended provides the benefits of liver's nutrient density without the risks of excessive iron accumulation.

## The Detoxification Organ

The liver is the body's primary detoxification organ, and this dual identity—both as food and as the organ that processes toxins—creates reasonable concern about eating it. Peat addressed this indirectly through his framework of how the liver handles [[PUFA]]. As he explained, the liver treats polyunsaturated fats like other toxins, "detoxifying them for excretion when it has sufficient energy."

The practical implication is that the quality of the animal's liver reflects the quality of its diet and metabolic health. Liver from animals raised on high-PUFA feed or exposed to environmental toxins would concentrate those substances. Peat's general preference for well-raised animal products applies with particular force to liver, where the organ's concentrating function works in both directions—concentrating nutrients in a healthy animal and toxins in an unhealthy one.

## Liver Oil vs. Whole Liver

Peat distinguished between whole liver and liver oils, which concentrate the fat-soluble vitamins while removing other components. He spoke favorably of halibut liver oil as a source of vitamins A and D with relatively low PUFA content. For cod liver oil, he was more cautious—it provides beneficial A and D but contains more polyunsaturated fish oils. He preferred vitamin A and D derived from fish liver oil with "most other fish oils removed."

For retinol supplementation specifically, Peat mentioned using retinyl palmitate—a supplemental form of preformed vitamin A—while cautioning that excessive retinol can also interfere with thyroid function. The dose makes the poison, and even protective nutrients have upper limits. Weekly consumption of whole liver, supplemented with occasional liver oil or retinyl palmitate, provided what Peat considered the optimal vitamin A strategy for most people.

## Practical Recommendations

Peat's standard recommendation was simple: eat liver once a week. Beef liver is the most nutrient-dense option, though chicken liver is milder in flavor and still provides substantial vitamin A, B vitamins, and copper. The once-weekly frequency provides the concentrated nutrition liver offers without the iron accumulation that more frequent consumption might promote.

Combined with daily [[eggs]], regular [[milk]] and [[cheese]], and occasional oysters, weekly liver consumption creates a dietary foundation that supplies virtually all the micronutrients Peat considered essential—without requiring the isolated supplements whose absorption and utilization are often inferior to food-form nutrients.
