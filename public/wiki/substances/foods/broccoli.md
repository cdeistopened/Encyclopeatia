# Broccoli

Broccoli occupies a peculiar position in modern nutrition: it is simultaneously one of the most recommended vegetables and one of the most thyroid-suppressive. Mainstream health authorities promote it as an anti-cancer powerhouse, citing its glucosinolate-derived compound sulforaphane. Ray Peat looked at the same chemistry and saw something else--a food whose primary bioactive compounds compete with [[iodine]] for thyroid uptake, suppressing the [[thyroid-hormone|thyroid hormone]] production that Peat considered foundational to cancer resistance in the first place. In a [[2008-08-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-metabolism-and-coconut-oil|2008 KMUD episode on thyroid metabolism]], Peat listed broccoli among the "uncooked goitrogenic foods" that directly suppress thyroid function--placing it in the same category as [[kale]], cabbage, cauliflower, and Brussels sprouts. The supposed anti-cancer vegetable, in Peat's framework, may be creating the metabolic conditions that allow cancer to develop.

## Glucosinolates: The Thyroid-Suppressing Chemistry

All cruciferous vegetables--the Brassica family that includes broccoli, kale, cabbage, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, turnips, and radishes--contain glucosinolates, sulfur-containing compounds that serve as the plant's chemical defense system. When broccoli is chewed, cut, or blended, an enzyme called myrosinase converts these glucosinolates into isothiocyanates and thiocyanates. It is these breakdown products that suppress thyroid function.

The mechanism is straightforward. Thiocyanates compete with [[iodine]] at the sodium-iodide symporter--the transport protein on thyroid cells responsible for concentrating iodine from the bloodstream. When thiocyanates occupy these receptor sites, the thyroid gland cannot take up sufficient iodine to produce [[t3|T3]] and T4. The result is a chemically induced iodine deficiency, regardless of how much iodine is present in the diet. This is the same basic mechanism by which goiter develops in populations whose staple foods are cruciferous vegetables--a phenomenon well documented in regions where cassava (another goitrogenic food) is a dietary staple.

Raw broccoli delivers the highest goitrogenic load because myrosinase is most active in uncooked tissue. Cooking deactivates myrosinase and reduces--but does not eliminate--the concentration of preformed thiocyanates. Boiling is more effective than steaming because the water-soluble goitrogens leach into the cooking water, which should be discarded. Even after thorough cooking, however, some glucosinolate derivatives persist, and the gut microbiome can convert residual glucosinolates into thiocyanates even when the plant's own myrosinase has been destroyed.

## The Sulforaphane Paradox

Broccoli's modern reputation rests largely on sulforaphane, an isothiocyanate that has been heavily promoted as an anti-cancer compound. Research has shown that sulforaphane activates Nrf2 pathways, induces phase II detoxification enzymes, and has anti-proliferative effects on cancer cells in laboratory settings. Broccoli sprouts, which contain dramatically higher concentrations of sulforaphane's precursor glucoraphanin than mature broccoli, have been marketed as concentrated cancer-prevention supplements.

Peat's framework reframes this chemistry. Sulforaphane is an isothiocyanate--the same class of compounds that suppresses thyroid function. Its biological activity is not separable from its goitrogenic activity; they are expressions of the same chemistry. In Peat's model, [[thyroid]] function is itself one of the body's primary defenses against cancer. Adequate thyroid hormone maintains high [[oxidative metabolism]], keeps cells in a differentiated state, opposes the shift toward glycolysis that characterizes cancer cells (the [[Warburg effect]]), and supports [[progesterone]] production--all of which are anti-cancer mechanisms. A compound that suppresses thyroid function to activate a detoxification pathway may be trading a systemic defense for a local one.

The broccoli sprout trend intensifies this concern. Sprouts contain the highest concentrations of glucosinolates of any stage of the plant's growth, and they are typically consumed raw--maximizing myrosinase activity and goitrogen delivery. In Peat's framework, a daily broccoli sprout supplement is functionally an anti-thyroid medication marketed as a health food.

## PUFA and Tannins: The Leafy Green Burden

As with other greens, broccoli carries what Peat identified in email exchanges as a "high content of [[pufa|PUFA]] and tannins." The polyunsaturated fatty acids in leaf and floret cell membranes are modest per serving but cumulative in heavy consumers. Tannins bind proteins and inhibit digestive enzymes, reducing the bioavailability of the nutrients broccoli provides. Combined with the goitrogenic compounds and the oxalates present in smaller but non-trivial amounts, broccoli delivers its vitamins and minerals wrapped in a package of anti-nutrients that partially or fully offset the benefit.

Peat's recommendation for anyone determined to eat leafy greens--boiling thoroughly and discarding the cooking water--applies to broccoli as well. This method leaches goitrogens, oxalates, and some PUFA into the water while retaining mineral content. But as Peat noted, the resulting product is essentially a "good [[magnesium]] supplement" rather than a complete food, and there are simpler ways to obtain magnesium without the anti-nutrient baggage.

## Better Vegetable Choices in the Peat Framework

Peat's framework does not require avoiding all vegetables, but it does require choosing wisely. The foods he repeatedly recommended as safe and beneficial occupy a different category entirely from the cruciferous family:

- **Well-cooked root vegetables** -- [[potatoes]], turnips (when thoroughly boiled), carrots (cooked as a food, raw as a [[carrot-salad|therapeutic fiber]])
- **Well-cooked [[mushrooms]]** -- Antimicrobial, anti-estrogenic, low in anti-nutrients after extended boiling
- **[[bamboo-shoots|Bamboo shoots]]** -- Antiseptic fiber that survives cooking
- **Winter squash** -- Mild, well-tolerated, low in anti-nutrients when cooked

These foods provide minerals, fiber, and carbohydrate without suppressing thyroid function, blocking mineral absorption, or delivering concentrated PUFA. For someone following Peat's principles, they represent what vegetables should be: nourishing rather than antagonistic.

> "Boiling the leaves and discarding all but the water can produce a good magnesium supplement."

The message is clear: broccoli's nutritional value, such as it is, requires aggressive cooking and water discarding to access safely. For most people, the simpler choice is to eat foods that do not require neutralizing before they become beneficial.
