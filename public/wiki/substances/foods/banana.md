# Banana

Few foods illustrate the gap between mainstream nutritional advice and Ray Peat's framework as sharply as the banana. Conventional dietetics treats bananas as an uncomplicated health food -- a convenient source of potassium and energy. Peat treated them as a conditional food requiring specific ripeness criteria, one that most people eat in a form that is actively harmful. An underripe banana delivers resistant [[starch]] granules directly into the bloodstream, triggers allergic responses possibly linked to modern cultivation methods, and supplies [[serotonin]] -- a substance Peat spent decades arguing against. The banana you grab from a grocery store display, still firm and yellow, is not the same food as the soft, spotted banana Peat might have cautiously permitted.

## The Starch Granule Danger: Persorption

The central problem with bananas in Peat's framework is not their sugar content but their starch content -- specifically, the intact starch granules present in any banana that has not fully ripened. As Peat explained in a [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 interview on stress and trauma]], "the very juicy fruits are best raw, but if they're starchy like bananas or plantains, they should be cooked by preference because the starches can enter the bloodstream and trigger allergic reactions." He identified the mechanism as [[persorption]] -- a process in which undigested starch particles pass through the intestinal wall and into circulation: "The particles enter the bloodstream and can block capillaries and arterioles, causing cells to die until the particle can be broken down."

This is not a minor digestive inconvenience. The starch granules act as physical obstructions within the vascular system. When a particle lodges in a capillary, the cells downstream lose their blood supply and die. Research Peat cited on mice fed high raw-starch diets showed premature aging across every organ -- widespread, diffuse cell death from circulatory blockage. The mechanism is the same whether the starch comes from a potato, a grain, or a banana. But the banana is uniquely deceptive because people eat it raw, often before it has fully ripened, assuming it is a simple, healthy fruit.

The ripening process converts the banana's resistant starch into simple sugars. A green banana is roughly 70-80% starch by dry weight; a fully brown-spotted banana has converted most of that starch to [[sugar]]. This transformation is the dividing line between a food that damages capillaries and one that provides usable energy. The spotted, soft banana that most people consider overripe is, in Peat's terms, the only version worth eating raw.

## A Strong Allergen with Latex Cross-Reactivity

Beyond the starch problem, Peat flagged bananas as unusually allergenic. In email correspondence on [[fruit]] selection, he wrote that "bananas and jack-fruit are strong allergens, possibly because of their cultivation methods." The parenthetical is significant -- Peat did not attribute the allergenicity solely to an inherent property of the banana species but speculated about how industrial monoculture cultivation might alter the fruit's chemistry. Modern banana production relies heavily on chemical treatments, early harvesting for shipping convenience, and gas-ripening with ethylene in warehouses -- practices that may leave residual compounds or produce incomplete biochemical development compared to a tree-ripened fruit.

Peat connected this to a broader pattern of plant defensive chemistry intensified by agricultural stress. As he wrote on raypeat.com, "reactions to latex (which is secreted by rubber trees in reaction to a wound), bananas, avocados, many other fruits and vegetables" share a common origin -- these are all plants that produce defensive compounds in response to injury. "Intensive agricultural methods are increasing the formation of the defensive chemicals," he argued, meaning that the modern banana, raised under the pressures of commercial monoculture, likely contains more allergens than its wild or traditionally cultivated ancestors. The latex-banana cross-reactivity, well documented in conventional allergy medicine, takes on additional significance in Peat's framework: both latex and banana proteins are wound-response compounds, and modern farming amplifies their production.

This concern placed bananas in a different category from the tropical fruits Peat generally recommended. "Mangos, apples, and pears are allergenic to some people," he noted, but bananas received a blanket warning rather than a qualified one. For someone already dealing with [[inflammation]], gut permeability, or immune dysregulation, the allergenic potential compounds every other problem the banana carries.

## Serotonin: The Hidden Payload

Bananas contain significant quantities of [[serotonin]] -- not a precursor like tryptophan, but the active neurotransmitter itself. In *"Serotonin, Depression, and Aggression: The Problem of Brain Energy,"* Peat wrote that "some fruits, including bananas, pineapples, and tomatoes, contain enough serotonin to produce physiological effects in susceptible people." In mainstream nutrition, this is treated as either neutral or mildly beneficial. In Peat's anti-serotonin framework, it is a serious concern.

Peat argued throughout his career that serotonin functions primarily as a stress mediator and inflammatory signal, not the "happiness molecule" of popular science. Elevated serotonin promotes intestinal inflammation, contributes to [[fibrosis]], suppresses [[thyroid function]], and drives the kind of cellular excitation that Peat associated with aging and disease. Eating a food that delivers serotonin directly -- bypassing the body's own regulatory mechanisms for its production -- works against the metabolic goals Peat outlined.

This is one reason Peat's dietary advice, as summarized in a [[2011-05-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-misconceptions-relating-to-serotonin-and-melatonin|2011 KMUD episode on serotonin and melatonin misconceptions]], emphasized replacing "starches -- grains, beans, and starchy vegetables -- with soft, ripe, juicy fruits." The juicy tropical fruits Peat preferred -- oranges, mangoes, papayas -- do not carry the serotonin load that bananas do. The banana occupies an awkward position: technically a fruit, but metabolically behaving more like a starchy vegetable with a pharmacological side effect.

## Where Bananas Sit in the Fruit Hierarchy

Peat's fruit recommendations followed a clear hierarchy. At the top were juicy, ripe tropical and citrus fruits -- [[orange-juice]], mangoes, papayas -- foods that deliver their energy as simple sugars (roughly equal parts [[fructose]] and [[glucose]]) with generous [[potassium]] and [[magnesium]]. These fruits require no cooking, carry minimal starch, and support rather than oppose the metabolic direction Peat advocated.

Bananas fall below this tier. Their high potassium content -- the feature most commonly cited in their favor -- is not a particular advantage in Peat's mineral framework. He emphasized [[sodium]] and [[calcium]] as the minerals most people are deficient in, and warned that excessive potassium relative to sodium could actually suppress cellular energy production. The banana's potassium-to-sodium ratio, while impressive by conventional standards, is precisely the kind of imbalance Peat cautioned against.

> "Bananas and jack-fruit are strong allergens, possibly because of their cultivation methods. Mangos, apples, and pears are allergenic to some people. Poorly ripened fruits of all sorts should be avoided."
> -- Ray Peat, email correspondence on fruit selection

The practical message is not absolute prohibition but conditional acceptance with significant restrictions: the banana must be fully ripe (dark spots, soft flesh, strong sweet aroma), and even then it remains a second-tier choice compared to juicier, less starchy, less serotonin-rich fruits. Plantains and green bananas can be redeemed through thorough cooking -- fried in [[butter]] or [[coconut-oil]], the heat gelatinizes the starch granules and the [[saturated-fat]] slows intestinal transit to allow complete digestion. But for someone choosing between a banana and a ripe mango, Peat's answer was never in doubt.
