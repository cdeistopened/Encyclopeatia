# Whole Wheat

The dietary advice to choose whole wheat over white flour is among the most universally accepted recommendations in modern nutrition. It is also, in Ray Peat's framework, precisely backwards. The bran and germ that whole wheat preserves -- the components that give it its brown color and its reputation for nutritional superiority -- are the parts that contain the highest concentrations of [[polyunsaturated-fats]], [[phytic acid]], and excess [[phosphate]]. When asked in an email exchange whether everyone should avoid [[gluten]], Peat's answer was unambiguous: "Yes." But whole wheat compounds the gluten problem with three additional mechanisms of harm that white flour at least partially avoids. In Peat's grain hierarchy -- nixtamalized corn, then [[white rice]], then real sourdough, then white bread, then whole wheat -- whole wheat occupies the bottom position. It is the worst way to eat wheat, marketed as the best.

## Rancid Fats in the Germ: Why White Flour Was Invented

The wheat germ -- celebrated in health food stores as a stand-alone supplement -- contains the fats that would fuel the plant's growth if the seed germinated. Those fats are predominantly polyunsaturated. Wheat germ oil is approximately 55% polyunsaturated, making it one of the more PUFA-dense oils in the food supply. When the germ remains in the flour, those fats begin oxidizing immediately upon grinding, reacting with oxygen and iron to generate the toxic byproducts Peat spent decades warning about.

Peat explained this in a [[2021-12-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-hormones-behind-inflammation|2021 KMUD episode on inflammation]]: "The reason flour and grains were polished -- like white rice and white flour -- is that the whole grains get rancid and toxic fairly quickly in storage. If you're going to eat whole wheat flour soon after it's ground, it's probably not so bad. But once it gets in your blood, those excess polyunsaturated fats start to react with iron to produce lipid peroxides and free radical toxins." The refining process that mainstream nutrition condemns as stripping away nutrients was, historically, a solution to the rancidity problem. Traditional peoples who polished their rice and sifted their flour were not ignorant of nutrition -- they were protecting themselves from the toxic fats in the germ.

White flour contains negligible polyunsaturated fat. Whole wheat flour retains it. Every slice of whole wheat bread delivers a dose of the PUFA that Peat identified as one of the four major metabolic disruptors, alongside excess [[estrogen]], excess [[serotonin]], and [[endotoxin]]. The quantities per serving may seem small compared to a tablespoon of corn oil, but they accumulate in cell membranes, in stored fat, and in tissues where PUFA progressively impairs [[oxidative metabolism]] and promotes [[lipid-peroxidation]].

## Seeds as Chemical Warfare

Peat situated wheat within his broader analysis of seeds as biological defense systems. In a [[2009-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-bowel-endotoxin|2009 KMUD episode on bowel health and endotoxin]], he identified the two major problems with seeds, especially grains like wheat: "Gluten -- this storage protein resembles a protein our tissues make under the influence of estrogen or stress," and what he called "chemical weapons" -- the plant's most intense toxins concentrated in its seeds because "seeds are the next generation of the plant." This framing positions wheat not as a generous gift of nourishment but as a reproductive structure defended by compounds evolved to discourage consumption.

The [[gluten]] problem is shared by both whole and refined wheat. Gliadin and glutenin -- the protein complex that gives dough its elasticity -- provoke intestinal irritation, increase gut permeability, and generate opioid-like peptides (gliadorphins) that elevate intestinal serotonin production. But whole wheat adds every other problem -- PUFA, phosphate, phytic acid -- on top of the gluten that both forms share. If white flour is problematic because of its gluten content, whole wheat is problematic for gluten plus three additional mechanisms of harm.

## Phytic Acid and the Mineral Robbery

The bran of whole wheat -- the outer layer that gives whole grain bread its brown flecks and chewy texture -- is where [[phytic acid]] concentrates most heavily. Phytic acid binds [[calcium]], [[magnesium]], [[zinc]], and [[iron]] in the intestinal tract, forming insoluble complexes the body cannot absorb. As Peat noted in email correspondence, "The phytic acid in the oats blocks absorption of much of the calcium" -- and the same principle applies to whole wheat's bran. A person eating whole wheat bread with a glass of [[milk]], believing they are maximizing nutrition, is allowing the phytic acid in the bran to chelate a portion of the milk's calcium and magnesium before those minerals reach the bloodstream.

White flour avoids this problem by removing the bran where phytic acid resides. The "solution" to phytic acid in whole wheat already exists. It is called refining.

## The Phosphate Burden

Whole wheat delivers [[phosphate]] in abundance. The bran and germ are where phosphate concentrates -- stored as phytic acid, the plant's phosphorus reserve for germination. Peat devoted extensive attention to the calcium-to-phosphate ratio in the diet, arguing that excess phosphate stimulates [[parathyroid hormone]], promotes [[calcification]] of soft tissues while weakening bones, increases [[inflammation]], and creates a metabolic environment favorable to aging and degenerative disease. White flour, with the bran and germ removed, has substantially less phosphate. For someone trying to maintain the high calcium-to-phosphate ratio Peat associated with youthful metabolism, the whole grain is always the worse option.

## Sourdough: The Traditional Exception

If modern whole wheat bread represents the worst form of wheat, traditional sourdough represents the least harmful. Peat drew a sharp distinction between the two. In a [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 One Radio Network episode]], he explained: "The old-fashioned 12-hour sourdough leavening process destroys almost all the gluten and converts it to nutritious protein. Modern bread is made quickly, leaving the gluten intact." This was not a minor historical curiosity -- it was a fundamental transformation of the food.

In a [[2015-05-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-sb-277-degradation-of-the-food-supply|2015 KMUD episode on the degradation of the food supply]], Peat elaborated on what the traditional process actually accomplished: "Even in my parents' and grandparents' generation, bread making was different. Letting it rise for 8 or 10 hours, the slow leavening process activated enzymes that broke down the starches into sugar and broke down the gluten. This can actually increase the protein by 100 or 200%." The long fermentation converted the problematic storage proteins into digestible amino acids while breaking down starches into simpler sugars -- transformations that modern quick-rise yeast bread, ready in two hours, never undergoes.

Peat confirmed this from personal experience in a [[2019-05-21-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-the-goods|2019 interview]]: "I experimented with soaking wheat for 12 to 24 hours. Ordinary bakery bread would cause gas, but I could eat a whole loaf of my homemade soaked bread with no problem." The difference was not the wheat itself but the time allowed for enzymatic degradation.

In email exchanges, Peat was even more precise: "Naturally fermented sourdough is less harmful than standard or unleavened wheat products, but any starch tends to stimulate appetite by activating fat synthesis." Even the best-case wheat product retained limitations. And when asked to choose between white bread and white rice, he was direct: "In general, I think white rice is safer. Real sourdough bread, in which most of the gluten has been degraded, is probably about as safe as rice. I think nixtamalized corn is the safest."

## The Grain Hierarchy

Peat's practical grain ranking emerged clearly across interviews and emails:

1. **Nixtamalized corn (masa harina)** -- The alkaline processing destroys most anti-nutrients, increases niacin bioavailability, and breaks down problematic proteins. Peat called it "the safest."
2. **[[White rice]]** -- No gluten, minimal PUFA and phosphate after polishing. The default safe starch.
3. **Real sourdough** -- Twelve-plus hours of fermentation degrades most gluten and converts starch to sugar. Rare in commercial form.
4. **White bread** -- Gluten intact but at least the bran and germ are removed, eliminating PUFA, excess phosphate, and most phytic acid.
5. **Whole wheat** -- Every problem combined: intact gluten, rancid PUFA from the germ, phytic acid from the bran, excess phosphate, and the full suite of seed defense chemicals.

> "Can white bread cause intestinal irritation and endotoxins? Yes, white bread is usually artificially leavened, and so contains unmodified starch and gluten."
> -- Ray Peat, email exchange

Even white bread was not safe -- but whole wheat was worse by every measure Peat applied.

## Better Carbohydrate Sources

For someone looking to replace wheat products entirely, Peat's framework offers a clear path. [[Fruit]] and [[fruit juice]] are the preferred carbohydrate sources: their sugars absorb quickly in the upper intestine, provide the metabolic advantages of [[fructose]], and leave minimal residue for bacterial fermentation that produces endotoxin. [[Honey]] and white [[sugar]] serve similar functions. Well-cooked [[potatoes]] with [[butter]] provide a starchy option that avoids gluten, offers higher-quality [[protein]] than any grain, and responds well to thorough cooking. [[White rice]] with butter is an acceptable fallback for those who need a grain-based staple.

The breakfast toast, the sandwich bread, the pasta -- these are the cultural forms through which wheat enters the diet. Replacing them requires not just different foods but a different understanding of carbohydrates, one where simple sugars are metabolically superior and "complex carbohydrates" in their whole-grain form represent the most problematic choice available.
