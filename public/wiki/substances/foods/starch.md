# Starch

If [[estrogen]] is the central hormonal villain in Ray Peat's framework and [[polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]] the central lipid villain, starch is the carbohydrate that carries most of his dietary criticism. For half a century, American nutrition policy has rested on the idea that "complex carbohydrates" are the foundation of a healthy diet: whole grains, beans, brown rice, whole wheat, oats. Peat spent decades arguing that this consensus is not merely mistaken but inverted. In his reading, starch stimulates more [[insulin]] than sugar, drives [[endotoxin]] production by feeding the wrong intestinal bacteria, physically embolizes capillaries through a phenomenon called persorption, and has been promoted by an industry alliance between dietetics associations and breakfast cereal manufacturers. "Judging by present and past statements of the American Dietetic Association," Peat wrote in his newsletter [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context"]], "I think some kind of institutional brain defect might account for their recommendations." Of all the mainstream nutritional dogmas Peat attacked, the "healthy whole grain" has no defenders left in his work.

## The Glycemic Index Inversion

The modern case against starch begins, in Peat's telling, with a British physician named David Jenkins. In the 1970s, American dietitians were instructing diabetics to eat more starch and less sugar on the assumption that starches were "slowly absorbed" and therefore gentler on blood glucose. Jenkins set out to measure this assumption in 1981 and found that it was wrong. He "did a series of measurements that showed that starch is more 'glycemic' than sucrose," Peat recounted in the same newsletter. The resulting table became the glycemic index: ranked by blood-glucose response, white bread, rice, and potatoes sat near the top, alongside pure glucose, while [[fructose]] and [[sucrose]] sat far below.

The speed of starch digestion had been obvious to physiologists decades earlier. Peat described his own first encounter with the evidence in a 1970s physiology lab: a rat was tube-fed ten grams of corn-starch paste and anesthetized; ten minutes later, no trace of the starch could be found anywhere along the alimentary canal. The point, Peat wrote, was "to impress us with the rapidity with which starch is digested and absorbed. The very rapid rise of blood sugar stimulates massive release of insulin, and rapidly converts much of the carbohydrate into fat." The "slow release" story was a fiction from the beginning.

Sarah Murray, Peat's longtime interviewer on KMUD, stated the resulting inversion plainly in a [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|2011 episode on sugar myths]]: "The glycemic indexes of some potatoes and rice are almost double that of white sugar. Fructose has a lower glycemic index than sucrose." The whole grain cereal the diabetic is told to eat for breakfast spikes blood sugar harder than the orange juice the diabetic is told to avoid.

## Why Starch Fattens More Than Sugar

The metabolic reason starch is more fattening than sugar runs through [[insulin]] and [[fructose]]. Starch is pure glucose in chain form. When it hits amylase in the small intestine, it converts to glucose so rapidly that blood sugar rises sharply, insulin is secreted in a bolus, and the excess glucose is converted to fat. Sucrose, by contrast, is half glucose and half fructose. The fructose half accelerates the cellular uptake of the glucose half (the [[randle-cycle]] bypass addressed in detail in [[fructose]]) while simultaneously moderating the insulin response.

Peat laid out the consequence in the same newsletter: "Starch and glucose efficiently stimulate insulin secretion, and that accelerates the disposition of glucose, activating its conversion to glycogen and fat, as well as its oxidation. Fructose inhibits the stimulation of insulin by glucose, so this means that eating ordinary sugar, sucrose, in place of starch, will reduce the tendency to store fat. Eating 'complex carbohydrates,' rather than sugars, is a reasonable way to promote obesity."

The appetite effect compounds the insulin effect. The insulin spike drives blood sugar down, and reactive hypoglycemia triggers [[cortisol]] and further hunger. "Eating starch, by increasing insulin and lowering the blood sugar, stimulates the appetite, causing a person to eat more, so the effect on fat production becomes much larger than when equal amounts of sugar and starch are eaten." A diet built on pasta and bread, Peat argued, engineers its own overconsumption. In [[sugar-issues|"Sugar Issues"]] he noted that "many studies have found that sucrose is less fattening than starch or glucose."

The epidemiology, by Peat's reading, matched the mechanism. "After decades of 'education' to promote eating starchy foods, obesity is a bigger problem than ever, and more people are dying of diabetes than previously. The epidemiology would appear to suggest that complex carbohydrates cause diabetes, heart disease, and cancer."

## Persorption: The Volkheimer Discovery

Peat's most dramatic argument against starch is also his most obscure. In the 1960s, a German immunologist named Gerhard Volkheimer rediscovered a century-old phenomenon called **persorption**: the passage of intact starch grains directly through the intestinal lining into the bloodstream. Peat encountered Volkheimer's work in the 1970s and returned to it repeatedly for the rest of his life.

The phenomenon is not subtle. Starch grains are the size of cells, ranging from roughly 5 microns for rice to 100 microns for potato. As Peat explained in a [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 episode on sugar]], Volkheimer found that "with a chronic diet of frequent starch feedings, he could demonstrate particles of starch in their bloodstream just minutes after they ate it, and in all of their body fluids about an hour later. He found that the mice were prematurely aged. When he examined their organs, he found little nests of dead cells where one of these fairly large grains of starch was stuck in an arteriole, killing all of the cells downstream from that plugged-up point." The human demonstration was equally striking: Volkheimer tested it on his medical students and found starch grains in their veins within 30 minutes, and in the urine, bile, and cerebrospinal fluid within the hour.

In a [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|2014 diabetes episode]], a caller asked Peat to elaborate. "Particles as big as 100 microns," Peat told him, "potato starch particles, for example, can pass through. Red blood cells are flexible, but starch particles will plug up an arteriole and shut down circulation to a whole block of capillaries fed by that arteriole." The result, in Volkheimer's autopsies, was "a shotgun working internally, killing little pockets of cells."

Peat noted in his newsletter [[food-junk-and-some-mystery-ailments-fatigue-alzheimers-colitis-immunodeficiency|"Food, Junk, and Some Mystery Ailments"]] that absorbed starch does more than block capillaries. "The polysaccharide components of connective tissue constitute a major part of our regulatory system for maintaining differentiated cell functioning, and absorbed starches act as 'false signals,' with a great capacity for deranging cellular functioning." A foreign polysaccharide floating through the tissues is a chemical intruder in a system that uses sugars as signaling molecules.

Peat tested one mitigation in Mexico in 1979. He had his students eat only atole, tamales, and tortillas made from alkali-treated corn. "None of the students could find any starch grains after centrifuging their blood and urine. That led me to substitute those foods whenever possible for other starches." Nixtamalization, by pre-digesting the grain structure, eliminated the persorption signature.

## Starch and Endotoxin: Feeding the Wrong Bacteria

The second major pathway of starch toxicity runs through the gut bacteria. A healthy, high-functioning digestive system, Peat argued, keeps the small intestine nearly sterile. "The small intestine is best when it's cleanest," he told listeners in a [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 discussion of food]]. "Very vigorous people with good digestion and high thyroid function have almost a sterile small intestine from their mouth to their appendix. In a healthy person, the bacteria grow just in the colon." What breaks this arrangement is food the small intestine cannot digest quickly enough. "Indigestible fibrous materials and types of starch that can't be broken down by animal or human enzymes become good food for bacteria," Peat explained in a [[2010-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endotoxins|2010 endotoxins episode]].

The bacteria do not merely multiply; they manufacture [[endotoxin]]. Peat's chemical definition in the same episode is worth noting: endotoxin, or lipopolysaccharide, is "a combination of a starch-like molecule with some fatty acids attached to it." Starch feeds the bacteria that make more starch, decorated with inflammatory lipids. The feedback loop runs through [[serotonin]] and inflammation. As Peat noted in a [[2011-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-endotoxins-stress|2011 episode on serotonin and endotoxin]], "Practically all of the fibers used as food additives--carrageenan, guar gum, oat bran, and even psyllium--have been identified as carcinogens for the intestine. The fermenting bacteria are known to increase serotonin, lactic acid production, and endotoxin. It becomes a vicious circle of starch, serotonin, and endotoxin."

Peat was particularly caustic about the "fiber fad" that began with Dennis Burkitt in the 1970s. Burkitt had observed that rural Africans had low rates of bowel and liver cancer and three bowel movements per day, but "he was talking primarily about potatoes. When he came to the US, people interpreted it as oat fiber and grain fibers. Research outside the US showed that those actually *increase* cancer incidence." The "prebiotic" industry that grew out of it--selling resistant starches, inulin, psyllium, carrageenan-thickened products--was, in Peat's reading, selling bacterial food dressed up as health food. "The industry has promoted the idea that 'resistant starches' are health foods because they feed bacteria to produce butyric acid, supposedly preventing bowel cancer. But in fact, absorbing these broken-down products causes changes in the brain and nervous system."

The inflammation loop closes at the intestinal barrier itself. A starch-damaged intestine leaks more persorbed particles and more bacterial toxins into the bloodstream, which drive further inflammation, which further weakens the barrier. This is the mechanism Peat placed upstream of a long list of conditions: chronic fatigue, autoimmune disease, Alzheimer's, colitis, immunodeficiency.

## Potatoes: The Exception

Potatoes occupy a strange place in Peat's writing. He criticized starch relentlessly, and the potato has one of the largest starch grains known (up to 100 microns, the worst-case persorption scenario). Yet he repeatedly described potatoes as "almost unique" and recommended them over beans, grains, and rice. The apparent contradiction dissolves when the potato is unpacked into its components.

The first reason is protein quality. Peat cited Norman Pirie's comparative work: "He rated rice at about 6% value compared to egg yolk at 100%. The protein in potato was about 105%--slightly better than egg yolk. So it is roughly 15 or 20 times better than rice or bean protein." The second reason is the keto-acid content of the potato juice. "The liquid part of the potato contains ketoacids, which are the equivalent of amino acids," Peat told interviewers in a [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|2011 conversation on glycemia, starch, and sugar]]. "These can be used as fuel by the brain and heart as a substitute for sugar. They are an ideal anti-stress fuel." He described a protocol of centrifuging potato juice to discard the starch, then cooking the juice until it coagulates like scrambled eggs: "I have seen cases where juicing potatoes provided recovery for people who were dying. It provides energy and essential amino acids without stimulating insulin or cortisol." The potato, stripped of its starch, is closer to a high-quality protein drink than a carbohydrate.

For the whole potato, the conditions for safety are specific. It must be well-cooked (Peat frequently recommended boiling for 45 minutes to an hour) so that the starch is fully broken down and the persorbable grains are destroyed. It should be paired with saturated fat, because "the butter's saturated fats will suppress bacterial growth." And it is best eaten new, before maximum starch formation: "Young turnips and potatoes that haven't maximized starch production, eaten with butter after being thoroughly cooked, are pretty safe." Peat also recommended peeling, both to reduce the solanine alkaloids concentrated under the skin and to reduce the fibrous material that reaches the colon intact.

Peat's email correspondence summarized the economic logic bluntly: "When a person has limited money for food, potatoes are a better staple than beans or oats." The relevant comparison is not potato versus orange juice but potato versus the cheap starches dietitians actually recommend. Within that category, potatoes win on protein, on mineral density, and on the availability of the juice as a fallback for people with compromised digestion.

## Rice, Oats, Wheat, and Beans: The Lesser and Greater Evils

Among grains, Peat treated white rice as the lesser evil and whole wheat, oats, and beans as the greater ones. The rice distinction turns partly on grain size. "Rice starch grains are down around 10 microns," Peat noted in a [[2015-05-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-sb-277-degradation-of-the-food-supply|2015 episode on food degradation]], compared to potato's 100 microns. A smaller particle is less likely to embolize an arteriole. The distinction also turns on why rice was refined in the first place. "The germs do contain some valuable nutrients, but they also contain enough of the polyunsaturated fats that they go rancid," Peat told Sarah Murray in the 2010 sugar episode. "That was why refining was invented by the Chinese several thousand years ago." The "whole grain" marketing of the 1970s reversed a piece of traditional food engineering without understanding why the engineering existed.

Whole wheat and oat bran fail both tests simultaneously. They retain the PUFA-rich germ, which oxidizes and generates [[lipid-peroxidation|lipid peroxidation]] products. They retain the phytate-bound minerals and fibrous hulls that feed intestinal bacteria. And they deliver phosphate in a form that amplifies bacterial fermentation: "Phosphate, which you get in grains, seeds, and nuts, stimulates the breakdown of these fibers by bacteria. Calcium blocks that phospholysis of the fibers. So a high calcium, low phosphate diet goes with saturated fats in suppressing the toxic effects of starches." The grain carries its own bacterial accelerant.

Beans were, in Peat's view, worse still: a combination of resistant starch, plant defensive toxins, high phosphate, and inadequate protein. The Mexican bean-and-rice combination, culturally valorized as a "complete protein," struck him as a nutritional workaround for poverty. "When I was in Mexico, I saw nutritionists trying to justify a bean and rice-based diet. It turns out that potatoes are infinitely better than beans as a source of protein, but that requires your intestine to be in fairly good health."

Bananas occupied a similar borderline category. "Bananas are almost all starch unless they are ripened to the point of being a translucent amber mush," Peat told listeners in the 2011 EastWest Healing episode. Before the starch has fully converted to sugar, the banana is a starch source with the same metabolic liabilities as any other.

## Traditional Preparations: Nixtamalization and Sourdough

Traditional cultures did not eat raw starches, and they did not eat industrial fast-rise bread. They nixtamalized corn, fermented sourdough for eight to ten hours, sprouted grains until the starch began converting to sugar. Peat noted the loss of these practices with dismay. "Even in my parents' and grandparents' generation, bread making was different. Letting it rise for 8 or 10 hours, the slow leavening process activated enzymes that broke down the starches into sugar and broke down the gluten. Since about 1940, the bread industry discovered they could make instant bread by adding chemicals to blow it up."

Nixtamalization--boiling corn in lye or lime--was Peat's favorite example of a traditional process that disarms starch. In a [[2020-02-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-menopause-estrogen-thyroid-coronavirus-and-glaucoma|2020 One Radio Network interview]], he noted that "nixtamalized corn--used in hominy, tamales, and tortillas--is very rich in calcium and niacin and is easy to digest. It is not like other grain starches." Masa is, in Peat's taxonomy, a different food from cornmeal.

## Cooked Starch with Butter: The Safety Conditions

For people who are going to eat starch anyway, Peat described a consistent set of conditions under which it becomes less dangerous. He was explicit that these conditions reduce harm rather than confer benefit. "When starch is well cooked, and eaten with some fat and the essential nutrients, it's safe, except that it's more likely than sugar to produce fat, and isn't as effective for mineral balance," he wrote in his email correspondence. The ceiling is safety, not therapy.

The first condition is thorough cooking, which breaks down the crystalline structure of the starch granule and reduces both persorbable particles and bacterial fermentation substrate. The second is pairing with saturated fat: butter, coconut oil, cream. "If you think of soap and its antibacterial effect, saturated fats are mildly antiseptic." Peat also warned that if starch is eaten with [[polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]] instead of saturated fat, the combination is worse than either alone: "If starch or glucose is eaten at the same time as polyunsaturated fats, which inhibit its oxidation, it will produce more fat."

The third condition is a fast transit time, which "usually goes with an abundance of digestive secretions, keeping the small intestine free of bacteria. Fiber, good thyroid function, and antiseptic foods such as cooked mushrooms, bamboo shoots, and raw carrots help." A healthy [[thyroid]], in Peat's integrated picture, is partly what protects the person from their own starch consumption. The fourth is adequate calcium and low phosphate elsewhere in the diet, which suppresses the phospholysis that amplifies bacterial fermentation. The calcium in dairy does this work directly, which is one reason Peat recommended [[milk]] and cheese alongside any starch consumption. Adequate sugar also protects: "Once you are poisoned by the endotoxin, the sugars protect by decreasing the inflammation reaction to them." Fruit sugar before bed, after a starchy dinner, was a practical intervention he recommended repeatedly.

The final condition is the state of the intestine itself. "If your digestive system is very healthy, you can get along with starch instead of sugar," Peat noted in a [[2022-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2022-kmud|2022 KMUD episode]] near the end of his life. "But many starches are hard to digest. For someone with sluggish digestion, starches tend to support bacterial overgrowth in the intestine. A low-starch fruit, such as citrus, can be a perfect source of carbohydrates." For the large and growing population of people with compromised digestion, bacterial overgrowth, low thyroid, or inflammation, starch advice that works for a vigorous farmhand becomes harmful.

## Starch, Bone Health, and Lactic Acid

One of the more surprising experimental results Peat cited involves bone. In a vitamin D deficient environment, the starch diet produced rickets; the sucrose diet, equally vitamin D deficient, did not. "An older experiment compared two groups with an otherwise well balanced diet, lacking vitamin D, containing either 68% starch or 68% sucrose. The rats on the vitamin D deficient starch diet had very low levels of calcium in their blood, and the calcium content of their bones was low. However, the rats on the sucrose diet, also vitamin D deficient, had normal levels of calcium in their blood. The sucrose, unlike the starch, maintained calcium homeostasis."

The mechanism Peat proposed runs through [[carbon-dioxide]] and [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]. "The first bone material laid down is calcium carbonate. In vitro experiments showed that you can have an acidic condition as long as it's based on carbon dioxide and have strong bones. The same amount of lactic acid pH will cause dissolution of the bones. Starch tends to produce a shift towards lactic acid rather than carbon dioxide." The argument generalizes beyond bone. Any metabolic process that depends on CO2 production--vasodilation, oxygen delivery via the Bohr effect, oxidative phosphorylation itself--is degraded when glucose is metabolized to lactate rather than fully oxidized. Starch, by spiking insulin and accelerating glycolysis faster than the mitochondria can keep up, tilts the whole system toward the reductive, lactate-producing mode Peat associated with cancer, stress, and aging.

## The Policy and Industry Story

Peat was unusually direct about the political economy behind starch promotion. Starch is cheap, shelf-stable, and the residue of industrial grain surpluses subsidized since the 1930s. "Starting in the 1930s, the US government designed and taught dietitians to promote three levels of nutrition: to simply prevent starvation, emphasizing beans and cereals. A middle-class, more health-oriented diet. The rich person's diet with plenty of animal protein. Originally, it was valuable to hungry people to know how to get by on a few cents a day for beans and grains. But for economic reasons, the breakfast cereal industry subsidized nutrition journals and taught dietitians to say that these things are healthful. The foods that were originally promoted just to survive are now called health foods."

Peat saw the dietetic associations as industry-captured: "The Dietetic Association's association with General Mills, the breakfast cereal empire, (and Kellogg, Nabisco, and many other food industry giants) might have something to do with their starchy opinions. Starch-grain embolisms can cause brain damage, but major money can also make people say stupid things."

## The Practical Summary

Peat's recommendation, distilled from decades of interviews and newsletters, is simple to state and difficult to follow in a grain-dominated food culture. Replace starch with fruit wherever possible. Where starch must be eaten, prefer well-cooked peeled potatoes to grains, prefer white rice to whole wheat, prefer masa or sourdough to industrial bread, and prefer any starch eaten with butter and adequate calcium to the same starch eaten alone. Avoid beans, oat bran, resistant starch supplements, and the entire category of "prebiotic fibers" designed to feed gut bacteria. Eat sugar to buffer against the endotoxin load whenever starch is unavoidable. Every one of these concessions is a harm reduction strategy, not a recommendation.

"There isn't anything wrong with a high carbohydrate diet, and even a high starch diet isn't necessarily incompatible with good health," Peat wrote in "Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context," "but when better foods are available they should be used instead of starches." The question Peat kept returning to was not whether a person can survive on rice and beans. The question was why, in a country with orange juice, milk, potatoes, and fruit available, anyone would choose to.
