# Cream

Heavy cream was one of Ray Peat's personal dietary staples -- a food he reached for daily in his coffee, used generously in cooking, and recommended as one of the safest concentrated sources of [[saturated-fat]] available. Where conventional nutrition saw arterial plaque in a carton, Peat saw a nutrient-dense food whose fatty acid profile had been rendered remarkably safe by the cow's digestive physiology. Cream, in his framework, belongs alongside [[butter]], [[coconut-oil]], and [[milk]] as a protective fat source that displaces the [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] responsible for most metabolic damage.

Peat made no secret of his preference: "I liked to eat butter, fat meat, ice cream, and thick cream in my coffee," he wrote in his email correspondence on diet. This was not indulgence but practice aligned with principle -- the same principle that led him to recommend saturated fats as the foundation of a protective dietary pattern.

## The Ruminant Advantage

The safety of cream depends on a biological fact that separates ruminant dairy from most other fat sources. Cows possess a rumen -- a specialized fermentation chamber where microorganisms convert the [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] in grass, weeds, and feed into saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids before they ever reach the animal's tissues. As Peat explained in a [[2022-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-june-2022-kmud|June 2022 episode]], "Assuming the mother is eating properly, human milk reflects the human diet. We don't have a rumen. The cow can eat grass, weeds, and quite a lot of polyunsaturated fats and detoxify them in the rumen."

This detoxification process is what makes dairy fat categorically different from chicken fat, pork fat, or the fat of any monogastric animal. A pig eating corn and soy will store those polyunsaturated fats directly in its tissues. A cow eating the same feed will convert much of that PUFA into [[saturated-fat]] through ruminal biohydrogenation. The result is that cream, even from conventionally fed cows, contains a far lower percentage of polyunsaturated fat than almost any other animal fat -- typically around 2-4% PUFA, compared to 10-20% in pork fat or poultry fat.

This is the metabolic reasoning behind Peat's consistent recommendation of ruminant fats. The cow has already done the detoxification work that the human liver would otherwise have to perform.

## Cream Versus Butter

Cream and butter come from the same source but differ in composition in ways that matter within Peat's framework. Butter is essentially pure butterfat -- cream that has been churned to separate the fat from the water, casein, and whey. Cream retains these components, which gives it a nutritional profile that butter alone cannot provide.

The water content of heavy cream (roughly 60% water, 36% fat) means it delivers less concentrated fat per volume than butter, but it brings along [[calcium]], casein protein, and small amounts of [[lactose]]. For someone using cream in coffee or cooking, these additional nutrients contribute to the favorable calcium-to-[[phosphate]] ratio that Peat considered essential for suppressing [[parathyroid hormone]] and preventing the soft-tissue [[calcification]] that paradoxically results from calcium deficiency.

Heavy cream (36% fat or higher) contains significantly less lactose than whole milk or half-and-half, making it suitable for people who experience digestive difficulty with milk's sugar content. The trade-off is that it provides proportionally less calcium and protein per calorie than milk itself.

## Fat-Soluble Vitamins

Cream concentrates the fat-soluble vitamins present in milk: [[vitamin A]], [[vitamin-d]], and [[vitamin K]]. These vitamins are dissolved in the butterfat and become more concentrated as the fat percentage increases. Cream from pasture-raised cows, which synthesize more vitamin A from beta-carotene in grass, tends to have a distinctly yellow color -- a visual indicator of higher carotenoid and vitamin content.

In Peat's framework, these vitamins work synergistically. Vitamin A supports [[thyroid]] function and opposes [[estrogen]]. Vitamin D regulates [[calcium]] absorption and immune function. Vitamin K directs calcium into bones and teeth rather than soft tissues. Cream delivers all three in their naturally occurring, bioavailable forms -- a significant advantage over supplements, where dosing and form can introduce their own problems.

## Practical Considerations

Peat's most common use of cream was in coffee -- a combination that provided saturated fat, calcium, and the metabolic-stimulating effects of [[caffeine]] in a single serving. The fat in cream slows the absorption of caffeine, smoothing its effects and reducing the likelihood of the hypoglycemic crash that black coffee can provoke by stimulating [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]].

For cooking, cream is more versatile than butter in applications requiring emulsification -- sauces, soups, and custards benefit from cream's natural combination of fat, protein, and water. The casein in cream acts as an emulsifier, creating stable mixtures that pure butter cannot achieve without additional processing.

When choosing commercial cream, the primary concern is additives. Ultra-pasteurized cream, while less ideal than conventionally pasteurized cream due to its higher processing temperature, remains acceptable as a saturated fat source -- the heat treatment does not alter the fatty acid profile that makes cream valuable. The additives to avoid are [[carrageenan]], polysorbate 80, and other stabilizers that Peat identified as intestinal irritants and promoters of [[inflammation]]. The ingredient list should read "cream" or at most "cream, milk" -- nothing more. Many organic brands and all heavy whipping cream sold as "pasteurized" (not ultra-pasteurized) meet this standard.

## Cream as a Calorie-Dense Protective Food

For individuals who struggle to maintain weight -- a common concern in the bioenergetic community, where increasing [[metabolic rate]] through [[thyroid]] support can outpace caloric intake -- cream provides one of the most efficient solutions. A cup of heavy cream contains roughly 800 calories, nearly all from protective saturated fat. Added to coffee, poured over fruit, or used as the base for homemade [[ice cream]], cream increases caloric density without introducing the metabolic disruptions that come from starchy or PUFA-rich calorie sources.

This calorie density is not a liability in Peat's framework. Adequate caloric intake suppresses the stress hormones -- cortisol, adrenaline, [[glucagon]] -- that the body mobilizes when energy supply falls short of demand. A person running a caloric deficit is, in Peat's terms, running on stress. Cream offers a concentrated, palatable way to close that gap with the right kind of calories.
