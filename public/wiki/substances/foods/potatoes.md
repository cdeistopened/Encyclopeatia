# Potatoes

Most nutritional authorities would never call a potato "almost a perfect food." Ray Peat did -- but with a condition so strict it amounts to a different food entirely depending on how it is prepared. A thoroughly boiled potato eaten with [[butter]] and [[salt]] is, in Peat's view, one of the most complete plant foods available: balanced [[B vitamins]], essential amino acids, and carbohydrate in proportions that few other vegetables can match. A hastily cooked or cooled potato, on the other hand, delivers [[starch]] granules that can pass intact through the intestinal wall and into the bloodstream, lodging in capillaries and accelerating aging. The distance between these two outcomes is about forty minutes of boiling time.

## Almost a Perfect Food -- With Conditions

Peat described the potato's nutritional completeness in notably generous terms. In a [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 interview on stress and trauma]], he called starch foods like potatoes "almost a perfect food if they're very well cooked because you want to break down the starch." The qualifier matters: it is not the potato itself that earns this praise but the potato after prolonged cooking has dismantled its starch architecture. Once that is accomplished, "the non-starch ingredients of a potato have almost a perfect balance of nutrients -- B vitamins, essential amino acids, and carbohydrate in the right proportion." The only additions he recommended were "salt, butter, and [[calcium]]" -- the three nutrients the potato does not adequately provide on its own.

This assessment places the potato in unusual company. Among plant foods in Peat's framework, very few receive this kind of endorsement. [[Fruit]] is preferred for its simple sugars that bypass the starch problem entirely. [[Milk]] and [[eggs]] are the gold-standard animal foods. But for someone wanting a substantial starchy food -- a category Peat generally treated with suspicion -- the well-cooked potato stands alone.

## Protein Quality That Rivals Animal Foods

One of Peat's most surprising claims about potatoes concerns their [[protein]] quality. In a [[2015-05-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-sb-277-degradation-of-the-food-supply|2015 KMUD episode]], a caller asked whether there was a difference between the starch in white rice and the starch in potatoes. Peat's answer went beyond starch to the broader nutritional picture, citing Norman Pirie's research on plant protein quality: Pirie "rated rice at about 6% value compared to egg yolk," while "potato is rated at about 40%."

A 40% rating against egg yolk -- the benchmark for biological protein value -- is remarkable for a plant food. It means that potato protein, gram for gram, is utilized by the body at nearly half the efficiency of the highest-quality animal protein. Rice, by contrast, is barely usable. This distinction matters because plant-based diets often treat all vegetable proteins as roughly equivalent, when in practice the differences are enormous. As Peat noted in a [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 episode on food]], "potatoes are infinitely better than beans as a source of protein, but that requires your intestine to be in fairly good health."

The intestinal health caveat is important. Potato protein can only be fully utilized when the gut is functioning well enough to complete digestion. For someone with [[endotoxin]]-driven intestinal [[inflammation]] or bacterial overgrowth, even the well-cooked potato may deliver less of its theoretical nutritional value. This is consistent with Peat's broader principle that [[nutrition]] is not just about what you eat but about what your body can actually extract.

## The Starch Granule Problem

The reason Peat insisted on thorough cooking -- and remained "cautious about starches" even in well-prepared forms -- is the starch granule. In a [[2020-03-16-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-what-is-a-virus-anyway|2020 interview]], he explained the biological danger clearly: "Any starch, even when well-cooked, can pass directly into your bloodstream. In mouse experiments, they showed that starch grains ranging from 5 to 100 microns could pass through the wall of the intestine into the bloodstream unless there was a lot of fat and fiber taken with it."

This is not a theoretical concern. Peat cited research from a German biologist who fed mice a high raw-starch diet and found, as Peat recounted in his [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 ELUV interview]], that "they were very prematurely aged because of the death of cells in all of their organs that were blocked by starch grains." The mechanism is mechanical: intact starch granules physically obstruct capillaries, killing the cells downstream by cutting off their blood supply. Across every organ in the body, this produces a pattern that looks like accelerated aging -- widespread, diffuse cell death from circulatory blockage.

The implications extend beyond potatoes to all starchy foods, but potatoes are the food where the stakes are clearest because of their high starch content and their ubiquity in most diets. A baked potato eaten quickly, a potato salad served cold (where the starch has retrograded back into resistant form), or french fries cooked at high temperature but for insufficient duration -- all of these may deliver starch granules that survive digestion and enter the blood.

## The Cooking Protocol: Forty Minutes of Boiling

Peat's solution was specific and non-negotiable: boil potatoes for forty to sixty minutes, and eat them with fat. In the [[2015-05-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-sb-277-degradation-of-the-food-supply|2015 KMUD episode]], he explained the dual mechanism: "If the starch is very well cooked -- like boiling it for 40 to 60 minutes -- and then eaten with fat, the fat slows down the absorption and allows the digestive enzymes to soften it up so it doesn't get through the intestine without being broken down."

The extended boiling serves the first function: it gelatinizes and breaks apart the crystalline starch structure, making the granules accessible to digestive enzymes. The fat -- ideally [[butter]] or [[coconut-oil]], the [[saturated-fat]] sources Peat recommended -- serves the second: by slowing gastric emptying and intestinal transit, it gives those enzymes time to complete their work. Without the fat, even well-cooked starch may move through the intestine too quickly for full digestion.

Young potatoes further reduce the risk. In a [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|2011 discussion on glycemia]], Peat recommended "young turnips and potatoes that haven't maximized starch production, eaten with butter after being thoroughly cooked" as "pretty safe." New potatoes, harvested before the tuber has fully matured, contain less total starch and a higher proportion of sugars -- meaning less of the problematic granular material to begin with.

## Where Potatoes Fit in the Dietary Hierarchy

In Peat's overall framework, potatoes occupy a middle tier. They are far superior to [[grains]], beans, and most other starchy plant foods -- rice is nutritionally impoverished by comparison, and beans carry their own burden of anti-nutrients and digestive irritants. But they require careful preparation that simpler foods do not. [[Fruit]], [[orange-juice]], [[honey]], and [[sugar]] provide energy without any starch risk. [[Milk]] provides superior protein and calcium. [[Eggs]] and [[liver]] provide nutrients the potato cannot.

The potato's real value emerges for people who want a substantial, filling, savory food that is not an animal product -- or who need variety beyond the fruit-and-dairy pattern that forms the core of Peat's recommendations. For that specific niche, nothing in the plant kingdom competes. The combination of high-quality protein (40% of egg value), complete B vitamin coverage, balanced amino acids, and adequate carbohydrate makes the well-prepared potato genuinely nutritious rather than merely tolerable.

> "Starch, like potatoes, is almost a perfect food if they're very well cooked because you want to break down the starch. The non-starch ingredients of a potato have almost a perfect balance of nutrients -- B vitamins, essential amino acids, and carbohydrate in the right proportion. The only things you have to add are salt, butter, and calcium."
> -- Ray Peat, [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|Effects of Stress and Trauma on the Body]] (ELUV, 2014)

The message is characteristically Peatian: the food itself is not the enemy, but the way industrial civilization prepares and consumes it usually is. A potato, properly handled, is almost perfect. Improperly handled, it accelerates aging at the capillary level. The difference is not subtle, and it is not optional.
