# Gelatin

Nutritionists have long dismissed gelatin as an "incomplete protein" because it lacks tryptophan and is low in cysteine and methionine. In Ray Peat's framework, this is precisely what makes it valuable. The amino acids gelatin lacks—tryptophan, cysteine, methionine—are the ones Peat identified as anti-metabolic, pro-inflammatory, and potentially carcinogenic in excess. The amino acids gelatin provides in abundance—glycine and proline—are protective, anti-inflammatory, and energy-supporting. Gelatin is not incomplete; it is complementary to the muscle meats that modern diets overemphasize.

## The Problem with Muscle Meat

Modern protein consumption is heavily weighted toward muscle meat—chicken breast, steak, pork loin—while traditional cultures consumed the whole animal, including skin, tendons, organs, and bones rich in collagen. This shift has skewed amino acid intake toward the problematic end of the spectrum.

As Peat explained in a [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-and-the-gut-part-1|2016 episode]], cysteine, tryptophan, and methionine "most easily promote cancer growth." In a [[2010-12-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-december-2010|2010 episode]], he cited research showing that animals restricted in methionine live 30-40% longer—a finding that has been replicated across multiple species. Gelatin, being completely free of tryptophan and very low in both cysteine and methionine, provides protein without the amino acids that accelerate [[aging]] and promote [[cancer]].

The excess tryptophan from muscle meat drives [[serotonin]] production in the gut, which Peat identified as a central mediator of [[inflammation]], intestinal problems, and depression. Excess methionine and cysteine promote homocysteine production and oxidative stress. By replacing a portion of muscle meat with gelatin, the amino acid balance shifts toward the protective profile that Peat associated with longevity and metabolic health.

## Glycine: The Key Amino Acid

Gelatin is approximately one-third glycine by weight. Peat described glycine and proline as "energy-providing and stabilizing amino acids"—substances that calm the nervous system, support liver detoxification, and promote efficient [[metabolism]].

Glycine acts as an inhibitory neurotransmitter, opposing the excitotoxic effects of excess glutamate and aspartate that Peat connected to neurodegeneration. It supports the liver's conjugation pathways, helping the body eliminate [[estrogen]], [[endotoxin]], and other toxins. And it is anti-inflammatory in a broad, systemic way—reducing the inflammatory signaling that excess tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine promote.

Proline, gelatin's second most abundant amino acid, supports collagen synthesis throughout the body—skin, joints, blood vessels, and the intestinal lining. Together, glycine and proline provide the raw materials for maintaining connective tissue integrity without the metabolic costs of the amino acids dominant in muscle meat.

## The Elderly Paradox

Peat often cited an observation that puzzled conventional nutritionists: elderly people who ate very little protein, subsisting largely on gelatin and simple carbohydrates, often maintained surprisingly good health. As he described in a [[2009-09-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2009|2009 episode]], nutritionists were confused by healthy old women eating "gelatin and toast," since the amino acids considered essential were largely absent from their diet.

The paradox dissolves in Peat's framework. After age twenty or so, the body's need for growth-promoting amino acids declines sharply, while the damage from their excess increases. As Peat explained in a [[2022-01-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-january-2022|2022 episode]], histidine, tryptophan, methionine, cysteine, aspartate, and glutamate become "potentially excitotoxic" when consumed beyond what the body can immediately use. The elderly who thrived on gelatin were inadvertently following an anti-aging amino acid profile—high in protective glycine, low in the inflammatory and excitotoxic amino acids that accelerate degeneration.

## Practical Use

Gelatin is safe in very large amounts precisely because it lacks the amino acids that become toxic in excess. Peat recommended it as a regular dietary addition—dissolved in warm liquids, used in cooking, or consumed as bone broth made from lamb shanks, chicken feet, pigs' feet, or joint bones.

The practical goal is to shift the overall amino acid ratio away from muscle meat dominance. This does not require eliminating muscle meat entirely—rather, balancing it with gelatin so that the total protein intake provides adequate glycine and proline without excessive tryptophan, methionine, and cysteine. [[Milk]], [[eggs]], and [[cheese]] already have a more favorable amino acid profile than muscle meat, and adding gelatin to a diet built around these foods further optimizes the balance.
