# Gelatin

Gelatin is the single most important protein in Ray Peat's dietary framework, not because it provides all the amino acids a body needs, but because it counterbalances the amino acids a modern diet provides in dangerous excess. In a nutritional landscape that prizes muscle meat, whey isolates, and high-protein formulas, Peat made the case that the protein profile of these foods replicates the biochemistry of extreme physiological stress, flooding the body with [[tryptophan]], [[cysteine]], and [[methionine]] while starving it of the protective amino acids [[glycine]] and [[proline]] that traditional diets provided in abundance. "Gelatin is unique in being free of the pro-aging amino acids," he stated on a [[2015-08-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-longevity-and-nootropics|2015 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on longevity]], distilling the argument to its core: the protein most people dismiss as incomplete is, for adults, the protein that corrects the imbalances driving inflammation, degeneration, and shortened lifespan. Peat devoted an entire newsletter to the subject, recommended gelatin in virtually every interview he gave, and considered it, alongside [[milk]], [[orange-juice|oranges]], and [[eggs]], one of the foundational foods of a pro-metabolic diet.

## The Tryptophan Problem: Why Muscle Meat Mimics Stress

The case for gelatin begins with the case against eating muscle meat in isolation. In his newsletter [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity" (2009)]], Peat laid out the argument in physiological terms: "When only the muscle meats are eaten, the amino acid balance entering our blood stream is the same as that produced by extreme stress, when [[cortisol]] excess causes our muscles to be broken down to provide energy and material for repair." The comparison is not metaphorical. During acute stress, cortisol breaks down skeletal muscle to liberate amino acids for emergency repair, releasing large quantities of tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine into the bloodstream. When a person eats a steak or chicken breast without any accompanying gelatin, they produce exactly this amino acid profile voluntarily.

The consequences cascade. Tryptophan is the precursor to [[serotonin]], which Peat associated with inflammation, immunodepression, fibrosis, and the generalized changes of aging. Cysteine and methionine both inhibit [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]] and suppress mitochondrial energy production. As Peat explained on a [[2009-09-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-ten-most-toxic-things-in-our-food|2009 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on food toxins]], "Muscle meats are extremely rich in tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine, and those happen to inhibit thyroid function, suppress metabolism, and promote inflammation."

The longevity research Peat cited was striking. In the same newsletter, he wrote: "Restricting only tryptophan, or only cysteine, produces a greater extension of the life span than achieved in most of the studies of caloric restriction." He then posed a question that his own dietary recommendations answered: "How great would be the life-span extension if both tryptophan and cysteine were restricted at the same time?" The answer, in practical terms, was gelatin. On a [[2019-07-18-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-milk|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on milk]], he was even more specific about methionine: "If you reduce methionine alone in an adult animal's diet, experiments found a 40% extension of lifespan."

Gelatin is the only common dietary protein that contains no tryptophan at all, and only trace amounts of cysteine, methionine, and histidine. Using it as a major protein source is the simplest way to shift the amino acid balance away from the stress-and-aging profile of pure muscle meat and toward the traditional profile that sustained human health for millennia.

## Glycine: The Anti-Serotonin

The protective case for gelatin rests on what it contains, not merely on what it lacks. Gelatin is approximately 32% glycine by amino acid units (22% by weight), with proline and alanine as the other major constituents. In Peat's framework, glycine functions as the biochemical mirror image of serotonin.

In his newsletter [[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|"Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging" (2006)]], Peat mapped the opposition systematically: "Serotonin excess produces a broad range of harmful effects: Cancer, inflammation, fibrosis, neurological damage, shock, bronchoconstriction, and hypertension. [...] The simplest, nonessential, amino acid, glycine, has been found to protect against carcinogenesis, inflammation, fibrosis, neurological damage, shock, asthma, and hypertension." Where serotonin promotes the formation of TNF (tumor necrosis factor), glycine inhibits it. Where serotonin impairs learning, glycine improves it. "In some situations," Peat noted, "glycine is known to suppress the formation of serotonin."

This opposition is not accidental. Glycine acts on a specific receptor that functions analogously to the [[GABA]] receptor, both of them stabilizing cells against excitatory stimulation. As Peat described in [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]], "GABA (gamma amino butyric acid) is a close analog of glycine (alpha amino acetic acid)." The "inhibitory" quality of glycine is not restricted to nerves. "In every type of cell," Peat wrote, "it apparently has the same kind of quieting, protective antistress action." Skin cells, nerve cells, liver cells, and immune cells are all electrically stabilized by glycine.

The connection between glycine and [[progesterone]] adds another dimension. Peat cited research showing that progesterone and its metabolites act on the glycine receptor, reinforcing its inhibitory effects. The phytoestrogen genistein from [[soy]], by contrast, antagonizes glycine's receptor. This places glycine in the broader anti-estrogenic constellation that includes progesterone, [[aspirin]], and [[thyroid]] hormone. "In the context of the excitatory actions of [[estrogen]], and the inhibitory action of glycine," Peat wrote, "it would be reasonable to think of glycine as one of the antiestrogenic substances."

## Traditional Diets: The Lost Protein Balance

A recurring theme in Peat's advocacy for gelatin was the traditional diet's natural protein balance. Collagen, the native form of gelatin in living tissue, constitutes roughly half the protein in an animal's body. When traditional cultures consumed the whole animal, including head cheese, oxtails, pigs' feet, chicken feet, and skin, they automatically ingested large amounts of glycine and proline alongside whatever tryptophan came from the muscle meat.

"People were getting about half as much tryptophan in proportion to other amino acids when they used foods such as 'head cheese,' ox-tails, and chicken feet," Peat wrote in [[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|"Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging"]]. The modern diet, by contrast, privileges the "expensive tender cuts of meat" that contain the highest concentrations of tryptophan and cysteine while discarding the very tissues that balanced them.

On a [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on natural healing]], Peat framed this in terms of life stage: "They are used for growth until you're about 20 or 25. After that, they are just needed for replacing skin. If you eat a young person's requirement of those amino acids when you're 40 or 50, they tend to promote overgrowth, inflammation, excitotoxicity of the nerves, and tumor growth." The practical upshot was that an adult's protein needs diverge sharply from a growing child's. What promotes growth in a teenager promotes degeneration in a middle-aged adult.

Peat relished the paradox. On a [[55-bioenergetic-nutrition-basics-the-ray-peat-diet-appetite-and-metabolism-with-ray-peat|*Generative Energy* episode on bioenergetic nutrition basics]], he recalled the nutritionist Adelle Davis noting that "she had seen lots of old women going for years living on a diet of toast, Jell-O, and tea, practically their only protein being gelatin." Mainstream nutrition dismissed this as inadequate. Peat saw it as proof that adults, especially elderly adults, have vanishingly small requirements for the so-called essential amino acids and can thrive on what gelatin provides.

## Anti-Inflammatory Action: From Fibrosis to Colitis

Gelatin's anti-inflammatory effects span virtually every tissue system Peat discussed. In [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]], he listed the conditions that glycine prevents or alleviates: "Fibrosis, free radical damage, inflammation, cell death from ATP depletion or calcium overload, mitochondrial damage, diabetes, etc." The breadth of this list reflects glycine's action at a fundamental cellular level rather than on any specific organ.

[[Fibrosis]] received particular attention. Peat explained that "inflammation produces fibrosis, because stress, hypoxia, and inadequate supply of glucose stimulate the fibroblasts to produce increased amounts of collagen." The seeming paradox is that gelatin, which is itself collagen, protects against the excessive collagen deposition of fibrosis. The resolution lies in the difference between structural collagen and scar tissue. Healthy collagen provides the extracellular matrix that cells need for normal differentiation. Fibrotic collagen is a stress response that stiffens and degrades tissue. Glycine opposes the inflammatory signals that drive fibrotic deposition, protecting lungs, kidneys, liver, and other organs.

The digestive system was another domain where gelatin's therapeutic history ran deep. Peat wrote that "the use of gelatin, especially in the form of a concentrated gelatinous beef broth, for colitis, dysentery, ulcers, celiac disease, and other diseases of the digestive system, goes far back in medical history." He noted that Pavlov's observation of gelatin stimulating digestive juice secretion occurred precisely because the therapeutic value of broth was already well established. A recent publication he cited showed glycine alleviating colitis, confirming what folk medicine had known for centuries.

The anti-tumor properties of glycine connect to the anti-inflammatory picture. "It has a wide range of antitumor actions," Peat wrote, "including the inhibition of new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis), and it has shown protective activity in liver [[cancer]] and melanoma." He added, with characteristic directness: "Since glycine is non-toxic (if the kidneys are working, since any amino acid will contribute to the production of ammonia), this kind of chemotherapy can be pleasant."

## Insulin, Blood Sugar, and Lipolysis

Gelatin's role in metabolic regulation operates through its effects on insulin sensitivity and fat mobilization. In [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]], Peat reported that "gelatin and glycine have recently been reported to facilitate the action of insulin in lowering blood sugar and alleviating [[diabetes]]. Gelatin has been used successfully to treat diabetes for over 100 years."

The mechanism Peat identified was glycine's suppression of [[lipolysis]], the process by which fat is released from storage into the bloodstream. In his framework, persistent lipolysis and the resulting elevation of [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] drive insulin resistance through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]: when free fatty acids flood the system, they block glucose oxidation, forcing cells to rely on inefficient fermentation. By inhibiting lipolysis, glycine removes one of the upstream causes of hyperglycemia. "Glycine inhibits lipolysis (another antiexcitatory, 'antiestrogenic' effect)," Peat wrote, "and this in itself will make insulin more effective, and help to prevent [[hypoglycemia|hyperglycemia]]." He added that "a gelatin-rich diet can also lower the serum triglycerides."

The combination of anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing effects made gelatin relevant to the entire cluster of conditions Peat associated with chronic stress: "Since persistent lipolysis and insulin resistance, along with a generalized inflammatory state, are involved in a great variety of diseases, especially in the degenerative diseases, it's reasonable to consider using glycine/gelatin for almost any chronic problem."

## Neurological Protection: Sleep, Seizures, and the Inhibitory Synapse

Glycine's role as an inhibitory neurotransmitter gave gelatin a direct pharmacological relevance to neurological conditions. "A small dose of glycine taken shortly after suffering a stroke was found to accelerate recovery," Peat wrote in his newsletter, "preventing the spreading of injury through its inhibitory and antiinflammatory actions." The nerve-stabilizing property, which increases the threshold of stimulation required to activate nerves, made glycine protective in [[epilepsy]] as well. "This effect is important in the regulation of sleep, breathing, and heart rhythm."

The sleep application was the one Peat tested most directly in his own life. In [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]], he described a personal experiment with unusual candor: "For years I hadn't slept through a whole night without waking, and I was in the habit of having some juice or a little thyroid to help me go back to sleep. The first time I had several grams of gelatin just before bedtime, I slept without interruption for about 9 hours." He shared this with friends, who reported that their relatives "had recovered from long-standing pain problems (arthritic and rheumatic and possibly neurological) in just a few days after taking 10 or 15 grams of gelatin each day."

On the *Generative Energy* episode, Peat confirmed the sleep connection: "Salt and gelatin are both very helpful for good sleep." The mechanism aligns with his broader model of nocturnal stress: during sleep, falling blood sugar triggers [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]], producing wakefulness. Gelatin's inhibitory action on nerve excitation, combined with its ability to modulate stress hormones, prevents the midnight cortisol spike that wakes people.

Other neurological applications Peat mentioned included multiple sclerosis ("Glycine's antispastic activity has been used to alleviate the muscle spasms of multiple sclerosis"), schizophrenia ("It is thought to moderate some of the symptoms of schizophrenia"), and the broader category of muscle pain and myositis: "Since excess tryptophan is known to produce muscle pain, myositis, even muscular dystrophy, gelatin is an appropriate food for helping to correct those problems, simply because of its lack of tryptophan."

## Gelatin as Historical Medicine

Before the pharmaceutical era displaced it, gelatin was a recognized medical intervention. Peat documented its clinical applications with evident satisfaction in [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]], citing The 1936 Cyclopedia of Medicine: gelatin solution was used "to quickly control nosebleeds, excessive menstrual bleeding, bleeding ulcers (using three doses of 18 grams as a 10% solution during one day), and bleeding from hemorrhoids and the lower bowel, and hemorrhage from the bladder." It had been administered orally, subcutaneously, and intravenously.

The context for this medical use was Hans Selye's research on stress-induced bleeding. "At the time Selye was observing stress-induced bleeding, standard medicine was putting gelatin to use to control bleeding," Peat wrote. The connection between stress, vascular leakage, and gelatin's therapeutic value was already known in the 1930s, then forgotten as synthetic drugs replaced traditional substances.

In his newsletter [[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|"Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer"]], Peat extended this to the modern context: "In many cases, the vascular leakage of inflammation and shock can be corrected by using osmotically active substances, such as starch solutions, gelatin, or concentrated sodium chloride." Gelatin's medical history was not folk tradition but documented clinical practice that the profession abandoned without refutation.

## Liver Protection and Intestinal Health

Gelatin's two dominant amino acids provide specific protection for the liver. In [[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|"Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer"]], Peat wrote that "both proline and glycine (which are major amino acids in gelatin) are very protective for the liver, increasing albumin, and stopping oxidative damage." The liver connection is significant because the liver is the organ most responsible for detoxifying the [[endotoxin]] that enters the bloodstream from the intestine, and because liver damage from [[PUFA]] accumulation is a central feature of Peat's model of aging.

The intestinal angle adds another layer. In his newsletter [[meat-physiology-stress-and-degenerative-physiology|"Meat Physiology, Stress, and Degenerative Physiology" (2014)]], Peat noted that "tryptophan and methionine contribute to the formation of polyamines, so gelatin, which lacks those amino acids and is soothing to the intestine, should be a regular part of the diet." Polyamines amplify the neurotoxic and carcinogenic effects of estrogen and PUFA. By limiting their formation, gelatin reduces the toxic load the liver must handle while simultaneously calming the intestinal lining that is their gateway into the body.

The practical tradition of feeding gelatinous broths to the sick connects to both the liver and the gut. As Peat told Patrick Timpone on the [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 *One Radio Network* episode]]: "When a person is sick, giving them a gelatinous chicken soup is therapeutic."

## Serotonin and Dopamine: The Neurotransmitter Shift

Gelatin's effects on neurotransmitter balance extend beyond simply reducing serotonin precursors. On a [[2019-10-15-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-a-plethora-of-wide-randing-questions|2019 *One Radio Network* episode]], Peat made a claim that connected gelatin to the other side of the neurotransmitter equation: "Gelatin contains no tryptophan, which is the source for serotonin. It also has the precursor for dopamine."

The anti-serotonin effect is straightforward: no tryptophan means no new serotonin synthesis from that protein source. On the same show, Timpone asked whether tryptophan was needed for sleep. Peat's response inverted the mainstream assumption: "No, not really. Like histamine, too much serotonin keeps you awake." The popular association of tryptophan with sleepiness (the Thanksgiving turkey myth) directly contradicted Peat's reading of the literature, where elevated serotonin promotes excitation and inflammation rather than restful sedation.

The dopaminergic dimension of gelatin is less thoroughly documented in Peat's public statements but consistent with his broader framework, in which dopamine represents the healthy, energy-producing side of nervous system function while serotonin represents the stressed, degenerative side. A protein that suppresses serotonin while supporting dopamine precursors aligns precisely with what Peat considered a pro-metabolic food.

## Thyroid and Protein Tolerance

One of Peat's most practically important observations about gelatin concerned its interaction with thyroid function. On the [[55-bioenergetic-nutrition-basics-the-ray-peat-diet-appetite-and-metabolism-with-ray-peat|*Generative Energy* episode]], he recounted an experiment by Broda Barnes: "Broda Barnes experimented eating a lot more meat in his diet. He found he had to double his thyroid dose. It's very easy to overdo the protein, displacing carbohydrate and liberating the thyroid-suppressing tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine."

The practical implication was that gelatin allows a higher total protein intake without the thyroid-suppressing consequences of muscle meat. "Experiments using natural gelatin as a protein, lacking those antithyroid amino acids, showed they could tolerate a fairly higher dose of protein." For anyone supplementing thyroid hormone or trying to support their metabolic rate through diet, this is a critical distinction. A hundred grams of protein from chicken breast has a different metabolic effect than the same amount split between chicken and gelatin, because the amino acid profile of the mixture determines whether the thyroid is supported or suppressed.

When Georgi Dinkov asked about an ideal ratio of gelatin to other protein, Peat's answer reflected his characteristic respect for individual variation: "I think that changes with age and activity level. A young person who is growing at a very high metabolic rate needs more of the methionine, cysteine, tryptophan type protein." The trajectory across the lifespan is clear: gelatin should constitute an increasing proportion of total protein as a person ages and growth requirements diminish.

Danny Roddy asked how someone could gauge if they were becoming too deficient in tryptophan. Peat's answer was practical and specific: "Digestion is usually the first thing to go wrong. They'll start getting gas or some digestive symptom, which could be that they're not replacing the lining of the intestine fast enough."

## Mead Acid and the Connective Tissue Matrix

Peat pointed to an additional protective factor in whole-animal gelatin sources that pure gelatin powder lacks. In [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]], he noted that "some types of cell damage are prevented almost as well by alanine and proline as by glycine, so the use of gelatin, rather than glycine, is preferable, especially when the gelatin is associated with its normal biochemicals. For example, skin is a rich source of steroid hormones, and cartilage contains 'Mead acid,' which is itself antiinflammatory."

[[Mead acid]] (n-9 eicosatrienoic acid) occupies a special place in Peat's model as the body's endogenous anti-inflammatory lipid, suppressed by the accumulation of PUFA over a lifetime. The fact that cartilage naturally contains Mead acid means that traditional gelatin-rich foods, consumed as oxtail or joint-bone soups, deliver not just the protective amino acids but also the anti-inflammatory lipids that complement them. This is one of several reasons Peat preferred natural gelatin sources over pure glycine supplements, which he considered useful as a drug but warned "shouldn't be thought of as a food, because manufactured products are always likely to contain peculiar contaminants."

## Practical Use: Sources, Dosage, and Preparation

### Food Sources

Peat's preferred gelatin sources were traditional cuts: oxtail, lamb shanks, lamb shoulder chops, pigs' feet and ears, chicken feet, chicken backs and wings, and prepared pig skin. "Traditional diets made very efficient use of things like chicken feet, pigs' ears and tails, beef and pork skin, and so on," he said on the 2009 KMUD episode. "It is really very pleasant and nutritious food."

In his [[45e596|email exchanges on gelatin]], he described his personal cooking practice: "I usually cook the ox-tail with just enough water to cover it, for about four hours, until the meat comes off the bone easily. It makes a very concentrated gelatin solution." For broth cooking time, he specified: "It's mostly for the attached cartilage, ligaments, and tendons, and most of the gelatin is released in 3 or 4 hours. Excess cooking oxidizes nutrients, especially if there's marrow in the bone."

He described his regular diet on a [[3ac8c3|2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on food]]: "Almost every day, some kind of gelatinous soup, like kale soup or chicken soup with all the fat skimmed off." The fat-skimming practice was deliberate. In his emails, he explained that pork skin in particular contains "a lot of fat, which in pork is highly unsaturated," and described his workaround: "I re-heat mine in coconut oil and then drain them well, to reduce the amount of polyunsaturated fat."

He issued one practical warning about chickens: "In the US, chickens are fed arsenic to make them grow faster, and it concentrates in the bones; you should find out what the chicken feeding practice is in your area."

### Commercial Gelatin

For powdered gelatin, Peat recommended Great Lakes Gelatin as his primary brand, specifying the green-label (hydrolyzed, more soluble) version. He also mentioned Gelatin Innovations as an economical alternative. On the 2019 One Radio Network episode, Timpone asked about Great Lakes specifically, and Peat confirmed: "That's a good one." He also noted that "USP gelatin sold for animals is the same as used for people, except that it doesn't contain the toxic additives of the tablets."

He distinguished gelatin from the marketing trend of "collagen" supplements: "A lot of people are selling gelatin calling it collagen. Collagen is the raw form in your tissues; to buy actual collagen is very expensive."

### Dosage

Peat's dosage recommendations scaled from modest supplementation to therapeutic quantities:

- **With meals:** 5 to 10 grams with a large serving of meat to balance amino acids. "If a half-pound of steak is eaten, it would probably be reasonable to have about 20 grams of gelatin at approximately the same time."
- **Daily general use:** "Some people feel better with a tablespoon or two of gelatin with their regular foods." On the One Radio Network, he stated: "30 grams a day is fine."
- **Therapeutic quantities:** For muscular dystrophy and myasthenia gravis, "30 grams of glycine was commonly used," which corresponds to roughly 90 to 100 grams of gelatin. "A daily intake of 100 grams of gelatin wouldn't seem unreasonable, and some people find that quantities in that range help to decrease fatigue."
- **Bedtime:** Several grams before bed for sleep support.

### Preparation

Peat was emphatic that gelatin must be fully dissolved before eating. On an [[672bcb|*EastWest Healing* Q&A episode]], he explained: "If you eat it undissolved, it feeds bacteria. It must be dissolved." For preparation methods, he suggested mixing it with sugar and heating it until clear, then adding orange juice to make gummy candies, or incorporating it into "mousses or marshmallows." In his newsletter, he listed custards, mousses, ice cream, soups, sauces, cheesecake, pies, and fruit juice desserts as vehicles for dissolving gelatin into the diet.

He also addressed fluoride concerns in his emails: "The only analysis of gelatin that I have seen showed very little fluoride. Since most of the fluoride in an animal is concentrated in the bones, and gelatin is made from skin, it probably doesn't contain much."

## Protein Powders: Why Gelatin Is the Exception

Peat was broadly skeptical of protein powders, but gelatin was the exception. On the [[2019-07-18-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-milk|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on milk]], he explained: "Powdered proteins contain two or three amino acids that are very unstable. When they break down, they can produce inflammatory toxins. Cysteine and tryptophan are the worst for breaking down during processing. The only protein that doesn't have these unstable amino acids is gelatin."

This observation extends to whey protein, which Peat noted "has been promoted as a protein supplement, but it contains a slightly higher proportion of tryptophan than milk does." [[Cheese]], by contrast, has the whey removed and therefore contains less tryptophan. The hierarchy of protein safety in Peat's framework runs: gelatin (safest), cheese and milk, eggs, and then muscle meats (most problematic when consumed without gelatin to balance them).
