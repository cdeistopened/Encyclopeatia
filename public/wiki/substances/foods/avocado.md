# Avocado

The avocado has become the unofficial mascot of contemporary health food culture -- smashed on toast, blended into smoothies, sliced onto grain bowls, and invoked with near-religious reverence in every "healthy fats" discussion. Ray Peat did not spend significant interview time on avocados specifically, which itself reveals something: they were not on his radar as either a recommended food or a food dangerous enough to single out. When asked in email exchanges to list his recommended fruits, he named "corossol, lychee, longan, guava, papaya, pawpaw, sapota, guanabana" -- tropical fruits rich in sugars and protective compounds, with minimal fat content. Avocado was absent from that list, and its absence tells the story. In Peat's framework, the avocado occupies a gray zone -- not as destructive as [[seed-oils]] or [[nuts]], but far from the protective [[saturated-fat]] sources he championed. The fat profile is the problem: avocado fat is roughly 71% monounsaturated (primarily oleic acid), 13% [[polyunsaturated-fats|polyunsaturated]], and only 16% [[saturated-fat]]. That 13% polyunsaturated fraction, in a food people eat in large quantities, adds up to roughly two grams of [[linoleic acid]] per fruit -- not trivial in a framework where PUFA avoidance is paramount.

## The Body Already Makes Its Own Oleic Acid

The central marketing claim for avocado is that it provides "healthy monounsaturated fats," as though the body were waiting helplessly for a dietary source of oleic acid. Peat dismantled this premise by explaining that the body manufactures oleic acid on its own whenever carbohydrate intake is adequate. In a [[2022-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2022-kmud|November 2022 KMUD]] episode, when asked about monounsaturated fats like those in avocado versus PUFAs versus saturated fats, he laid out the metabolic logic: "When we eat carbohydrates in excess, we make our own saturated fats. If we are free of polyunsaturated fats, we can modify a certain percentage of our saturated fats. We can unsaturate them midway down the fatty acid molecule at carbon number nine." That carbon-nine desaturation produces oleic acid -- the same monounsaturated fat the avocado is famous for delivering.

This biochemical fact reframes the entire avocado conversation. The body does not need dietary oleic acid. Given sufficient carbohydrate and freedom from PUFA interference, the liver synthesizes exactly as much monounsaturated fat as the body requires, and it does so from the saturated fatty acids it also synthesizes. The avocado is offering what the body can already make for itself, while simultaneously delivering polyunsaturated contaminants the body cannot safely process.

## Omega-9 Fats and the Mead Acid Pathway

Peat did not merely tolerate monounsaturated fats -- he identified a specific pathway by which the body converts its self-made oleic acid into something actively beneficial. In a [[2019-01-24-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fats-and-questions|2019 One Radio Network episode on fats]], he explained: "If we eat only sugar for calories, we synthesize monounsaturated fat -- oleic acid, the main fat of olive oil. From oleic acid, we can make Mead acid. These are Omega-9 polyunsaturated fats. An Australian researcher found that Omega-9 fats are powerfully anti-inflammatory and don't break down or oxidize easily."

[[Mead acid]] is the body's native polyunsaturated fat -- produced endogenously from oleic acid only when the omega-6 and omega-3 pathways are not dominant. It is anti-inflammatory, resistant to oxidation, and a marker of a metabolic state Peat considered ideal: one in which the body is running on its own lipid chemistry rather than being overwhelmed by dietary PUFA. The irony is that eating avocado for its oleic acid actually undermines the Mead acid pathway, because the linoleic acid that accompanies avocado's oleic acid competes for the same desaturase enzymes and suppresses Mead acid production. The food marketed as a source of "good fats" contains just enough bad fat to interfere with the body's best fat.

## The PUFA Contamination Problem

A medium avocado delivers roughly 3 to 4 grams of polyunsaturated fat, of which approximately 2 grams are [[linoleic acid]] -- the omega-6 PUFA that Peat identified as one of the most metabolically destructive substances in the modern diet. This quantity, consumed daily as part of the avocado-toast lifestyle, contributes meaningfully to the body's total [[PUFA]] burden. Those polyunsaturated fats incorporate into cell membranes, accumulate in tissues, and persist for extended periods. Over months and years of regular avocado consumption, the tissue-level PUFA concentration rises, and with it the downstream effects Peat associated with polyunsaturated fat accumulation: suppressed [[oxidative metabolism]], increased [[lipid-peroxidation]], and a hormonal environment tilted toward [[estrogen]] dominance.

The comparison that matters is not avocado versus canola oil but avocado versus [[butter]] and [[coconut-oil]]. Butter is approximately 63% saturated fat and 4% polyunsaturated. Coconut oil is over 90% saturated and under 2% polyunsaturated. These are the fats Peat recommended because their saturated structure resists oxidation, supports mitochondrial function, and does not accumulate as the kind of reactive, peroxidation-prone material that PUFA becomes in tissues. Measured against this standard, avocado is a dilute delivery vehicle for the wrong kind of fat, bundled with a monounsaturated fat the body already knows how to make.

## Avocado Oil for Cooking

Avocado oil has gained popularity as a high-heat cooking oil, with a smoke point around 480 degrees Fahrenheit. From a purely practical standpoint, its high smoke point and predominantly monounsaturated composition make it more stable under heat than most seed oils. But the same concerns apply: roughly 13% of avocado oil is polyunsaturated, and high-temperature cooking can accelerate the oxidation of that fraction, generating the lipid peroxidation products -- aldehydes, [[malondialdehyde]], 4-hydroxynonenal -- that Peat identified as directly toxic to cells.

Coconut oil and butter remain the superior cooking fats in Peat's framework. Coconut oil's near-total saturation makes it essentially impervious to heat-induced oxidation. Butter, while it has a lower smoke point, provides the [[vitamin A]], butyric acid, and conjugated linoleic acid that make it nutritionally active rather than merely thermally stable. Avocado oil offers thermal stability without nutritional benefit -- a cooking medium, not a health food.

## The "Healthy Fat" Framework Peat Rejected

Much of the avocado's cultural prestige rests on the "healthy fat" narrative -- the idea, derived from the lipid hypothesis and Mediterranean diet research, that monounsaturated fats are cardioprotective and that replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat reduces heart disease risk. Peat rejected this narrative at its root. He argued that [[saturated-fat]] is the protective fat, that [[cholesterol]] is a vital structural and hormonal molecule, and that the dietary advice to replace butter with vegetable fats was based on flawed science that confused correlation with causation.

Within this inversion, the avocado's monounsaturated fat profile is neither a strength nor a clear danger -- it is simply not what Peat was looking for in a dietary fat source. The marketing positions avocado as if its fat were actively therapeutic. In Peat's framework, it is at best redundant and at worst a quiet contributor to the polyunsaturated fat burden that accumulates over a lifetime.

> The avocado's reputation as a health food rests on a framework Peat rejected: that unsaturated fats are protective and saturated fats are dangerous. Reverse those premises, and the avocado becomes an overrated source of the wrong kind of fat -- delivering oleic acid the body already makes while contaminating tissues with the linoleic acid it cannot safely handle.

## Where Avocado Fits in the Hierarchy

Avocados are not dangerous the way [[seed-oils]] are dangerous. They do not deliver the concentrated PUFA assault of a tablespoon of corn oil or the estrogenic compounds of [[soy]]. A person who eats an occasional avocado in an otherwise pro-metabolic diet built around [[fruit]], [[eggs]], [[milk]], [[cheese]], and [[saturated-fat]] is not undermining their health in any dramatic way. The problem is the person who has replaced butter with avocado, who eats avocado daily as a fat staple, and who believes they are making a healthful choice because the marketing told them so. For that person, the 13% polyunsaturated fraction compounds day after day, the Mead acid pathway remains suppressed, and the fats they are not eating -- the protective saturated fats of butter, coconut oil, and cocoa butter -- remain absent from their tissues.

The simplest summary of Peat's position: the body can make its own oleic acid from [[sugar]]. It cannot make its own saturated fat protection if that protection is never eaten. Choosing avocado over butter is choosing a food the body does not need over a food it does.
