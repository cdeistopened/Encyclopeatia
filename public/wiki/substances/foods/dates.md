# Dates

Dates represent a case where general Peat principles must be applied in the absence of specific endorsement. Ray Peat did not discuss dates extensively in his published work, interviews, or email correspondence--they never appeared on his lists of recommended fruits alongside [[orange-juice|orange juice]], [[watermelon]], guavas, and [[cherries]]. This silence is itself informative. As a concentrated source of simple sugar, dates satisfy one axis of Peat's dietary framework. But as a dried fruit with elevated [[iron]] content and diminished nutrient complexity, they fall short of the fresh, juicy tropical fruits he actively promoted.

## The Sugar Case For Dates

In Peat's metabolic framework, sugar is not a guilty pleasure but a physiological necessity. Simple sugars--glucose and fructose--suppress [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]], support [[liver]] glycogen stores, and fuel the [[oxidative metabolism]] that Peat equated with health and youth. By this standard, dates have genuine merit. A Medjool date is roughly 66% sugar by weight, delivered primarily as glucose and fructose with minimal starch. This makes dates categorically better than [[starch|starches]] and [[grains]] as an energy source, which is the baseline comparison Peat applied to all carbohydrate foods.

Dates also avoid several of Peat's primary dietary concerns. They contain no significant [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fat]], no [[serotonin]], no [[pectin]] in quantities that would feed intestinal [[bacteria]], and no alkaloids or goitrogenic compounds. Their sugar is readily absorbed in the upper intestine, sparing the lower gut the bacterial fermentation that Peat associated with [[endotoxin]] production and systemic [[inflammation]]. As a portable, shelf-stable sugar source, dates solve a practical problem: they deliver clean fuel in situations where fresh fruit or juice is unavailable.

## The Iron Problem

The principal concern with dates from a Peat-informed perspective is iron. Peat viewed iron not as the essential nutrient celebrated by mainstream nutrition but as a pro-oxidant that accumulates with age and accelerates every degenerative process from [[cancer]] to cardiovascular disease. As he discussed in a [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode on dementia and progesterone]], excess iron catalyzes the formation of free radicals, damages mitochondria, and promotes the lipid peroxidation cascade that Peat considered central to aging.

Dried fruits concentrate iron by removing water while preserving minerals. A 100g serving of Medjool dates contains roughly 0.9mg of iron--modest in absolute terms but elevated compared to the fresh fruits Peat preferred. Orange juice, his staple recommendation, contains approximately 0.2mg per 100g. Over weeks and months of regular consumption, the difference compounds. For someone already working to reduce their iron burden through coffee, [[milk]] (whose calcium inhibits iron absorption), or occasional blood donation--strategies Peat endorsed--replacing fresh fruit with dried fruit moves in the wrong direction.

The drying process also concentrates any pesticide residues, creates a denser caloric package that is easy to overconsume, and eliminates the hydrating quality that makes fresh fruit valuable. A glass of orange juice provides sugar, water, [[potassium]], [[vitamin C]], and [[bioflavonoids]] in a single package. Dates provide sugar and some potassium, but the water, vitamin C, and flavonoid content are negligible.

## Where Dates Sit in the Sugar Hierarchy

Peat's practical sugar hierarchy, derived from his broader work, follows a clear order: fresh fruit juice (especially orange juice) at the top, whole fresh ripe fruit next, then [[honey]] and white [[sugar]], with dried fruit occupying a lower tier. Dates fall into that last category--acceptable as an energy source but not specifically recommended.

> The logic is simple: every step away from fresh, hydrated fruit reduces the nutrient-to-calorie ratio and increases the concentration of potentially problematic minerals like iron, while providing no compensating advantage that fresh fruit or simple sugar cannot deliver more cleanly.

This does not make dates harmful in the way that [[PUFA]]-containing foods or [[starch|resistant starch]] are harmful. A few dates as an afternoon snack or travel food represent a pragmatic choice within Peat's framework--far better than a granola bar, a piece of bread, or the nut-and-seed mixtures that dominate conventional "healthy snacking." The issue is not that dates are dangerous but that better options exist for regular use.

## Medjool vs. Deglet Noor

The two most common commercial varieties differ in ways that matter within this framework. Medjool dates are larger, softer, and sweeter, with a higher moisture content that keeps them closer to fresh fruit in texture and sugar availability. Deglet Noor dates are drier, firmer, and more concentrated--properties that increase their iron density per bite and make them more likely to be consumed in large quantities. For occasional use within a Peat-informed diet, Medjool's softer, wetter profile is modestly preferable.

## Practical Guidelines

For those incorporating dates, a few principles from Peat's framework apply. Consume them alongside [[milk]], coffee, or other calcium-rich foods that inhibit iron absorption. Treat them as a travel or emergency sugar source rather than a daily staple. Prefer fresh ripe fruit whenever it is available. And recognize that dates occupy a position in the dietary landscape analogous to white sugar dissolved in coffee--useful fuel, practically convenient, but nutritionally incomplete compared to the juicy tropical fruits Peat placed at the center of his recommendations.
