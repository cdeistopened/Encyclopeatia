# Honey

For most of the twentieth century, the nutritional establishment treated all sugars as interchangeable villains -- empty calories at best, metabolic poison at worst. Honey, despite thousands of years of medicinal use, was swept into the same condemnation. Ray Peat saw this as a profound misunderstanding. In his framework, honey belongs alongside [[fruit]], [[milk]], and white [[sugar]] as one of the well-metabolized simple sugars that protect against the real metabolic dangers: [[starch]] that feeds intestinal bacteria, [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] that block glucose oxidation, and the stress hormones that accumulate when cells cannot produce enough energy.

## A Well-Metabolized Sugar

Peat consistently grouped honey with the sugars he considered safest and most useful. In a [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 episode on sugar]], he listed "sucrose, glucose, fructose, and lactose" as "very good, well-metabolized sugars," and placed honey squarely in that category. The composition explains why: honey is roughly 55% [[fructose]] and 45% [[glucose]], a ratio close to sucrose (which splits evenly into one molecule of each). This means honey delivers the same metabolic benefits as table sugar -- the glucose provides immediate fuel for cells, while the fructose component stimulates [[oxidative metabolism]] in ways that have been documented for decades.

What distinguishes honey from more complex carbohydrates is its digestibility. When asked whether "sugar" meant "just fructose or honey, natural sugars," Peat clarified that while glucose from [[starch]] can also be beneficial, the problem is that many starchy foods are "mixtures containing woody material and fats -- indigestible things." As he put it, "You can't say that any starch is equivalent to glucose, sucrose, or honey. It depends on whether it is digestible." Honey, being almost entirely simple sugars dissolved in water, is absorbed quickly in the upper intestine before it can reach the bacterial populations of the lower gut -- a critical advantage in Peat's framework, where [[endotoxin]] from bacterial fermentation is a primary driver of [[inflammation]] and disease.

## The Fructose Advantage in Diabetes

One of Peat's most counterintuitive positions was his defense of fructose for diabetics -- the exact population told most strenuously to avoid it. In a [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 discussion on sugar and the glycemic index]], he noted that "thirty or forty years ago, there was quite a bit of publishing activity relating to the anti-diabetes effects of fructose because of that stimulating effect on the metabolism." The mechanism is straightforward: fructose limits insulin secretion while intensifying metabolic activity, effectively helping cells burn fuel more efficiently without the insulin spikes that characterize the diabetic pattern.

This is why Peat recommended that diabetics eat "fruits and honey and stay away from starchy carbohydrates." The logic inverts conventional dietary advice entirely. Rather than restricting all sugars to control blood glucose, the goal is to provide easily oxidized fuel -- simple sugars like those in honey -- while avoiding the poorly digestible starches that feed bacteria, provoke [[endotoxin]] absorption, and trigger the [[Randle cycle|Randle effect]] through bacterial byproducts and stress hormone activation.

## Wound Healing and Topical Uses

Long before Peat incorporated honey into his metabolic framework, it had an ancient pedigree as a wound treatment. Peat was keenly aware of this history and saw it as evidence that sugar's biological effects extend far beyond nutrition. In a [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|2011 episode on sugar myths]], he described how "honey was used for thousands of years to cure wounds," and connected this to modern surgical experience: "In emergency situations where they didn't have antibiotics, they found they could do open heart surgery and pack the wound with pure white sucrose to prevent scarring and promote healing better than the fancy antibiotics."

The antibacterial mechanism is osmotic -- the high sugar concentration draws water out of bacterial cells, killing them -- but Peat suggested the effects go deeper. Sugar applied topically also supports the energy metabolism of healing tissue, promoting [[regeneration]] over scar formation. In his email correspondence, he recommended "topical baking soda, honey, and granulated sugar" as helpful for wounds, treating honey as one tool in a broader anti-inflammatory toolkit rather than a folk remedy to be romanticized.

## Practical Considerations

Peat was notably pragmatic about honey. He did not fetishize raw or artisanal varieties the way many health communities do. In his framework, the relevant variable is the sugar composition -- the fructose-glucose ratio and the absence of problematic additives -- not whether the honey comes from a local apiary or a supermarket shelf. Raw honey may contain small amounts of beneficial enzymes and antimicrobial compounds, but Peat never made this a point of emphasis. What mattered was that honey, like white sugar and ripe fruit, provided clean, rapidly absorbed fuel for cellular [[energy production]].

Within Peat's practical hierarchy of sugar sources, honey occupies a middle position. [[Fruit]] juice (especially [[orange-juice]]) was his most frequent recommendation, valued for its potassium, [[vitamin C]], and other co-factors. White sugar dissolved in coffee or milk was the simplest option. Honey offered an alternative with a slightly higher fructose ratio and the added benefit of topical versatility. [[Milk]] provided sugar in the form of [[lactose]], with the bonus of calcium and high-quality [[protein]]. The point was never that one source was categorically superior, but that all of them were preferable to the starches and complex carbohydrates that mainstream nutrition considered healthier.

> "Honey and more purified fructoses have been used to treat diabetes, stomach ulcers, and other things. Sugar has been used to cure wounds."
> -- Ray Peat, [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|Sugar Myths I]] (KMUD, 2011)

## Part of the Larger Sugar Defense

Honey cannot be understood in isolation from Peat's broader argument that sugar is protective rather than destructive. The fear of sugar, in his view, arose from confusing the effects of [[PUFA]] and [[endotoxin]] with the effects of glucose itself. Diabetic complications blamed on blood sugar are more accurately attributed to the [[free fatty acids]] that block glucose oxidation via the [[Randle cycle]]. Sugar -- whether from honey, fruit, or the sugar bowl -- suppresses [[lipolysis]], keeping those dangerous fatty acids stored rather than circulating.

In this framework, honey is not a superfood or a miracle cure. It is simply one of several clean fuel sources that support the high [[metabolic rate]] Peat associated with health, youth, and resilience. Its millennia of therapeutic use in wound care, its documented anti-diabetic effects through its fructose content, and its rapid digestibility all point to the same conclusion: the oldest sweetener known to humanity turns out to be one of the safest.
