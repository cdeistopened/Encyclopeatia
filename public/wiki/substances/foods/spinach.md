# Spinach

Spinach may be the most successfully marketed vegetable in history. From Popeye's cartoon cans to the modern green smoothie, it has been sold as a powerhouse of iron, calcium, and vitality. Ray Peat's framework tells a different story. Spinach is one of the highest-oxalate foods in the human diet, and those oxalates bind the very minerals--[[calcium]] and [[iron]]--that spinach is supposed to deliver. The Popeye myth turns out to be doubly wrong: not only is the iron in spinach poorly absorbed, but the oxalates actively prevent the absorption of calcium from other foods eaten at the same meal. In a framework where adequate calcium intake is one of the most important protective factors against [[inflammation]], [[estrogen]] excess, and degenerative disease, a food that systematically blocks calcium is not a health food. It is a metabolic liability.

## The Oxalate Problem: Calcium's Enemy

Oxalic acid is spinach's defining anti-nutrient. When oxalates encounter calcium in the digestive tract, they form calcium oxalate--an insoluble compound that passes through the body without being absorbed. This does not merely neutralize the calcium in the spinach itself; it sequesters calcium from whatever else was eaten alongside it. A glass of [[milk]] consumed with a spinach salad delivers less usable calcium than the same glass of milk consumed alone.

This directly opposes one of the most consistent threads in Peat's nutritional framework. Peat recommended high calcium intake--from [[milk]], [[cheese]], and [[orange-juice|orange juice]]--as protective against a cascade of metabolic problems. Adequate calcium suppresses [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]], which in excess promotes [[inflammation]], bone loss, and cellular calcification. It opposes the effects of excess [[phosphate]]. It supports [[thyroid]] function and [[progesterone]] production. A food that systematically removes calcium from the dietary equation undermines this entire protective strategy.

Andrew Murray, Peat's frequent co-host on KMUD, addressed this directly in a [[2021-05-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-may-2021-kmud|May 2021 episode]], noting that "the only negative thing perhaps is some of the oxalates it contains, but spinach does the same thing"--grouping spinach with other high-oxalate greens as foods whose mineral-blocking properties overshadow their theoretical nutritional value.

## Kidney Stone Risk: Oxalate Accumulation

Beyond blocking calcium absorption in the gut, dietary oxalates that are absorbed into the bloodstream must be excreted through the kidneys. When oxalate concentrations in the urine exceed the threshold for solubility, they crystallize into calcium oxalate stones--the most common type of kidney stone. Approximately 80% of kidney stones are calcium oxalate, and spinach is consistently identified as one of the highest dietary contributors.

The risk is not limited to people with diagnosed kidney problems. Anyone consuming large quantities of raw spinach--particularly in the concentrated form of daily green smoothies--is delivering a chronic oxalate load that the kidneys must process. Over time, this can produce subclinical oxalate accumulation in tissues throughout the body, a condition some researchers have termed "oxalate dumping" when dietary oxalate is suddenly reduced and stored crystals begin to mobilize. In Peat's framework, where chronic low-grade [[inflammation]] and mineral imbalance are understood as upstream drivers of disease, this kind of silent tissue burden is precisely the sort of problem that manifests years later as seemingly unrelated pathology.

## The Iron Myth and PUFA in Leaves

The popular belief that spinach is rich in bioavailable iron persists despite having been debunked for decades. The original claim traces to a nineteenth-century decimal point error that inflated spinach's iron content by a factor of ten. Even at its actual iron content, the oxalates in spinach bind much of the iron into insoluble forms, and the non-heme iron that remains is poorly absorbed compared to the heme iron in [[meat]] and [[liver]]. Spinach delivers a fraction of the usable iron that its reputation suggests.

Peat's concerns about leafy greens extended beyond oxalates to their [[pufa|PUFA]] content. As he noted in email exchanges about vegetables, greens carry a "high content of PUFA and tannins." Leaf cell membranes contain polyunsaturated fatty acids as a structural component, and while the per-serving quantity is modest, the cumulative intake from daily large-volume smoothies or salads adds up--particularly in a framework where minimizing total PUFA exposure is a central dietary goal.

## Raw Spinach Smoothies: Concentrated Anti-Nutrition

The green smoothie trend has made raw spinach one of the most commonly consumed raw vegetables in health-conscious households. Blending raw spinach into a smoothie does not reduce its oxalate content--it increases the effective dose by allowing far more spinach to be consumed in liquid form than most people would eat as a salad. Two or three cups of raw spinach, which would be a large salad, blend down into a few ounces of liquid that can be swallowed in minutes. The oxalate load of what amounts to multiple servings gets delivered in a single concentrated dose.

> "Boiling the leaves and discarding all but the water can produce a good magnesium supplement."

Peat's recommendation for anyone insistent on consuming leafy greens was to boil them thoroughly and discard the cooking water--a method that leaches a significant portion of the oxalates (and PUFA and tannins) into the water while retaining some of the mineral content. But even this approach yields less [[magnesium]] and calcium than simply drinking milk, eating cheese, or supplementing directly. The elaborate preparation required to make spinach safe is itself an argument for choosing better foods.

## Better Alternatives for the Nutrients Spinach Promises

Every nutrient spinach is supposed to provide is available in more bioavailable form from foods that do not come packaged with anti-nutrients:

- **Calcium**: [[milk]], [[cheese]], eggshell calcium, calcium-fortified [[orange-juice|orange juice]]
- **Iron**: [[liver]], [[oysters]], [[beef]]
- **Magnesium**: [[milk]], [[coffee]], well-cooked root vegetables, supplemental magnesium
- **Vitamin K**: Peat did not emphasize dietary vitamin K as a priority nutrient
- **Folate**: [[liver]], [[orange-juice|orange juice]]

In Peat's dietary framework, the foods that deliver minerals without blocking their own absorption are the ones worth eating daily. Spinach is not among them.
