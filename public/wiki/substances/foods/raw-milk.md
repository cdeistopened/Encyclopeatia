# Raw Milk

Few topics in natural health generate more passionate advocacy than raw milk. The movement to legalize and promote unpasteurized dairy has produced an entire subculture of farm-share programs, legal battles, and quasi-religious devotion to milk in its unprocessed form. Ray Peat, despite sharing many of the same concerns about industrial food processing that animate the raw milk movement, did not share its central conviction. His position was characteristically contrarian -- not contrarian relative to mainstream nutrition, which opposes raw milk, but contrarian relative to the alternative health community that embraced so much of his other work. On the question of raw versus pasteurized, Peat's answer was disarmingly simple: "The difference isn't enough to worry about."

## Peat's Personal Practice

Peat did not drink raw milk. He stated this plainly in his email correspondence: "I normally use pasteurized (and homogenized) milk, and I know people who do best when they use ultrapasteurized milk, and many people who, especially in certain seasons, don't tolerate raw milk."

This was not a reluctant concession to convenience. Peat had considered the arguments for raw milk -- enzyme preservation, beneficial bacteria, superior nutritional content -- and found them insufficient to outweigh the practical risks and individual variability he observed. His choice of pasteurized milk reflected the same pragmatic cost-benefit analysis he applied to every dietary recommendation: what matters most, and what is noise?

The answer, in Peat's framework, was that [[calcium]] intake matters enormously, [[PUFA]] avoidance matters enormously, and whether the milk has been heated to 161 degrees Fahrenheit for fifteen seconds matters very little. The person who drinks a quart of pasteurized milk daily is in a vastly better metabolic position than the person who drinks no milk at all while waiting for access to a raw milk source.

## The Bacterial Variable

The argument Peat found most compelling against raw milk was not the standard public health concern about pathogens -- though he acknowledged that risk -- but the subtler problem of bacterial variability. As he explained in his emails, "Cows' bacteria change according to what they are eating, and sometimes even the low level of bacteria in pasteurized milk can upset the person's intestinal balance of bacteria."

This observation cuts against the raw milk movement's foundational claim that the bacteria in raw milk are universally beneficial. In Peat's understanding, the bacterial population of a cow's milk shifts with the seasons, the feed, the cow's health, and the conditions of milking. What arrives in a jar of raw milk in June may be bacteriologically different from what arrives in December. Some of those bacterial populations will be benign or beneficial for a given person; others will disrupt intestinal flora in ways that produce [[endotoxin]], [[inflammation]], and digestive distress.

The seasonal intolerance Peat described -- "many people who, especially in certain seasons, don't tolerate raw milk" -- points to a reality that raw milk advocates rarely acknowledge. Raw milk is not a standardized product. Its bacterial composition is a moving target, and the person drinking it is conducting an uncontrolled experiment with each batch.

## The Enzyme Preservation Argument

The most frequently cited benefit of raw milk is enzyme preservation. Pasteurization destroys lactase, lipase, phosphatase, and other enzymes naturally present in milk. Raw milk advocates argue that these enzymes aid digestion and nutrient absorption, making raw milk tolerable for people who are otherwise lactose intolerant.

Peat acknowledged that pasteurization destroys enzymes but did not consider this a critical loss. The body produces its own digestive enzymes, and the quantities present in raw milk are small relative to what the pancreas and intestinal brush border generate. More importantly, Peat viewed lactose intolerance as largely reversible through gradual reintroduction of dairy -- the enzyme [[lactase]] upregulates with consistent dairy consumption, regardless of whether the milk itself contains the enzyme.

The enzyme argument, in Peat's assessment, was a distraction from the variables that actually determine milk's metabolic impact: the calcium content, the protein quality, the fatty acid profile (determined by the cow's diet), and the absence of problematic additives.

## Homogenization Is Not a Concern

Alongside pasteurization, homogenization -- the process of forcing milk through fine screens to break fat globules into smaller particles that remain suspended rather than rising to the top -- has attracted criticism from natural health advocates. The concern is that smaller fat particles might be absorbed differently, potentially carrying the enzyme xanthine oxidase into the bloodstream where it could damage arterial walls.

Peat dismissed this concern directly: "Homogenized milk: it's o.k." The xanthine oxidase hypothesis, while biologically plausible in theory, has not been supported by evidence of actual harm from homogenized milk consumption. Peat's willingness to state this flatly reflects his broader approach: he followed the metabolic evidence rather than the anxieties of any particular health community.

## What Actually Matters

If pasteurization status and homogenization are not critical variables, what is? In Peat's framework, the factors that determine milk's health impact fall into a clear hierarchy.

**The cow's diet** ranks first. Cows eating their natural grass diet produce milk with more [[saturated-fat]], more conjugated linoleic acid, more [[vitamin A]], and less [[PUFA]] than cows fed corn and soy. The rumen converts much of the dietary PUFA into saturated fat, but the conversion is not complete -- grain-fed cows still produce milk with a higher polyunsaturated fat percentage. This is a meaningful difference that affects every glass of milk, raw or pasteurized.

**Adequate intake** ranks second. Peat recommended quantities that most people find surprising -- a quart or more daily -- to achieve the [[calcium]] intake he considered necessary for suppressing [[parathyroid hormone]] and maintaining metabolic rate. Whether that quart comes from raw, pasteurized, or ultrapasteurized milk is far less important than whether it is consumed at all.

**Additive avoidance** ranks third. Some commercial milk contains [[carrageenan]] (in flavored varieties), vitamin D2 instead of D3, or comes from cows treated with recombinant bovine growth hormone. These are legitimate concerns that apply across all processing methods.

## Ultrapasteurized Milk

Peat's mention of people who "do best when they use ultrapasteurized milk" is perhaps the most surprising element of his position. Ultra-high-temperature (UHT) pasteurization heats milk to 280 degrees Fahrenheit for two seconds, killing virtually all microorganisms and extending shelf life dramatically. This process destroys more enzymes and alters more proteins than conventional pasteurization, making it the furthest possible departure from raw milk.

Yet Peat observed that some individuals with sensitive intestinal flora tolerated this most processed form of milk better than any other. The near-total elimination of bacteria -- including the residual bacteria present in conventionally pasteurized milk -- allowed these people to consume dairy without the intestinal disruption that even pasteurized milk provoked. In Peat's framework, where [[endotoxin]] from intestinal bacteria is a primary driver of [[inflammation]] and metabolic stress, this makes biological sense. For someone whose gut flora converts even small bacterial loads into problematic endotoxin, sterile milk is the safest milk.

## The Real Milk Question

Peat's position on raw milk ultimately redirects attention from a question he considered secondary to one he considered primary. The alternative health community asks: "Should I drink raw or pasteurized milk?" Peat's answer reframes the question entirely: "Are you getting enough calcium?"

The obsession with processing method, in his view, distracts from the metabolic urgency of adequate dairy consumption. A person drinking two quarts of pasteurized milk daily, getting 2,000+ milligrams of calcium, maintaining low [[parathyroid hormone]], and keeping their [[metabolic rate]] elevated is in a fundamentally different physiological state than a person drinking no milk at all -- regardless of how that person feels about pasteurization.
