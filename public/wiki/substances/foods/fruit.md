# Fruit

Fruit is the carbohydrate vehicle Peat preferred above all others — above the starches mainstream nutrition treats as benign, and even above [[milk]] sugar, which fills a slightly different role in his daily pattern. Ripe fruit delivers what he wanted a carbohydrate to deliver: roughly equal parts [[fructose]] and [[glucose]], a generous mineral load weighted toward potassium and magnesium, bioflavonoids and [[vitamin-c|vitamin C]] in their natural redox context, no [[pufa|PUFA]], no starch granules, and — when genuinely ripe — none of the defensive chemistry the plant uses to protect its seeds before they're ready to be eaten. The daily formula Peat repeated for decades — "two quarts of milk and a quart of orange juice" — was the operational core; the broader category sat behind it. This page is the umbrella. The applied tool ([[orange-juice]]), the bioflavonoid chemistry ([[oranges]], [[grapes]], [[berries]]), the cooked-form rescue ([[apple]], [[banana]]), and the individual tropical and citrus profiles ([[mango]], [[papaya]], [[watermelon]], [[guava]], [[grapefruit]], [[cherries]], [[honey]], [[dates]]) live in their own pages. What follows is the framework that decides which fruit goes where.

## What counts as good fruit

Ripeness is the central variable. Almost every problem Peat flagged with fruit — allergy, gut irritation, capillary blockage by [[persorption|persorbed]] starch granules, [[serotonin]] release, defensive-chemical exposure — collapses or substantially reduces when the fruit is fully ripe. Almost every benefit concentrates in the ripe form. The email collection on fruit puts the rule in one line: "Poorly ripened fruits of all sorts should be avoided" ([[fruit|fruit emails]]). A green banana, a hard apple, a sour strawberry, a fibrous mango — these are not the same foods as their ripe counterparts in any nutritional sense Peat cared about. They are the plant's defensive system, eaten prematurely.

The hierarchy is consistent across forty years of Peat's correspondence. At the top sit the **ripe tropical and citrus fruits**. The [[fruit|fruit email collection]] enumerates the safe list: "corossol, lychee, longan, guava, papaya, pawpaw, sapota, guanabana." Add the citrus tier — [[oranges|orange]], mandarin, [[grapefruit]] — and the high-water-content fruits Peat singled out by name when asked to rank the very best. The direct question, from the same exchange: "I think you have stated that the best fruits, if properly grown, are oranges, watermelons, and grapes. Is guava close to them in quality?" Yes, he replied. "They rank with oranges for their protective qualities." That four-name top tier — [[oranges|oranges]], [[watermelon|watermelon]], [[grapes|grapes]], [[guava]] — is as explicit an endorsement as Peat ever gave in a single sentence.

Below the top tier sit the **ripe stone fruits and tree fruits that pass the digestibility test when fully soft**. [[cherries|Cherries]] in season, ripe peaches, apricots, plums. Peat listed "ripe papayas" among "good sources of sugar to have regularly," alongside orange juice, guavas, watermelons, cooked apples, and cherries (see [[papaya]]). [[mango|Mango]] sits in this tier with an explicit allergy caveat: "mangos, apples, and pears are allergenic to some people" ([[fruit|fruit emails]]) — the only tropical fruit that gets a per-individual warning. [[honey|Honey]], though not technically fruit, slots adjacent as a concentrated fruit-sugar analog.

A third tier — **seedy and firm-fleshed fruits** — needs preparation. Raw [[apple|apples]] and pears feed bacteria through their [[pectin]] more than they feed the person; cooked applesauce removes the problem (see [[apple]]). [[berries|Berries]] carry potent bioflavonoids in the pigment but deliver them through a seed-and-fiber matrix that requires straining, cooking, or juicing (see [[berries]]). [[dates|Dates]] are useful as concentrated sugar but lose the hydrating profile of the top tier.

A fourth tier — **fruits needing active caution or careful cooking**. Underripe [[banana|bananas]] deliver raw starch granules that can [[persorption|persorb]] across the gut wall; ripe-spotted bananas are tolerable but flagged as a strong allergen and [[serotonin]] carrier (see [[banana]]). Pineapples and tomatoes share the serotonin problem in a milder form. Jackfruit gets the same allergen warning as banana ([[fruit|fruit emails]]: "bananas and jack-fruit are strong allergens, possibly because of their cultivation methods").

The ripeness rule is mechanical. Ripening is the process by which the plant's enzymes break down its own structural starch and pectin into simple sugars, soften the cell walls, and dismantle the defensive tannins and organic acids that protected the immature fruit. From the [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 ELUV interview]]: "The very juicy fruits are best raw, but if they're starchy like bananas or plantains, they should be cooked by preference because the starches can enter the bloodstream and trigger allergic reactions." The juicier the fruit, the more the ripening has done its work; the firmer and starchier, the more further ripening or external heat is needed.

## Why fruit works in this framework

The functional payload of ripe fruit is a small package of four things that are hard to get together anywhere else.

**Substrate the [[liver]] uses fastest.** The roughly 1:1 fructose-to-glucose ratio in fruit is, by Peat's repeated statement, close to the ideal therapeutic carbohydrate composition — the same ratio that defines [[sucrose|table sugar]] and [[honey]]. The full mechanism — fructose's bypass of the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]], its preferential conversion into liver glycogen, its catalytic effect on glucose oxidation — lives at [[fructose]], [[sucrose]], [[blood-sugar]], and [[orange-juice]]. The operational point for this page: fruit is the natural delivery vehicle. "All fruits contain approximately the same ratio of fructose and glucose as white sugar," Peat noted in a [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 episode on sugar]]. "However, other sources of carbohydrates like grains, beans, starches, bread, pasta, and potatoes are almost all glucose; they are devoid of fructose." Starches are missing exactly the half of the molecule that does the catalytic work; fruit is not.

**Minerals in a balanced matrix.** The unique signal of fruit that is hardest to replicate elsewhere. A glass of orange juice supplies around 450 mg of potassium, 25–30 mg of magnesium, 22 mg of calcium, and measurable copper, selenium, and manganese; ripe mango or papaya supplies a comparable but not identical profile; [[watermelon]] is rich in potassium and citrulline; [[grapes]] carry potassium and protective tartrate compounds. The potassium-to-sodium balance is what conventional nutrition treats as the main feature, but Peat cared more about the *whole pattern* — magnesium, copper, manganese, trace selenium — that ripe fruit delivers in a single matrix. Peat referred to fruit and milk as "osmotically balanced with minerals and sugar" ([[fruit-juice|fruit-juice emails]]), meaning the mineral-to-sugar-to-water ratio is close enough to extracellular fluid that drinking fruit doesn't dilute the blood the way plain water does in a hypothyroid person. This is what lets him treat a quart of orange juice as a mineral therapy as much as a sugar therapy.

**Bioflavonoids in their natural redox context.** Naringenin and hesperidin in citrus, resveratrol in grapes, anthocyanins in berries, apigenin in guava, lycopene in watermelon — covered in [[oranges]], [[grapes]], [[berries]], [[guava]], and [[watermelon]]. They supply [[aromatase]] inhibition, [[serotonin]] modulation, and the secondary anti-stress effects that compound with daily consumption. Their relevance here is that they make the [[vitamin-c|vitamin C]] in fruit safe at high volume in a way isolated ascorbic acid is not, and they explain why fruit in volume does not behave like the rodent-study fructose load it superficially resembles.

**What's not there.** No [[pufa|PUFA]]. No [[starch]] (in the ripe form). No grain-protein allergens. No [[caffeine]]. No standalone protein load to provoke an [[insulin]] surge or cortisol response. Ripe fruit is almost optimally clean substrate.

Underneath all four points is the [[blood-sugar|blood-sugar]] argument. Peat treated *falling* blood sugar as the upstream pathology — the trigger for the [[adrenaline]] / [[cortisol]] / free-fatty-acid cascade. Fruit is the food category that refills [[liver|liver]] glycogen fastest, holds blood sugar steadiest, and supplies the [[mitochondria|mitochondrial]] substrate that keeps [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]] running. The natural-history case lives in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues*]]: "In monkeys living in the wild, when their diet is mainly fruit, their cortisol is low, and it rises when they eat a diet with less sugar." A diet dominated by ripe fruit runs at lower baseline stress-hormone tone.

## Forms

The choice of form changes which problems a given fruit creates and which it solves.

**Fresh, ripe, eaten raw.** The default for the top tier — a ripe mango, a tray of cherries, a chilled watermelon, a peeled orange or grapefruit. No processing necessary because the plant's own ripening enzymes have already done it. This is the form Peat ate when fruit was actually ripe and available, and the form he was clear about most North American supermarkets rarely supplying at the level of equatorial markets he'd known in Mexico.

**Juiced.** The form that handles gut-irritation when the whole fruit is too fibrous or seedy. The operational deep dive — dosing, timing, anti-stress applications, failure modes — lives at [[orange-juice]]. The same logic applies to [[watermelon]] juice (Peat's named substitute for OJ), pressed pineapple in moderation, [[grapes|grape]] juice, and strained berry juice. Juicing removes seeds and most fiber, concentrates the sugar and minerals, and delivers the bioflavonoids without the bacterial-fermentation downside. The hazards — added [[citric-acid|citric acid]], enzyme-treated pulp, PUFA-carrying emulsifiers — are catalogued at [[orange-juice]].

**Frozen.** Largely preserves the nutrient profile. The [[fruit|fruit email collection]] explicitly endorses several: "some frozen and canned fruits are good; applesauce, corossol, guanabana, longans, and lychees for example." The cautions are pesticide load (frozen berries and cherries are near the top of EWG's "Dirty Dozen") and how green the fruit was when frozen — green fruit frozen never finishes ripening.

**Canned (in sugar syrup, not seed oil).** Acceptable. The same email names canned applesauce, lychees, longans, and guanabana. Light sugar syrup or own-juice is fine; thickeners and emulsifiers introduce other problems; anything packed with PUFA carriers is to be avoided.

**Cooked.** The rescue path for fruits problematic raw. [[apple|Cooked apples]] convert [[pectin]] from a bacterial substrate into a more digestible form (full mechanism at [[apple]]). Same logic for pears, stone-fruit compote, and the rare cooked green banana or plantain — from the [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 ELUV interview]]: "starchy like bananas or plantains... should be cooked by preference because the starches can enter the bloodstream and trigger allergic reactions."

**Dried.** Concentrated sugar and minerals — but also concentrated pesticide residue, no water, and (in raisins, dried cranberries, dried cherries) added seed oils and sulfites in many commercial products. [[dates|Dates]] are the cleanest dried fruit but still not first-line. Use for emergencies and baking, not as a daily staple.

## Fruits to be careful with

**Apples and pears for sensitive guts.** [[pectin|Pectin]] in raw apples and pears feeds [[endotoxin]]-producing bacteria and releases [[serotonin]] from the gut wall. From the [[intestine-allergy|intestine-allergy emails]]: "The fructose content of pears is probably helpful, but you should watch for what effect it might be having on your intestine, from the pectin. Pectin tends to increase serotonin by irritating the intestine." Cooking is the fix (see [[apple]]).

**Berries with the seeds intact.** [[berries|Raspberries, blackberries, and to a lesser extent strawberries and blueberries]] deliver excellent bioflavonoids inside a matrix of indigestible fiber and PUFA-bearing seeds. Peat's narrow allowance is in the [[fruit|fruit emails]]: "If they are eaten without pulverizing the seeds I think they are o.k." Prefer strained juice or cooked-and-strained preparations. Full mechanism at [[berries]].

**Unripe bananas and jackfruit.** Singled out as strong allergens "possibly because of their cultivation methods" ([[fruit|fruit emails]]). The banana failure mode — raw starch [[persorption]], [[serotonin]] payload, allergen load — is detailed at [[banana]]. Both improve substantially with full ripening and, for the banana, with cooking.

**Salicylate-sensitive individuals.** A small subset react to certain fruits with sore throats or migraines. From the [[fruit|fruit emails]]: "The fruits you mention all seriously increase serotonin. A sore throat is a quick effect, but some people get migraines from them. The pectin in raw apples causes the intestine to release serotonin into the blood, so well cooked apples have much less effect. Fruits contain almost no salicylic acid." Peat's read: the mechanism is gut-serotonin, not salicylate. Workaround is the same — cook, strain, or substitute.

**Pineapples and tomatoes.** Both contain enough [[serotonin]] to "produce physiological effects in susceptible people" (Peat, *Serotonin, Depression, and Aggression*).

**Frozen-fruit pesticide load.** Strawberries, nectarines, apples, grapes, peaches, cherries, and pears routinely top EWG's "Dirty Dozen." Peat did not publish quantitative thresholds, but the [[vegetables-etc-who-defines-food|*Vegetables, etc.*]] newsletter is unambiguous on the broader exposure: "Toxic pesticides are found in higher concentrations in the urine and fat of slaughtered animals than in their livers." Organic for the thin-skinned (berries, stone fruit); conventional acceptable for the thick-skinned (citrus, banana, watermelon, pineapple) since the peel filters most exposure.

**Severe fructose malabsorption.** Real but narrow. The full treatment lives at [[orange-juice]]. Peat was dismissive of the population-scale claim — "the fructose cult generalizes crazily from any apparent evidence they can find" ([[fructose|fructose emails]]). Orange juice, with its 1:1 ratio, is the friendliest first juice; fructose-heavy fruits (watermelon, pear, apple, mango) are worse-tolerated by the truly malabsorptive minority.

## Failure modes

The general shape of how fruit goes wrong:

- **Pesticide-laden conventional varieties**, especially thin-skinned berries, stone fruit, and grapes — residues survive freezing and ordinary washing.
- **Unripe fruit eaten raw** — defensive chemistry exposure, [[serotonin]] release, [[pectin]] fermentation, raw-starch [[persorption]] (in [[banana|bananas]] and plantains specifically).
- **Commercial-juice processing** — enzyme-treated pulp, added [[citric-acid|citric acid]], PUFA-carrying emulsifiers. Covered in [[orange-juice]].
- **Fructose malabsorption in established gut dysfunction** — real but narrow, often mistaken for a general fructose-toxicity claim. Distinguishing test: whether the same dose of glucose alone is tolerated.
- **Excess without minerals and protein** — fruit-only diets strip out the calcium, complete protein, and saturated fats Peat treated as foundational ([[fruit-diet-vegan-veganism|fruit-diet emails]]: "For best resistance to stress, more protein is desirable").
- **Dried fruit in volume** — concentrated sugar and pesticides, no water.

## Tracking signal

Fruit added to a diet that previously ran on starch and PUFA tends to show up fast.

**Same-day.** Warming of the hands and feet within an hour or two of a meaningful fruit intake — a glass of juice, a ripe mango, a bowl of cherries — particularly the morning dose. Steadier mood and energy across three to four hours. Less afternoon crash.

**Week-scale.** Easier stools, with regularity that does not depend on a daily high-fiber load. Resting pulse and morning oral [[temperature-pulse-tracking|temperature]] drift upward within five to ten days. Fewer 3 a.m. wake-ups when fruit is part of an [[eat-before-bed|evening snack]].

**Month-scale.** Skin tone improvement (bioflavonoids and copper compounding on [[collagen]] synthesis), less bruising, denser hair, less PMS, calmer baseline mood. For people coming off long-term [[low-carb|low-carb]] diets, a return of normal thyroid metabolism that often manifests as initial weight stability followed by a slow downward trend in body fat as [[randle-cycle]] inhibition lifts.

**Anti-signals.** Persistent gas or diarrhea on whole fruit means the prep is wrong: try cooked applesauce, strained berry juice, or pulp-free juice. New migraines or sore throats on specific fruits (apples, bananas, pineapples) means serotonin-sensitivity is in play; switch, cook, or strain. A *falling* pulse or temperature on high fruit intake means the protein side is missing — fruit was never prescribed alone.

## Counter-arguments

Fruit sits in the crosshairs of more mainstream and counter-cultural dietary positions than almost any food category.

**The paleo / ancestral "nature's candy" framing.** The argument that fruit was a rare seasonal treat for ancestral humans and that modern fruit has been bred far sweeter than its wild ancestor. Empirically mostly true; prescriptively it does not follow. Peat's framework is therapeutic — directed at PUFA-loaded, stress-hormone-elevated, [[endotoxin]]-burdened modern adults, not at reconstructing ancestral macronutrient ratios. Selective breeding for sweetness is, in this framework, an advantage; the wild ancestor of the modern apple was a small tannic crab-apple that almost no one would choose to eat.

**The fructose-equals-NAFLD position (Lustig and collaborators).** That fructose specifically drives [[nafld|non-alcoholic fatty liver disease]] and is metabolically distinct from glucose in a uniformly bad way. The full Peat counter lives at [[fructose]] and [[orange-juice]]. The summary: rodent studies use 60% of calories as pure fructose with minimal translational validity; observational human data conflate sugar-sweetened beverages with the PUFA-rich processed-food matrix they're consumed in; and de novo lipogenesis from fructose becomes significant only when liver glycogen is full *and* the diet is high in PUFA. In a glycogen-depleted liver — the state of most hypothyroid modern adults — fruit refills glycogen rather than being converted to fat.

**The low-FODMAP / IBS blanket fruit restriction.** Modern functional medicine frequently restricts whole categories of fruit long-term for anyone with bloating. Some people do improve on the restriction; the framework that turns it into a permanent diet does not follow. The underlying problem in most "fructose intolerance," from Peat's read, is [[endotoxin]] load and bacterial overgrowth — a [[carrot-salad]], [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], [[salt]], and ripe-fruit therapy fixes the substrate rather than treating fruit as the problem.

**The carnivore / all-meat exclusion.** A more recent position that excludes fruit entirely, relying on insulin resistance and [[ketosis]] as therapeutic targets Peat rejected. Some patients with severe gut dysfunction improve initially on carnivore — fewer fermentable substrates means less endotoxin — but Peat's framework treats the inability to tolerate sugar as the disease, not the solution.

**The "sugar feeds cancer" argument.** Derived from a partial reading of Warburg. The full counter lives in [[orange-juice]] and the [[cancer]] mega-brief: the Warburg effect is the symptom of damaged mitochondrial respiration, not a feeding signal that more sugar accelerates. Fruit, in this view, is part of the anti-cancer diet, not against it.

## Data gaps

Honest limits on what the Peat framework actually settles:

- **Pesticide-load thresholds.** Peat did not publish quantitative numbers, and EWG's Dirty Dozen is a priority signal rather than a dose-response document. Practical rule: organic for thin-skinned routinely-eaten fruit; conventional acceptable for thick-skinned.
- **Individual fructose-malabsorption rates.** Gastroenterology cites 30–50% on high-dose breath testing; Peat was dismissive. Real-world clinically significant rates are probably in between — most "fructose problems" are mislabeled [[endotoxin]] / fermentable-fiber problems — but the corpus does not formalize prevalence.
- **Conventional-vs-organic mineral content.** Some research suggests organic fruit has marginally higher mineral density; magnitude is small and operational relevance unknown.
- **Geographic ripeness availability.** Peat's framework was substantially developed in Mexico, where genuinely tree-ripened tropical fruit was available at equatorial-market quality. The translation problem for a North American supermarket shopper is real and underexplored.
- **Upper bound on fruit intake.** Peat was explicit that fruit alone is inadequate, but the corpus does not formalize how much is too much in someone otherwise eating well, or pediatric dosing.
