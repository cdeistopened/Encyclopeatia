# Fructose

Fructose is arguably the substance where Ray Peat's framework diverges most sharply from orthodox nutrition. Mainstream medicine has spent two decades building a case against fructose as a driver of fatty liver, obesity, and metabolic syndrome. Peat spent three decades arguing that this case was constructed on experiments that systematically combined fructose with [[polyunsaturated-fats|polyunsaturated fats]], then blamed the fructose for the damage the fats caused. His counter-thesis is that fructose is not merely safe but therapeutic--that diabetics are deficient in it, that it bypasses the metabolic blocks that define diabetes, that it detoxifies the liver rather than destroying it, and that it raises the metabolic rate by mechanisms no other sugar can match. "It stimulates the cells to take up glucose very much the way insulin does," Peat told listeners in a [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|2011 episode on sugar myths]]. "You can't distinguish the effects on some of the crucial enzymes. Fructose, and the pyruvic acid that it turns into, stimulate the enzymes that oxidize pyruvic acid just the same way insulin stimulates that same enzyme. One replaces the other perfectly."

## The Randle Cycle Bypass

The central metabolic argument for fructose begins with a problem: in [[diabetes]], in [[stress]], in aging, and in diets high in [[polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]], the cell's ability to oxidize [[glucose]] is progressively blocked. This is the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]--the competition between fatty acids and glucose for oxidative machinery, first described by Philip Randle in the 1960s. When [[free-fatty-acids]] flood the bloodstream, they inhibit the enzyme [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]] (PDH), the gatekeeper that allows pyruvate to enter the mitochondria for full oxidation. Glucose piles up in the blood while the cell burns fat instead--inefficiently, and with accumulating damage.

Fructose enters the glycolytic pathway below the point at which fatty acids block glucose. As Peat explained in [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]], "one of the points at which fatty acids suppress the use of glucose is at the point at which it is converted into fructose, in the process of glycolysis. When fructose is available, it can by-pass this barrier to the use of glucose, and continue to provide pyruvic acid for continuing oxidative metabolism." The bypass is not merely theoretical. Peat cited multiple experiments showing that even small amounts of fructose catalyze a dramatic increase in total glucose oxidation--a 30% to 50% increase in metabolic rate across numerous studies. In a [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 discussion of sugar]], he quantified it: "It raises your temperature by a variety of mechanisms, which all by itself will increase your metabolic rate. It increases your conversion of carbohydrate to energy by about 20%. It'll do that even in a fairly small amount."

The mechanism operates at the level of pyruvate dehydrogenase itself. Fructose activates this enzyme the same way [[insulin]] does--and the same way [[thyroid]] hormone does. In a [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 episode on diabetes and nerve protection]], Peat explained the phosphate dimension: "Fructose absorbs excess phosphate ions. The absorption of phosphate ions by fructose is a direct defensive system. The pyruvate dehydrogenase enzyme is suppressed in cancer--that's why dichloroacetate is gaining interest, as it reactivates that enzyme. But simply lowering the free phosphate in the cell tends to reactivate this crucial enzyme at the top of the energy-producing chain." Free fatty acids do the opposite--they increase intracellular phosphate, poisoning PDH. Fructose and fatty acids are, at this enzymatic chokepoint, exact antagonists.

## Diabetes: A Fructose Deficiency

The orthodox framing treats diabetes as a disease of excess sugar. Peat reframed it as a disease of blocked sugar oxidation, in which fructose is not the cause but a missing corrective. The historical evidence begins in 1874, when E. Kulz in Germany first reported that diabetics metabolize fructose better than glucose. "After the publication of that paper," Peat noted in the [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|sugar myths episode]], "it was fairly common for people to recommend the use of fructose or sucrose to diabetics rather than plain glucose." Even earlier, in the 1850s and 1860s, French and English physicians treated diabetes by adding [[sucrose]] to the diet--and saw improvement.

The modern evidence is equally striking. Peat cited research by Osuagwu and Madumere showing that diabetics have almost no fructose circulating in their bloodstream. "If a fructose deficiency exists in diabetes," he wrote in [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]], "then it is appropriate to supplement it in the diet." The deficiency explains why diabetics respond to fructose supplementation as well as or better than healthy people: "Since they aren't able to metabolize glucose, that's apparently why they metabolize fructose to carbon dioxide even a little faster than healthy people do."

The clinical logic follows directly. When a diabetic eats starch, the starch rapidly converts to glucose, which the diabetic cannot oxidize because PUFA-blocked pyruvate dehydrogenase will not allow pyruvate to enter the mitochondria. [[Insulin]] is secreted but cannot overcome the block. Blood sugar rises, more insulin is secreted, and the excess insulin drives fat deposition and further metabolic suppression. When the same diabetic eats [[sucrose]] or fruit, the fructose component bypasses the Randle cycle block, activates PDH, and catalyzes the oxidation of the glucose component. As Sarah Murray summarized in the [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|2011 episode]]: "When diabetics avoid the starches and eat honey, white sugar, and fruits, their sugar numbers become much more manageable."

Fructose also moderates [[insulin]] secretion itself. Pure glucose triggers a sharp insulin spike; fructose "besides not itself stimulating insulin, actually slightly inhibits the release of insulin," Peat explained in the [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 sugar episode]]. The result is that sucrose, with its 50/50 glucose-fructose ratio, produces a gentler insulin curve than starch while simultaneously accelerating glucose disposal--the exact opposite of the metabolic syndrome pattern.

## Fructose and Thyroid Function

Fructose does not merely substitute for [[insulin]]--it also mimics [[thyroid]] hormone at several intracellular points. Peat identified this overlap repeatedly. In the [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|first sugar myths episode]], he stated that "fructose in particular acts very much like T3 (active thyroid hormone). Both glucose and fructose increase the conversion of the inactive thyroxine (T4) to the active T3." The overlap extends to the respiratory chain: "The key respiratory enzyme, cytochrome oxidase, is activated both by thyroid and fructose," Peat noted in the [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|second sugar myths episode]]. [[Polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]] blocks both thyroid and fructose at these same intracellular sites.

The practical implication is that fructose provides a degree of metabolic support even when [[thyroid]] function is compromised. "Even if you're deficient in thyroid hormone, you can keep the activity going to a great extent with sucrose, salt, and calcium," Peat told listeners in the [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 episode]]. This makes [[sucrose]] and fruit not merely energy sources but partial thyroid substitutes--an idea with no counterpart in mainstream endocrinology, but one that follows logically from the shared enzymatic targets Peat identified.

The thyroid-fructose connection also runs through the [[liver]]. Hypothyroidism impairs the liver's ability to store [[glycogen]], convert T4 to T3, and produce [[cholesterol]] for hormone synthesis. Fructose addresses all three problems. As Peat wrote in his article on [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]]: "I encourage people to eat sweet fruits, rather than starches, if they want to increase their production of cholesterol, since fructose has that effect." The liver, properly fueled with fructose, can produce the cholesterol that becomes [[pregnenolone]], [[progesterone]], and other protective hormones.

## Liver Protection, Not Liver Destruction

The mainstream case against fructose centers on the liver: fructose supposedly causes non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Peat considered this one of the most perverse inversions in nutritional medicine. His counter-evidence was extensive.

First, fructose detoxifies the liver. In the [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|first sugar myths episode]], Peat addressed the comparison between fructose and alcohol head-on: "For many years people have been showing that fructose detoxifies many things that enter the liver, including alcohol. It can almost double the rate of destruction of alcohol and prevent liver damage in the process. Many other toxins are detoxified in the presence of fructose." In his newsletter [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]], he elaborated on the mechanism: "Fructose has been known for many years to accelerate the oxidation of ethanol (by about 80%). Oxygen consumption in the presence of ethanol is increased by fructose more than by glucose."

Second, fructose protects the liver's redox balance. Alcohol damages the liver by shifting the NAD+/NADH ratio toward the reductive side--the same shift that occurs in cancer, stress, and aging. Fructose reverses this shift: it "prevents the oxidative damage, by maintaining or restoring the cell's redox balance, the relatively oxidized state of the NADH/NAD+, lactate/pyruvate, and GSH/GSSG systems." This redox-restoring capacity is, in Peat's view, "a general feature of fructose, sometimes allowing it to have the opposite effect of glucose on the cell's redox state."

Third, the studies that produce fatty liver with fructose invariably add large amounts of PUFA. As Peat noted in [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]], "the 'high fructose' diets consistently add 10% or more corn oil or other highly unsaturated fat to the diet. These large quantities of PUFA aren't necessary to prevent a deficiency, but they are needed to obscure the beneficial effects of fructose." Strip the PUFA from the experiment, and fructose protects the liver rather than harming it. Peat noted that "even feeding enough sucrose to cause fat deposition in the liver can protect the liver from oxidative stress" in [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes]].

## Glycogen: The Best Replenisher

The liver's glycogen reserves are the body's primary buffer against [[hypoglycemia]] and [[stress]]. When glycogen runs low, the body must produce [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]] to convert protein into glucose--a catabolic process that cannibalizes muscle and suppresses immune function. Peat identified fructose as uniquely effective at refilling this buffer.

In an [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|interview on glycemia]], Peat stated: "Fructose happens to be the best stimulant for the liver to store glycogen. The current phobia is that fructose increases triglycerides. But before it does that, it powerfully helps the liver replenish glycogen stores." The distinction matters practically: the [[liver]] will convert fructose to glycogen preferentially. Only when glycogen stores are full does fructose conversion to triglycerides begin--and even then, the triglycerides produced from fructose in a PUFA-free context are far less harmful than the lipid peroxidation products generated from stored polyunsaturated fats.

In his email exchanges, Peat confirmed that this glycogen-replenishing effect extends beyond the liver: "I think that's one of its basic protective effects, and I think it increases it in the brain, too."

## The Redox System and Fetal Development

Fructose occupies a unique biological position that predates its role in the adult diet. It is the primary sugar of reproduction--the dominant sugar in seminal fluid, intrauterine fluid, and the environment of the developing fetus. The placenta actively converts the mother's glucose into fructose and pumps it into the fetal circulation. Peat noted in [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]] that "although glucose can move back from the fetus into the mother's blood, fructose is unable to move in that direction, so a high concentration is maintained in the fluids around the fetus."

The reason, Peat proposed, is that fructose maintains the cell's oxidative state--its redox potential--under conditions of low oxygen. "It has been suggested that the predominance of fructose rather than glucose in the embryo's environment helps to maintain ATP and the oxidative state (cellular redox potential) during development in the low-oxygen environment." This is not a minor metabolic detail. The redox state of a cell determines whether it differentiates and functions or proliferates and degenerates. The shift toward the reductive state--increased NADH relative to NAD+, increased [[lactic-acid|lactate]] relative to pyruvate--is the shift that characterizes [[cancer]], [[stress]], and aging. Fructose pushes in the opposite direction.

This redox-regulating capacity explains why fructose protects against both reductive and oxidative stress. As Peat wrote: "Besides protecting against the reductive stresses, fructose can also protect against the oxidative stress of increased hydrogen peroxide. Its metabolite, fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, is even more effective as an antioxidant." The same metabolite, fructose diphosphate, "can reduce mast cell histamine release and protect against oxidative and hypoxic injury and endotoxic shock," reducing the expression of inflammatory mediators including TNF-alpha, IL-6, nitric oxide synthase, and NF-kB activation.

## The Anti-Fructose Literature: PUFA Confounding

Peat devoted considerable attention to dismantling the experimental literature that condemns fructose. His central critique is methodological: the experiments that produce harmful results from fructose invariably include large amounts of polyunsaturated fat in the diet.

The historical evidence begins with George Burr's essential fatty acid experiments. "Burr didn't understand that it was his rats' high sugar diet, freed of the anti-oxidative unsaturated fatty acids, that caused their extremely high metabolic rate," Peat wrote in [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]]. When experimenters in 1963 and 1964 tested this directly, "the effects of glucose and fructose were radically affected by the type of fat in the diet." Only 0.6% of calories as PUFA prevents the appearance of [[mead-acid]] (the marker for essential fatty acid deficiency), yet "the 'high fructose' diets consistently add 10% or more corn oil or other highly unsaturated fat to the diet." The excess PUFA is not there to prevent deficiency--it is there to create the metabolic dysfunction that gets attributed to fructose.

One experiment Peat cited repeatedly involved rats fed different sugar-and-fat combinations. "The weight of their fat pads was greatest in the fructose+starch group, and least in the sucrose group." In another study designed to show that fructose causes insulin resistance, rats were fed a fructose-heavy diet for five weeks. The researchers announced that the rats had become insulin resistant. What they did not emphasize, Peat noted, was that "if you had continued that same diet for the rest of the year, the ones eating the starch would have weighed twice as much as the ones eating the sucrose diet."

Studies that compare glucose and fructose without PUFA confounding tell a different story. Peat cited an experiment in which people received a 300-calorie drink of glucose, fructose, or [[orange-juice|orange juice]]: "Those receiving the glucose had a large increase in oxidative and inflammatory stress (reactive oxygen species, and NF-kappaB binding), and those changes were absent in those receiving the fructose or orange juice."

## HFCS: Starch, Not Fructose

Peat distinguished carefully between fructose in fruit or sucrose and the [[high-fructose-corn-syrup|high fructose corn syrup]] (HFCS) that dominates the American food supply. The problem with HFCS is not its fructose content--which is roughly comparable to sucrose's--but its hidden starch content. In the [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 sugar episode]], Peat described research by Wahjudi and colleagues who "analyzed samples of it before and after hydrolyzing it in acid, to break down other carbohydrates present in it. They found that the carbohydrate content was several times higher than the listed values." The extra carbohydrate exists as "starch-like materials"--essentially hidden flour dissolved in the syrup.

This matters because starch converts rapidly to pure glucose, which spikes insulin, drives fat synthesis, and lacks fructose's catalytic and insulin-moderating properties. As Peat summarized: "If you have four or five times more food in your soda pop than you were thinking, you're going to be more likely to get fat--not because fructose is fattening, but just because you're getting a huge amount of pretty much the equivalent of eating flour."

## Phosphate, Bone, and Mineral Balance

An underappreciated dimension of fructose's effects involves its interaction with phosphate and mineral metabolism. Fructose lowers serum phosphate--a fact that mainstream researchers cite as evidence of harm, but that Peat placed in the context of longevity research.

The anti-aging gene Klotho, discovered in 1997, "suppresses the reabsorption of phosphate by the kidney," Peat noted in [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]]. Animals lacking this gene have high serum phosphate and age prematurely. In humans, "a very close association has been documented between increased phosphate levels, within the normal range, and increased risk of cardiovascular disease." Fructose's phosphate-lowering effect, far from being a deficiency, aligns it with the longevity-promoting direction.

The mineral balance data is similarly inverted from mainstream interpretation. Peat cited a study comparing 20% fructose and 20% cornstarch diets that concluded "dietary fructose enhances mineral balance"--including calcium, magnesium, and copper retention. A bone study found that "the rats getting the glucose had reduced phosphorus in their bones, and more calcium in their urine, than the rats that got fructose," leading the authors to conclude that "glucose rather than fructose exerted more deleterious effects on mineral balance and bone."

Most remarkably, Peat cited an experiment in which vitamin D-deficient rats fed sucrose maintained normal blood calcium and near-normal bone development, while vitamin D-deficient rats fed starch developed the expected rickets. "The sucrose, unlike the starch, maintained calcium homeostasis." The mechanism, Peat proposed, involves fructose's stimulation of [[carbon-dioxide]] production, which is "largely responsible for proper calcification of bone."

## Stress, Cortisol, and the Sugar Instinct

Peat connected fructose intake to the body's stress response system in a way that reframes sugar cravings as adaptive rather than pathological. "In monkeys living in the wild, when their diet is mainly fruit, their [[cortisol]] is low, and it rises when they eat a diet with less sugar," he wrote in [[sugar-issues|Sugar Issues]]. Sucrose consumption directly lowers ACTH, the pituitary stress hormone. Animals whose adrenal glands have been removed--deprived of cortisol--choose to consume more sucrose. "Stress seems to be perceived as a need for sugar."

The mechanism connects to protein metabolism. Without adequate sugar, eating protein triggers an insulin response that lowers blood sugar, forcing the body to produce [[cortisol]] or [[adrenaline]] to compensate. Peat explained in the [[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|sugar myths episode]]: "If your liver doesn't have glycogen stored, you depend on cortisol, which activates the conversion of protein to sugar and fat. You end up destroying a big part of the protein you've just eaten." Fructose, by maintaining liver glycogen and moderating insulin secretion, prevents this catabolic cascade.

## Practical Recommendations

Peat's baseline dietary formula was consistent across decades of interviews and correspondence: "A daily diet that includes two quarts of [[milk]] and a quart of [[orange-juice|orange juice]] provides enough fructose and other sugars for general resistance to stress, but larger amounts of fruit juice, [[honey]], or other sugars can protect against increased stress, and can reverse some of the established degenerative conditions."

### Sources of Fructose

Peat's preferred sources, in rough order of therapeutic value:

- **Fruit and fruit juice** -- especially [[orange-juice|orange juice]], which combines fructose with potassium, magnesium, and [[bioflavonoids]] that provide additional metabolic support. "Fruits usually have about a 50/50 balance of fructose and glucose," Peat noted, "and besides that balance, fruits contain potassium and magnesium, which help regulate glucose."
- **[[Honey]]** -- roughly equal parts free fructose and free glucose, with documented anti-inflammatory effects. Honey "lowers CRP and homocysteine, as well as triglycerides, glucose, and cholesterol," Peat wrote. It has been used therapeutically "for thousands of years" for wound healing, ulcers, and colitis.
- **White sugar ([[sucrose]])** -- "extremely pure, but it lacks all of the essential nutrients, so it should be considered as a temporary therapeutic material, or as an occasional substitute when good fruit isn't available, or when available honey is allergenic."
- **Dates and raisins** -- Andrew Murray identified dates as containing 32% fructose plus B vitamins and minerals.

### Quantity

Peat recommended "170 to 250 grams of sugar per day" for a person of normal activity eating mixed foods. The fructose component of this--roughly half, from sucrose-based sources--would be 85 to 125 grams daily.

### Context Matters

Fructose's benefits depend on the metabolic context. Combined with saturated fat and adequate protein, fructose supports oxidative metabolism. Combined with PUFA, the benefits are blocked or reversed. Sarah Murray captured the practical distinction: "A really bad example of a way to eat sugar is a donut: white flour (glucose) fried in canola, sunflower, or corn oil (PUFA). You have pure glucose and pure polyunsaturated fatty acids blocking your cells from using that glucose. A good example is ice cream with fresh fruit. There you have a saturated fat and the fruit providing fructose."

### Alcohol Detoxification

Peat noted that fructose paired with alcohol accelerates ethanol clearance by approximately 80%. "If someone's going to drink, they want to have a vodka and orange juice," Sarah Murray suggested, to which Peat agreed: "The fructose actually detoxifies the alcohol better than ordinary glucose."
