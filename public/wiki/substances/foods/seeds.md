# Seeds

The modern superfood movement has elevated seeds to an almost sacred status. Chia pudding, flax smoothies, hemp hearts sprinkled on acai bowls--these are the sacraments of a nutritional philosophy that equates plant compounds with health. Ray Peat's bioenergetic framework dismantles this equation at its foundation. Seeds are not health foods; they are embryos armed with chemical defenses. Every mechanism that makes a seed viable--its mineral-binding [[phytic acid]], its digestion-blocking enzyme inhibitors, its stores of [[polyunsaturated-fats]]--works against the metabolic needs of any animal that eats it. As Peat explained in a [[2022-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2022-kmud|2022 KMUD interview]], "plants create a storage form of nutrients to support the embryo's development. Those nutrients have to be in an insoluble form so they don't wash away while the seed is waiting to germinate." The very insolubility that protects the seed in the soil makes its nutrients unavailable in the gut. And the chemical defenses layered on top--designed to disable the digestive enzymes of any animal that threatens the embryo--constitute an active assault on human [[absorption|nutrient absorption]].

## The Biological Logic of Seed Defense

Seeds exist in a state of suspended animation. A chia seed can sit in dry storage for years without germinating, its minerals locked in phytate complexes, its fats stabilized by natural antioxidants, its proteins shielded by enzyme inhibitors that prevent premature breakdown. Peat described these "preparations for the seed embryo" as going "with specific defenses against those same enzymes"--meaning the enzymes an animal's digestive system would use to extract nutrition are precisely the ones the seed has evolved to neutralize.

Phytic acid is the primary mineral defense. It chelates [[calcium]], [[magnesium]], [[iron]], and [[zinc]] into insoluble complexes that pass through the digestive tract without being absorbed. For the seed, this ensures its mineral reserves remain intact until germination conditions are met. For the person eating a tablespoon of chia seeds in their morning yogurt, it means those minerals--and potentially some of the minerals from the yogurt itself--are being escorted out of the body unused. The anti-nutritional effect extends beyond the seed's own mineral content to impair absorption from the entire meal.

Enzyme inhibitors represent the second line of defense. These are proteins specifically designed to bind and inactivate trypsin, chymotrypsin, and other proteases--the enzymes the human pancreas secretes to digest dietary [[protein]]. When seeds are consumed raw or lightly processed, these inhibitors force the pancreas to work harder, secreting more enzymes to compensate for the ones being neutralized. Over time, this chronic overstimulation has been associated with pancreatic stress and reduced digestive efficiency.

## PUFA: The Metabolic Cost of Seed Fats

The most consequential problem with seeds, as with [[nuts]], is their fatty acid profile. Sarah Murray stated the principle plainly during a [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 KMUD episode on dementia]]: "PUFAs, also known as Omega-3 and Omega-6 oils, are found in all nuts and seeds." This universality is the critical point. There is no low-PUFA seed in the way that macadamia is a low-PUFA nut. Every seed marketed as a superfood is, by the standards of Peat's framework, a concentrated source of the fats most damaging to [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]].

The polyunsaturated fats in seeds suppress [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]] by interfering with every stage of the thyroid hormone pathway--from the binding proteins that transport it in blood to the cellular receptors that respond to it. They promote [[estrogen]] production by increasing [[aromatase]] activity. They accumulate in cell membranes and mitochondrial membranes, where their chemical instability--the very double bonds that define them as "unsaturated"--makes them vulnerable to [[lipid-peroxidation]], generating toxic aldehydes that cross-link proteins and contribute to [[age-pigment|age pigment]] formation. When Peat listed his safe foods, he explicitly excluded "seed-related parts such as nuts," a phrasing that underscores the categorical nature of the exclusion.

## Chia Seeds: The Omega-3 Trap

Chia seeds deserve particular attention because they are marketed on the basis of the very compound that makes them most problematic: alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), an omega-3 polyunsaturated fat. Chia seeds are approximately 60% fat by weight, and roughly 60% of that fat is ALA. This makes chia one of the most concentrated plant sources of omega-3 PUFA in the human food supply.

The health claim--that omega-3 fats reduce inflammation and protect the heart--rests on a biochemical framework that Peat fundamentally rejected. In his view, both omega-3 and omega-6 polyunsaturated fats are immunosuppressive, metabolism-suppressing, and pro-aging. The distinction between "good" omega-3 and "bad" omega-6, which dominates mainstream nutrition, collapses in Peat's framework into a single category: polyunsaturated fats that damage [[mitochondrial-respiration|mitochondrial respiration]] regardless of which carbon the first double bond appears on. Chia seeds deliver an enormous dose of these fats in a form that health-conscious consumers eagerly seek out, believing they are preventing the very diseases that Peat argued PUFA promotes.

## Flax Seeds: PUFA Plus Phytoestrogens

Flax seeds combine a PUFA profile similar to chia--roughly 55% ALA--with an additional concern: lignans. Flax is the richest dietary source of lignans, a class of phytoestrogens that bind to [[estrogen]] receptors and exert estrogenic effects in the body. The health food industry markets flax lignans as beneficial, citing their supposed ability to "balance" hormones. In Peat's framework, any compound that activates estrogen receptors is a metabolic disruptor, not a balancer. Estrogen suppresses [[thyroid]], promotes water retention and [[edema]], stimulates cell proliferation, and drives the inflammatory cascade that underlies [[degenerative-diseases|degenerative disease]].

The combination of high PUFA and high lignan content makes flax seeds doubly problematic--they deliver both the fatty acids that promote aromatase activity and the phytoestrogens that directly stimulate estrogen pathways. Ground flax, recommended by nutritionists for better "nutrient absorption," simply makes both the PUFA and the lignans more bioavailable.

## Sunflower, Pumpkin, Hemp, and Sesame

**Sunflower seeds** are dominated by [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]]--the omega-6 fat that has increased most dramatically in the modern diet. Their fat is approximately 65% linoleic acid, making them one of the most concentrated dietary sources of the specific PUFA that Peat identified as most problematic for human metabolism. Sunflower oil, extracted from these seeds, is a staple of processed food manufacturing.

**Pumpkin seeds** carry a somewhat lower but still substantial polyunsaturated load, with linoleic acid as their primary PUFA. They are marketed for their zinc and magnesium content, but phytic acid ensures much of those minerals remain unabsorbed--an irony that captures the central problem with seeds as mineral sources.

**Hemp seeds** derive roughly 75% of their fat from polyunsaturated fatty acids, split between linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid. The health food industry celebrates this as an "ideal omega ratio," but in Peat's framework, the ratio between two harmful fats is irrelevant. Total PUFA burden is what matters.

**Sesame seeds** add oxalates to the standard seed problems of PUFA and phytic acid. Oxalic acid binds calcium into insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, further depleting the mineral that Peat considered most protective. The tahini and sesame paste common in Middle Eastern cuisine concentrate both the PUFA and the oxalates.

## Soaking, Sprouting, and the Limits of Preparation

Traditional food preparation cultures discovered that soaking, sprouting, and fermenting seeds could reduce some of their anti-nutritional properties. Soaking activates phytase enzymes that partially break down phytic acid. Sprouting initiates the germination process, mobilizing some of the locked minerals and reducing enzyme inhibitor concentrations. These observations are valid as far as they go, and Peat never disputed the biochemistry of traditional preparation.

But preparation methods address the mineral-binding and enzyme-inhibiting problems while leaving the fatty acid composition completely unchanged. A sprouted flax seed still contains the same 55% alpha-linolenic acid as an unsprouted one. A soaked chia seed has slightly less phytic acid but the same massive PUFA load. The anti-nutrient problem is reducible through preparation; the PUFA problem is not. And in Peat's hierarchy of dietary concerns, PUFA accumulation--with its effects on thyroid, estrogen, and mitochondrial function--ranks as the more serious and lasting damage.

## The Superfood Marketing Inversion

The popularity of seeds as health foods represents one of the sharpest inversions between mainstream nutrition and Peat's framework. The compounds that make seeds popular--omega-3 fatty acids, lignans, "fiber" (often phytic acid by another name)--are precisely the compounds Peat identified as metabolically harmful. The seed industry has successfully marketed the seed's chemical defense system as a nutritional benefit, rebranding anti-nutrients as "bioactive compounds" and polyunsaturated fats as "essential fatty acids."

> PUFAs, also known as Omega-3 and Omega-6 oils, are found in all nuts and seeds.

Peat's position was uncompromising: the human body has no dietary requirement for polyunsaturated fat in any meaningful quantity. The so-called "essential fatty acid" doctrine, which provides the scientific justification for seed consumption, was a framework Peat challenged throughout his career. The small amounts of PUFA that the body genuinely needs are abundantly available in [[eggs]], [[milk]], and [[meat]]--foods that deliver them alongside [[saturated fats]], [[protein]], and minerals in bioavailable form, without the anti-nutritional payload that seeds carry. There is no metabolic function that requires eating seeds, and their consumption introduces compounds that actively impair the metabolic functions Peat considered most important.
