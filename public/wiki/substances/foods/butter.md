# Butter

Butter is one of Ray Peat's recommended [[saturated fat]] sources—a food demonized by decades of misguided nutritional policy but now increasingly vindicated by research. In Peat's framework, butter from pasture-fed animals provides protective saturated fats, conjugated linoleic acid (CLA), butyric acid, and [[vitamin A]] in forms the body can readily use. The key distinction is always source: butter from grain-fed animals contains more [[PUFA]], undermining the very benefits that make butter valuable.

As Peat explained in a [[2008-07-21-politics-and-science-politics-science-fats|2008 discussion of fats]], "As a major part of the diet, I think butter, beef fat, lamb fat, and [[coconut oil]] are the safe ones. And cocoa butter—chocolate."

## The Protective Saturated Fats

Butter's saturated fats provide metabolic stability that polyunsaturated fats cannot. Where PUFA oxidize readily, suppress [[thyroid]] function, and promote [[inflammation]], saturated fats remain stable and support efficient oxidative [[metabolism]].

Peat's research interest in saturated fats extended to their therapeutic applications: "Some of the best studies are using the long-chain saturated fats as well as ordinary butter fats for treating liver disease. Alcoholic hepatitis, for example, is being successfully treated with saturated fat."

This protective effect was observed clinically before it was tested experimentally. As Peat recounted, "An Indian researcher noticed that in the butter regions of India, alcoholics didn't develop hepatitis or cirrhosis. He tested his observation on rats and found that if he fed them unsaturated oils, alcohol caused cirrhosis and hepatitis. If he fed them saturated fats, it didn't."

## CLA and Butyric Acid

Butter contains unique fatty acids not found in other fats. Peat addressed these directly in email exchanges: "They [conjugated linoleic acid and butyric acid] aren't necessary, but the CLA in cream and butter are probably responsible for some of their good effects."

CLA has anti-cancer and metabolic benefits documented in research. Butyric acid supports intestinal health and has anti-inflammatory properties. These compounds are more concentrated in butter from grass-fed animals, adding another reason to prioritize pasture-raised sources.

## Practical Integration

Peat's own dietary practice combined butter with other protective fats: "I use 1% [[milk]], some butter and hydrogenated [[coconut oil]], to keep polyunsaturated fats to a minimum."

The combination reflects his systematic approach—using multiple saturated fat sources while minimizing PUFA exposure. The goal is not maximizing any single food but creating a dietary pattern that supports [[metabolism]] rather than undermining it.

For cooking, butter's relatively high smoke point and stability make it superior to vegetable oils. Its saturated fats don't oxidize under heat the way polyunsaturated fats do, avoiding the lipid peroxidation products that damage tissues.

## Source Quality

Like [[eggs]] and [[milk]], butter quality depends heavily on the animal's diet. Cows eating their natural grass diet produce butter with more saturated fat, more CLA, more vitamin A, and less PUFA. Cows fed corn and soy—the industrial standard—produce butter with a less favorable fatty acid profile.

The practical guidance from Peat's dietary framework: seek pasture-fed butter when available, use it generously for cooking and eating, and pair it with other protective fats like coconut oil to create a saturated-fat-dominant dietary fat profile.
