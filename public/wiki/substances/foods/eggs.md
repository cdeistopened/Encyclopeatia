# Eggs

Eggs occupy an unusual position in Ray Peat's dietary framework—highly valued for their [[cholesterol]] and nutrient density, but with significant caveats about quality. The industrialization of egg production has fundamentally changed what an egg contains, with modern commercial eggs having lower cholesterol and higher [[PUFA]] content than eggs from genuinely pastured chickens. This matters because the cholesterol in eggs is one of their primary nutritional virtues, while the polyunsaturated fats in industrial eggs undermine their benefits.

As Peat explained in email exchanges, "There can be a great difference between eggs from chickens that really have adequate pasture, and the standard ones, but the labels aren't likely to contain enough information. 'Organic-free range' chickens in the US are usually fed soy and corn in a crowded outdoor pen."

## The Cholesterol Advantage

Peat consistently defended dietary cholesterol against decades of misguided medical advice. In a [[2011-09-29-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-cholesterol-and-saturated-fats|2011 episode on cholesterol]], he explained the industrial transformation: "With the industrialization of the egg industry, the cholesterol content went down and the polyunsaturated content went up. If you can get non-industrial eggs from chickens that have pastures, they are going to be a lot better."

The reason cholesterol matters: "600 milligrams of cholesterol in your diet increases the oxidative stability of your lipid particles in your bloodstream." Dietary cholesterol helps stabilize blood lipids against the oxidation that drives atherosclerosis—the opposite of what conventional medicine claimed for decades.

For people wanting to raise their cholesterol levels, Peat noted that eggs are not the most efficient method: "You can make cholesterol if you have enough of all the other nutrients. I recommend drinking a quart or two of [[orange juice]] per day for a person who wants to bring their cholesterol up quickly. It's much more efficient than eating a dozen eggs."

## Preparation and Pairing

Peat's practical advice on eggs focused on preparation methods and nutrient synergy. "Soft boiling eggs is probably best," he wrote. "Scrambling them probably does cause some heat damage, but the difference in vitamin content is too small to matter."

Raw egg yolks are acceptable in appropriate contexts—"eggnogs for example"—though whole raw eggs carry some risk from the avidin in raw egg whites binding [[biotin]].

The pairing recommendation reflects Peat's understanding of protein utilization: "To use the protein of 2 eggs efficiently it would be good to have a glass of [[milk]] and a large glass of orange juice." The combination provides the full amino acid profile and supportive nutrients that optimize protein assimilation.

## Quality Matters More Than Quantity

Peat's own consumption pattern reflected his quality concerns: "In the US I seldom eat more than one large egg per day, in Mexico where I know where the chickens live and what they eat, I eat more of them."

This is not a blanket restriction on eggs but a practical response to industrial agriculture. When chickens eat their natural diet of insects, worms, and grass rather than soy and corn, their eggs contain more [[saturated fat]], more cholesterol, and less PUFA. The difference is substantial enough to change whether eggs are a metabolic asset or liability.
