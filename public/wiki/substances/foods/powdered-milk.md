# Powdered Milk

In a nutritional landscape dominated by freshness anxiety--where the assumption is that processing always degrades food--Ray Peat's position on powdered milk is characteristically pragmatic. He did not romanticize it. Fresh [[milk]] and [[cheese]] remained his preferred dairy sources. But when those were unavailable, powdered milk was not merely acceptable--it was, in Peat's words, "a good addition to the diet, because of the high ratio of calcium to phosphate, as well as other nutrients." The same mineral logic that made fresh milk the cornerstone of his dietary framework applied to its dried form. Calcium was calcium, and the body's need for it did not pause when the refrigerator was empty.

## The Calcium-to-Phosphate Ratio: Why It Matters

The nutritional case for powdered milk rests on the same principle that elevates all dairy in Peat's framework: its [[calcium]]-to-[[phosphate]] ratio. Modern diets overwhelmingly favor phosphate, which is concentrated in [[meat]], [[grains]], nuts, seeds, and virtually every processed food. When phosphate exceeds calcium, [[parathyroid hormone]] rises to mobilize calcium from bones--a compensatory mechanism that, over time, promotes [[inflammation]], soft tissue [[calcification]], and the very bone loss that calcium-phobic dieters fear.

Peat's recommendation was unambiguous: "Unless you like cheese and milk, a calcium supplement would be the only way to balance the phosphate." Powdered milk offers a third option--not as pleasurable as fresh dairy, but nutritionally effective. A hundred grams of nonfat dry milk powder contains roughly 1,250 mg of calcium against approximately 1,000 mg of phosphorus, maintaining the favorable ratio that keeps parathyroid hormone suppressed and calcium in the bones where it belongs.

This is not a minor physiological detail. In Peat's framework, elevated parathyroid hormone is a driver of aging itself--it promotes intracellular calcium accumulation (distinct from dietary calcium), increases [[cortisol]], and contributes to the degenerative calcification of arteries, kidneys, and other soft tissues. Adequate dietary calcium from sources like powdered milk works against this entire cascade.

## Processing Matters: Spray-Dried Over Drum-Dried

Peat's acceptance of powdered milk came with a qualification about heat damage. As he noted, "Reduced milk is OK if the heat wasn't very high." This distinction matters because not all drying methods are equivalent.

Spray-drying, the dominant commercial method, atomizes liquid milk into a fine mist that passes through a chamber of hot air. The milk particles are exposed to heat for only seconds, preserving most of the protein structure and nutrient content. Drum-drying, by contrast, spreads milk onto heated metal cylinders, subjecting it to prolonged high temperatures that can denature proteins, destroy vitamins, and produce Maillard reaction products--the browned, caramelized compounds that signal excessive heat exposure.

For practical purposes, most commercially available nonfat dry milk powder in the United States and Europe is spray-dried. The flavor is bland rather than cooked, and the powder dissolves cleanly in liquid--both signs of minimal heat damage. Drum-dried powder, which has a slightly toasted flavor and grainier texture, is less common in retail settings but still found in some bulk and institutional products. Peat's guidance suggests choosing the mildest processing available, which in most cases means standard spray-dried powder from a conventional grocery store.

## Peat's Own Use: The Ice Cream Recipe

Peat was not merely a theorist of powdered milk--he used it in his own kitchen. His homemade [[ice-cream]] recipe, shared in email correspondence, reveals its practical role: "I blend an egg (warmed to 40 degrees C) with a cup of sugar (also warmed) and a cup of [[coconut-oil]] until it's smoothly emulsified, and maybe half a cup of powdered milk for extra texture, then add milk to fill the blender."

The powdered milk serves dual purposes in this formulation. Nutritionally, it increases the calcium and protein density of the mixture. Texturally, the additional milk solids improve the body of the ice cream, preventing the icy crystallization that plagues homemade frozen desserts. The casein and lactose in powdered milk act as natural stabilizers--achieving what commercial ice cream manufacturers accomplish with guar gum, carrageenan, and other additives that Peat warned against.

This recipe also illustrates a broader point about Peat's dietary philosophy: he built his preferred foods from simple, whole ingredients and was willing to use processed forms (like powdered milk) when they served a clear nutritional or functional purpose without introducing harmful additives.

## Practical Applications

Powdered milk's versatility explains its popularity in the bioenergetic community. Common uses include:

**Coffee and tea.** A tablespoon or two of nonfat dry milk powder stirred into coffee provides a calcium boost without the volume of liquid milk--useful for people who drink multiple cups daily and want to increase calcium intake without consuming quarts of fluid. The powder dissolves easily in hot beverages and adds a mild creaminess.

**Smoothies and shakes.** Blended with [[fruit]], [[honey]], or [[sugar]] and liquid milk, powdered milk increases the protein and calcium content of what is already a Peat-friendly meal. Combined with [[orange-juice]], it creates a calcium-rich drink that balances the phosphate from other dietary sources.

**Baking and cooking.** Added to pancakes, custards, or puddings, powdered milk enriches the nutritional profile without altering flavor significantly. It is particularly useful in recipes that already call for milk, where it can simply be added to the dry ingredients for extra mineral content.

**Travel and limited access.** This is perhaps the most straightforward application--and the one Peat himself emphasized. For people living in areas where fresh dairy is expensive, unavailable, or of poor quality, powdered milk provides the calcium-to-phosphate ratio that Peat considered non-negotiable. A bag of milk powder in the pantry is insurance against the metabolic consequences of calcium deficiency.

## Dosage: A Hundred Grams or More

Peat's recommendation of "100 grams or more" of powdered milk per day as a dietary supplement is substantial--roughly three and a half ounces, or about a cup of powder. This amount provides approximately 1,250 mg of calcium, which aligns with his broader recommendation of 2,000-2,500 mg of daily calcium from all sources.

For someone already drinking a quart or two of fresh milk daily--as Peat himself did, averaging "a gallon a day" over the years--powdered milk would be unnecessary. The recommendation is specifically for situations where fresh dairy intake falls short. A person eating meat-heavy meals without much dairy, for instance, would accumulate significant phosphate excess that powdered milk could help offset.

## Not a Replacement, But a Reliable Backup

Peat's hierarchy was clear: fresh milk first, cheese as an excellent alternative, powdered milk as a practical backup. The distinction is not about some essential nutrient lost in drying--the calcium, protein, and lactose survive the process intact. Rather, fresh milk contains living enzymes, intact immunoglobulins, and a full complement of water-soluble vitamins (particularly B vitamins) that are partially diminished by processing. Fresh milk from well-fed cows also contains fat-soluble vitamins A, D, and K2 in the cream fraction, which are absent from nonfat powder.

But nutritional perfectionism was never Peat's style. A person drinking reconstituted powdered milk with adequate [[sugar]], [[eggs]], and other protective foods is in a vastly better metabolic position than someone avoiding dairy entirely out of concern about processing. The calcium-to-phosphate ratio--the primary reason Peat valued dairy above all other food categories--is fully preserved in the powder. And in a world where metabolic stress from calcium deficiency, [[parathyroid hormone]] excess, and phosphate overload drives much of the degenerative disease Peat spent his career studying, a bag of powdered milk on the shelf is a simple, inexpensive countermeasure.
