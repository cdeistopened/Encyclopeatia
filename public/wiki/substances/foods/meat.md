# Meat

Meat occupies a complex position in Ray Peat's dietary framework—valuable for its complete protein and nutrients, but problematic because of its [[phosphate]] load, [[tryptophan]] content, and potential [[PUFA]] contamination from industrial animal feeding. Unlike the unconditional endorsement Peat gave to [[milk]], [[eggs]], and [[fruit]], his recommendations for meat always came with caveats about quantity, preparation, and balance.

As Peat explained in email exchanges, "It's best to have more calcium than phosphate, and meat is heavy on phosphate. Cheese would be a good way to get enough calcium."

## The Phosphate Problem

Muscle meat's high phosphorus-to-[[calcium]] ratio creates metabolic stress. In a [[2019-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-tryptophan|2019 episode]], Peat explained: "The high phosphate content relative to calcium in meat is a big problem. It wastes energy. It acts like a constant subliminal stimulus that tends to load the cells up with calcium as if they were fatigued already."

This phosphate burden activates [[parathyroid hormone]], which mobilizes calcium from bones to counteract the mineral imbalance. Over time, high-meat diets without adequate calcium create the conditions for [[osteoporosis]] and soft tissue calcification—calcium leaving bones where it belongs and depositing in arteries and joints where it causes damage.

The solution is not eliminating meat but balancing it with calcium-rich foods. Peat's practical advice: "Unless you like cheese and milk, a calcium supplement would be the only way to balance the phosphate. Powdered eggshells are the best calcium supplement, oyster shells are the next best."

## Pairing with Fruit

Peat consistently recommended eating fruit with meat: "Having some fruit, such as [[orange juice]] each time you eat meat will make the protein assimilation much more efficient, so less is needed."

The mechanism involves both blood sugar and cortisol. "Eating protein by itself can cause a big surge of [[cortisol]]. Preceding the protein with some carbohydrate makes the protein go farther, otherwise under the influence of cortisol a lot of protein is used just for energy."

The sugars in fruit—particularly the fructose component—stabilize blood sugar during protein digestion, reducing the stress hormone response that would otherwise waste protein as fuel.

## Gelatin Balance

Beyond phosphate, meat's amino acid profile presents problems for adults who have stopped growing. The [[tryptophan]], [[cysteine]], and [[methionine]] in muscle meat become inflammatory when consumed in excess.

Peat's solution was [[gelatin]]—the protein from connective tissue that lacks these amino acids entirely. By using gelatin-rich foods (bone broth, commercial gelatin) alongside or instead of some muscle meat, the overall amino acid balance shifts toward the anti-inflammatory profile that supports adult tissue maintenance without promoting inflammation.

## Source Quality

Like all animal foods in Peat's framework, meat quality depends on what the animal ate. Grain-fed animals, raised on corn and soy, produce meat with higher PUFA content. Their fat stores contain the same polyunsaturated oils that suppress [[thyroid]] function and promote [[inflammation]] in humans.

Grass-fed or pasture-raised animals produce meat with more [[saturated fat]] and less PUFA—a better fatty acid profile that doesn't undermine the meat's nutritional benefits.
