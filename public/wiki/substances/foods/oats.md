# Oats

Oatmeal is the breakfast cereal that launched a thousand health claims. The American Heart Association endorses it for cholesterol lowering. Nutrition labels trumpet its beta-glucan fiber. Instagram influencers soak it overnight in mason jars and photograph it with berries. In Ray Peat's framework, nearly every one of these selling points is either irrelevant or actively harmful. Oats are a grain -- a seed -- and they carry the full arsenal of anti-nutritional defenses that Peat associated with seeds: [[phytic acid]], enzyme inhibitors, gut-irritating proteins, and indigestible polysaccharides that feed intestinal bacteria. When Peat compiled his safe food list in a [[2022-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2022-kmud|November 2022 KMUD interview]], he specified "milk, cheese without additives, eggs, low-starch fruit, and generally meat and fish" -- a list that explicitly excludes "grains, legumes, and other seed-related parts such as nuts." Oats did not make the cut.

## Avenin: The Gluten That Isn't Called Gluten

The protein fraction of oats includes avenin, a prolamin structurally related to the [[gluten]] found in [[whole-wheat|wheat]], barley, and rye. While oats are often marketed as "gluten-free," this designation is technically about the absence of wheat gluten specifically, not the absence of prolamin proteins altogether. Avenin can trigger similar intestinal reactions in sensitive individuals -- gut inflammation, increased intestinal permeability, and immune activation -- because the prolamin family shares structural features that human digestive enzymes handle poorly. For someone already dealing with intestinal [[inflammation]] or bacterial overgrowth, avenin adds another insult to an already compromised barrier.

The distinction matters because the "gluten-free oats" label creates a false sense of safety. A person avoiding wheat for gut health reasons who switches to oatmeal may be trading one prolamin irritant for a closely related one, while believing they have solved the problem.

## The Cholesterol Paradox

The flagship health claim for oats -- that they lower [[cholesterol]] -- is precisely the kind of claim Peat's framework inverts. Mainstream cardiology treats cholesterol reduction as inherently beneficial. Peat argued the opposite: cholesterol is a protective substance, essential for the synthesis of [[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], [[DHEA]], and other steroids that defend against [[stress]] and aging. A food that lowers cholesterol is, in this view, depleting the body's raw material for its most important protective hormones.

The beta-glucan fiber in oats accomplishes its cholesterol-lowering effect by binding [[bile]] acids in the intestine and preventing their reabsorption, forcing the liver to pull cholesterol from the bloodstream to synthesize replacement bile. This is the same mechanism Peat acknowledged when he mentioned "well-cooked oat bran" as one of several fiber options that can assist bile excretion in a KMUD episode on inflammation. The distinction is critical: Peat recognized that binding and excreting bile can be useful for removing [[endotoxin]] and [[estrogen]] metabolites that are excreted through bile, but this is a targeted therapeutic use of fiber, not an endorsement of oatmeal as a daily breakfast food. The difference between using oat bran as a bile-binding tool and eating a bowl of oatmeal every morning is the difference between a specific intervention and a dietary staple.

## The Mineral Absorption Problem

Like all [[grains]], oats are rich in [[phytic acid]] -- a phosphorus storage compound that binds [[calcium]], [[magnesium]], [[zinc]], and [[iron]] in the digestive tract, forming insoluble complexes that pass through without being absorbed. A person eating oatmeal with [[milk]] is effectively neutralizing a portion of the milk's calcium before it can reach the bloodstream. This is the opposite of what Peat's framework requires: a high calcium-to-[[phosphate]] ratio, with minerals available for absorption rather than locked up by anti-nutrients.

Soaking oats overnight -- the trendy "overnight oats" preparation -- is sometimes claimed to reduce phytic acid through enzymatic breakdown. But the reduction is partial at best, and the overnight soak introduces a separate problem: raw or minimally processed grain sitting at room temperature becomes a medium for bacterial growth. The uncooked starch granules that survive this preparation are among the least digestible forms of [[starch]], available to feed bacteria in the lower intestine and generate [[endotoxin]] and intestinal gas.

## Processing Methods: Better and Worse, but Never Good

Steel-cut oats, rolled oats, and instant oats represent different degrees of mechanical and thermal processing. Steel-cut oats are minimally processed and require the longest cooking time; rolled oats have been steamed and flattened; instant oats have been pre-cooked and dried. From a digestive standpoint, more processing is actually less problematic -- the pre-cooking of instant oats breaks down more starch granules than the raw steel-cut form. But even thorough cooking does not eliminate avenin, does not fully neutralize phytic acid, and does not change the fact that oats remain a grain with all the seed-defense characteristics Peat cautioned against.

> Well-cooked oat bran can help bile excretion.

That single concession -- well-cooked oat bran, specifically, for bile binding -- represents the outer limit of Peat's tolerance for oats. It is a narrow therapeutic application, not a general dietary recommendation. The bran fraction, heavily cooked, used as a fiber supplement rather than a food, occupies a different category than the bowl of oatmeal that most people imagine when they think of oats as "healthy."

## What to Eat Instead

The breakfast problem is where oats gain their cultural power. People need something to eat in the morning, and oatmeal feels substantial, warm, and wholesome. Peat's framework offers alternatives that are genuinely nourishing rather than anti-nutritional: [[eggs]] cooked in [[butter]], ripe [[fruit]] with [[cheese]], [[milk]] with [[coffee]], [[orange-juice]] with [[gelatin]], or a glass of [[milk]] with [[honey]]. These foods provide high-quality [[protein]], easily absorbed minerals, simple sugars that support [[thyroid]] function, and [[saturated-fat]] that stabilizes metabolism -- none of which oats deliver, and several of which oats actively interfere with.
