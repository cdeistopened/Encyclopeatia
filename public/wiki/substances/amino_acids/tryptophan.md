# Tryptophan

Tryptophan is the amino acid that becomes dangerous when you stop growing. In Ray Peat's framework, this precursor to [[serotonin]] is essential during development but becomes a source of chronic excitation and [[inflammation]] in adults whose protein intake doesn't adjust to their reduced need for tissue building. The solution isn't eliminating tryptophan—impossible, since all proteins except [[gelatin]] contain it—but shifting the dietary protein balance away from muscle meats and toward gelatin-rich foods.

As Peat explained in a [[2019-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-tryptophan|2019 episode on tryptophan]], "Tryptophan and its metabolites, including serotonin, are essential for life. Every kind of organism is built with proteins containing tryptophan."

## The Excitability Problem

Peat traced tryptophan's importance to the electronic properties of proteins—the fluorescence and conductivity that Albert Szent-Györgyi identified as the physical basis of life. "This electronic property is at the heart of sensation, movement, secretion, and consciousness," Peat explained. "When something goes wrong with tryptophan or its metabolites, it disturbs life at its deepest level."

The problem arises after growth stops. During development, tryptophan is consumed building new tissues. In adults maintaining existing tissue, the same dietary intake creates a surplus: "During the growth period, you need the full balanced range of amino acids. But when growth stops, all you need is to maintain the renewal of skin, intestine lining, lungs, and brain proteins. That is a very small amount of growth compared to infancy. Those amino acids are now at risk of becoming random, excitatory, inflammation-promoting processes."

## Serotonin: The Downstream Problem

Tryptophan converts to serotonin via an enzyme activated by [[estrogen]]. As Peat described in a [[2011-08-25-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-serotonin-and-endotoxin|2011 episode on serotonin]], "Estrogen activates the enzyme that turns tryptophan into serotonin. It is inhibited by [[progesterone]]. The route *out* of serotonin—monoamine oxidase type A—is inhibited by estrogen, so it doesn't break down serotonin."

The estrogen-serotonin connection means that hormonal imbalance amplifies tryptophan's harmful effects. Rising estrogen increases tryptophan-to-serotonin conversion while blocking serotonin breakdown—a double effect that helps explain why serotonin-related problems increase with age and metabolic decline.

## The PUFA Amplification

[[PUFA]] worsen tryptophan metabolism through a specific mechanism Peat identified: "One of the events caused by the excess of n-3 in particular is that the metabolism of tryptophan, which should go largely to form [[niacin]] and [[melatonin]], is diverted. This type of unsaturated fat causes tryptophan to be metabolized in a toxic direction, producing less niacin and more of the excitotoxic quinolinic acid and carcinogenic indole derivatives."

The healthy pathway converts tryptophan to niacin (vitamin B3). Polyunsaturated fats divert metabolism toward toxic products—another mechanism by which PUFA promote disease beyond their direct effects on [[thyroid]] and [[oxidative metabolism]].

## The Gelatin Solution

Gelatin contains no tryptophan, [[cysteine]], or [[methionine]]—the three amino acids Peat identified as problematic in excess. As he explained, "Gelatin is a major protein used for building connective tissues, and it has a complete lack of these excitable proteins. If gelatin becomes a major dietary protein when growth is completed, you reduce the risk of inflammatory and degenerative diseases."

This doesn't mean eliminating other proteins. Muscle meats, [[eggs]], and [[milk]] all contain tryptophan and remain valuable foods. The recommendation is shifting the ratio—using gelatin (from bone broth, commercial gelatin, or collagen supplements) to displace some muscle meat protein, reducing total tryptophan intake without eliminating essential amino acids.

## Meat and Phosphate

Peat added another reason to limit muscle meat beyond tryptophan: "The high phosphate content relative to [[calcium]] in meat is a big problem. It wastes energy. It acts like a constant subliminal stimulus that tends to load the cells up with calcium as if they were fatigued already."

The combination of high tryptophan and high phosphate makes muscle meat a food to balance carefully in adult diets—valuable for its complete protein but needing the counterbalance of gelatin, dairy, and adequate calcium intake.
