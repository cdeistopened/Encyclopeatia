# Glycine

Glycine is the protective amino acid—the counterweight to the inflammatory amino acids ([[tryptophan]], [[cysteine]], [[methionine]]) that become problematic in adult diets. In Ray Peat's framework, glycine represents the anti-aging, anti-inflammatory pole of protein nutrition, concentrated in [[gelatin]] and connective tissues but largely absent from the muscle meats that dominate modern protein intake.

As Peat explained in a [[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|2019 episode]], "Glycine and proline are the main reasons for using gelatin. Gelatin is completely free of the amino acids that are pro-inflammatory, anti-thyroid, and even carcinogenic: tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine."

## The Protective Amino Acid

Unlike the excitatory amino acids that drive [[inflammation]] and [[oxidative stress]], glycine stabilizes cellular function and supports energy production. As Peat characterized the amino acid spectrum in a [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 episode]]: "Tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine" are the harmful ones, while "glycine and proline are the protective, energy-providing, and stabilizing amino acids found in gelatin."

This protective function becomes increasingly important after growth stops. During development, the body uses tryptophan and methionine for tissue building. In adulthood, these amino acids become surplus materials that promote inflammatory processes. Glycine, by contrast, remains beneficial throughout life.

## Gelatin: The Glycine Source

The practical way to increase glycine intake is through gelatin—the protein from connective tissues that consists primarily of glycine and proline. As noted in a [[2022-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-february-2022-kmud|2022 episode]], gelatin "contains the amino acid glycine, which has lots of other health benefits. So not only is it deficient in the mTOR-stimulating methionine, it's also practically pure glycine."

Traditional cuisines that used the whole animal—including bones, skin, and connective tissues in broths and stews—naturally provided abundant glycine. Modern diets emphasizing muscle meat without these traditional preparations create an amino acid imbalance that promotes aging and degenerative disease.

## Anti-Inflammatory Mechanism

Glycine's protective effects operate through multiple mechanisms. It opposes the excitatory effects of glutamate and the inflammatory effects of methionine. It supports the liver's detoxification processes. It helps maintain the collagen matrix that structures tissues throughout the body.

Peat described gelatin as "a very protective protein for a mature person who isn't growing." The distinction matters: children building new tissues need complete proteins with all amino acids. Adults maintaining existing tissues benefit from shifting toward glycine-rich proteins that don't fuel inflammatory processes.

## Supplementation

Glycine can be supplemented directly or consumed through gelatin. Commercial gelatin, collagen supplements, and homemade bone broth all provide glycine in the context of the other amino acids (particularly proline) that make gelatin beneficial.

For people with functioning kidneys, glycine is well-tolerated in substantial amounts. As Peat noted in a [[2014-10-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-longevity|2014 episode on longevity]], "if the kidneys are working, glycine is non-toxic."

The goal is not to eliminate other proteins but to shift the ratio—using gelatin to balance [[meat]] rather than replacing protein entirely.
