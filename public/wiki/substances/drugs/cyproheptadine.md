# Cyproheptadine

Cyproheptadine (brand name Periactin) is the pharmaceutical Ray Peat reached for more frequently than any other after [[thyroid]] and [[aspirin]]. It is an old, off-patent antihistamine — first marketed in the early 1960s — whose continued usefulness comes from a side effect that the regulatory file treats as incidental: it blocks the [[serotonin|serotonin]] receptors that Peat considered the chemical signature of stress and inflammation. In his framework, the drug is not a niche allergy medicine but a broad rescue tool. "I think cyproheptadine is a safe antiserotonin drug," he wrote in a message preserved in his [[antiserotonin-drugs|antiserotonin-drugs correspondence]], and he treated it as such across migraines, sleep problems, anxiety, intestinal spasm, cancer-related wasting, and acute reactions to allergens or supplements. The dosing he taught — fractional tablets, often half a milligram or one milligram, escalated cautiously — is one of the most consistent operational details in his entire body of writing.

## What it is

Cyproheptadine is a first-generation antihistamine that combines unusually strong antagonism at the 5-HT2 serotonin receptors with conventional H1 (histamine) blockade and mild anticholinergic activity. Pharmacology texts add calcium-channel blockade and aldosterone antagonism to the list. The combination is what Peat valued. In a [[cyproheptadine|message preserved in his cyproheptadine correspondence]] he described the spread of actions in a single sentence: "Cyproheptadine, 2 to 4 mg at bedtime, would help with his sleep as well as the cancer. It also has calcium blocking action, aldosterone antagonism, and antagonizes serotonin's antidiuretic effect."

The drug is sold under the brand name Periactin in the United States and as generic cyproheptadine internationally. It is available in 4 mg tablets and as a syrup intended for pediatric use; the syrup makes very low doses (a fraction of a milligram) easy to titrate. In the United States it requires a prescription. Many countries — notably France, much of Latin America, and several Asian markets — sell it over the counter, often as an appetite stimulant. The drug has been in continuous clinical use for more than sixty years, which means its long-term safety profile, side-effect spectrum, and contraindications are unusually well-characterized for a substance Peat recommended.

The pharmacokinetics matter for how it is dosed. Cyproheptadine has a half-life of roughly eight to twelve hours, with onset of effect within thirty to sixty minutes of an oral dose. This is fast enough to function as an acute intervention and short enough that a small evening dose has cleared substantially by morning, which Peat exploited when using it for sleep.

## Peat's applied uses

Five clinical settings recur in his correspondence and interviews. Each one maps to a specific facet of serotonin and histamine biology, and each one carries a characteristic dose range.

**Sleep onset and sleep maintenance.** Histamine and serotonin both promote wakefulness; cyproheptadine blocks both. Peat treated insomnia as primarily a gut problem driven by [[endotoxin|endotoxin]]-stimulated histamine and serotonin release, but he was willing to interrupt the cycle pharmaceutically while the underlying physiology was being fixed. "Histamine and serotonin promote wakefulness, and cyproheptadine blocks those," he wrote in a [[sleep|sleep email]], "but its other effects aren't very pleasant. Have you tried diphenhydramine?" The qualification matters: he preferred [[diphenhydramine]] (Benadryl) as the routine antihistamine for sleep, and turned to cyproheptadine when the serotonin component was prominent — when, for example, the patient was waking with anxiety, intestinal symptoms, or hot flashes rather than simple difficulty falling asleep. The starting dose is the smallest fraction of a tablet that produces an effect. "It's good to start with about half a milligram, at bedtime, to judge its effects when sedation isn't risky."

**Migraine, headache, and supplement reactions.** Cyproheptadine has a long history as a migraine prophylactic in conventional pediatric and neurological practice, and Peat used it in the same role. In a [[headaches-and-migraines|message about headaches triggered by vitamin A or magnesium chloride contaminants]], he asked: "Is cyproheptadine available where you are? It's probably the safest of the antiserotonin drugs." In another exchange in the same file, he advised: "Cyproheptadine, a small amount at bedtime, reduces night stress, might help the headaches and shakiness." The pattern is consistent — small dose, evening, used both prophylactically and as a rescue when an allergen or contaminant has provoked a vascular-inflammatory reaction.

**Anxiety, panic, and acute serotonin events.** When the symptom picture is acute — a panic episode, a serotonin syndrome from over-supplementation with tryptophan or 5-HTP, a reaction triggered by withdrawing from an SSRI — cyproheptadine is the rescue drug in Peat's toolkit. "Benadryl and cyproheptadine are probably both helpful" for SSRI withdrawal, he wrote in his [[anti-depressants|anti-depressants correspondence]], placing them alongside [[progesterone]] and [[pregnenolone]] in his standard wean-off protocol. Conventional emergency medicine reaches for the same drug in serotonin syndrome for the same reason — it is the most reliable oral 5-HT2 antagonist available without a controlled-substance prescription. See [[anxiety-depression]] for the broader framework into which Peat fit these acute uses.

**Appetite restoration and wasting.** The most widely-known mainstream use of cyproheptadine — appetite stimulation in undereaters, in pediatric failure-to-thrive, in cancer cachexia, in some eating disorders — is also a serotonin-mediated effect. Serotonin is anorexigenic; blocking it restores appetite. Peat used the same property therapeutically. "Cyproheptadine, 2 to 4 mg at bedtime, would help with his sleep as well as the cancer," he wrote in his [[cyproheptadine|primary cyproheptadine file]], referencing a cancer patient. He cited the conventional literature on the use of cyproheptadine for "appetite stimulation and prevention of pain and vomiting in abdominal migraine and cyclic vomiting syndrome." The appetite effect explains a substantial fraction of cyproheptadine's reported weight gain — it is more accurately described as restoration of normal hunger in someone whose serotonin tone had been suppressing it.

**Irritable bowel, intestinal spasm, and gut-driven symptoms.** Because 95% of the body's serotonin is in the intestine, the gut is where cyproheptadine's antiserotonin action is most easily demonstrated. "Cyproheptadine might be helpful for reducing sensitivity to intestinal irritants," he wrote in his [[serotonin|serotonin email correspondence]]. The conventional literature he cited included a controlled trial in children with functional abdominal pain showing 86% improvement on cyproheptadine versus 36% on placebo (Sadeghian et al., 2008). For an adult with IBS, he framed the same intervention in a different message: "Have you tried cyproheptadine for the IBS?" The dose for gut symptoms is typically lower than the sleep dose — a fraction of a tablet, often taken with the meal most likely to provoke symptoms.

The dose range across all of these uses is narrower than its breadth of indications might suggest. Half a milligram to four milligrams covers most of the field. "Some people get very sleepy with just two milligrams of Periactin, so I think it's good to start with one mg. the first night. Two milligrams can make a big difference, and when symptoms stop the effects can last for days without using it." That last detail — durable effect from intermittent dosing — is unusual among psychoactive drugs and is part of why Peat treated cyproheptadine as a rescue tool rather than a chronic medication.

## Why it works

Cyproheptadine's clinical effects map directly onto Peat's [[serotonin|serotonin]] framework. The fully developed argument lives on the serotonin page; the short version is that serotonin is not a happiness molecule but a damage-induced inflammatory mediator that shifts metabolism from oxidative respiration toward glycolysis, raises [[cortisol|cortisol]] through the pituitary stress axis, increases intestinal permeability, promotes [[fibrosis|fibrotic remodeling]], and reproduces in slow motion much of the pathology that defines aging and degeneration. Anything that lowers serotonin tone is therefore broadly protective; anything that raises it is suspect even when it produces transient mood improvement.

The drug's unique signal is that it is a *direct* receptor blockade rather than a synthesis inhibitor or a reuptake modulator. SSRIs raise extracellular serotonin and gradually downregulate postsynaptic receptors over weeks. Tryptophan restriction or [[gelatin|gelatin]] loading reduces the precursor supply that feeds serotonin synthesis, with effects that emerge over days. Cyproheptadine occupies the 5-HT2 receptor within an hour of an oral dose, and the receptor remains occupied for several hours. The therapeutic response is correspondingly fast.

The dual blockade matters. [[Histamine|Histamine]] and serotonin are released together from mast cells under the same stimuli — heat, friction, irritation, allergen exposure, and especially [[estrogen|estrogen]]. A pure antihistamine like loratadine or fexofenadine leaves the serotonin half of the cascade intact. A pure 5-HT2 antagonist like ketanserin leaves the histamine half intact. Cyproheptadine blocks both simultaneously, which is why a single small dose can interrupt symptom patterns — premenstrual migraines, hot flashes, post-meal flushing, multi-organ allergy reactions — that conventional drugs treat one symptom at a time.

The auxiliary actions reinforce the main effect. Aldosterone antagonism reduces water retention; calcium-channel blockade contributes to the relaxation of vascular smooth muscle and the antimigraine effect; mild anticholinergic action contributes to the sedation that Peat treated as a feature rather than a bug for nighttime use. Each of these would, on its own, justify a class of patentable drugs. Cyproheptadine has all of them in one molecule that has been off-patent for half a century.

Hans Selye's experimental work provided Peat with the model demonstration. In the [[endotoxin-stress-depression|2011 *Endotoxin, Stress, Depression* newsletter]], he summarized it: "Selye devised animal experiments, in which he showed that an interference with blood circulation that normally didn't produce serious harm, would cause similar (stercoral) ulcers, and also paralysis of the hind legs, if he injected serotonin into the animal. If the anti-serotonin drug, cyproheptadine, was given to the animal before the serotonin, they were protected." A combination of restricted circulation and serotonin produced ulcers and paralysis; the same circulation without the serotonin did not. Cyproheptadine was the agent that demonstrated which factor was doing the damage.

## Failure modes / what to watch

The most common reported effect is sedation. Whether this is a failure mode or a feature depends on the indication. For evening dosing aimed at sleep, anxiety, or migraine prophylaxis, sedation is what is wanted; for daytime use aimed at appetite or intestinal symptoms, it can be limiting. The standard mitigation is dose reduction. Peat repeatedly emphasized that the smallest fraction of a tablet that produces an effect is the correct dose. "Some people get very sleepy with just two milligrams of Periactin, so I think it's good to start with one mg. the first night."

Dry mouth, mild constipation, and urinary hesitancy are the classical anticholinergic complaints. They are usually modest at the small doses Peat recommended and resolve when the drug is withdrawn. Weight gain is a near-universal finding in the appetite-stimulant literature; Peat treated this as a desirable effect in low-weight or wasting patients and as an unwanted effect in patients already at or above weight goals. The mechanism is at least partly the restoration of normal hunger by removing the appetite-suppressing action of serotonin, with a secondary contribution from sedation and reduced movement.

The anticholinergic concern looms larger in the elderly. Several large observational studies have associated chronic, high-dose anticholinergic burden with increased risk of [[dementia|dementia]]. This is covered in detail in the Counter-arguments section below. The practical implication is that cyproheptadine in an eighty-year-old is not the same drug as cyproheptadine in a twenty-five-year-old, and the threshold for chronic daily use is correspondingly higher.

Tolerance to the sedating effect develops within a few days of regular dosing and is one of the reasons Peat treated the drug as a rescue tool rather than a maintenance medication. The antiserotonin effect does not seem to tolerate in the same way; the drug continues to block 5-HT2 receptors as long as it is present in the blood, regardless of how long it has been used. The combination — fading sedation, persistent antiserotonin activity — is part of why intermittent dosing works as well as it does.

Cyproheptadine is not a panic-attack rescue at sub-therapeutic dose. A patient who takes a quarter of a tablet during an acute episode and finds it unhelpful has often simply underdosed. The drug's onset is real but takes thirty to sixty minutes, and the dose has to be high enough to occupy enough receptors to matter. Two milligrams at the onset of a panic episode is more useful than half a milligram, even though half a milligram may be the right maintenance dose for the same patient.

Drug interactions follow predictable lines. Cyproheptadine should not be combined with monoamine oxidase inhibitors. Combination with other anticholinergic drugs increases the burden additively. Peat noted one specific surgical caution: "I don't think anesthesia should be combined with cyproheptadine," he wrote in a [[teeth-gums|dental message]] about pediatric anesthesia. The interaction is not absolute but the combined sedative load is unpredictable enough to warrant deferring the drug until after the procedure.

## Tracking signal

The effects of cyproheptadine show up the same day. For evening use aimed at sleep, the signal is faster onset of sleep, fewer wakings, and absence of the early-morning anxiety wake-up that characterizes high-serotonin sleep architecture. For anxiety or panic, the signal is reduced reactivity within an hour of dosing. For appetite restoration, hunger typically returns within twenty-four hours of the first dose. For intestinal symptoms, post-meal cramping or urgency diminishes within days. For migraine, prophylactic effect is measured over weeks of consistent evening dosing.

The durability signal is also distinctive: Peat repeatedly observed that small, intermittent doses can produce extended periods of symptom relief without further dosing. "When symptoms stop the effects can last for days without using it." This is not the pattern of a drug that has to be taken continuously to work; it is the pattern of a drug that interrupts a self-perpetuating cycle and allows the underlying physiology to recover. Tracking whether the off-drug interval is lengthening over time is a better measure of clinical progress than tracking the dose itself.

The negative tracking signal — that cyproheptadine is *not* helping — usually means one of three things. Either the dose is too low to occupy enough receptors; or the underlying problem is not serotonin-mediated (a frank thyroid deficiency, for example, will not yield to an antiserotonin drug); or the dose is high enough that the anticholinergic and sedative load is creating new symptoms that confound the assessment. The remedy in each case is different: raise the dose, address the underlying physiology, or lower the dose.

Peat was emphatic that the drug works best embedded in a broader framework. "It's important to keep adjusting [[thyroid]] and [[progesterone]] according to temperature, pulse, etc.," he wrote in his [[antiserotonin-drugs|antiserotonin-drugs correspondence]] when asked whether antiserotonin drugs can permanently fix a problem with short-term use. The answer was qualified yes, on the condition that the broader physiology was being moved at the same time. Cyproheptadine alone, without thyroid restoration, dietary correction, and reduction of the serotonin-and-histamine triggers (estrogen, endotoxin, [[PUFA]]), is symptomatic relief rather than treatment.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream position on cyproheptadine is that it is a useful, modestly-prescribed second-line agent for allergic rhinitis, urticaria, and pediatric appetite stimulation, and that newer agents are preferred for most indications. Three specific lines of mainstream concern push back against Peat's enthusiasm.

The first is the **anticholinergic-burden literature on dementia risk**. A series of observational studies (notably the Adult Changes in Thought cohort and the analyses by Gray and colleagues) found associations between cumulative use of strongly anticholinergic medications and later [[dementia|dementia]] diagnosis. Cyproheptadine is on the relevant lists. The studies are observational, the confounders are substantial, and the doses involved are typically much higher and more chronic than the fractional-tablet, intermittent regimen Peat recommended. But the signal is real enough that geriatric guidelines now treat strongly anticholinergic antihistamines as relatively contraindicated in patients over sixty-five for chronic use. A Peat-aligned practitioner using cyproheptadine in an older patient has to weigh this against the alternatives, none of which are pharmacologically equivalent. The most defensible compromise is to use the smallest effective dose, intermittently, and to substitute progesterone, [[thyroid]], and dietary corrections wherever they can carry the load.

The second is the **standard-of-care position that serotonin excess is the cause of depression and anxiety, and that SSRIs are the appropriate treatment**. Peat's framework is the exact inverse of this position. He treated cyproheptadine as a partial rescue from the iatrogenic damage caused by serotonin-promoting drugs. The two frameworks are not reconcilable; whichever one is right, the other is significantly wrong. Readers who want to evaluate the dispute on its merits should read the [[serotonin]] hub article, which lays out the biochemical case in detail. The clinical question for any individual patient is whether their symptoms improve when serotonin is lowered (cyproheptadine, [[thyroid]], [[progesterone]], gelatin, raw carrot) or when it is raised (SSRIs, 5-HTP, tryptophan loading). For most of Peat's correspondents, the first answer was the right one. Whether it generalizes to the wider population is an open empirical question that the literature has not seriously attempted to answer.

The third is the **appetite-stimulant-as-doping critique**. Cyproheptadine has been used illicitly to fatten livestock and, in some countries, to fatten women for cultural beauty standards. The drug functions in the way it is advertised — it restores appetite — but the social uses are not always therapeutic. The critique applies to deceptive or coercive uses of the drug, not to its use in genuine wasting, undereating, or post-illness recovery, which is what Peat recommended it for.

A separate, narrower objection that has appeared in Peat-skeptical writing concerns hepatic effects. Asked whether aspirin and cyproheptadine could worsen liver problems, Peat answered in his [[liver-diseased|liver correspondence]]: "The excipients often used with them are probably as likely to affect the liver as the chemicals themselves." The implication — that the pure active ingredients are well-tolerated by the liver, and that hepatic reactions are usually to the binders, dyes, or coatings — is consistent with the conventional pharmacovigilance literature, which records hepatic adverse events as rare and usually mild for cyproheptadine.

## Data gaps

The most significant gap is the absence of long-term, low-dose, intermittent-use safety data. The clinical trials that exist used either short courses at standard doses (4–16 mg/day for migraine prophylaxis or appetite stimulation, weeks to months) or single doses for acute indications. The Peat-style use — half a milligram to two milligrams, intermittently, over years — has not been formally studied. Inference from the existing literature is reassuring but not equivalent to direct evidence.

A second gap is comparative pharmacology. Other antiserotonin drugs — ritanserin, ketanserin, ondansetron, the older agent methysergide, the never-marketed compounds Peat occasionally mentioned by name — have different receptor profiles and side-effect spectra. Peat had less direct clinical experience with them and recommended them less. Whether cyproheptadine is in fact the optimum drug in its class or simply the most accessible one is not established. "I haven't had any experience with ritanserin, don't recommend it, and don't recall discussing it," he wrote when asked about an alternative. The honest answer is that ritanserin and several other agents might be better choices in some cases, but the comparative trials do not exist.

A third gap concerns interactions with [[thyroid|thyroid]] replacement. Peat consistently paired cyproheptadine with thyroid restoration as the primary intervention and the symptomatic rescue, respectively, but the interaction between exogenous T3 and the cyproheptadine-blocked serotonin receptor pathway has not been characterized at a mechanism level. The clinical observation that the combination works better than either alone — that thyroid-restored patients need lower doses of cyproheptadine, and that cyproheptadine reduces the dose of thyroid required for symptomatic improvement — is well documented in Peat's correspondence and not, as far as I can find, in any controlled study.

A fourth gap is the pediatric long-term safety profile. Cyproheptadine is approved and used for pediatric appetite stimulation and migraine prophylaxis, often for months at a time. The long-term cognitive and developmental data are thin. Peat's own use of the drug in children was cautious and time-limited; he treated growth, hormonal development, and dietary correction as the primary interventions, with the drug as a rescue.
