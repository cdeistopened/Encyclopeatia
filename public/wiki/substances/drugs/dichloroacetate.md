# Dichloroacetate (DCA)

Dichloroacetate is the simplest molecule that does what Ray Peat said the diseased cell needs — it forces the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex back open and pushes glucose carbon out of the cytoplasmic lactate pool and into the mitochondrion to be oxidized completely to carbon dioxide. Structurally it is chloride-substituted acetic acid — two chlorines on the methyl carbon of an acetate — small enough to fit in the pyruvate-binding pocket of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK). When DCA binds, PDK cannot phosphorylate the E1 subunit of [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|PDH]]. PDH is dephosphorylated by its constitutively active phosphatase, switches on, and the cell resumes oxidative metabolism. The biochemistry is elementary, the molecule has been in clinical use for congenital lactic acidosis since the 1970s, and the patent expired decades ago. Those three facts — mechanistic simplicity, clinical track record, and unpatented status — are the reason DCA became the most-discussed and least-developed cancer drug of the past twenty years.

## The Two Pharmacological Doors

DCA is the sister drug to [[methylene-blue|methylene blue]]. Both reverse the [[warburg-effect|Warburg shift]] — the fermentative phenotype of cancer cells and many chronic-disease tissues — but they enter the oxidative cascade at different points. The pairing is instructive because it shows that the same metabolic failure can be addressed at the substrate-entry level OR the electron-flow level, and that the two interventions complement rather than substitute for each other.

| Drug | Entry point | Mechanism | When it helps |
|------|-------------|-----------|---------------|
| **Dichloroacetate** | Substrate gate (PDH) | Inhibits PDK → PDH stays dephosphorylated → pyruvate enters mitochondrion → acetyl-CoA → Krebs cycle | The substrate gate is closed; pyruvate is being diverted to lactate |
| **Methylene blue** | Electron transport chain | Bypasses partially impaired cytochrome oxidase by accepting electrons from NADH and donating to oxygen directly | The chain downstream of PDH is impaired; electrons can't get to oxygen |

The substrate-entry drug opens the door; the electron-chain drug keeps the downstream flow moving. In a tumor cell with both PDH phosphorylation AND cytochrome dysfunction — the common case — combining them addresses both bottlenecks. Peat referenced the pair often, treating them as different proofs of the same underlying claim: oxidative phosphorylation in the diseased cell is suppressed, not destroyed, and reactivating it produces the apoptotic signaling tumors have learned to evade.

This page is about DCA. The [[methylene-blue|methylene blue page]] covers the electron-chain side. The [[cancer-protocol|cancer protocol]] is where they meet operationally.

## Peat's Positioning

Peat treated DCA the way he treated most pharmacology: as an interesting proof of concept whose mechanism vindicated his underlying claim about metabolism, while pointing out that the same effect can be achieved with simpler, safer substrate-level interventions. In his [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues* (2012)]] newsletter he placed the drug in a four-item list: "The chemical DCA, dichloroacetate, is effective against a variety of cancers, and it acts by reactivating the enzymes that oxidize pyruvic acid. Thyroid hormone, insulin, and fructose also activate these enzymes." The grouping is the whole argument: a niche cancer drug and three ordinary metabolic substrates all converge on the same enzyme. The drug works because the substrate logic Peat described is correct. The substrates work for the same reason the drug works.

The corollary mattered to Peat. If [[thyroid]] hormone, insulin, and [[fructose]] reactivate PDH non-pharmacologically — by suppressing PDK4 transcription, lowering free fatty acids, and absorbing intracellular phosphate respectively — then optimizing those substrate states first is the rational sequencing. DCA is the option to add when substrate optimization isn't enough, or when the disease state is severe enough that pharmacological PDK inhibition is needed alongside dietary and hormonal repair.

This page is about the drug itself — how to think about it, when to consider it, and what the failure modes look like. It is not a recommendation. DCA is a prescription or compounded substance with real toxicity, used in narrow clinical contexts under clinician supervision. The Peat framework treats it as an option in the [[cancer-protocol|cancer adjunct stack]], not as a daily supplement.

## Pharmacology

DCA is a pan-PDK inhibitor. Mammalian cells express four PDK isoforms (PDK1 through PDK4) with different tissue distributions; DCA inhibits all four by occupying the kinase's pyruvate-binding pocket and stabilizing it in an inactive conformation. With PDK off, the constitutively active pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase (PDP) wins the balance and PDH is rapidly dephosphorylated. In a tumor cell with chronically phosphorylated PDH — the molecular signature of the [[warburg-effect|Warburg shift]] — the effect is fast: glucose carbon that was being dumped into lactate starts being routed to acetyl-CoA, the Krebs cycle resumes turnover, the electron transport chain reduces oxygen to water, and the cell produces carbon dioxide instead of [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]].

Sodium dichloroacetate is orally bioavailable. Absorption from the gut is nearly complete. The plasma half-life is unusual — short on first dose (one to two hours) and long after repeated dosing (over a day) because DCA inhibits its own metabolism by inhibiting glutathione-S-transferase zeta (GSTZ1), the hepatic enzyme that converts it to glyoxylate. The result is that chronic dosing produces unpredictable accumulation. Patients who tolerate a dose well in week one may show rising blood levels in week three or four without changing the dose. This pharmacokinetic peculiarity is the proximate reason chronic-dosing protocols use pulsed regimens — typically two weeks on, one or two weeks off, sometimes shorter cycles — and the reason serum DCA monitoring is recommended in long-term off-label use.

Typical dosing in the oncology literature is 10-25 mg/kg/day in two divided doses; older protocols in congenital lactic acidosis sometimes ran higher (up to 50 mg/kg/day in pediatric populations with adequate cofactor support). At the upper end of this range, dose-limiting toxicity emerges within weeks; at the lower end, with adequate cofactor support, regimens have been sustained for months. The compound is bitter, dissolves easily in water, and is normally compounded as an oral solution or capsule.

## The Michelakis 2007 Paper

The Bonnet, Archer, Michelakis et al. paper in *Cancer Cell* in January 2007 is the inflection point at which DCA stopped being an obscure metabolic drug and became a global object of interest. Evangelos Michelakis and colleagues at the University of Alberta showed that DCA reversed the Warburg shift in non-small-cell lung cancer, breast cancer, and glioblastoma cell lines. Treated tumor cells shifted from glycolytic to oxidative metabolism, restored mitochondrial membrane potential, opened the mitochondrial transition pore, and resumed apoptosis. In rat xenograft models, oral DCA in drinking water slowed tumor growth without obvious systemic toxicity. The conceptual punchline — tumor mitochondria are not absent but suppressed, and reactivating PDH restores the apoptotic signaling that tumors have evaded — vindicated decades of fringe metabolic theory of cancer dating back to [[otto-warburg|Otto Warburg]] in the 1920s.

Michelakis followed in 2010 with a small five-patient open-label glioblastoma series (*Sci Transl Med* 2:31ra34). Three of the five patients showed radiographic and clinical stabilization or partial response. Tumor tissue from biopsied patients showed the predicted shift — dephosphorylated PDH, restored mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced HIF-1α. The series was too small to support regulatory approval but was mechanistically clean: the drug did in human tumor tissue what it did in cell lines.

What followed was the cautionary half of the story. The drug was off-patent, which meant no pharmaceutical company stood to recover the cost of phase III trials. The University of Alberta ran small-scale clinical work funded by donations and a single benefactor. A handful of glioblastoma patients treated under compassionate-use protocols showed encouraging tumor responses but the trials were too small to support regulatory approval. Patients and patient communities turned to off-label sources — compounding pharmacies in Canada, online sources of variable quality, self-administration without monitoring. Peripheral neuropathy emerged as the consistent dose-limiting toxicity. Mainstream oncology bodies issued cautionary statements. Larger phase III investment never materialized.

## The Patentability Problem

The DCA story is the textbook case of pharmaceutical-economic gravity defeating mechanistic promise. The molecule was synthesized in the 19th century. Its patent life expired before its anticancer mechanism was characterized. By the time Michelakis published in 2007, no entity had financial standing to recoup the $100M–$1B cost of phase III oncology trials, and no entity has gained that standing since.

The structural facts:

- **No composition-of-matter patent possible.** DCA is a simple two-carbon molecule with no novel chemistry. Method-of-use patents for specific indications (anticancer use, specific dosing regimens) are weaker than composition patents and harder to enforce.
- **No prodrug or formulation moat.** Salt forms (sodium, potassium) and formulations (capsule, solution) are obvious and unpatentable. A delivery system patent might capture some value but would not justify the trial cost.
- **Generic-on-day-one for any new indication.** If a sponsor ran a successful phase III in glioblastoma, generic manufacturers could enter the market the day FDA approved the new indication, undercutting the sponsor's recovery.
- **Charity / government as the only realistic funder.** The Alberta program ran on donations and a single benefactor. NIH and Canadian agency funding has been intermittent and modest. The Repurposing Drugs in Oncology (ReDO) project has flagged DCA as a high-priority repurposing candidate; the structural funding to advance it is missing.

The Peat reading of this situation was unsentimental: the drug works, the mechanism is real, and the economic system that develops drugs is not designed to develop drugs like this one. The same logic explains why aspirin, thyroid, niacinamide, and other substrate-level interventions remain badly under-studied in oncology — they are all old, off-patent, and mechanistically embedded in metabolic biology that pharmacology textbooks dismissed for half a century. The takeaway is not despair; it is that the patient and the clinician have to operate without the phase III dataset that would normally be available, which raises the bar on protocol discipline and clinician supervision.

As of 2026, DCA remains FDA-designated as an orphan drug for congenital lactic acidosis, used off-label for cancer in a small specialty practice, and unsupported by completed phase III oncology trials. The Michelakis story is told most often as a story about pharmaceutical economics — what happens when a promising metabolic intervention has no business model. The Peat reading is more pointed: the drug works because the substrate theory of cancer is correct, and the same metabolic logic suggests cheaper, safer interventions (thyroid sufficiency, [[fructose]], [[niacinamide]], [[aspirin]], PUFA depletion) that achieve overlapping ends with far less complexity.

## Indications in the Peat Frame

Three clinical contexts make DCA worth considering in the Peat framework, all of them specialized.

**Cancer adjunct.** This is the headline indication and the one Peat referenced most often. In a patient with a histologically confirmed tumor showing the Warburg metabolic signature (high FDG-PET avidity, elevated serum or tissue lactate, suppressed PDH activity if measurable), DCA can be added to a metabolic protocol that already includes thyroid optimization, [[aspirin]], [[niacinamide]], saturated-fat support, PUFA depletion, and dietary sugar. The mechanism is complementary — substrate-level interventions reduce the fatty-acid load driving PDK activation; DCA inhibits PDK directly. Peat referenced the drug as a specific recommendation in at least one email exchange about recurrent tongue cancer: "Besides large amounts of aspirin (grams per day), and vitamin K1 or K2 to prevent abnormal bleeding from the aspirin, I think I would use DCA (dichloroacetate), which is available from Canada on the internet (and forums describe its use)." Full protocol at [[cancer-protocol]].

**Chronic fatigue with a documented Warburg-state metabolic signature.** This is a more speculative indication and is not standard medical practice. Patients with chronic fatigue syndrome, post-viral fatigue, or chronic Lyme who show persistently elevated serum lactate, low resting body temperature, and a metabolic profile consistent with chronic PDH suppression are sometimes considered by metabolic-medicine clinicians for short-pulse DCA trials. The logic is the same as in the cancer case: pyruvate is backing up and being reduced to lactate; reactivating PDH lets oxidation resume. In practice this is a clinician-supervised experiment, not a recognized treatment, and the same metabolic logic argues for trying thyroid, niacinamide, thiamine derivatives, and PUFA depletion first.

**Congenital lactic acidosis.** This is the established medical indication for which DCA holds FDA orphan-drug status. Children with mutations in pyruvate dehydrogenase subunits or in upstream electron-transport-chain components accumulate lactate, develop neurological deterioration, and have historically had no pharmacological options beyond ketogenic diets. DCA reactivates whatever residual PDH activity remains and lowers lactate measurably. The clinical experience in this population — including the long-term peripheral neuropathy data — is what defines the safety envelope for off-label use in cancer.

## Dosing

The dosing range used in the oncology literature is 10-25 mg/kg/day, typically divided into two doses. A 70 kg adult on the low end of this range takes 700 mg/day; on the high end, 1750 mg/day. Most off-label protocols start at the low end, escalate over weeks, monitor for neuropathic symptoms, and back off at the first sign of paresthesia.

**Thiamine cofactor is non-negotiable.** The dose-limiting toxicity of DCA is peripheral neuropathy, and the cofactor that prevents and partially reverses it is thiamine. Every published DCA protocol that has achieved sustained dosing without crippling neuropathy has paired the drug with daily thiamine — 100-200 mg of B1 daily as a minimum, often with one of the fat-soluble derivatives (benfotiamine, allithiamine, or TTFD) for cases where the hydrochloride salt is poorly absorbed. The mechanism is not fully worked out — DCA appears to interfere with thiamine-dependent enzyme handling and possibly with myelin lipoamide turnover — but the empirical pattern is consistent across the clinical literature. Skipping thiamine cover is the single most common failure mode in self-administered DCA protocols.

**Pulsed regimens are standard.** Continuous dosing produces accumulation through the GSTZ1-inhibition mechanism described above. The common pattern in off-label cancer protocols is two weeks on, one week off, or one week on, one week off for more sensitive patients. The off-week allows hepatic GSTZ1 activity to recover, plasma levels to fall, and any subclinical neuropathic signal to resolve. Patients who develop paresthesia during the on-week are usually advised to pause immediately and extend the off-week until symptoms resolve fully.

**Clinician coordination is essential.** This is a prescription or compounded substance with a non-trivial pharmacokinetic profile and a real toxicity. A clinician who has used DCA before — typically in a metabolic-medicine, integrative-oncology, or naturopathic-oncology practice — should be in the loop on dose, schedule, cofactor cover, and monitoring (baseline lactate, periodic neurological exam, periodic liver function tests). Self-administration from internet sources without monitoring is the default failure pattern and the reason the off-label literature is full of preventable neuropathy.

## Peripheral Neuropathy Warning

Peripheral neuropathy is the dose-limiting toxicity of DCA and the single most important thing to track. The presentation is a symmetric, length-dependent, predominantly sensory polyneuropathy — tingling, numbness, or burning in the toes and fingers first, sometimes with diminished proprioception. Motor involvement is rare. The symptoms appear gradually over weeks to months of continuous dosing, more rapidly at the higher end of the dose range, more slowly with adequate thiamine cover and pulsed regimens.

The neuropathy is reversible if caught early. Patients who stop DCA at the first paresthesia typically see full resolution over weeks to months. Patients who push through symptoms develop more entrenched neuropathy that takes longer to resolve and may be incompletely reversible. The clinical decision rule that has emerged in the off-label literature is straightforward: any new persistent tingling, numbness, or sensory change is grounds to pause the drug, extend the off-week, and reassess. Pressing on through paresthesia is the worst mistake in DCA protocols.

The thiamine cofactor protocol exists specifically to prevent this toxicity. Patients on DCA without daily thiamine develop neuropathy at lower doses and earlier; patients with adequate B1 tolerate higher doses for longer. Some clinicians use thiamine status as a proxy for DCA tolerance — patients with marginal baseline thiamine (alcohol-dependent, malabsorption syndromes, refeeding states, elderly with reduced absorption) are not good candidates for DCA without aggressive correction of the deficiency first.

Tracking signal: ask weekly about tingling, numbness, or any new sensory change in fingers and toes. If present, pause DCA and continue thiamine until full resolution.

## Other Side Effects

Beyond peripheral neuropathy, DCA has a small set of recognized adverse effects.

**Sedation and confusion** at higher doses or with accumulation. The mechanism is not fully understood but probably reflects DCA's direct effects on cerebral lactate-pyruvate ratios and possibly on GABAergic signaling. Patients describe a dulled, slowed feeling that resolves within days of dose reduction. Confusion at the level of dose-limiting toxicity is rare but reported.

**Gastrointestinal symptoms** — nausea, dyspepsia, occasional diarrhea — are common at initiation and usually resolve within a week or two. Taking DCA with food and titrating slowly minimizes this. The bitter taste of sodium dichloroacetate solution is a separate complaint that capsule formulations resolve.

**Hepatotoxicity** is rare and usually mild — transient transaminase elevations that resolve on dose reduction or holiday. Severe hepatotoxicity has been reported but is uncommon and seems to correlate with high accumulation in patients with reduced GSTZ1 activity. Periodic liver function tests are standard in chronic dosing.

**Mood effects** are reported anecdotally — both improvement (the metabolic-energy effect of restored oxidative phosphorylation) and depression or anxiety at higher doses. The pattern is not well characterized.

## Synergies

DCA does not act in isolation in the Peat frame. It is one tool that works because the substrate logic underneath it is correct, and it works better when paired with interventions that address the upstream substrate state.

**Methylene blue.** [[methylene-blue|Methylene blue]] is a mitochondrial electron acceptor that supports respiration directly, particularly when cytochrome oxidase is partially impaired. In the cancer-adjunct stack, DCA reactivates the PDH gate while methylene blue supports the chain that PDH feeds into. The two address adjacent failures in the oxidative cascade — substrate-entry and electron-flow respectively. Dosing is independent — methylene blue at 1-15 mg morning, DCA on its pulsed schedule.

**Niacinamide.** [[niacinamide]] does two things relevant to DCA. It supplies the NAD+ that the E3 subunit of the PDH complex requires to regenerate the lipoyl arm — without NAD+ the complex stalls within a single turnover. And it lowers circulating free fatty acids, which removes one of the upstream signals driving PDK activation. The DCA inhibits the kinase pharmacologically; niacinamide removes the substrate signal that drives the kinase. Daily 100-500 mg, divided.

**Thiamine and thiamine derivatives.** Already covered above as a non-negotiable cofactor; here as a synergy. Thiamine is the E1 cofactor — DCA can only reactivate PDH if the active site has B1 to do its decarboxylation chemistry. Adequate B1 status is the difference between DCA working as intended and DCA producing neuropathy without metabolic benefit.

**Thyroid optimization.** [[thyroid|Thyroid hormone]] induces PDH subunit transcription and suppresses PDK4 expression. A hypothyroid cell has fewer PDH complexes to begin with, and the ones it has are more aggressively phosphorylated. DCA in a hypothyroid patient is fighting a transcriptional headwind. Optimizing thyroid status before adding DCA is the standard sequencing in the Peat frame — and in many cases thyroid alone is enough.

**Aspirin and PUFA depletion.** [[aspirin]] inhibits lipolysis, reducing the free-fatty-acid load that activates PDK. PUFA depletion over months reduces the lipid-peroxidation products that poison the electron-transport chain downstream of PDH. Both interventions address the substrate state DCA is fighting against pharmacologically. They make DCA work better and may, in milder cases, make DCA unnecessary.

**Saturated fat support.** Replacing PUFA in the diet with saturated fat (coconut oil, butter, dairy fat) shifts the steady-state free-fatty-acid composition over months toward the less inhibitory short-chain saturated species, which apply much milder [[randle-cycle|Randle-style]] PDH inhibition than long-chain PUFA.

## Pharmacy Access and Legal Status

DCA is not an FDA-approved cancer drug in the United States. It holds FDA orphan-drug status for congenital lactic acidosis, which means it can be legally prescribed and dispensed for that narrow indication. For oncological off-label use, three access routes have historically been available.

**Compounding pharmacies.** US compounding pharmacies can prepare sodium dichloroacetate as capsules or oral solution from bulk pharmaceutical-grade powder, dispensed against a prescription. The cost is modest — typical month-supply pricing has been in the low-hundreds-of-dollars range, orders of magnitude below most cancer drugs. The quality is variable across compounders; reputable compounders provide certificates of analysis with each batch.

**International sources.** Canadian and other international suppliers sell DCA without prescription. The quality is highly variable. Peat himself referenced this access route — "available from Canada on the internet (and forums describe its use)" — but the lack of analytical verification makes it the riskier path. Patients who pursue this route should arrange independent analytical testing of the powder before use. Customs interception risk has fluctuated; this is not a hidden activity but it is in legal gray space depending on jurisdiction.

**Clinical trials.** Small numbers of investigator-initiated trials for specific cancers run periodically. ClinicalTrials.gov is the place to check for current studies. Active sites have included Medicor Cancer Centres in Toronto (Akbar Khan's practice, the most-cited off-label DCA clinic) and a handful of academic centers running open-label series.

**Jurisdictional notes.** Canada permits naturopathic and compounding-pharmacy access more easily than the United States. The EU varies by member state. Australia restricts DCA fairly tightly. The UK requires a specialist consultant prescription. A clinician familiar with DCA protocols is the best entry point to the supply chain regardless of jurisdiction. The integrative-oncology, metabolic-medicine, and naturopathic-oncology communities are where the working knowledge lives.

## Escalation Criteria

DCA is not a first-line intervention in any Peat-frame protocol. The sequencing logic in a metabolic cancer or chronic-fatigue case:

1. **Optimize thyroid first.** Restore eu-metabolic status — body temperature 98.0-98.6°F, pulse 75-85, TSH suppressed if needed, T3 and T4 in functional ranges. This alone reactivates PDH transcriptionally and de-suppresses PDK4.
2. **Optimize substrate state.** Adequate calories, sugar, salt, [[fructose]], saturated fats, low PUFA. Add [[niacinamide]] (100-500 mg/day) and [[aspirin]] (with vitamin K cover) for the substrate-level effects on PDK and on lipolysis respectively.
3. **Add [[methylene-blue|methylene blue]] for electron-chain support.** 1-15 mg morning, on a tolerance-titrated basis.
4. **Then consider DCA.** Only after substrate-level interventions have had several months to work and the metabolic state is still showing Warburg signatures (elevated lactate, low CO2, FDG-PET avidity in tumors), and only with a clinician who has used the protocol before.

Escalation to DCA without the prerequisite substrate optimization is the most common failure pattern. The drug works in a properly prepared metabolic milieu; in a hypothyroid, PUFA-loaded, calorie-restricted patient it fights uphill and produces neuropathy without sufficient benefit.

## Failure Modes

Five patterns account for most DCA failures in self-administered protocols.

**Skipping thiamine.** This is the modal mistake. Patients who treat DCA as a drug rather than a paired drug-plus-cofactor protocol develop neuropathy at low doses and conclude the drug doesn't work or is too toxic. The neuropathy was always preventable. Thiamine cover at 100-200 mg/day from the first dose of DCA is the protocol minimum, and in cases of marginal baseline B1 status, much higher doses of fat-soluble derivatives are warranted.

**Continuous dosing without pulses.** DCA accumulates because it inhibits its own metabolism. Patients who dose continuously for weeks accumulate to neurotoxic levels even at modest mg/kg doses. Pulsed regimens are not optional — they are the safety mechanism that lets hepatic clearance recover and plasma levels fall.

**Pushing through paresthesia.** Patients who develop tingling and decide to continue dosing usually end up with persistent neuropathy that takes months to resolve and may not resolve fully. The decision rule is clear: any new sensory symptom is grounds to pause.

**Using DCA without baseline metabolic workup.** The drug is a substrate-level intervention. Patients with profound hypothyroidism, severe PUFA accumulation, ongoing high stress, and no thyroid replacement are fighting a transcriptional headwind that no amount of PDK inhibition can fully overcome. The simpler interventions belong first.

**Expecting overnight tumor response.** DCA works through restored apoptotic signaling that develops over weeks to months as mitochondrial function gradually normalizes. Patients who measure response at two weeks and conclude the drug isn't working stop too early. The clinical reports of meaningful tumor response typically describe months of sustained dosing with cofactor cover. Sustained, monitored, properly cofactored, pulsed administration is what works when it works.

## Tracking Signals

The clinician-monitored DCA protocol tracks at least the following.

- **Lactate** — baseline serum lactate, then periodically. Falling lactate is the expected and reassuring biochemical signal. Rising lactate suggests inadequate dose or worsening underlying disease.
- **Energy and warmth** — subjective metabolic markers Peat emphasized. A patient whose oxidative metabolism is restoring usually feels warmer (peripheral circulation improves) and has more sustained energy. The signal is qualitative but consistent.
- **Tumor markers** — where applicable. CEA, CA-125, PSA, AFP and the like, on whatever schedule the oncology team is already running.
- **Imaging** — FDG-PET avidity should fall as glucose oxidation replaces fermentation. Some integrative-oncology practices use FDG-PET specifically as a treatment-response marker on DCA protocols.
- **Neurological status** — weekly check for any new sensory symptoms in fingers and toes. Periodic formal exam if dosing is prolonged.
- **Mood and cognition** — both because oxidative metabolism restoration improves both, and because the drug can produce sedation and dulling at the dose-limiting end.
- **Liver function** — baseline transaminases, repeated periodically.

The protocol is not "take this and wait." It is a structured, monitored, iterative intervention with regular feedback loops.

## Counter-arguments

Three lines of pushback against the case for DCA deserve explicit treatment.

**Mainstream oncology skepticism.** The dominant view in academic medical oncology is that DCA's mechanistic story is interesting but the clinical evidence is thin, the trials are small, and the off-label experience is heterogeneous and uncontrolled. Major cancer centers do not offer DCA. Position statements from oncology professional societies caution against off-label use. The argument is reasonable — phase III evidence is the standard, and DCA does not meet it. The Peat framework reply is that the mechanism is well-characterized enough, the safety envelope is well-mapped from the congenital-lactic-acidosis literature, and the absence of phase III trials reflects pharmaceutical economics rather than scientific doubt. Both observations are correct; which one is more decisive depends on how much weight you give mechanistic plausibility versus completed RCTs.

**Post-Michelakis trial results have been mixed.** Several small trials in glioblastoma, refractory solid tumors, and metastatic breast cancer have reported modest activity at best, with response rates in the single digits and progression-free intervals measured in months rather than years. The mixed picture does not vindicate the drug as a stand-alone cancer treatment. The Peat framing — that DCA is one component of a metabolic protocol, not a magic bullet — is consistent with these results: a single-agent intervention against a multi-substrate metabolic state will produce modest single-agent results, while the substrate-level interventions need to be running simultaneously to do the heavy lifting. Whether properly designed multi-agent metabolic protocols would show meaningfully different results in larger trials remains an open empirical question that has not been resolved because the trials have not been done.

**The "DCA causes neuropathy rather than benefit" critique.** Some commentators argue that the neuropathy observed in chronic dosing reflects the drug damaging the same mitochondria it claims to support, that any acute metabolic benefit is offset by chronic mitotoxicity, and that the drug should not be used outside the narrow lactic-acidosis indication. The counter is that neuropathy emerges only in chronic dosing at doses high enough for accumulation through GSTZ1 inhibition, that pulsed regimens with thiamine cofactor support do not produce the same toxicity, and that the same mitochondrial signal that produces neuropathy in nerve tissue produces apoptosis in tumor tissue — the toxicity and the mechanism of action are related, and the dosing protocol is specifically designed to exploit the tumor-selective vulnerability. The critique is real and is the reason DCA is a clinician-supervised intervention rather than a casual supplement, but it does not argue against properly designed protocols.

**"Better metabolic alternatives exist."** A separate Peat-internal critique notes that thyroid hormone, niacinamide, fructose, and aspirin all reactivate PDH or remove the substrate signal driving PDK, with vastly better safety envelopes than DCA. Why use a drug with a real dose-limiting toxicity when the substrate substitutes work? The answer is that in advanced or refractory disease the substrate-level interventions are necessary but often insufficient — the disease state is severe enough that pharmacological PDK inhibition adds meaningful effect on top. In milder cases, the substrate interventions alone often do the job. The framework is sequential, not either/or.

## Data Gaps

Several questions in the DCA literature remain unresolved.

- **Optimal dosing schedule.** The two-weeks-on / one-week-off pattern that emerged in off-label practice is a reasonable compromise between mechanism and toxicity, but the comparative evidence for shorter pulses, lower daily doses, or different cycle lengths is undeveloped.
- **Biomarker-guided dosing.** Plasma DCA monitoring is available but not standardized in off-label use. Whether targeting a specific plasma concentration range would reduce neuropathy without sacrificing efficacy is empirically open.
- **Combination protocols.** The Peat framing pairs DCA with thyroid, niacinamide, aspirin, methylene blue, and PUFA depletion, but no formal trial has tested the multi-agent combination against any single component.
- **Tumor-type selectivity.** The Michelakis 2007 data were strongest in glioblastoma and lung cancer. Whether other tumor histologies — pancreatic, hepatocellular, sarcomas — respond comparably is undersettled. FDG-PET avidity is a plausible imaging biomarker for "Warburg-state" tumors that might respond, but this has not been validated as a predictive marker.
- **The chronic-fatigue indication.** The use of DCA in chronic fatigue with documented metabolic Warburg signatures is anecdotal. No formal trials exist. Whether the indication is real or wishful is empirically open.
- **The role of GSTZ1 polymorphisms.** Genetic variation in GSTZ1 activity probably explains much of the inter-individual variability in DCA tolerance. Genotype-guided dosing has been proposed but is not standard.
- **The DCA-plus-methylene-blue interaction.** Theoretically complementary, anecdotally combined in some integrative-oncology practices, but no formal pharmacokinetic or efficacy data on the pair.
