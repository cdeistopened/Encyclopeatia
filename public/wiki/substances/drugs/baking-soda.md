# Baking Soda

Sodium bicarbonate—common baking soda—occupies a curious position in Ray Peat's pharmacopeia: it's not a daily supplement, but it's a legitimate emergency intervention for metabolic acidosis, hyperventilation, and situations where rapid [[carbon-dioxide]] elevation is needed. Its value lies not in neutralizing stomach acid, as antacid marketing suggests, but in producing CO₂, the protective gas that Peat positioned at the center of cellular metabolism and stress resilience.

## The CO₂ Connection

When sodium bicarbonate dissolves in water and encounters acid (whether in the stomach or bloodstream), it produces carbon dioxide, water, and sodium ions. In Peat's framework, this CO₂ generation is the therapeutic mechanism. "In a crisis situation," he wrote in correspondence on carbon dioxide, "it (or baking soda in water) can be helpful, but it's more effective to rebreathe in a paper bag."

That comparison to [[bag-breathing]]—Peat's preferred method for acute CO₂ restoration—reveals baking soda's niche: it's a chemical shortcut to raise CO₂ when mechanical rebreathing isn't practical or sufficient. The CO₂ produced acts as a vasodilator, an anti-inflammatory signal, and a metabolic regulator that shifts cells away from stress-driven glycolysis toward efficient oxidative metabolism.

## Alkalinity and Metabolic Health

Peat consistently associated alkalinity with health and longevity. Chronic stress, cortisol elevation, and lactic acid accumulation all drive the body toward acidosis, a state characterized by shallow breathing (which blows off CO₂), reduced oxygen delivery to tissues, and increased cellular excitability. Baking soda counters this by directly raising blood pH and, crucially, by generating CO₂ that improves oxygen utilization via the Bohr effect.

This isn't about chasing an arbitrary pH target. It's about correcting the respiratory and metabolic dysfunction that accompanies chronic stress. When someone is hyperventilating—whether from panic, altitude, pain, or metabolic disturbance—they're losing CO₂ faster than they produce it. Alkalosis paradoxically results, which constricts blood vessels and reduces oxygen delivery to the brain and other tissues. Baking soda, by producing CO₂, helps break this cycle.

## Practical Applications

Peat recommended baking soda primarily in three contexts:

1. **Hyperventilation and panic**: When [[bag-breathing]] isn't feasible or doesn't fully resolve symptoms, dissolving a quarter to half teaspoon of baking soda in water can provide relief within minutes. The CO₂ produced dampens the excitatory cascade.

2. **Sodium source for sensitive individuals**: In correspondence on sodium, Peat noted that "baking soda in water is helpful for some people" as an alternative when salt causes gut irritation or exacerbates inflammation. This is particularly relevant for individuals with compromised gut integrity, where sodium chloride can irritate damaged mucosa but sodium bicarbonate is better tolerated.

3. **Acute metabolic acidosis**: During illness, intense exercise, or situations where lactic acid builds up faster than the body can clear it, baking soda provides a buffer. It's not a substitute for addressing the underlying metabolic problem (usually inadequate [[thyroid]] function or excessive reliance on glycolysis), but it can stabilize a crisis.

## The Stomach Acid Question

A common concern is whether taking baking soda with meals will "neutralize stomach acid" and impair digestion. Peat addressed this directly: "It slows digestion down for a few minutes." The implication is that the effect is transient and not metabolically harmful. Stomach acid production is not fixed; the parietal cells respond to need. If bicarbonate briefly raises gastric pH, acid secretion increases to compensate.

More importantly, Peat's focus was not on acid-pepsin digestion of protein—he considered high-protein meals problematic for other reasons—but on the metabolic effects of nutrients once absorbed. A slight delay in gastric emptying is trivial compared to the systemic benefit of improved CO₂ status in someone who is acidotic or hyperventilating.

## Dosing and Timing

Peat did not prescribe rigid dosing protocols. The typical recommendation, based on his correspondence and the practices of people working with his ideas, is to dissolve ¼ to ½ teaspoon (about 1-2 grams) of sodium bicarbonate in 4-8 ounces of water. This can be taken between meals or, if needed, with food.

Some individuals use baking soda as a daily supplement, but Peat's framing suggests it's better suited for intermittent use during periods of stress, illness, or respiratory disturbance. Chronic reliance on external buffers may mask underlying metabolic dysfunction that needs to be corrected through [[thyroid]] optimization, dietary adjustments (more [[sugar]], less [[PUFA]]), and stress reduction.

## Cautions

Sodium bicarbonate is generally safe in moderate doses, but excessive or prolonged use can lead to metabolic alkalosis, electrolyte imbalances (particularly low potassium), and sodium overload in individuals with compromised kidney function or heart failure. It should not be used by people on sodium-restricted diets without medical guidance.

Peat's broader point was that baking soda is a tool, not a solution. If someone needs it daily, the question is why their CO₂ production and pH regulation are chronically impaired. The answer usually lies in hypothyroidism, chronic hyperventilation, or a diet too high in [[PUFA]] and protein and too low in [[sugar]] and [[gelatin]].

## Alternatives and Complements

While baking soda produces CO₂ chemically, Peat preferred behavioral interventions when possible: [[bag-breathing]], nasal breathing, altitude exposure, and activities that naturally elevate CO₂ (slow walking, relaxed conversation, laughter). Carbonated water or [[carbon-dioxide]] baths were other options for CO₂ delivery, though neither matches the systemic buffering effect of oral sodium bicarbonate.

For sodium supplementation without the CO₂ effect, Peat recommended salt (sodium chloride) in most cases. Baking soda's unique role is in situations where both sodium and CO₂ are needed, or where sodium chloride is poorly tolerated.
