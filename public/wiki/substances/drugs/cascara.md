# Cascara

Cascara sagrada is the bark of *Rhamnus purshiana*, a Pacific Northwest tree whose name — "sacred bark" — was given by Spanish missionaries who learned its use from Indigenous practitioners. The medical culture knows cascara as a "stimulant laxative," a category the FDA pushed off OTC shelves in 2002 with the warning that it might cause permanent colon damage. Peat's framework inverts the category and the warning. The dark pigmentation gastroenterologists attribute to "melanosis coli" from stimulant laxatives is, in Peat's reading, lipofuscin produced by the chronic constipation that brought patients to laxatives in the first place — a marker of the underlying problem, not a consequence of the herb. More than that, cascara's active anthraquinone, emodin, sits in the same electron-shuttling quinone family as [[vitamin-k]], coenzyme Q₁₀, and the naphthoquinones William F. Koch worked with in the 1920s. Peat treated cascara not as a laxative that has some interesting side properties but as a respiratory adaptogen that happens to move the bowels.

Three threads run through Peat's writing on it: bowel motility as endotoxin control, emodin as a low-toxicity anti-tumor adjunct, and the FDA's 2002 reclassification as a political event rather than a regulatory one. The 2012 newsletter [[cascara-energy-cancer-and-the-fdas-laxative-abuse|*Cascara, Energy, Cancer and the FDA's Laxative Abuse*]] is where all three braid together; this article is the substance-page complement to it.

The unique signal of cascara — what makes it worth its own entry next to the [[cascara-sagrada]] page — is the FDA story, the dose distinction between laxative and sub-laxative use, the one-year aging requirement, and the failure-mode set that separates Peat's selective endorsement from the chronic-laxative-abuse pattern modern medicine reflexively conflates it with.

## Botany and preparation

*Rhamnus purshiana* — also called chittam bark or sacred bark — grows in the temperate rainforest from British Columbia to Northern California. Traditional harvest takes the bark from young trees in spring, dries it in the sun, and ages it for at least one year before use. Fresh bark contains harsh anthrone glycosides that produce severe cramping, griping, and irritation; the aging process (sun, oxygen, time) converts the anthrones to the corresponding anthraquinones — primarily cascarosides A through D, with emodin, aloe-emodin, and chrysophanol as the principal aglycones. The aged form is the medicine. The unaged form is a punishment.

Sarah Murray, the herbalist Peat appeared with on the 2010 Ask the Herb Doctor *Endotoxins* show, stated the rule plainly: "Cascara is an herb that decreases transit time and is a laxative. If aged properly (sun-dried and aged for one year), the anthraquinones are safe and effective." Peat repeated this requirement in correspondence. The practical implication is that source matters more than dose: a commercial cascara whose label cannot or will not confirm the aging is the wrong source, regardless of price or potency claims. Peat's own preference, expressed in a personal email, was the bulk powder sold by Farmalabor in Italy or — when customs complicated the Italian source — the glycerine-mixed preparation from Naturlich Kost Co-op in Millersburg, Ohio.

The active constituents that survive aging are the 1,8-dihydroxyanthraquinones. Emodin (1,3,8-trihydroxy-6-methylanthraquinone) is the most studied. The structural family overlaps with the tetracycline antibiotics, which Peat noted explicitly: "Cascara has a structure similar to tetracycline," carrying not only laxative but antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects.

## Pharmacology

The bowel-moving mechanism is dual. Cascara's anthraquinones stimulate the enteric nervous system (a mild prokinetic effect at the level of the myenteric plexus), and they also reduce water absorption from the colon, leaving more fluid in the lumen for stool consistency. Onset is six to twelve hours, which is why evening dosing produces a morning bowel movement.

But the mechanism set Peat cared about extended well beyond peristalsis. Emodin:

- **Inhibits nitric oxide synthesis.** This is the effect Peat called out as cascara's most important. Endotoxin (LPS) absorbed from the colon drives the inducible form of [[nitric-oxide]] synthase; the resulting NO poisons cytochrome c oxidase and shifts cells from oxidative respiration to glycolysis. Cascara reducing both LPS absorption (via faster transit) and iNOS activity (via emodin) attacks the same lesion from two angles. The email passage is direct: "I think cascara's most important effect is the reduction of the pro-inflammatory nitric oxide, which poisons mitochondrial energy production."
- **Increases mitochondrial respiration and ATP synthesis.** Multiple animal studies show emodin raises mitochondrial oxygen consumption and reduces oxidative damage — the opposite of what one would expect from a "stimulant" drug. The Du and Ko cardioprotection papers (2005, 2006) are the cleanest demonstrations.
- **Inhibits angiogenesis, invasiveness, fatty acid synthase, HER-2/neu, and tyrosine phosphorylases.** The cancer-relevant emodin literature accumulated through the 1990s and 2000s ([[cancer-protocol]] cites the Ha et al. 2011 work on COX-2, VEGF, HIF-1α, and MMP suppression). The Srinivas et al. 2007 review in *Medicinal Research Reviews* is titled "Molecular Mechanism of Emodin Action: Transition from Laxative Ingredient to an Antitumor Agent" — Peat called it the academic mainstream catching up.
- **Inhibits NF-κB and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression** in macrophage cell lines, consistent with the anti-inflammatory clinical effects.
- **Sits in the Koch-Szent-Györgyi quinone lineage.** Peat repeatedly placed emodin in the family that starts with vitamin K and runs through CoQ₁₀ — molecules whose biological function is electron transport, and whose ability to act as electron acceptors makes them, in [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Szent-Györgyi]]'s frame, agents of organized cellular function rather than disorganized stress.

The dose distinction matters here. The laxative dose of cascara — enough to produce a bowel movement — is higher than the emodin dose required for the systemic anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects. A sub-laxative cascara regimen, taken intermittently, can be a slow-drip emodin source without producing the watery-stool experience that signals laxative action.

## The FDA laxative-abuse story

The cleanest read of how the FDA arrived at its 2002 cascara ruling is in the [[cascara-energy-cancer-and-the-fdas-laxative-abuse|2012 newsletter]]. The chronology is short and worth holding together:

- Late 1990s through early 2000s: a stream of papers reports that emodin has antiviral, antimutagenic, and antitumor activity, plus protective effects against several known carcinogens. The drug industry begins investigating emodin derivatives as potential cancer drugs.
- 2002, August: Novartis receives FDA approval for tegaserod (Zelnorm), a serotonin-receptor agonist designed to "act like serotonin" on bowel motility. The drug is approved for 4-to-6 weeks of use despite a side-effect list that includes abdominal pain, gallbladder disease, breast cancer, attempted suicide, depression, asthma, polyuria, and a multibillion-dollar annual sales projection.
- 2002, November 5: Less than three months later, the FDA's Final Rule on "Certain Additional Over-the-Counter Drug Category II and III Active Ingredients" takes effect, declaring that cascara sagrada and aloe (in all their preparations) are "not generally recognized as safe and effective or are misbranded."
- 2007: The FDA withdraws Zelnorm's approval after evidence emerges that it causes heart attacks and intestinal ischemia/necrosis. Novartis is permitted to continue dispensing it under an Investigational New Drug program until April 2008, when even that ends.

Peat's reading: "Historically, the FDA has ruled against traditional generic drug products when the drug industry is ready to market a synthetic substitute product." The cascara ruling was not a safety determination. It was the removal of a cheap, unpatentable, traditional competitor in the same window the drug industry was attempting to bring synthetic alternatives — both for constipation and, separately, for cancer — to market. The cancer-drug interest in emodin made cascara a "serious threat to the drug industry" twice over. The 2002 ruling closed the OTC shelf at the moment that threat was about to materialize.

This is a load-bearing piece of context for any practitioner thinking about cascara, because the regulatory framing — "stimulant laxatives damage your bowel and lose effectiveness over time" — is downstream of a political event whose timing has nothing to do with the herb's pharmacology.

## Indications in the Peat frame

Three clean use cases, all linked into existing protocols:

**Chronic constipation, as adjunct.** Two phases. Phase 1 is root-cause work — thyroid, dietary [[sugar]] and [[gelatin]], magnesium, raw carrot, fixing endotoxin reabsorption. Phase 2, when Phase 1 hasn't yet restored regular bowel function, is cascara as a low-dose evening bridge for two to four weeks while the upstream work catches up. The intent is never to make cascara the permanent solution; the intent is to break the constipation–endotoxin–inflammation feedback loop long enough for motility to come back on its own. See [[constipation-protocol]].

**Endotoxin-load reduction.** Slow transit through the colon increases bacterial fermentation, LPS production, and LPS absorption — Peat's central gut-physiology concern. Cascara reduces transit time and, separately, inhibits NO synthesis, attacking the LPS-NO-respiratory-failure cycle from both ends. In the [[endotoxin-reduction]] protocol it sits alongside the carrot salad, activated charcoal, and antibiotic-when-warranted as part of the intermittent gut-decontamination toolkit.

**Cancer adjunct.** Used not for the laxative effect per se but for the emodin's separate anti-tumor signature — COX-2, VEGF, HIF-1α, MMP, HER-2/neu, fatty acid synthase suppression — and as part of the broader bowel-toxin/cancer link that runs through Peat's cancer thinking. Intermittent low-dose cascara routes some emodin systemically without producing daily-laxative dependency. Pair with the cancer-protocol's primary stack (progesterone, aspirin, niacinamide, thyroid, low PUFA). See [[cancer-protocol]] for the full integration.

## Dosing

The laxative dose of properly aged cascara is roughly 100 to 500 mg of bark powder, taken in the evening for a morning effect. Liquid extracts and tinctures vary widely in concentration; the manufacturer's "1 teaspoon" recommendation is the right anchor, not a milligram count.

The sub-laxative emodin-source dose is lower — one capsule of a standard 250 mg cascara preparation taken once or twice weekly as part of a cancer-adjunct or endotoxin-load regimen, calibrated to bowel response. The goal is to dose just below the laxative threshold most days, with occasional fully-laxative doses if transit is slow.

Pulsed-regimen patterns Peat referenced in correspondence and the protocols build on:

- Two- to four-week courses during gut-restoration or post-antibiotic phases, then off
- One or two evenings per week as standing low-grade endotoxin-reduction support
- Cancer adjunct: intermittent, two to four evenings per week, indefinitely while the rest of the protocol runs

**What chronic daily use looks like in practice**: a watery stool every morning, dependence on the herb to produce any bowel movement at all, atonic colon, electrolyte loss (potassium especially), and the conventional "stimulant laxative dependency" picture. This is the failure mode the FDA built its 2002 ruling around. Peat's recommendation is unambiguous: don't reach that pattern. If you find yourself at three weeks of nightly cascara with no improvement in baseline motility off it, the problem is upstream — thyroid, food choice, dysbiosis — not cascara underdose.

## The aging requirement, restated

Fresh cascara bark contains anthrone glycosides that cause severe abdominal cramping, griping, and irritation. One year of sun-drying is the minimum to convert these to the anthraquinone form. The practical implications:

- Buy from a source that explicitly states the bark is aged.
- Tincture and extract preparations whose labels disclose neither bark age nor production process are suspect.
- The "cascara made me sick / cramps me up" experience that some users report is almost always a sourcing problem, not a sensitivity problem. The aged form is mild.

## Failure modes

**Daily chronic use → bowel atonia.** The colonic smooth muscle adapts to repeated stimulant exposure by becoming less responsive to native peristaltic signaling. This is the picture conventional gastroenterology paints; Peat did not deny the picture exists, only that the herb itself is the cause when used as he prescribed.

**Expecting overnight cancer benefit.** Emodin's anti-tumor effects accrue over months and operate as part of a broader metabolic-restoration stack. Cascara is not a chemo substitute.

**Ignoring root causes of constipation.** Hypothyroidism, low sugar, low gelatin, dysbiosis, estrogen excess relative to progesterone, magnesium deficiency, and dehydration all drive slow transit. Cascara without addressing these is symptom management.

**Stacking with other stimulant laxatives.** Senna, bisacodyl, magnesium citrate at high dose, and cascara together compound the dependency and electrolyte-loss risks. If cascara is in play, it is the bowel-acting agent, not one of several.

**Use in obstruction or acute inflammatory bowel disease.** Stimulating peristalsis in a swollen, edematous, or obstructed intestine is dangerous. Acute Crohn's flare, severe ulcerative colitis, suspected bowel obstruction, and acute appendicitis are absolute contraindications.

## What to avoid

- **Chronic daily cascara use** beyond a short-bridge window — three to four weeks maximum, longer only under clinician supervision and with documented inability to restore motility through upstream work
- **Pediatric use** generally — children's constipation almost always resolves with milk, magnesium, fluid, and addressing thyroid
- **Pregnancy** — theoretical risk of pelvic congestion and uterine stimulation from emodin; not a categorical Peat contraindication but the conservative position is to avoid
- **History of GI bleeding** — any laxative is wrong here until the bleeding source is identified
- **Electrolyte-depleted states** — chronic diarrhea, severe vomiting, diuretic use, or known hypokalemia; cascara amplifies the loss
- **Fresh or unaged cascara bark** — the only categorical product-level contraindication

## Tracking signal

For bowel-motility use: a soft, formed, complete bowel movement once or twice daily, twelve to twenty-four hours from meal to elimination. Cascara is working when the morning bowel movement is consistent and comfortable, not when stool is watery or urgent. If you are taking cascara and stool is urgent or watery, the dose is too high.

For cancer adjunct use: the cascara-specific signal is bowel motility and comfort; the broader metabolic signal lives at the level of inflammatory markers (CRP, ferritin), tumor markers as appropriate, body temperature, pulse, and the rest of the [[cancer-protocol]] tracking set. Do not expect cascara to move tumor markers on its own.

For endotoxin-load work: morning energy, post-meal stability, skin clarity, mood, and the absence of post-prandial bloat are the practical signals. A multi-week trial of intermittent cascara on top of the carrot-salad baseline should produce noticeable change; if it doesn't, the gut problem is elsewhere.

## Counter-arguments

**The FDA position.** Cascara is not generally recognized as safe and effective for over-the-counter use, and stimulant laxative dependency is a recognized clinical pattern. Peat's reading of the 2002 ruling as a political act does not erase the real cases of bowel atonia from chronic laxative use. The Peat-frame answer is that the failure mode is real but exists at a use pattern (daily, indefinite, high-dose) Peat explicitly rejected.

**Modern gastroenterology's anti-stimulant-laxative orthodoxy.** Osmotic agents (polyethylene glycol, magnesium) and stool softeners are preferred over anthraquinones. The Peat-frame answer is that the orthodoxy ignores the structural difference between cascara's intermittent low-dose use as a respiratory adaptogen and the chronic-abuse pattern it derives its objection from.

**The "emodin is hepatotoxic" position.** Some studies — primarily high-dose rodent work — report hepatotoxicity from isolated emodin. The doses involved are orders of magnitude above what an intermittent cascara user receives. No clear hepatotoxicity signal exists at traditional cascara use levels. The conservative position is to avoid cascara in known active liver disease and to space it from other hepatically-cleared agents.

**Drug interactions.** Cascara may reduce absorption of orally administered drugs by accelerating transit. Coumadin/warfarin interaction is theoretical (anthraquinones share the K-quinone family) and not well documented. Time other oral medications away from cascara dosing.

## Data gaps

- No human RCTs on intermittent low-dose cascara for cancer adjunct or endotoxin reduction; the mechanism literature is animal and cell-culture
- Optimal frequency for the sub-laxative emodin-source pattern is underdetermined
- The aging-time threshold (one year minimum) is traditional rather than empirically optimized; whether longer aging yields better outcomes is unknown
- The interaction between cascara and the [[carrot-salad]] / [[activated-charcoal]] / [[gelatin]] stack on LPS reduction has not been studied as a combined regimen
