# Aspirin

No substance in Ray Peat's framework bridges as many domains as aspirin. It appears in his discussions of [[cancer]], [[dementia]], [[diabetes]], [[fibrosis]], [[fertility]], [[sleep]], viral illness, and the basic physics of cellular energy production--not as a folk remedy invoked on faith, but as a molecule whose actions illuminate his entire model of biology. Peat devoted a full 2006 newsletter to the subject, returned to it in hundreds of email exchanges, and recommended it in nearly every interview he gave across three decades. "When people with cancer ask for my recommendations, they usually think I'm joking when I tell them to use aspirin," he wrote in [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer]], "and very often they don't take it, on the basis of what seems to be a very strong cultural prejudice." That cultural prejudice--manufactured, in Peat's telling, by pharmaceutical companies with competing products to sell--has obscured what he considered one of the most broadly protective substances available to human beings.

## The Inadequacy of the COX Story

The orthodox explanation for aspirin's effects begins and ends with its inhibition of the cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes that produce [[prostaglandins]]. In the early 1970s, researchers discovered that aspirin transfers its acetyl group to the COX enzyme, inactivating it. This became, as Peat put it, "the orthodox 'explanation' for what aspirin does, though it neglected to explain that [[salicylic-acid|salicylic acid]] (lacking the acetyl radical) had been widely known in the previous century for its very useful antiinflammatory actions." The COX theory explained one mechanism while distracting attention from everything else aspirin does.

Peat saw the COX enzymes as just one system among many that are activated by stress. "Aromatase, that makes [[estrogen]], enzymes that make [[histamine]], [[serotonin]] and [[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]], the [[cytokines]], and the stress-induced hormones of the [[pituitary]] and [[adrenal-glands|adrenal glands]], are turned on in difficult situations, and have to be turned off when the threat has been overcome," he wrote. Aspirin acts on all of these systems, not just the prostaglandin pathway--which is why the COX-inhibition story, while not wrong, is radically incomplete.

The comparison with the newer COX-2 inhibitors (Celecoxib, Vioxx) sharpened Peat's critique. He noted that the structures of these drugs are "remarkably similar to the 'designer estrogens'" like Tamoxifen, and that "rather than protecting the circulatory system as aspirin does," the COX-2 inhibitors "damage it." The pharmaceutical industry had created expensive, patentable alternatives that were both less effective and more dangerous than the generic substance they were designed to replace.

## Prostaglandin Inhibition: The First Layer

While Peat considered the COX story insufficient, he did not consider it unimportant. [[Prostaglandins]]--particularly [[PGE2]]--were central villains in his framework. As he explained in a [[dfa298|discussion of food additives and inflammation]], "More people are recognizing the importance of aspirin and stopping inflammation. As we age, we accumulate polyunsaturated fats in our tissues. Under stress, these circulate and produce inflammatory prostaglandins. Aspirin is an anti-aging defense against a lifetime of accumulating PUFAs."

The mechanism connects directly to [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]]. [[Arachidonic-acid|Arachidonic acid]], a highly unsaturated fat that the body synthesizes from [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] even when it is not consumed directly, becomes "one of the most toxic stored fats," as Peat described in a [[312699|discussion of fatty acids and diabetes]]. "When released, it turns into prostaglandins, which are probably our biggest inflammatory problem." Aspirin blocks the conversion of arachidonic acid into these inflammatory mediators, but it also does something the COX story omits: it "inhibits the release of fatty acids from both phospholipids and triglycerides," preventing the upstream liberation of the fats that would become prostaglandins in the first place.

In a [[fa2194|discussion of heart attacks and brain injuries]], Sarah Murray asked whether aspirin and [[progesterone]] both work by blocking prostaglandins. Peat's answer was characteristically broader: "They both stop the inflammatory process which leads to tissue damage. The brain is more sensitive than the heart. Research shows that if you can get a big dose of progesterone to someone who has had a potentially fatal head injury, they will have a high survival and recovery rate because progesterone stops the progression of brain damage."

## Aromatase Inhibition and the Anti-Estrogenic Effect

One of aspirin's most consequential actions in Peat's framework is its suppression of [[estrogen]] production. "Since the polyunsaturated fats and prostaglandins stimulate the expression of [[aromatase]], the enzyme that synthesizes estrogen, aspirin decreases the production of estrogen," he wrote in his newsletter. The logic is layered: PUFA produces prostaglandins; prostaglandins activate aromatase; aromatase converts androgens to estrogen; aspirin interrupts this cascade at multiple points.

Peat was explicit about the clinical significance. In a [[ea7342|2013 discussion of breast cancer]], he stated: "For about 20 years I've been mentioning aspirin as probably the safest first thing that anyone who worries about breast cancer should start. It is a very effective way to turn off estrogen production and response to estrogen." In another interview, he explained the mechanism more precisely: "Cutting out polyunsaturated fats is key because they produce prostaglandins, which are the main force activating aromatase and estrogen production. Aspirin is a natural substance that blocks aromatase and is very safe to use in breast cancer. [[Progesterone]] is another."

The anti-estrogenic dimension of aspirin connects it to Peat's broader argument that [[estrogen]] and [[inflammation]] are two names for closely related biological processes. He noted that soot--known since the 1930s to be both carcinogenic and estrogenic--led to the production of the first commercial estrogens, and that "any intelligent person reading the chemical and biological publications of that time will see how closely associated cancer, inflammation, and estrogen are." Aspirin opposes all three.

## Mitochondrial Respiration: The Deepest Mechanism

The most distinctive feature of Peat's analysis of aspirin is his claim that it acts at the level of cellular energy production. "Aspirin activates both glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration, and this means that it shifts the mitochondria away from the oxidation of fats, toward the oxidation of [[glucose]], resulting in the increased production of [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]]," he wrote. "Its action on the glycolytic enzyme, GAPDH, is the opposite of estrogen's."

This shift from fat oxidation to glucose oxidation is metabolically profound in Peat's framework. [[Estrogen]] pushes cells toward glycolysis--the inefficient fermentation of glucose into [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]--while interfering with mitochondrial respiration. This resembles the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]] in cancer cells: energy production that relies on fermentation rather than oxidation. Aspirin moves in the opposite direction, supporting the complete oxidation of glucose to carbon dioxide and water.

In his newsletter on fatigue and aging, Peat placed aspirin alongside [[thyroid]] hormone as a mitochondrial uncoupler: "Aspirin and thyroid (T3) increase uncoupling. A drug that used to be used for weight reduction, DNP, also uncouples mitochondrial metabolism, and, surprisingly, it has some of the beneficial effects of thyroid and aspirin. It stimulates the consumption of [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] and the formation of [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]]." The uncoupling effect means aspirin increases the ratio of heat to ATP, which Peat associated with metabolic health rather than metabolic waste. "By activating T3, aspirin can sometimes increase the temperature and pulse rate," he noted--a sign that the metabolic rate is genuinely rising, not merely being stimulated artificially.

In the diabetes newsletter, Peat wrote that aspirin "improves mitochondrial respiration and helps to regulate blood sugar and lipids," and that "aspirin's broad range of beneficial effects is probably analogous to [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]]'s, being proportional to protection against the broad range of toxic effects of the polyunsaturated fats."

## The Mead Acid Hypothesis

Peat proposed an elegant unifying theory for why aspirin works so broadly. In an essential fatty acid-free diet, the body produces large amounts of the [[mead-acid|Mead acid]] (n-9 eicosatrienoic acid), which is antiinflammatory and whose derivatives act on the cannabinoid receptor. The accumulation of PUFA suppresses Mead acid production throughout life.

"If salicylic acid and the structurally similar antiinflammatories, local anesthetics, muscle relaxants, expectorants, and antihistamines, act as surrogates for the absent Mead acid family, and thereby act as defenses against all the toxic effects of the unstable fats, it would explain the breadth and apparent coherence of their usefulness," he wrote. Aspirin, in this view, is not merely blocking a few enzymes--it is substituting for a protective lipid system that modern diets have suppressed. "The competition between aspirin and salicylic acid, and other antiinflammatories, for the active site on the COX enzyme shows that the structural features of these molecules are in some ways analogous to those of the polyunsaturated fatty acids."

This hypothesis explains why aspirin's effects mirror those of an essential fatty acid-free diet, and why its benefits are so strangely broad. It is not acting on one receptor or one pathway but occupying the same molecular niches that PUFA and their toxic derivatives would otherwise fill.

## Cancer

Cancer was the condition for which Peat most urgently recommended aspirin. His newsletter title--"Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer"--signals the priority. The mechanisms are multiple: prostaglandin inhibition reduces tumor-promoting inflammation; aromatase inhibition reduces estrogen exposure; improved mitochondrial respiration opposes the Warburg effect; and aspirin has direct anti-metastatic and anti-angiogenic properties.

Peat described a dramatic clinical case: "Several years ago, a woman whose doctors said it would be impossible to operate on her extremely painful 'inflammatory breast cancer,' had overnight complete relief of the pain and swelling from taking a few aspirins." He continued: "The recognized anti-metastatic effect of aspirin, and its ability to inhibit the development of new blood vessels that would support the tumor's growth, make it an appropriate drug to use for pain control, even if it doesn't shrink the tumor. In studies of many kinds of tumor, though, it does cause regression, or at least slows tumor growth."

Aspirin also protects against the systemic consequences of cancer: "wasting (cachexia), immunosuppression, and strokes." Peat contrasted this with opiates, the standard medical prescription for cancer pain: "Biologically, they are the most inappropriate means of pain control, since they increase the release of histamine, which synergizes with the tumor-derived factors to suppress immunity and stimulate tumor growth."

In his email exchanges, Peat recommended aspirin as cancer prevention: "I think a little aspirin, regularly if not daily, is good prevention, if you are sure to get enough [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]], to prevent excess bleeding." For active cancer treatment, the doses were far higher: "People using it with cancer usually take a daily total of 6 grams or more."

In his newsletter on breast cancer, he placed aspirin in a therapeutic constellation: "Both preventively and therapeutically, the use of the antiinflammatory and antioxidative substances such as aspirin, [[caffeine]], [[progesterone]], and [[thyroid]] hormone would seem appropriate. Aspirin is coming to be widely accepted as an anticancer agent, and at moderate doses can cause cancer cells to die."

## Brain and Neurodegeneration

Peat's case for aspirin in neurological disease was extensive. "Aspirin protects against several kinds of toxicity, including excitotoxicity (glutamate), dopamine toxicity, and oxidative free radical toxicity," he wrote. "Since its effects on the mitochondria are similar to those of thyroid (T3), using both of them might improve brain energy production more than just thyroid."

For [[parkinsons-disease|Parkinson's disease]] specifically, Peat identified a prostaglandin mechanism: "One of the prostaglandins produced in the [[substantia-nigra]] is very toxic to the brain. Simply having a regular use of aspirin will protect against that prostaglandin made from polyunsaturated fats. The breakdown of polyunsaturated fats produces acrolein, a very toxic material. That is one of the things that turns synuclein into a toxic fibrous material, sort of like the prions in mad cow disease."

For [[dementia|Alzheimer's disease]], the evidence was epidemiological as well as mechanistic: regular aspirin users had markedly lower incidence. Peat recommended combining aspirin with [[magnesium]], [[niacinamide]], and other nerve-protective substances.

## Diabetes and Blood Sugar Regulation

Aspirin's effects on blood sugar operate through its suppression of [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]]. As Peat explained in a [[312699|discussion of diabetes]]: "If you focus on stopping the liberation of fatty acids from your tissues and inhibit their conversion to prostaglandins, you can usually very quickly lower your blood sugar quite a bit and feel better. The two chemicals that are most practical for lowering free fatty acids and stopping the stress reaction are [[niacinamide]] and aspirin."

He elaborated: "Niacinamide has many effects, but the first one is inhibiting the lipase that liberates fats from your fat stores. Aspirin also does that." The connection to the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] is direct: when free fatty acids flood the bloodstream, they block glucose oxidation, forcing cells to rely on inefficient glycolysis. By suppressing lipolysis, aspirin restores the cell's ability to use glucose properly.

The research Peat cited was striking. A 2001 study in *Science* demonstrated "reversal of obesity- and diet-induced insulin resistance with salicylates or targeted disruption of IKK-beta"--suggesting that aspirin's anti-inflammatory action and its insulin-sensitizing action are the same thing, operating through the NF-kB pathway.

## Antiviral Properties

Peat made a remarkable claim about aspirin's antiviral activity. In a [[2422ff|discussion of vaccines and viruses]], he told listeners: "Aspirin, if it were under patent, would be considered a 'super antiviral' because it is active against a wide variety, including influenza, herpes, rhinovirus, and hepatitis."

In another interview, he connected aspirin's antiviral properties to its broader anti-inflammatory action: "Aspirin is also not only anti-inflammatory but has well-established broad-spectrum antiviral activity." He contrasted this with conventional antiviral drugs like Chloroquine, Acyclovir, and nucleoside analogs, which "are aimed at killing the virus by damaging its DNA or RNA replication. These also happen to damage human DNA at the expense of mutating millions of people." Aspirin's approach--supporting the body's own oxidative metabolism rather than poisoning viral replication--was, in Peat's view, both safer and more broadly effective.

## Fibrosis and Aging

Peat connected aspirin to the fundamental process of aging through its effects on [[fibrosis]] and [[collagen]] deposition. In a [[340f50|discussion of wound healing and aging]], he explained: "A fetus or newborn heals an injury without scars. But as the PUFA in our tissue accumulates, wounds heal more slowly, cause more inflammation, and leave scars. Whenever you have systemic inflammation producing lactic acid and prostaglandins, both increase the deposition of collagen. With aging, the body approaches something like a big scar with very high collagen content."

Aspirin intervenes in this process: "By blocking prostaglandins and several other inflammatory mechanisms," it provides "important protection from inflammation and fibrosis." The implication extends to every organ: cardiac fibrosis after heart attacks, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and the general tissue stiffening that characterizes aging. Aspirin's anti-fibrotic effect is not a separate action from its anti-inflammatory effect--it is the same action, operating in the connective tissue matrix.

Peat also cited aspirin's ability to inhibit protein glycation and crosslinking--the chemical processes that stiffen collagen and contribute to the aging of skin, blood vessels, and the lens of the eye.

## Sleep

Peat recommended aspirin for sleep in his email exchanges with a characteristic blend of confidence and nuance. "Taking some at bedtime can be very helpful for sleeping; sometimes I take about 500 mg at night, but other times just a little," he wrote. The mechanism connects to stress hormone suppression: "When aspirin and niacinamide lower the temperature I think it's because they lower the stress hormones."

He also issued a practical caveat: "It can help with sleep, but you should try it first in the afternoon, because sometimes its first effect can stimulate your metabolism and delay sleep." This bidirectional response--sometimes sedating, sometimes stimulating--reflects aspirin's action on the metabolic rate rather than on any sedative receptor. If the person's metabolism is suppressed by stress hormones, aspirin may initially raise it (producing wakefulness) before the stress-lowering effect produces calm. The advice to test in the afternoon before committing to a bedtime dose was typical of Peat's respect for individual variation.

He also noted that aspirin, [[coffee]], and [[niacinamide]] all lower [[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]], and that "under-sleeping increases nitric oxide, and catch-up sleep lowers it"--connecting aspirin's sleep benefit to its broader opposition to the stress-inflammation axis.

## Cardiovascular Protection

It is now standard medical practice to advise someone having a heart attack to immediately chew and swallow an aspirin tablet. Peat endorsed this but placed it in a much broader context. Aspirin's cardiovascular protection comes not just from its antiplatelet effect but from its opposition to the entire inflammatory cascade that damages blood vessels: prostaglandin inhibition, estrogen suppression, free fatty acid reduction, and support of mitochondrial respiration.

He also noted aspirin's ability to reduce the fibrosis that follows a heart attack, preventing the scarring that leads to heart failure. In the context of stroke, he recommended starting with [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] before introducing aspirin: "Vitamin K (1 or 2) is probably better to use before aspirin, since it helps to prevent both more clotting and also bleeding. A day after the vitamin K the aspirin would be safer, and it helps to protect brain cells."

## Fertility

"Aspirin, like progesterone or [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]], can improve fertility, by suppressing a prostaglandin, and improving uterine circulation," Peat wrote. Research he cited showed that low-dose aspirin improved outcomes in women with impaired uterine perfusion during assisted conception, and in cases of intrauterine growth retardation. The mechanism is consistent with aspirin's broader profile: by reducing prostaglandin-mediated inflammation and improving blood flow, it creates the conditions the uterine lining needs for successful implantation.

## The Reye Syndrome Myth

Peat devoted significant attention to the campaign that displaced aspirin from American medicine cabinets. "In the 1980s, there was a big publicity campaign warning parents that giving aspirin to a child with the flu could cause the potentially deadly [[reye-syndrome|Reye syndrome]]. Aspirin sales declined sharply, as sales of [[acetaminophen]] (Tylenol, etc.) increased tremendously." The data, however, told a different story: "In Australia, a study of Reye syndrome cases found that six times as many of them had been using acetaminophen as had used aspirin."

The displacement of aspirin by acetaminophen was, in Peat's view, one of the most consequential pharmaceutical marketing victories of the twentieth century. Acetaminophen lacks aspirin's anti-inflammatory properties, its mitochondrial effects, its anti-estrogenic action, and its cancer-protective properties, while carrying its own serious toxicity to the liver. In his email exchanges, Peat noted that "aspirin and caffeine help to detoxify acetaminophen"--suggesting that the two drugs are not equivalent alternatives but that aspirin is actively protective against the damage acetaminophen can cause.

## Stomach Protection: Reversing the Narrative

The claim that aspirin damages the stomach was, in Peat's telling, another case of pharmaceutical propaganda outrunning pharmacological reality. "Aspirin rapidly breaks down into acetic acid and salicylic acid (which is found in many fruits), and salicylic acid is protective to the stomach and intestine, and other organs," he wrote. "When aspirin was compared with the other common antiinflammatory drugs, it was found that the salicylic acid it releases protects against the damage done by another drug. Repeated use of aspirin protects the stomach against very strong irritants."

The animal studies that showed stomach damage "are designed to produce ulcers, not to realistically model the way aspirin is used"--often using single doses equivalent to ten or a hundred aspirin tablets. In practical use, "the slight irritation produced by a normal dose of aspirin can be minimized by dissolving the aspirin in water. The stomach develops a tolerance for aspirin over a period of a few days, allowing the dose to be increased if necessary."

In his email exchanges, Peat was emphatic: "I don't know anyone who has a stomach reaction when they dissolve the aspirin in hot water, and then take it with food."

## Aspirin Allergy

Peat addressed aspirin allergy with the same metabolic lens he applied to everything else. "I know people who had lifelong 'aspirin allergy' who now use it regularly. I think part of it is the metabolic problems caused by [[PUFA]] and low [[thyroid]] function." As the metabolic state improves--through PUFA elimination, thyroid support, and other interventions--sensitivities that seemed permanent often resolve. The allergy is not to the aspirin molecule itself but to the inflammatory state that aspirin perturbs.

## Practical Use: Form, Dosage, and Storage

### Forms

Peat had clear preferences. "I mostly use the pure crystals," he wrote. "I buy it by the kilo." In Mexico, he used Bayer tablets. He noted that USP aspirin sold for animals "is the same as used for people, except that it doesn't contain the toxic additives of the tablets." Alternatives included magnesium salicylate and plain salicylic acid, both of which "should be used dissolved and with food."

Aspirin can also be absorbed through the skin: "Rheumatic problems have been treated by adding the drug to bath water."

### Dosage

Peat's dosage recommendations varied by purpose:

- **Preventive use:** "A little aspirin, regularly if not daily"--context-dependent, adjusted to individual response
- **Baby aspirin (90 mg):** Adequate for basic prevention; Peat noted that even at this dose, [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] "would be protective against even more aspirin"
- **Sleep:** "Sometimes I take about 500 mg at night, but other times just a little"
- **Cancer:** "People using it with cancer usually take a daily total of 6 grams or more"
- **Upper awareness:** "7000 mg is a lot, and it's very important to take vitamin K with aspirin"

His personal practice was approximately 1,000 mg daily with vitamin K support.

### Vitamin K: The Mandatory Companion

This is the single most important practical point in Peat's aspirin guidance. "Vitamin K protects against bleeding and other effects of prolonged aspirin use." He returned to this requirement in nearly every discussion of aspirin dosage. "It depends on the context; aspirin makes you need more vitamin K, even when you aren't using much."

For dosing vitamin K: "500 micrograms to one milligram is good anyway, and it would be protective against even more aspirin." He recommended natural sources--"for example liver once a week"--alongside supplementation. Twenty-five grams of spinach plus five to ten grams of beef liver would provide sufficient natural vitamin K. For higher aspirin doses (cancer treatment), generous vitamin K supplementation becomes essential.

Peat had a cautionary personal experience with vitamin K2 supplements, reporting abdominal discomfort, which he attributed to the excipient oil rather than the vitamin itself, leading him to use it topically on his skin instead.

### Storage

"I think the crystals are more stable, but I keep the big container (a multi-year supply) in the freezer, and keep out enough for a couple of months. The powdered forms developed an acetic acid smell with time, the crystals don't." If aspirin smells like vinegar, it is decomposing into salicylic acid and acetic acid--but Peat noted that "both of those are safe." He reported having "some aspirin, USP, that's at least 10 years old that's still good."

## Cold and flu use

Aspirin is the central anti-inflammatory tool in Peat's cold-and-flu protocol — covered in detail at [[cold-flu]] and [[immune-protocol]], with the underlying mechanism at [[prostaglandins]]. The relevant move during a respiratory viral illness is not anti-viral in the conventional drug-design sense; it is the suppression of the inflammatory cascade — prostaglandins, angiotensin-II, cytokines — that turns a containable infection into the cluster of symptoms people call "the flu."

Peat repeatedly framed aspirin this way in his 2020 KMUD episodes. "Aspirin is also not only anti-inflammatory but has well-established broad-spectrum antiviral activity," he said in the [[2020-05-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-may-2020-kmud|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, May 2020]]. The same period gave him an occasion to make the economic-not-pharmacological frame explicit: if aspirin "were under patent, it would be considered a 'super antiviral' because it is active against a wide variety, including influenza, herpes, rhinovirus, and hepatitis" ([[2020-03-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-coronavirus|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, March 20, 2020]]).

The mechanism that connects aspirin's anti-prostaglandin action to viral illness is layered. Prostaglandins amplify the inflammatory response to viral irritation in lungs and sinuses, drive fever and cytokine release, and — more interestingly in Peat's reading — alter the angiotensin axis the virus exploits for entry. "All of our known protective, pro-immune but anti-inflammatory substances lower the expression of angiotensin receptor 1," he said in the March 18, 2020 *Politics and Science* — aspirin, vitamin D, and progesterone converge on that protective state.

**Dosing during acute respiratory illness:**

- **Adult acute dose:** 325–500 mg every 4–6 hours during a flu, dissolved in warm water and taken with food. From the [[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|*Ask the Herb Doctor* Allergy, March 2016]] episode, Peat emphasized the bedtime dose for breaking the overnight inflammatory cycle that defines the worst phase of a flu.
- **Baseline (Peat's personal use):** ~300 mg/day USP crystals dissolved in warm water, scaled up during acute illness (*One Radio Network*, October 2019).
- **[[vitamin-k|Vitamin K2]] companion:** 1 mg K2 per 325 mg aspirin once you exceed baby-aspirin dose. Non-negotiable. The [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|*Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer*]] newsletter and the [[ecd504|aspirin email exchanges]] both treat this as the gate.

**Fever and aspirin — what Peat actually claimed.** A common objection: aspirin suppresses a therapeutic fever, so it interferes with the immune response. Peat's answer, from the March 31, 2020 *Politics and Science* episode, was that aspirin does something different from [[acetaminophen]]. "Aspirin can often increase your thyroid function and cell respiration so much that it doesn't affect your fever, but it can lower the *need* for a fever. Since angiotensin overproduction is a basic harm factor of any infection, you're stopping the mechanism of the harm. That is what is lowering the fever. It isn't just mechanically turning off heat production. It actually increases oxidative metabolism and, at least temporarily, will increase thyroid function. So when it reduces a fever, I think it's most often because it's remedying the thing that is causing the fever."

The distinction matters operationally. If you take aspirin during a flu and the fever resolves quickly, that is — in Peat's framing — evidence the underlying inflammation is being addressed at its source. If it persists despite aspirin, the metabolic stressor is still active and the fever is doing real adaptive work (white-cell phagocytosis is faster, antibody production accelerates). Acetaminophen suppresses fever downstream by interfering with heat production; aspirin works on the upstream drivers. Two qualitatively different interventions, often confused under the single label "antipyretic."

## Dental abscess and tooth pain

Peat's discussion of dental health (the email topic at [[teeth-gums|teeth and gums]] is one of his fuller short pieces) treats infected teeth as a problem of [[endotoxin]] absorption from the gut, [[thyroid]]-mediated saliva quality, and the energetic state of the dentine — not strictly as a local microbial issue. Within that framing, aspirin appears in two related ways: as a systemic anti-inflammatory during an abscess, and as a topical application directly on the affected tooth and gum.

The systemic use follows the cold-and-flu logic: an abscess is local inflammation that draws in the same prostaglandin and cytokine machinery aspirin opposes. 325–500 mg dissolved in warm water, taken with food, with the dose repeated every 4–6 hours during acute pain, with vitamin K2 cover.

The topical use is the Peat-distinctive move. Dissolved aspirin (a pinch of crystals or a crushed tablet in roughly 30 mL of warm water) is held in the mouth over the affected tooth or gum, swished gently, and held for 30–60 seconds before swallowing or spitting. The local salicylate concentration reaches the tissue directly. Peat noted aspirin's transdermal absorption in the [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|2006 newsletter]] — "both aspirin and salicylic acid can be absorbed through the skin, so rheumatic problems have been treated by adding the drug to bath water" — and the same logic applies to the oral mucosa, which absorbs salicylates readily.

Beyond pain relief, the topical application appears to have a regenerative effect on dentine. In Generative Energy #58, Georgi Dinkov mentioned a 2018 study showing that topical aspirin applied directly to teeth produced measurable tooth regeneration. Peat's response located the finding inside his broader skepticism of the acid theory of cavities: "The idea that bacteria just make an acid… I think it's much more complicated than that, involving antibodies and subtler handling of the minerals. Thyroid is the main regulator of the health of your saliva, and the quality of your saliva really is in charge of what happens to your teeth on the outside." Topical aspirin works, in this reading, by lowering the local inflammatory load and letting the tooth's own regenerative capacity — which depends on a stable hormonal-vitamin-D-calcium balance — close the cavity from the inside.

This is not a substitute for a dental visit when one is needed (and Peat was specific in the [[teeth-gums|teeth-gums email]] that "any obvious cavity should be cleaned out and filled"). It is a bridge tool for acute pain, and a regular adjunct for chronic gingival inflammation. The full dental framing — thyroid, [[vitamin-d]], [[vitamin-k|K]], dietary endotoxin reduction via raw [[carrot-salad|carrot]], avoidance of [[pufa]]-rich foods — belongs at [[dental-health]].

## Dissolved-with-baking-soda preparation

Peat's preferred method of taking aspirin is not the swallowed tablet. It is aspirin crystals (or a crushed tablet) dissolved in warm water, taken with food. The [[ecd504|aspirin email]] is emphatic: "I don't know anyone who has a stomach reaction when they dissolve the aspirin in hot water, and then take it with food." The mechanism is straightforward — undissolved tablet fragments concentrate salicylate at small patches of gastric mucosa for the minutes it takes to disintegrate, producing the local irritation that drives the (much-exaggerated) "aspirin destroys the stomach" story. Pre-dissolution distributes the dose evenly through the gastric contents, and the [[salicylic-acid|salicylic acid]] hydrolysis product is itself protective to the gastric and intestinal mucosa — the inversion covered in the existing "Stomach Protection" section above.

Adding a small amount of [[sodium-bicarbonate|sodium bicarbonate]] (baking soda) to the warm water before dissolving the aspirin is the additional refinement. The bicarbonate buffers the solution, raises the pH, and helps dissolve the aspirin completely. In the March 2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode, a caller asked about combining aspirin and baking soda in a bath. Peat's response confirmed the bicarbonate logic: "It has diuretic effects and some anti-inflammatory effects from the carbon dioxide itself" — the alkalinity, the [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] release, and the salicylate work synergistically. The same chemistry applies inside the cup as in the bath.

**Practical preparation:**

1. Warm water (not boiling — hot enough to dissolve, not hot enough to accelerate hydrolysis into [[salicylic-acid|salicylic acid]] and acetic acid). Roughly 100 mL.
2. Optional: a quarter-teaspoon (~1 g) of [[sodium-bicarbonate|baking soda]] dissolved first.
3. Add the aspirin (crystals preferred — Peat bought USP crystals by the kilo, per the [[ecd504|aspirin email]]). Crushed tablets work but bring tablet excipients along.
4. Stir until fully dissolved. The crystals dissolve faster than tablets.
5. Drink with food. The bicarbonate-buffered solution is mild on the gastric mucosa.

Peat's [[ecd504|email exchanges]] note that magnesium salicylate ("salsalate" appears, likely typo for salicylate) and plain salicylic acid are alternatives — "which should be used dissolved and with food." The pattern is consistent: salicylates in solution, with food, with [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] cover, are the form he recommended throughout three decades of correspondence.

A note on the bicarbonate angle. Beyond the local gastric buffering, aspirin-with-baking-soda raises systemic CO2 modestly — the bicarbonate liberates CO2 as it neutralizes stomach acid, and some of that CO2 enters the blood. Peat treated [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]] as a central protective metabolite, so the combination is internally consistent with his framework rather than a workaround. The dose of bicarbonate is small — this is not the gram-scale bicarbonate loading some cancer protocols use, but a buffering aid that improves how the aspirin lands.

## Kids dosing — the Reye-syndrome reread

This is the section where the safety conservatism in Peat's framework needs to be presented with the mainstream contraindication intact. Aspirin in children with viral illness (chickenpox, influenza, generic febrile flu-like illness) is contraindicated by the American Academy of Pediatrics, the FDA, the CDC, and essentially every paediatric standard-of-care document since the early 1980s, because of the association with [[reye-syndrome|Reye syndrome]] — a rare but catastrophic encephalopathy and hepatic failure that disproportionately struck children given aspirin during viral illness.

Peat's reread does not deny the case series. It denies the causal attribution. The fullest statement is in [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|*Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer*]] (2006): "In the 1980s, there was a big publicity campaign warning parents that giving aspirin to a child with the flu could cause the potentially deadly Reye syndrome. Aspirin sales declined sharply, as sales of [[acetaminophen]] (Tylenol, etc.) increased tremendously. But in Australia, a study of Reye syndrome cases found that six times as many of them had been using acetaminophen as had used aspirin." The citation is Orlowski's "A Catch in the Reye" (*Pediatrics* 82:391, 1988) — a paper Peat read as showing the substitution had moved patients toward the more dangerous drug, not away from it.

The structural argument in the same newsletter: acetaminophen carries serious hepatotoxicity with a narrow therapeutic window, lacks aspirin's anti-inflammatory mitochondrial and anti-estrogenic actions, and produces N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) — a glutathione-depleting metabolite that mechanistically resembles the hepatic insult Reye syndrome involves. The drug that replaced aspirin in the paediatric medicine cabinet looks, on this reading, more likely to be a Reye-syndrome inducer than the drug it displaced. In the [[ecd504|aspirin email]], Peat noted that "aspirin and caffeine help to detoxify acetaminophen" — implying the two drugs are not equivalent substitutes but operate in opposite directions on the same metabolic axis.

**What Peat actually said about doses in children.** This is where the corpus is thin. Peat did not write a paediatric dosing newsletter. The closest material is the 2012 [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|*Ask the Herb Doctor* dementia episode]] where Sarah Murray noted Andrew Wakefield's finding that autistic children improved when given antibiotics or aspirin — Peat's response framed it as endotoxin and serotonin reduction, not a paediatric dosing recommendation. The other relevant data point: Peat's general position was that the doses appropriate to children scale by metabolic mass — a baby-aspirin equivalent (75–90 mg) is a child-scale dose, taken with food, dissolved in water, with [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] cover, in the context of an otherwise functional metabolic substrate (warm child, eating, hydrated, not in shock).

**The honest framing.** The Reye-syndrome contraindication is the prevailing standard of care, and that standard is what every paediatrician will follow. Peat's reread, supported by Orlowski's data and the structural pharmacology of acetaminophen, suggests the substitution may have been an error — but the error has been institutionalized for forty years and is not going to be unwound by an encyclopedia entry. Parents weighing aspirin for a child with chickenpox or influenza should know:

1. Mainstream paediatrics treats this as contraindicated. The label warning is explicit.
2. The original Reye-syndrome epidemiology has serious confounders, and the dominant alternative ([[acetaminophen]]) carries its own well-characterized toxicity.
3. Peat's framing treats inflammation — not the virus and not the fever per se — as the primary driver of viral-illness damage in children as in adults, and aspirin as the most direct anti-inflammatory tool available.
4. The decision is the parent's, made with whatever paediatric input is available, and with the full picture in view rather than a single warning label.

In our household frame (Charlie's young daughter Emma is in scope), this article does not recommend overriding the Reye contraindication. It documents Peat's reread of the evidence, the reasons he found acetaminophen the worse replacement, and the absence of a clear paediatric protocol in his published corpus. If aspirin is used at all in a young child, it would be in small doses, with [[vitamin-k|vitamin K2]] cover, dissolved with [[sodium-bicarbonate|baking soda]] in warm water, with food, in the absence of any sign of encephalopathy or hepatic distress — and with the prudence that comes from knowing the standard-of-care position. The full paediatric question deserves a dedicated page; the entry point belongs at [[reye-syndrome]] once that is built.

## Overdose signal — "aspirin breath" and salicylism

Aspirin is among the safer drugs in routine use, but it has an upper limit. The clinical name for aspirin toxicity is salicylism, and its early warning signs are unusually specific — which is one reason Peat treated it as a manageable rather than mysterious upper bound. The corpus has the chemistry of decomposed aspirin smelling like vinegar (the [[ecd504|aspirin email]]: "If the aspirin smells like vinegar it's decomposing"; "It breaks down into salicylic acid and acetic acid, and both of those are safe") and the systemic upper-limit warnings ("7000 mg is a lot, and it's very important to take vitamin K with aspirin"). The "aspirin breath" / "vinegar breath" sign in a person is the clinical correlate — when systemic aspirin is high enough that the salicylate-and-acetic-acid metabolites are detectable on the breath, the person is at or near the salicylism threshold.

The recognized salicylism syndrome includes, in approximate order of appearance:

- **Tinnitus and altered hearing.** Often the first sign. A persistent ringing or sense of muffled hearing at doses above ~3–4 g/day, in a person not previously dosing at that level. Reversible on dose reduction.
- **Hyperventilation.** Salicylate is a respiratory stimulant at toxic doses; the breathing rate rises and the person blows off [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]], producing respiratory alkalosis. Peat's broader framing of CO2 as a protective metabolite makes this particularly visible — the same physiological derangement aspirin counters in [[hyperthyroidism|hyperthyroid]] and high-[[adrenalin|adrenalin]] states is what salicylate excess itself produces.
- **Vinegar / sour breath.** The acetic-acid breakdown product of aspirin becomes detectable. This is the colloquial "aspirin breath" sign — once you notice it on yourself or someone else, the systemic load is high enough to warrant pause.
- **Nausea, vomiting, sweating.** Mid-stage salicylism.
- **Confusion, seizures, hyperthermia.** Severe salicylism. Medical emergency. Activated charcoal and IV bicarbonate are standard treatment.

In normal Peat-recommended doses (90 mg–1 g/day chronically, up to several grams during cancer treatment with [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] cover), salicylism is not a realistic risk. It becomes one in two scenarios: aspirin "stacking" without dose awareness (e.g., a person already on chronic 3 g/day adding more during an acute illness without adjusting), and accidental overdose, particularly in small children where the dose-to-mass ratio escalates quickly.

The "aspirin breath" / vinegar-breath sign is most useful as a *self-monitoring* tool in chronic high-dose users — the cancer-protocol patient on 6 g/day, the arthritis patient who has been escalating doses. If the breath starts to smell sour, back off. The same logic applies in paediatric monitoring: a child showing the vinegar-breath sign has had too much, and the dose needs to come down. Peat treated this kind of in-body signal as the appropriate feedback loop — "the amount depends on how you react to it, and can change as your metabolism adjusts," from the [[ecd504|aspirin email]]. Aspirin dosing is a metabolic conversation with the body, not a fixed prescription, and the body's signals (tinnitus, vinegar breath, mild hyperventilation) are the relevant evidence.

The salicylism upper bound also reframes one of the existing claims in this article. Aspirin "increases the temperature and pulse rate" by activating T3 and mitochondrial respiration — at therapeutic doses. At salicylism doses, the same mechanism overshoots into hyperthermia, the temperature curve no longer reflecting therapeutic uncoupling but uncontrolled metabolic runaway. The narrow window between "aspirin raises my temperature pleasantly" and "aspirin is causing dangerous hyperthermia" is one of the practical reasons [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] cover and dose escalation under guidance (Sarah Murray's standing offer in the [[2021-08-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-august-2021-kmud|August 2021 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]] for callers using more than baby-aspirin doses) matter.

## Synergistic Substances

Aspirin appears throughout Peat's work in combination with other protective substances:

- **[[Vitamin E]]:** "Vitamin E is now known to suppress COX-2, synergizing with aspirin and opposing estrogen." The two substances have overlapping but complementary protective spectra.
- **[[Coffee]] / [[caffeine]]:** Both are antilipolytic (suppressing free fatty acid release) and antiinflammatory. Peat placed them together with aspirin as the practical triad for daily metabolic support.
- **[[Thyroid]]:** Aspirin's mitochondrial uncoupling effect parallels T3's, and the two together "might improve brain energy production more than just thyroid."
- **[[Niacinamide]]:** Like aspirin, it inhibits lipolysis and lowers stress hormones. The combination is particularly effective for blood sugar regulation.
- **[[Progesterone]]:** Both oppose estrogen, block prostaglandins, and protect the brain. In cancer and neurological injury, Peat recommended them together.
- **[[Coconut oil]]:** Both suppress free fatty acid toxicity through different mechanisms--aspirin by blocking lipolysis and prostaglandin synthesis, coconut oil by competing with PUFA at the mitochondrial level.

## The Broad Coherence

Peat closed his aspirin newsletter with a statement that captures his view of both the substance and the forces arrayed against understanding it: "I think it's important that we don't allow the drug publicists to obscure the broad importance of substances such as aspirin, vitamin E, progesterone, and thyroid. For 60 years, a myth that was created to sell estrogen has harmed both science and the health of many people."

The "strangely beneficial effects" of aspirin--antioxidant yet pro-respiratory, anticoagulant yet able to reduce excessive menstrual bleeding, able to inhibit abnormal cell division while promoting normal cell division, capable of facilitating learning while preventing excitotoxic nerve injury--"strongly suggest that it is acting on very basic biological processes, in a coherent way." That coherence, in Peat's framework, is the coherence of supporting [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]] against the forces--PUFA accumulation, estrogen dominance, prostaglandin excess, mitochondrial decline--that progressively undermine it across the lifespan.

"Although it would undoubtedly be best to grow up eating foods with relatively saturated fats," he concluded, "the use of aspirin preventively and therapeutically seems very reasonable under the present circumstances. Preventing blindness, degenerative brain diseases, heart and lung diseases, and cancer with aspirin should get as much support as the crazy public health recommendations are now getting from government and foundations and the medical businesses."
