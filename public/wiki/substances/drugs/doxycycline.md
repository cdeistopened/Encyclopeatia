# Doxycycline

Doxycycline is the antibiotic Ray Peat actually liked. In a framework hostile to most pharmaceuticals and especially hostile to long-course broad-spectrum antibiotics, the tetracycline class — tetracycline itself, minocycline, and doxycycline — kept earning the rare endorsement. Peat returned to these three drugs across decades of emails, broadcasts, and podcast conversations not as antimicrobial agents in the conventional sense but as anti-inflammatory and anti-endotoxin tools that happen to also be antibiotic. At the low doses he recommended, the antibacterial activity against gram-negative bowel flora is part of the mechanism, but the matrix-metalloproteinase inhibition, the suppression of inducible nitric oxide synthase, the lactate-lowering quinone-like behavior, and the protection against systemic [[endotoxin]] absorption are arguably the dominant therapeutic effects. "Both emodin (from cascara or Chinese rhubarb) and tetracycline (and related minocycline and doxycycline) are anti-inflammatory as well as antiseptic," he told Andrew Murray on the [[53dbf8|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, December 2014]] broadcast. "They have a surprising range of good effects."

That "surprising range" is the point. A drug that lowers serum estrogen and cortisol while raising progesterone, suppresses inducible nitric oxide, drops lactate at sub-antimicrobial doses, shows signal in Alzheimer's prevention, helps receding gums and dental abscesses, treats acne and rosacea by hitting both bacterial and inflammatory layers, and bridges SIBO management with Peat's broader endotoxin framework — that is a different drug than the one most physicians prescribe. "The only problem with tetracycline is that it's too cheap," Peat noted on the [[250122|2015 nitric-oxide episode]], "so doctors are not educated to use it." Doxycycline is a prescription pharmaceutical with real contraindications. What follows is documentation of Peat's framework, not medical advice.

## Pharmacology

Doxycycline is a semi-synthetic tetracycline introduced in 1967. The four-ring naphthacene scaffold with its chelation sites for divalent cations is the basis for all three Peat-relevant tetracyclines. Doxycycline has the longest half-life (16-22 hours, supporting once-daily dosing), the highest oral bioavailability (~95%), the least food interference, and deep tissue penetration including CNS and biofilm matrices. Minocycline penetrates CNS even more efficiently — why it shows up in the dementia literature — but carries higher risk (pigmentation, drug-induced lupus, hepatotoxicity), so doxycycline is usually the safer first choice in the class.

The tetracyclines are bacteriostatic: they bind reversibly to the 30S ribosomal subunit and block bacterial protein synthesis. At antimicrobial doses (100-200 mg/day) this suppresses most gram-negatives, many gram-positives, atypicals (chlamydia, mycoplasma, rickettsia), spirochetes (Borrelia, Treponema), some protozoa, and biofilm-forming organisms that resist other classes.

The non-antibiotic effects are what makes doxycycline interesting in Peat's framework:

- **Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) inhibition.** Tetracyclines chelate the zinc and calcium in the active sites of MMP-2, MMP-8, MMP-9 independent of their ribosomal effect. This is the basis for the only FDA-approved sub-antimicrobial indication — 20 mg twice daily (Periostat) for periodontitis. The MMPs are the collagenases Peat described in *The Cancer Matrix* as "involved in metastasis, allowing cells to escape from the condensed collagen." A drug that inhibits collagen-dissolution at doses below the antibacterial threshold is hitting the [[fibrosis]] and tissue-damage layer of chronic inflammation.
- **Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) suppression.** Minocycline carried the signal first ("I saw articles demonstrating that minocycline lowered the inducible form of nitric oxide," Andrew Murray reported on the [[250122|2015 nitric-oxide episode]]; Peat: "It directly blocks the enzyme [that creates nitric oxide], the way niacinamide does"), but the effect extends across the class. A drug that drops iNOS directly belongs alongside niacinamide, methylene blue, and vitamin K.
- **Quinone-like electron-transport behavior.** The most distinctively Peat-frame observation. On [[995e2a|*Generative Energy* #36]], Georgi Dinkov flagged "a study using low-dose doxycycline for osteoporosis where lactic acid dropped dramatically. Being a quinone itself, was it acting like methylene blue?" Peat: "Yeah, that is very reasonable." The tetracycline scaffold's redox properties may let it act like [[methylene-blue]] and [[vitamin-k]] as an electron carrier supporting oxidative metabolism against the lactic-acid backdrop of inflammation.

Doxycycline is partly excreted in bile with reabsorption from the gut — meaning it concentrates in the intestinal lumen where the gram-negative endotoxin-producers live. That enterohepatic concentration is likely part of why the tetracyclines hit the gut-LPS axis as hard as they do at low systemic doses.

## Why Peat preferred it

The enthusiasm is not antibacterial enthusiasm. It is anti-endotoxin enthusiasm with the antibiotic effect as the upstream mechanism. The case sits on Peat's model of [[endotoxin]] as the constant background metabolic opponent — bacterial lipopolysaccharide leaking continuously from the gut, triggering the estrogen-serotonin-cortisol cascade, driving chronic-inflammation-mediated conditions from heart disease to dementia to autoimmune flare.

The fertility-clinic story he repeated for thirty years is the cleanest evidence. As recounted on [[6acd4d|*Generative Energy* #19]]: "Around 1990, I read an article from a fertility clinic where women were trying to conceive, but the fertilized ovum wouldn't implant. The doctors thought it might indicate an infection in the uterus, so they gave all their patients a course of antibiotics. Besides improving their fertility, a lot of the patients said their chronic headaches had disappeared. They gave them hormone tests and found that the antibiotics had lowered their cortisol and estrogen production and increased progesterone." A single short course produced "a fertile endocrine pattern." Peat's read: the bacteria were the upstream cause; the hormone profile was downstream of bacterial endotoxin and bacterial deconjugation of bile-excreted estrogen. On the same episode he elaborated the cancer parallel: "In 1965, a doctor sent me an argument that he was curing cancer with antibiotics. I didn't at that time think of it in terms of cutting out the endotoxin, but people have been doing it repeatedly over several decades."

The mechanism is layered. Gram-negative gut bacteria continuously produce LPS as part of cell-wall turnover. Bacterial beta-glucuronidase in the lower bowel deconjugates the estrogen the liver has already conjugated and excreted in bile, allowing reabsorption. Endotoxin absorbed through an inflamed intestinal wall activates the liver, raises cortisol and serotonin, suppresses thyroid, and feeds the estrogen-aromatase loop. Doxycycline at low dose preferentially suppresses gram-negative bowel flora, lowers LPS production and absorption, reduces beta-glucuronidase activity, breaks the enterohepatic estrogen recycling, and — through MMP inhibition, iNOS suppression, and quinone-like electron-transport effects — reduces the downstream amplification.

The class hits all of this. Most other antibiotics hit only the first layer, often flattening the flora in ways that reseed worse. "It sterilizes the intestine," Peat warned in the [[4db17a|antibiotics email]]; "if you don't pay attention to hormones governing digestive enzymes, a sterile intestine is a good place for fungus." Low-dose tetracyclines can be used surgically — short course, low dose, vitamin K cover, upstream thyroid and diet work — without the scorched-earth outcome. The same scaffold also makes doxycycline (with minocycline) a candidate for slowing neurodegeneration: on the [[53dbf8|2014 You Are What You Eat broadcast]], "Minocycline is being found to prevent or possibly improve dementia and Alzheimer's disease."

## Indications in Peat-frame

The tetracyclines were never Peat's first move. The first move was always diet, thyroid, sugar, gelatin, salt, raw [[carrot-salad|carrot]] — the substrate that determines whether the gut and endocrine system stay out of the dysbiotic pattern. Doxycycline enters when substrate work plateaus, when an acute dysbiotic episode is driving systemic decompensation, or when a specific identified infection requires direct treatment.

- **Chronic gut dysbiosis and SIBO.** The foundational use case. The [[gut-restoration|gut restoration protocol]] places tetracyclines in Phase 2, after substrate work has plateaued. Doxycycline 50-100 mg/day for 5-7 days with K2 cover and a clear exit criterion is the typical pattern. Mainstream SIBO standard is rifaximin; Peat's framework would accept it but prefers tetracyclines for the additional anti-inflammatory spillover.
- **Acne and rosacea.** The longest-running dermatological applications. From the [[2540f6|acne email]]: "Antibiotics can be used topically, but the tetracycline type is usually taken internally, for their antiinflammatory effect. Changing the diet while using an antibiotic can make the effect permanent." That last sentence is the operative one — the antibiotic creates a window; the diet closes it. The [[acne-protocol|acne protocol]] specifies 50-100 mg doxycycline daily for 2-3 months in cystic cases, coordinated with vitamin A, zinc, thyroid, PUFA depletion, aspirin, and [[estrogen-detox|estrogen detox]] in women. The dermatology default of 6-12 months of monotherapy without diet change inverts the right sequencing. For rosacea, the [[f2583a|*Rosacea, Inflammation, and Aging*]] article: "Some of the benefit from antibiotics probably results from the reduced endotoxin stress when intestinal bacteria are suppressed."
- **Autoimmune flares with gut origin.** Peat treated the autoimmune label with suspicion — many presentations resolved with thyroid, endotoxin reduction, and PUFA depletion. Where active flare needs a circuit-breaker, doxycycline or minocycline provides one. The minocycline-in-rheumatoid pilot studies and the broader observation that endotoxin amplifies autoimmune symptoms point at the same intervention.
- **Multiple sclerosis.** Sits at the intersection of [[endotoxin]], [[pufa]], and demyelination. MMP inhibition, iNOS suppression, and endotoxin reduction make doxycycline and especially minocycline candidate adjuncts. See [[multiple-sclerosis]].
- **Lyme disease.** From the [[6bbd09|2014 Field Biology episode]] and [[532e51|2012 P&S fundraiser]]: "Hypothyroidism is often misdiagnosed as Lyme disease." When the diagnosis is real: "A course of antibiotics for a week or two is a way to eliminate it. The Lyme disease can be eradicated, just as syphilis would be." Doxycycline is CDC first-line for early Lyme — a rare case where mainstream medicine and Peat's framework converge.
- **Sarcoidosis.** The Marshall Protocol's targeted-antibiotic component is consistent with the doxycycline logic, though Peat would reject the vitamin D restriction.
- **Dental and periodontal disease.** The use case with the strongest sub-antimicrobial-dose evidence. From the [[f90411|teeth-gums email]]: "The problem usually involves endotoxin absorption, so small daily amounts of minocycline help some people." Periostat 20 mg twice daily sits below resistance threshold but above MMP-inhibition threshold in gingival tissue. For dental abscess, standard antimicrobial dose 5-7 days.
- **Ménière's disease.** From the [[37bbbe|Ménière's email]]: "Salt, magnesium, calcium, aspirin, antihistamine, progesterone, tetracycline (or minocycline, doxycycline) and thyroid can correct the fluid imbalance" — entering an anti-nitric-oxide stack consistent with iNOS suppression.

## Dosing

Peat's framework treats dosing as a symptom-titrated conversation, not a fixed prescription. The [[4db17a|antibiotics email]] is the cleanest source: "I usually break the tablets up, and use fourths or halves, at intervals according to need... if I feel an effect from a first dose, I take a smaller dose, usually 100mg, the next time, and similar amounts as long as the symptom is decreasing, and when I don't notice any symptom, I take a few smaller doses... 3 doses of 100mg per day for a few days is usually enough, after one or two bigger doses." The replicable element is the principle: low dose, symptom-titrated, short course, vitamin-K-supported, with substrate work in parallel.

- **Sub-microbial anti-inflammatory dose (20-40 mg/day).** FDA-approved Periostat is 20 mg twice daily. MMP inhibition is therapeutic; antibacterial selection pressure stays below the resistance threshold. Indications: chronic periodontitis, rosacea, mild persistent acne maintenance, adjunctive use in low-grade chronic inflammation. Cleanest chronic-safety evidence.
- **Standard antimicrobial dose (50-100 mg/day, occasionally 200 mg/day).** Acute treatment for most infections, most acne and rosacea flares, most Peat-frame dysbiotic-state interventions. Duration 5-14 days for acute infection; 2-3 months for cystic acne; 2-3 weeks for early Lyme.
- **Loading dose.** For acute presentations, 200 mg day one then 100 mg daily. Peat's "one or two bigger doses" followed by smaller doses matches this.
- **Vitamin K2 cover.** Non-negotiable. From the [[4db17a|antibiotics email]]: "It's important to get some vitamin K1 or K2 when you use an antibiotic (liver or kale, or supplements)." The gut bacteria the antibiotic suppresses include the K-producing flora. Practical: 1-5 mg K2 daily during the course, with liver or kale alongside.
- **Calcium, iron, and dairy separation.** The chelation rule violated most often. The scaffold binds divalent cations strongly; calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, and aluminum all bind doxycycline in the gut and block absorption. Doses must be taken at least two hours away from dairy, calcium or iron supplements, and antacids. Peat's milk-heavy framework makes this a real timing challenge.
- **Stay upright.** Doxycycline causes esophagitis if a capsule lodges. Full glass of water, stay upright for 30 minutes after dosing, never immediately before lying down.

## Photosensitivity warning

The most consistent acute side effect of doxycycline is phototoxic sun sensitivity. Skin exposed to UV during doxycycline use sunburns at a much lower dose than baseline — sometimes within 15-30 minutes of incidental exposure that would normally produce no burn. The mechanism is the tetracycline molecule absorbing UV-A and generating reactive oxygen species in skin.

Standard advice during the course: broad-spectrum sunscreen on all exposed skin, hats with brim, sun-protective clothing, minimization of midday sun.

This is where Peat's framework introduces tension. Peat treated daily sun exposure as central to thyroid function, vitamin D synthesis, cholesterol-to-pregnenolone conversion in skin, and mood regulation. Strict sun avoidance during a doxycycline course collides with that. The corpus does not contain a direct discussion of this tradeoff, but the implication: conservative sun protection for the duration of a short course, then return to normal exposure on completion. Long-term sub-microbial doxycycline (months to years for rosacea or sarcoidosis) raises a chronic sun-avoidance question this article does not resolve. Topical [[aspirin]], topical caffeine, and adequate vitamin E may reduce severity if phototoxic injury occurs.

## Contraindications

The standard tetracycline-class contraindications apply:

- **Pregnancy after 8 weeks gestation.** Tetracyclines cross the placenta and deposit in fetal tooth enamel and bone. Peat's [[2540f6|acne email]]: "Antibiotics generally shouldn't be used during pregnancy."
- **Children under 8 years.** Same enamel-discoloration mechanism in developing dentition. For acute serious infection (Rocky Mountain spotted fever, certain tick-borne illnesses) the CDC now permits doxycycline use in this group; for chronic non-life-threatening indications the contraindication holds.
- **Severe hepatic impairment.** Doxycycline is partly hepatically metabolized.
- **Known hypersensitivity** to any tetracycline.
- **Concurrent isotretinoin (Accutane).** The combination raises intracranial pressure (pseudotumor cerebri). Avoid.
- **Anticoagulant therapy.** Doxycycline potentiates warfarin and similar agents; the vitamin K co-supplementation Peat recommended is in tension with this and requires anticoagulation-clinic coordination.

## Failure modes

- **Overuse driving resistance.** Long-course or repeated full-dose courses select for tetracycline-resistant organisms; the sub-antimicrobial Periostat dose does not. Peat's framework — short courses, low doses, substrate work to prevent recurrence — is partly an anti-resistance strategy. The dermatology default of months-to-years of full-dose monotherapy is the pattern he opposed.
- **Insufficient duration producing relapse.** A two-day partial course for a real infection lets surviving bacteria reseed. Symptom-titrated termination, not arbitrary short duration.
- **Stopping the antibiotic without rebuilding the gut.** The most common Peat-frame failure. From the [[2540f6|acne email]]: "Changing the diet while using an antibiotic can make the effect permanent." The antibiotic alone is not the treatment; the antibiotic plus diet change is.
- **Taking with calcium, iron, or dairy.** Chelation reduces absorption to a fraction of the intended dose. Patients who take doxycycline with milk often see no effect and assume the diagnosis was wrong.
- **Ignoring vitamin K.** Long courses without K supplementation deplete K-producing gut bacteria and risk bleeding tendency.
- **Lying down after dosing.** Preventable esophagitis.
- **Sun exposure during the course.** Easily prevented; commonly forgotten.
- **Treating doxycycline as the protocol rather than the bridge.** Years of low-dose doxycycline for chronic rosacea or acne without addressing PUFA, thyroid, estrogen, or gut substrate is the inversion of the framework.

## What it pairs with

Doxycycline does not work as monotherapy in Peat's frame. The course sits inside a larger restoration pattern: [[gut-restoration|gut restoration]] (the rebuild is what makes the course's effect permanent — without it the dysbiotic flora reseeds within weeks); [[endotoxin-reduction|endotoxin reduction]] daily practice (raw [[carrot-salad|carrot]], bamboo shoots, cooked mushrooms, transit management, [[anti-stress-diet]] baseline); [[vitamin-k|vitamin K2]] cover (mandatory); [[thyroid]] support (slow transit and low gastric acid are downstream of hypothyroidism); [[pufa-depletion|PUFA depletion]] (the substrate inflammatory prostaglandins are made from); [[aspirin]] at 325 mg with food once or twice daily; [[methylene-blue]] 1-5 mg AM (fellow quinone-electron-transport molecule); [[niacinamide]] 100-300 mg three times daily (fellow iNOS-suppressor); [[progesterone]] in women (the hormonal correction the antibiotic enables); [[activated-charcoal]] intermittently to bind released LPS; [[cascara-sagrada]] low evening dose if transit is slow.

## Tracking signals

The protocol works if these change, roughly in this order: mental clarity within days (endotoxin-driven brain fog is the cleanest acute marker); energy and warmth within 1-2 weeks; skin within 2-4 weeks for acne or rosacea; gut function within days to weeks (stool form, bloating-gas pattern); joint pain within 2-6 weeks in autoimmune and Lyme cases; hormone profile in weeks (cortisol drops, estrogen drops in the deconjugation-driven pattern, progesterone rises in women on supplementation); inflammation markers — CRP, ESR, fibrinogen typically fall; lactate may drop based on the osteoporosis-study signal from [[995e2a|#36]]; morning oral temperature stabilizing at ≥97.8°F within 1-3 months. If a 2-3 week course produces no movement, the diagnosis was wrong, the dose was inadequate, calcium chelation neutralized the absorption, or the substrate work was not running in parallel.

## Counter-arguments

**The antibiotic-stewardship consensus against chronic low-dose use.** Mainstream infectious-disease practice treats chronic antibiotic exposure as a primary driver of resistance. Peat's endorsement of low-dose chronic tetracycline (sub-microbial Periostat for periodontitis, months-long courses for severe acne or rosacea) sits uneasily with this. Partial resolution: the sub-microbial dose appears not to drive significant resistance, and Peat opposed the conventional chronic full-dose pattern as much as stewardship does. The point remains contested.

**The "long-term antibiotics damage microbiome" position.** Microbiome research has a strong consensus that prolonged antibiotic exposure depletes diversity in ways that may never fully recover. The Peat framework partly accepts this (broad-spectrum scorched-earth antibiotics damage the flora) and partly inverts it (targeted low-dose tetracyclines used briefly don't produce the same outcome; high lower-bowel diversity is more often a disease signal than a health signal in this frame).

**The rosacea-treatment-with-azithromycin alternative.** Azithromycin shares the anti-inflammatory profile through a different mechanism. Peat did not address it specifically; the framework would accept it as second-line but prefer tetracyclines for the stronger gut-LPS-axis hit.

**The dermatology default of 6-12 month doxycycline monotherapy.** The most common real-world use, and the one Peat opposed most directly. Mainstream argument: it works, resistance risk is manageable. Peat's argument: it treats symptoms while leaving the PUFA-thyroid-estrogen-endotoxin pattern intact, so dependence becomes chronic. Recent dermatology literature increasingly favors shorter courses with topical and dietary interventions — closer to Peat than to the year-long courses of the past decade.

**The chronic Lyme disease industry.** Some specialists prescribe multi-year combination regimens for "chronic Lyme" — a diagnosis the CDC and IDSA don't formally recognize. Peat's position was that many such patients were actually hypothyroid with secondary inflammation. A place where mainstream medicine and the Peat framework agree against a specific subspecialty practice.

## Data gaps

- The sub-microbial dose for indications other than periodontitis is not well characterized.
- The quinone-electron-transport claim — Peat's framing of tetracyclines as functionally analogous to methylene blue and vitamin K — rests on the osteoporosis lactate-drop study and structural inference, not direct redox-pharmacology evidence.
- Peat's symptom-titrated dosing pattern is not validated against fixed-dose courses in any clinical setting.
- The interaction between long-term sub-microbial doxycycline and Peat's sun-exposure framework is not addressed in the corpus.
- Whether doxycycline (better tolerated than minocycline) would show comparable signals in the MS and dementia literature has not been adequately tested.

## Safety and scope

Doxycycline is a prescription pharmaceutical. The applications above require a clinician's evaluation, prescription, and monitoring. The contraindications and interactions are real, the phototoxicity risk is real, the resistance risk at chronic full-dose use is real. This article documents Peat's framework and the corpus support — not medical advice. Patients considering doxycycline for any of the indications described should bring this material to a clinician willing to evaluate it on its merits and prescribe — or decline to prescribe — accordingly. Self-administration of a prescription antibiotic without clinician oversight is not appropriate and not endorsed.
