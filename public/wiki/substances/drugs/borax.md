# Borax

Borax -- sodium tetraborate decahydrate -- occupies a peculiar space in the Ray Peat community. It is a laundry detergent ingredient that doubles as a source of boron, a trace mineral with roles in bone health, hormone metabolism, and fluoride detoxification. Peat discussed boron's importance and fluoride's dangers extensively, though he was less explicit about borax as a supplement compared to his detailed protocols for [[thyroid]] or [[progesterone]]. Borax supplementation is more a grassroots practice among Peat's followers than a core Peat recommendation, but it aligns with his broader principles: optimize trace mineral status, counter environmental toxins, and support the metabolic conditions that allow steroid hormones to function.

## Boron and the Fluoride Problem

The most compelling rationale for borax in Peat's framework is fluoride antagonism. Peat viewed [[fluoride]] as one of the most underappreciated environmental toxins in modern life -- ubiquitous in municipal water supplies, toothpaste, tea, processed foods, and non-stick cookware. Fluoride accumulates over a lifetime in bones, the pineal gland, and soft tissues, where it suppresses [[thyroid]] function by displacing iodine, inhibits key metabolic enzymes, and promotes the pathological calcification that Peat associated with aging and degeneration.

Boron competes with fluoride for binding sites in bone and other tissues and increases renal fluoride clearance. Animal studies and human case reports have demonstrated that boron supplementation reduces skeletal fluoride burden and improves symptoms of fluorosis -- the joint pain, stiffness, and skeletal deformity that result from chronic fluoride accumulation. For someone living in a fluoridated water area or carrying decades of fluoride exposure, boron supplementation addresses a real metabolic burden.

Borax is simply the cheapest and most widely available form of boron. Pharmaceutical-grade boron supplements exist (boron citrate, boron glycinate, fructoborate), but borax has the advantage of being the same chemical form -- sodium tetraborate -- used in much of the research on boron's biological effects. A pinch dissolved in water provides a pharmacologically meaningful dose of elemental boron at trivial cost.

## Hormone Support and Bone Metabolism

Beyond fluoride antagonism, boron participates in steroid hormone metabolism. Research suggests it modulates the enzymes involved in the synthesis and metabolism of [[testosterone]], [[progesterone]], and [[vitamin-d|vitamin D]]. Boron supplementation has been shown to reduce urinary excretion of [[calcium]] and [[magnesium]], supporting bone density through mineral retention rather than the drug-induced suppression of bone turnover that characterizes bisphosphonates.

These effects align with Peat's priorities. He favored interventions that support the body's own hormone production and mineral metabolism rather than overriding them with pharmaceuticals. Boron's role is permissive -- it allows calcium, magnesium, vitamin D, and steroid hormones to function in their normal ranges -- rather than directly stimulatory. It is a trace mineral, not a drug, and its effects are subtle, cumulative, and difficult to isolate from other dietary and metabolic variables.

One caveat: some research also shows boron can increase [[estrogen]] levels, which in Peat's framework is undesirable. At the trace doses typically used (3-6 mg of elemental boron per day), this effect appears modest and may be outweighed by the benefits to testosterone, progesterone, and vitamin D metabolism. But individuals with estrogen dominance should be aware of the possibility.

## The Pinch Protocol

The typical borax protocol, circulated in Peat-adjacent forums and attributed to researchers like Rex Newnham and Walter Last, involves dissolving a small amount of borax in water:

A common approach is to dissolve 1/8 to 1/4 teaspoon of borax (roughly 500-1000 mg) in one liter of water, creating a dilute stock solution. Drinking one to two tablespoons of this solution daily provides approximately 3-6 mg of elemental boron -- within the physiological range that overlaps with dietary intake in populations with high fruit and vegetable consumption. Some people take a simpler approach: a pinch of borax (30-50 mg) dissolved directly in a glass of water, delivering roughly 2-3 mg of boron.

The 5-days-on, 2-days-off cycling protocol is sometimes recommended to avoid accumulation, though at these doses the body excretes boron efficiently through the kidneys and accumulation is unlikely in healthy individuals.

## Safety and Toxicology

The main objection to borax supplementation is that it carries a warning label as a cleaning product. This reflects regulatory classification, not toxicological reality. The LD50 for borax in rats is comparable to table salt -- it is not inherently toxic. At supplemental doses of 3-10 mg of elemental boron per day, chronic toxicity has not been documented in human studies.

Cautions do apply. Pregnant women should avoid borax supplementation, as animal studies suggest high-dose boron can affect fetal development. People with kidney disease should be cautious, since boron is renally excreted and impaired function could lead to accumulation. And some individuals report mild nausea or digestive upset at the outset, which typically resolves by reducing the dose and increasing gradually.

## Context Within Peat's Framework

Peat did not promote borax specifically in his published writings or major interviews. His relevance to the borax discussion lies in the metabolic principles he articulated: fluoride is a serious toxin that accumulates silently; trace minerals are chronically undervalued in modern nutrition; and cheap, accessible interventions are often more useful than expensive pharmaceutical ones. Borax fits this pattern. It is not a panacea, and it is not a substitute for the foundational Peat interventions -- [[thyroid]] optimization, [[PUFA]] elimination, adequate [[calcium]] and [[vitamin-d|vitamin D]], stress reduction. But for someone with significant fluoride exposure, joint pain, or suspected boron deficiency, it is a reasonable, low-risk addition to a metabolically oriented protocol.

Boron-rich dietary sources -- raisins, prunes, and certain fruits -- provide maintenance levels. For therapeutic fluoride displacement, supplementation through borax or pharmaceutical boron is likely necessary. The choice between them is a matter of cost, convenience, and personal comfort with an unconventional supplement form.
