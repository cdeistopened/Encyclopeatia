# Naltrexone

Naltrexone occupies an unusual place in Ray Peat's pharmacopeia. He recommended it less often than [[thyroid]], [[aspirin]], or [[cyproheptadine]], but when he did recommend it the language was direct and the indication was specific: a patient stuck in a stress-locked pattern — depression that wouldn't lift, multiple sclerosis symptoms that wouldn't quiet down, cancer that wasn't responding to the standard nutritional moves, the lingering aftermath of injury or chronic infection — could often be broken out of that pattern by three or four days of an oral opioid antagonist. The dose he used was low. The course was short. And the underlying theory was the inverse of the one used by the doctor (Bernard Bihari) who pioneered low-dose naltrexone for AIDS patients in the 1980s and whose framework later spread to the autoimmune community. Bihari believed naltrexone worked by causing a rebound increase in endogenous endorphins. Peat believed the opposite — that the endorphins themselves were typically the problem, and that blocking their receptors lowered the system back toward a less inflamed equilibrium. The clinical practice was nearly identical. The mechanistic story was almost entirely different. That divergence is the key to reading everything Peat wrote about the drug.

This article is documentation, not medical advice. Naltrexone is a prescription medication in the United States and most other jurisdictions. Concurrent or recent opioid use is an absolute contraindication — taking naltrexone while opioid-dependent precipitates acute withdrawal. Anyone considering this drug needs a prescribing clinician who can evaluate concurrent medications, liver function, and the specific clinical situation.

## What it is

Naltrexone is a synthetic opioid receptor antagonist, structurally related to the older injectable antagonist naloxone (Narcan). Where naloxone is given intravenously or by injection to reverse acute opioid overdose (its oral bioavailability is too low to be useful by mouth), naltrexone was developed in the early 1960s by Endo Laboratories as a long-acting oral agent. It binds with high affinity to the mu-opioid receptor and with lower but still meaningful affinity to the kappa and delta receptors. The standard pharmacological description is "competitive, reversible, non-selective opioid antagonist." It occupies the receptors that endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins, and exogenous opioids would otherwise bind to, and it occupies them for several hours per dose without producing any agonist activity of its own.

The drug was approved by the FDA in 1984 for opioid addiction relapse prevention at 50 mg per day and in 1994 for alcohol use disorder at the same dose. Both indications use the full anti-craving dose. The off-label low-dose use that Peat referenced — typically 0.5 to 4.5 mg daily — emerged from Bernard Bihari's observations in AIDS patients in Greenwich Village in the mid-1980s. Bihari noticed that very low doses appeared to stabilize immune function in his patients, theorized a brief overnight receptor blockade followed by a rebound rise in endogenous opioids the next day, and built a clinical practice around the protocol. The protocol spread first through HIV networks, then through the multiple sclerosis community, and from there into Crohn's disease, fibromyalgia, complex regional pain syndrome, Hashimoto's, and a broad range of chronic inflammatory conditions. None of these uses are FDA-approved. All of them depend on compounding pharmacies or on patients splitting commercial 50 mg tablets.

Oral bioavailability is roughly 5-40% with substantial first-pass hepatic metabolism. The drug's primary metabolite, 6-beta-naltrexol, is itself an active opioid antagonist. Plasma half-life is roughly 4 hours for naltrexone and 13 hours for 6-beta-naltrexol, so receptor occupancy from a single dose typically extends across most of a day. The drug is metabolized in the liver and excreted in urine. A monthly extended-release intramuscular formulation (Vivitrol) exists for addiction use; Peat-style applications use the oral form exclusively.

## The endorphin frame

Bihari's theory, in summary form: a brief blockade of opioid receptors (4-6 hours, achieved by a small evening dose) is registered by the brain as a relative deficiency. The brain compensates by upregulating its production of endorphins. The new, higher daytime endorphin level then exerts immunomodulatory effects through the opioid receptors expressed on T-cells, B-cells, macrophages, and natural killer cells. This is the standard LDN story, repeated in nearly every patient-facing source on the drug.

Peat's reading was inverted. He treated the endorphins not as a beneficial natural anti-inflammatory but as a stress-induced inhibitory signal whose chronic excess was part of the problem in many of the conditions LDN was being used for. From his [[naloxone-naltrexone|email correspondence on naloxone and naltrexone]]: "Bihari thinks naltrexone works by increasing endorphins, I think excess endorphins are often the problem, and the antagonist can sometimes be helpful." From his 2012 *Politics and Science* radio interview: "When you're under stress, you produce endorphins. The endorphins compensate for injury or stress and they should go away. But prolonged stress can leave you inhibited in various ways with the wrong pattern of endorphins. If a person has been exposed to prolonged stress, sometimes you can break out of the pattern and recover functions with an anti-morphine treatment."

The clinical implications of the inversion are subtle but real. If Bihari is right, LDN works through a rebound mechanism and needs continuous nightly dosing to maintain the rebound. If Peat is right, the drug works by directly antagonizing a chronically elevated endorphin signal, the receptor blockade itself is the therapeutic event, and the effect should be visible in days rather than months. Peat's clinical observation matches the second story. "I think it usually has its effect in just a few days, though. If it hasn't really made a big difference in just a few days, then it's probably some other problem," he told John Barkhausen on the air in 2012. He used the same time frame in his 2009 *Ask the Herb Doctor* appearance: "I usually see good results in just two or three days. I think the basic treatment is a good diet and thyroid supplement. Naltrexone is good to try to break up a pattern."

The mechanistic divergence also explains Peat's preference for intermittent dosing. If the drug is breaking a stuck signaling pattern rather than building up a tonic effect, there is no reason for indefinite continuous use. "I have seen good results from using naloxone for 3 or 4 days; naltrexone has similar effects… I think it's safe to take 5 or 10 mg of naltrexone daily for a few days, but I don't think it should be used continuously; I have known people who had good results, repeating the short courses two or three times in a year." That schedule is closer to how he used [[doxycycline]] for chronic infection than to how the LDN community uses naltrexone for autoimmune disease.

## Where the endorphins come in

To understand why Peat treated endorphin antagonism as therapeutic, the broader role of the endorphin system in his framework has to be reconstructed. The endorphins are stress-induced peptides whose physiological purpose is to dampen excitation, suppress pain, and slow metabolism during overwhelming stimulus or injury. In the short term this is protective. The same shutdown signal that stops a wounded animal from feeling the wound long enough to escape the predator is, in the laboratory, the signal that allows experimental animals to survive injuries that would otherwise kill them. The endorphins limit physiological functions, in Peat's phrase, "in a protective way, like hibernation."

The problem is that the protective hibernation signal can become chronic. In the [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*Alzheimer's Disease as a Clue to Immortality*]] newsletter, he listed the endorphins among the aging signals: "Endorphins: Stress induced, laterally specific, involved in [[estrogen]] action, antagonized by naloxone and similar anti-opiate drugs. I have proposed that the endorphins can cause or sustain some of the symptoms of aging. Naloxone appears to be a useful treatment for senility." From the same newsletter's section on signals in the abstract: chronic endorphin elevation works in concert with estrogen, [[endotoxin]], serotonin, and the other long-acting stress mediators to produce the picture of accelerated aging — cold brain, depressed mood, suppressed immunity, slowed digestion, chronic inflammation. The drug Peat recommended for each of those problems addressed a different mediator; naltrexone addressed the endorphin component.

The 2015 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on breast cancer made the cross-link to estrogen explicit. "Serotonin and endorphins are often called the happy hormones, but actually they are the most important mediators of stress. Even though estrogen turns on lactic acid, endorphins, and nitric oxide, the endorphins in turn activate estrogen receptors, aromatase, and prolactin, which acts as an amplifier of estrogen's effect. You get this back-and-forth action increasing inflammation, estrogenicity, and lowering energy production." The endorphins are not just downstream of estrogen but upstream of it as well — they activate aromatase, amplify estrogen signaling, and contribute to the self-reinforcing inflammatory loop that drives many of the female-pattern degenerative diseases. Naltrexone, by interrupting the endorphin half of the loop, indirectly reduces the estrogen signaling on the other side.

The drug also has non-opioid effects. From the email correspondence: "Naloxone and naltrexone have some effects that aren't directly related to the endorphins, on estrogen and histamine." Several of the clinical effects Peat attributed to the drug — the laxative action discussed in his [[failures-of-immunity|*Failures of Immunity*]]-era writing, the antitumor effects in breast and prostate cancer cells, the modulation of mast cell histamine release — operate through pathways independent of the canonical opioid receptors. They are real and probably account for a meaningful fraction of the drug's clinical effect.

## Indications

Several specific clinical settings recur across Peat's writing and interviews. They are not formally indications in the regulatory sense — none of the off-label LDN uses are FDA-approved — but they are the situations in which Peat repeatedly suggested the drug.

**Hypothyroid-pattern depression and lethargy.** The 2009 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode contains the clearest single statement of the mechanism. A caller with Hashimoto's asked about LDN, and Peat explained: "When you are hypothyroid and produce lactic acid, you accumulate endorphins. Endorphins limit physiological functions in a protective way, like hibernation. Naltrexone clears those out. Sometimes in two or three days, a person can come out of depression or a lethargic state." The setting is specific — hypothyroid metabolism producing chronic lactic acid producing chronic endorphin elevation producing the hibernation-like depression. The intervention is a short course of naltrexone. The expected timeline is days. The longer-term fix is the underlying [[thyroid]] correction.

**Multiple sclerosis and demyelinating disease.** [[multiple-sclerosis|MS]] is one of the longest-established off-label LDN indications in the broader clinical community, and Peat addressed it directly in the 2012 *Politics and Science* episode. His framing was that MS reflects a chronic stress pattern in which inflammation, endorphin elevation, and demyelination feed each other. "All kinds of stabilizing anti-inflammatory things. Thyroid, progesterone, short-chain fatty acids, and sugars are all things that help restore injured, demyelinated nerves." Naltrexone was one tool in a broader stack that emphasized progesterone, thyroid, pregnenolone, and PUFA depletion as the durable interventions, with the opioid antagonist as a pattern-interrupt during flares. See [[multiple-sclerosis]] for the full protocol.

**Cancer adjunct, particularly hormone-sensitive cancers.** The [[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone]] newsletter is unusually explicit about the drug as a cancer adjunct. "Naloxone or naltrexone, which blocks the actions of the endorphins and morphine, is being used to inhibit the growth of various kinds of cancer, including breast cancer and prostate cancer. Leptin (which is promoted by estrogen) is a hormone produced by fat cells, and it, like estrogen, activates the POMC-related endorphin stress system. The endorphins activate histamine, another promoter of inflammation and cell division." And, later in the same article: "Naltrexone has been successfully used to treat prostate and breast cancer." Peat cited Abou-Issa and Tejwani's 1991 paper *Antitumor Activity of Naltrexone and Correlation with Steroid Hormone Receptors* in *Biochemical and Biophysical Research Communications*. The mechanism he emphasized was the disruption of the estrogen-endorphin-histamine loop that drives hormone-sensitive tumor growth, not a direct cytotoxic effect. See [[cancer-protocol]] for the broader stack.

**Chronic fatigue syndrome.** The 2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on progesterone and estrogen included a CFS-and-LDN question from a caller named Shauna. Peat's reply began with the framework — CFS as a low-progesterone, high-estrogen, energy-depleted state with the cell stuck in a swollen, over-excitable configuration — and then turned to the drug: "I have experience using Naloxone more than Naltrexone, but they are essentially identical. I found that 1 to 4 milligrams per day of Naloxone would break a depressed condition by suppressing the endorphins, which are induced by lactic acid and fatigue. Endorphins are an emergency measure to turn off excitation. Some people use even a hundredth of a milligram and find a therapeutic effect. I consider 4 milligrams a fairly large dose."

**Fibromyalgia and chronic pain syndromes.** Peat did not write extensively on fibromyalgia by name, but the framework is identical to his framework for CFS — a chronic, low-progesterone, high-serotonin, lactic-acid-dominant state in which the central pain processing system has been sensitized by inflammatory mediators. Mainstream LDN protocols for fibromyalgia (the work of Jarred Younger at UAB is the canonical reference) explicitly invoke microglial modulation through TLR4 receptors as the mechanism. Peat would not have disagreed with the observation — he agreed with the clinical effect — but the framing through the endorphin-stress signal rather than the glial-receptor signal is his own.

**Septic shock.** A short answer in the [[septic-shock|septic shock email]]: "What would you recommend for someone experiencing septic shock? Sugar, aspirin, naloxone or naltrexone, antibiotics and laxatives." The opioid antagonist appears in the rescue stack alongside the basic metabolic supports because [[endotoxin]] activates the endorphin system as part of the septic cascade, and the drug interrupts that arm of the response. This is not a self-administered intervention — septic shock is an ICU emergency — but the citation captures how seriously Peat took the endotoxin-endorphin link.

**Addiction recovery and alcohol use disorder.** The full 50 mg/day dose used for opioid relapse prevention and alcohol use disorder is the original FDA-approved indication and the use case for which the drug was developed. Peat did not write extensively on this use, but it is the standard application a prescribing clinician will be most familiar with, and it is the situation in which the opioid-free prerequisite is most critical — a patient who has been using opioids in the days before starting full-dose naltrexone is at risk of precipitated withdrawal, which is medically serious.

## Dosing

Peat's dose range, across all the indications above, was consistently low. The numbers that appear in his correspondence and interviews:

- **0.01 mg ("a hundredth of a milligram") to 4 mg** as the daily dose for breaking depression, CFS, or autoimmune flare patterns. The very low end represents the floor at which some patients still respond; the 4 mg figure he described as "a fairly large dose."
- **1 mg or less per day** is the figure he gave in his email correspondence for many indications: "Doses of one milligram or less can sometimes be effective."
- **5 to 10 mg per day** for short 3-4 day courses, the regimen he described as "safe… for a few days" but not for continuous use.
- **2-3 short courses per year** as the long-term cadence for patients who responded.

The broader LDN community converges on a slightly different number: 4.5 mg taken once nightly at bedtime, titrated up from 0.5 mg over several weeks, used continuously for months to years. Both protocols use the same drug and probably produce overlapping clinical effects. The difference reflects the underlying theory — continuous nightly dosing if the rebound endorphin story is right, intermittent short courses if the receptor-blockade story is right.

A practical compromise that many patients and prescribers use: start at 0.5 mg taken at bedtime, titrate up by 0.5-1 mg per week to a target of 3-4.5 mg, evaluate response over 60-90 days, and reassess. If the response is durable after a course, attempt withdrawal and see whether the benefit persists. If the response is sustained, continuous dosing may be appropriate. If the response fades, periodic short courses may be sufficient.

The drug is taken at bedtime for two reasons. First, sedation can be a side effect for some patients early in treatment, and bedtime dosing makes it irrelevant. Second, the canonical LDN theory holds that the brief overnight receptor blockade triggers the next day's endorphin rebound. Peat did not endorse the rebound theory, but the bedtime timing has become the convention regardless.

Compounded LDN through a specialty compounding pharmacy is the standard supply route for the low-dose protocol. Commercial 50 mg tablets (sold under the brand names Revia, Depade, and various generics) can be split, dissolved, or quartered, but the resulting doses are coarse and inconsistent. Liquid suspensions compounded to 1 mg/mL are the most flexible form for titration. Sublingual troches and transdermal creams have also been formulated but have weaker absorption data.

## High-dose naltrexone

The 50 mg/day full dose used for alcohol and opioid addiction treatment is a different clinical animal from low-dose naltrexone. At this dose the drug is being used as an anti-craving and relapse-prevention tool, not as an immune modulator. The mechanism — sustained, near-complete opioid receptor blockade — eliminates the reinforcing effect of opioid use and also dampens the reinforcing effect of alcohol consumption, which is partially endorphin-mediated.

The Sinclair Method, developed by Finnish researcher David Sinclair in the 1980s, uses 50 mg of naltrexone taken about an hour before alcohol consumption as a pharmacological extinction protocol. The pairing of alcohol with blocked endorphin reinforcement gradually extinguishes the conditioned craving. Published outcomes in selected populations have been substantially better than abstinence-based programs, though the protocol is unfamiliar to most American addiction medicine and is more common in Finland and the UK.

Peat did not write extensively about full-dose naltrexone for addiction. The use case is outside his metabolic-framework focus and was already well-established in conventional pharmacology by the time he was writing. The relevant point for anyone reading Peat material on naltrexone is to distinguish the low-dose, off-label, immune-modulatory use that he discussed from the high-dose, on-label, addiction-treatment use that he did not. They are the same molecule in different roles.

## Synergies

Naltrexone in Peat's framework rarely stands alone. It is a pattern-interrupt embedded in a broader stack.

**With thyroid.** The 2009 caller transcript made this explicit: "I think the basic treatment is a good diet and thyroid supplement. Naltrexone is good to try to break up a pattern." Hypothyroidism produces the chronic lactic acid and endorphin elevation that the drug is interrupting. Without [[thyroid]] correction, the pattern reasserts itself when the drug is withdrawn. [[Thyroid-dosing]] is the upstream lever.

**With aspirin.** [[Aspirin]] addresses the inflammatory prostaglandins, the small clots that show up in MS plaques and around cancer, the estrogen-amplification loop, and the platelet serotonin release. Where naltrexone targets the endorphin signal in the stress cascade, aspirin targets the prostaglandin signal. They are complementary anti-inflammatory tools in the Peat stack, and the combination appears in his cancer, MS, and septic shock recommendations.

**With cyproheptadine.** [[Cyproheptadine]] is the antiserotonin tool in the same stack. Serotonin and the endorphins are both stress-induced mediators; cyproheptadine blocks one, naltrexone blocks the other. In a patient with prominent serotonin symptoms (anxiety, insomnia, intestinal urgency, hot flashes) cyproheptadine is the first reach. In a patient with prominent endorphin symptoms (chronic depression, lethargy, hibernation-pattern fatigue, autoimmune flares) naltrexone is the first reach. Many patients have features of both, and short trials of each can clarify which mediator is more active.

**With progesterone and pregnenolone.** For autoimmune and neuroinflammatory conditions, particularly MS, the steroid layer (described in detail at [[brain-orthomolecular-protocol]]) provides the durable structural correction. Naltrexone, like aspirin and cyproheptadine, is a complementary acute tool. Peat consistently emphasized the steroid layer as the load-bearing intervention.

**With doxycycline for endotoxin-driven inflammation.** When the inflammatory signal is being driven primarily by gut-derived endotoxin, [[doxycycline]] reduces the endotoxin load at the source. Naltrexone reduces the endorphin response downstream. Peat did not describe a formal pairing of the two, but the mechanistic logic is straightforward.

## Failure modes

The most consequential failure mode is administering naltrexone to a patient with concurrent or recent opioid exposure. The drug displaces opioids from the mu-receptor and precipitates an acute withdrawal syndrome — sweating, agitation, vomiting, diarrhea, tachycardia, severe anxiety — that can be medically serious. The minimum opioid-free interval before starting naltrexone is typically 7-10 days for short-acting opioids and longer for methadone or buprenorphine. This is not a precaution that can be eyeballed. Patients taking any opioid-containing prescription (including codeine-containing cough suppressants, tramadol, and post-surgical pain medications) must clear them before naltrexone, and the clearance interval is set by the prescribing clinician.

Sleep disruption is the most commonly reported low-dose side effect — vivid dreams, sometimes insomnia or fragmented sleep in the first weeks. Several explanations have been proposed (the brief brain stimulation from receptor blockade, increased adrenergic tone, REM rebound), but the practical observation is that it usually settles within 2-3 weeks. Moving the dose to morning rather than evening helps in some patients. Reducing the dose temporarily helps in others.

Headache, mild nausea, and fatigue can occur in the first days. They typically resolve as the system adjusts. Persistent symptoms beyond a few weeks should prompt reassessment of the dose or the indication.

Hepatotoxicity at the 50 mg full dose is a documented but uncommon complication, more often seen in patients with pre-existing hepatic disease. The low-dose protocol (0.5-4.5 mg) has not been associated with meaningful hepatic effects in the published literature. Pre-existing liver disease, particularly active hepatitis, is a relative contraindication that the prescribing clinician evaluates.

The pattern-not-cure mistake is the most common failure mode in the patient who has read about LDN online and started it without addressing the broader picture. Peat was direct on this point — the drug breaks a pattern but does not address the underlying physiology. A patient who responds to a short course but whose hypothyroidism, PUFA load, endotoxin burden, and estrogen exposure go uncorrected will relapse when the drug is withdrawn. The intervention is the whole stack, not the single agent.

The expectation-mismatch failure mode applies to patients who take LDN for months without effect. Peat's stated timeline — "it usually has its effect in just a few days, though. If it hasn't really made a big difference in just a few days, then it's probably some other problem" — sets a different expectation than the LDN community's typical 3-6 month evaluation window. A patient on Peat's framework who has not responded within a week or two should reconsider whether the endorphin signal is the active mediator in their specific case, rather than continuing to titrate the drug indefinitely.

## Contraindications

- **Active opioid use or opioid dependence** (absolute). Precipitates withdrawal. Minimum opioid-free interval set by prescriber.
- **Severe acute hepatitis or hepatic failure** (absolute at full dose; relative at low dose). Liver function should be assessed before starting.
- **Pregnancy and lactation** (relative). Safety data are limited. Some practitioners use LDN cautiously during pregnancy for established autoimmune conditions; the decision is individual.
- **Anesthesia and surgery** (relative). Naltrexone blocks the analgesic effect of opioid pain medications. Patients on naltrexone who need surgery require coordinated management — typically discontinuing the drug 72 hours before elective surgery and resuming after opioid analgesia is no longer needed.
- **Acute hepatitis or transaminase elevations greater than 3x upper limit of normal** (relative).

## Tracking signals

Within days of starting a Peat-style short course, the patient should be tracking:

- **Mood and energy.** The expected change is a reduction in the hibernation-pattern flatness — increased emotional engagement, ability to initiate action, recovery of motivation. If the response is going to happen, it is usually visible within 2-4 days.
- **Pain levels.** For chronic pain or autoimmune flares, reduction in baseline pain or in flare frequency. Some patients note a transient pain increase in the first few days as the endorphin blockade lifts a chronic dampening signal; this usually resolves.
- **Sleep architecture.** Vivid dreams or fragmented sleep are common early. Persistent disruption beyond 2-3 weeks suggests dose adjustment.
- **Energy in late afternoon.** The hypothyroid pattern that the drug is interrupting typically produces a late-afternoon energy crash. Improvement here is a high-signal indicator.
- **Cognitive clarity.** Reduction in brain fog, faster mental processing, return of executive function.
- **Basal temperature and resting pulse.** The metabolic substrate. Improvement in temperature and pulse during a naltrexone course is unusual; persistence of low temperature and pulse despite mood improvement points back to [[thyroid]] as the upstream lever.

The negative tracking signal — no change in 3-7 days — is the same signal Peat described in 2012. Either the endorphin signaling is not the active mediator in this patient's case, or the dose is wrong, or the broader physiology has to move first.

## Pharmacy access

Naltrexone is a Schedule I-IV-exempt prescription drug in the United States. It is not a controlled substance and does not require a DEA-registered prescriber, but it does require a standard prescription. Commercial 50 mg tablets are widely available; compounded 1 mg/mL liquid or fixed-dose capsules (0.5 mg, 1.5 mg, 3 mg, 4.5 mg) are available through compounding pharmacies. The Compounded LDN sources most commonly used in the patient community include Skip's Pharmacy (Florida) and Belmar Pharmacy (Colorado), among many others. International access varies — the drug is available in most countries with similar prescription requirements.

Insurance coverage is unreliable for compounded LDN because the use is off-label. Out-of-pocket cost for a month of compounded liquid is typically $30-60. Commercial 50 mg tablets are cheaper per milligram but require splitting or dissolving for low-dose use, with the resulting loss of precision.

Specialty practitioners — particularly functional medicine, integrative medicine, and certain rheumatology and neurology clinicians — have become more familiar with the off-label protocol over the last decade. A patient seeking access often has more success with these specialists than with a typical primary care provider unfamiliar with the low-dose use.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream medical position on naltrexone is that it is an effective and well-validated treatment for alcohol use disorder and for opioid addiction relapse prevention at the 50 mg/day dose, and that the low-dose off-label uses are biologically interesting but inadequately evidenced. This is not an unfair summary of the literature. The peer-reviewed evidence base for LDN consists primarily of small open-label trials and a limited number of randomized controlled trials, mostly in Crohn's disease, multiple sclerosis, and fibromyalgia. The trials that exist tend to show modest but statistically meaningful improvements in symptom scores; none of them are large enough or long enough to support regulatory approval. The Cochrane reviews of LDN in MS and Crohn's both conclude that the evidence is insufficient for routine clinical use.

The mainstream pain-medicine position is that opioid agonists, used appropriately, are the gold standard for moderate-to-severe pain, and that opioid antagonists are tools for managing addiction rather than for treating pain. The LDN paradigm — using an opioid antagonist *as* an analgesic — is, in this view, theoretically incoherent. The standard counter-counter-argument is that LDN works at doses far below those required for clinically meaningful opioid receptor blockade and may operate primarily through non-opioid mechanisms (TLR4 modulation on microglia, in particular). This is the framing Jarred Younger and the academic LDN researchers have used; it is plausible and partially evidenced but not as well-established as the patient community presentation would suggest.

The addiction-medicine community is divided on the broader use of LDN. Some practitioners view the extension from full-dose addiction treatment to low-dose autoimmune and pain management as scientifically reasonable; others view it as a poorly-controlled application of an addiction drug to populations where the long-term safety data are thin. The strongest internal critique is not that LDN does not work — patient outcomes are reported widely enough that *something* is happening — but that the mechanism is not what its proponents claim and that the proper use cases have not been carefully characterized.

The Bihari-Peat disagreement on mechanism, discussed above, is itself a counter-argument internal to the LDN-friendly community. If Peat is right and the drug works by lowering chronic endorphin tone rather than by triggering rebound elevation, then the standard continuous nightly dosing protocol is unnecessary and may be wasting resources on patients who would respond to shorter courses. If Bihari is right and the rebound theory is correct, then Peat's intermittent-course protocol is under-dosing patients who would benefit from sustained treatment. The clinical experience of practitioners using both protocols is overlapping enough that the question has not been resolved.

A specific Peat-skeptical position from the conventional autoimmune literature: large-scale studies of naltrexone in well-defined autoimmune conditions (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, type 1 diabetes) have not shown the dramatic effects that the patient community describes. The interpretation depends on prior assumptions — either the drug works only in particular subsets that the large trials missed, or the patient-community reports are confounded by placebo effects in a self-selected sample that did not improve on conventional treatment. The same critique applies to most off-label uses of any drug.

A separate concern: the LDN community has occasionally veered into framing the drug as a near-universal anti-inflammatory cure. Peat's own framing was much more restrained. He treated it as one tool among many, useful in specific stress-locked patterns, with a clear expected timeline and a clear set of underlying corrections (thyroid, diet, PUFA depletion, hormonal balance) that needed to happen alongside. The drug-as-cure presentation that sometimes appears in online LDN communities is not the framing Peat used and should not be read into his recommendations.

## Data gaps

Several gaps in the published literature are worth marking. First, there are no head-to-head trials of Peat's intermittent short-course protocol against the standard continuous LDN protocol. The two communities have developed in parallel and tested their own protocols against placebo, not against each other. The question of which dosing schedule produces more durable benefit in which patient population remains open.

Second, the mechanistic question — endorphin rebound versus endorphin blockade — has not been resolved at the biochemical level for the low-dose protocol. The bulk of mechanistic studies on naltrexone use the full 50 mg dose in addiction contexts, where the receptor blockade story is unambiguous. At 4.5 mg the receptor occupancy is partial and time-limited, and what the brain does in response over 24 hours is empirically underexplored.

Third, long-term safety data for continuous low-dose use over years to decades are weak. Most published trials run 12-24 weeks. Patients in the broader LDN community have been on the drug for a decade or more without obvious problems, but the absence of a signal in observational follow-up is not the same as the presence of safety data.

Fourth, Peat's specific suggestion that 0.01 mg ("a hundredth of a milligram") can produce a therapeutic effect in some patients is not well-supported in the broader pharmacological literature, which generally treats doses below 1 mg as subtherapeutic. Whether the very-low-dose effect he described is real, a placebo response, or a subset of patients with unusually high receptor sensitivity is an empirical question that has not been seriously studied.

Fifth, the interaction between naltrexone and the broader Peat stack (thyroid, progesterone, aspirin, cyproheptadine) has not been formally studied. The combinations are coherent on a mechanistic level but the formal evidence is single-agent.
