# Cholesterol

Cholesterol may be the single substance where Ray Peat's framework collides most directly with public health orthodoxy. Fifty years of medical advice has trained people to treat cholesterol as a slow-acting venom that accumulates in their arteries and kills them. Peat spent his career arguing that this story is inverted. Cholesterol is the body's master protective substance, a structural antioxidant present in every cell, bound to chromosomes, stabilizing mitochondria, and serving as the raw material from which every protective steroid hormone is synthesized. "It has been known for 80 or 90 years that cholesterol is an antitoxin, an anti-inflammatory, and our basic protective substance," Peat told listeners in a [[2008-12-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cholesterol-is-an-important-molecule|2008 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on cholesterol]]. "It is like a protective hormone in itself." The central diagnostic insight is the thyroid-cholesterol mirror: elevated cholesterol is not a disease but a sign that [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]] is inadequate to convert cholesterol into the hormones the body needs. Fix the thyroid, and cholesterol normalizes. Lower it artificially with [[statins]], and you deplete the very substance the body raised to defend itself.

## Everything Doctors Know Is Wrong

"Everything that doctors know about cholesterol is wrong," Peat wrote in his article [[cholesterol-longevity-intelligence-and-health|"Cholesterol, longevity, intelligence, and health"]]. The history he traced reveals compounding errors that became self-reinforcing once commercial interests attached to them.

The story begins with Russian experiments nearly a century ago in which rabbits fed cholesterol developed atherosclerosis. But subsequent work showed that rabbits are metabolically unusual in responding this way, and that even rabbits do not develop atherosclerosis from cholesterol if given a thyroid supplement. By 1936, the medical literature had established that elevated cholesterol in humans was caused by [[hypothyroidism]], and that hypothyroidism caused both cardiovascular disease and cancer. "There was already more reason at that time to think that the increased cholesterol was a protective adaptation than to think that it was maladaptive," Peat wrote.

The idea that cholesterol causes heart disease was resurrected in the 1950s not by new science but by new commerce. As Peat explained in a [[2011-09-29-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-cholesterol-and-saturated-fats|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on cholesterol and saturated fats]], the paint and plastics industry had discovered how to make their products from petroleum, collapsing the market for seed oils. The soy, cottonseed, and safflower oil industries needed a new customer. "The only biological effect established for the seed oils, other than sterility and dementia, was that they lowered blood cholesterol," Peat observed. The American Heart Association, the USDA, the FDA, and the AMA all enlisted to help advertise. "The correspondence between heart disease and consumption of saturated fat and cholesterol is little more than advertising copy," Peat concluded.

The pig industry had discovered the same effect from the opposite direction. George Burr, credited as the inventor of the [[essential-fatty-acid]] concept, found that feeding animals [[polyunsaturated-fats|polyunsaturated fats]] from soy and corn suppressed their metabolic rate, allowing farmers to fatten pigs more cheaply. One of the side effects was lowered cholesterol. The toxic suppression of metabolism was repackaged as a health benefit.

Over the last several decades, the Swedish physician Uffe Ravnskov has systematically dismantled the statistical case for a causal connection between blood lipids and heart disease. "The cholesterol seems to be there as a defensive reaction to protect the injured blood vessels," Peat said.

## The Body's Master Protector

Cholesterol is not a passive structural lipid. Experiments dating to the 1920s demonstrated that it could neutralize a remarkable range of toxins. "They used about 10 different toxins, from pus to snake venom to heavy metals," Peat recounted on *EastWest Healing*. "They found that if they gave a lethal dose of the poison to an animal but also gave it a good dose of cholesterol, it would survive."

The protective range is not limited to exotic poisons. Cholesterol also protects against radiation damage and hemolysis of red blood cells caused by heat. Cholesterol, [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]], [[progesterone]], and [[vitamin-d|vitamin D]] are classified as "structural antioxidants" that protect against oxidation partly by stabilizing molecular structures.

This protection extends into the deepest architecture of the cell. "Cholesterol is an essential component of every living process," Peat told the *Ask the Herb Doctor* hosts. "It is stuck to the chromosomes and the structural, architectural microtubules and microfilaments all through the cell. It isn't just a membrane-associated thing. The ability of cells to move requires cholesterol." Cell division depends on it. Red blood cell stability depends on it. Learning ability in laboratory animals improves with increased cholesterol. "Every level of physiology is protected by adequate cholesterol," Peat said.

One of the most fundamental of these functions is the stabilization of [[mitochondria]]. When asked directly what cholesterol has to do with mitochondrial respiration, Peat answered: "First, it stabilizes and protects the mitochondria against free fatty acids. It actually stimulates respiration and oxygen consumption. The proteins that make up the mitochondrion have a very liquid property; that protein structure is reinforced and stabilized by binding with cholesterol molecules." Serious stress lowers ATP, [[magnesium]], and [[carbon-dioxide]]. When ATP and intracellular magnesium decrease, cholesterol synthesis increases. The body manufactures more of its protective substance precisely when the cells are under siege.

## The Steroid Cascade: Cholesterol as Raw Material

Cholesterol's role as a direct protector is only the first half of the story. It is also the precursor for [[pregnenolone]], which becomes progesterone and [[DHEA]], which in turn become [[cortisol]], [[testosterone]], [[estrogen]], aldosterone, and dozens of other hormones. In the liver, cholesterol becomes bile salts. The body cannot produce any of these substances without adequate cholesterol, thyroid hormone, and [[vitamin-a|vitamin A]].

The conversion is not a trickle. "If the blood contains Vitamin A and thyroid hormone, the amount of progesterone being produced by the gland varies directly with the amount of cholesterol going into the gland," Peat explained. "It is a massive quantitative conversion." On the *EastWest Healing* episode, he elaborated: "If thyroid hormone and Vitamin A nutrition were adequate, the amount of progesterone coming out of an ovary or adrenal gland exactly corresponded to the amount of cholesterol going in."

This is where the thyroid connection becomes decisive. Thyroid hormone and vitamin A are required for the enzymatic step that converts cholesterol into pregnenolone. When thyroid function declines, this conversion slows and cholesterol accumulates. The elevated reading on a blood test is not evidence of excess production; it is evidence of inadequate conversion. Peat wrote that "in the 1930s, a rise in the concentration of cholesterol was considered to be one of the most reliable ways to diagnose hypothyroidism." The test that now triggers a statin prescription was once understood to be a thyroid test.

The implications for brain function are stark. Peat confirmed that low cholesterol starves the brain of protective neurosteroids: "Progesterone, for example, is extremely protective to the brain. It promotes healing and regeneration after the brain has been injured. It is now being tested in people who have had car accidents with brain damage, and it makes a tremendous difference in survival and recovery rates. If your cholesterol is artificially lowered, your brain is going to be deficient in progesterone."

## The Thyroid-Cholesterol Mirror

The relationship between thyroid function and cholesterol is perhaps the most clinically actionable principle in Peat's entire framework: cholesterol rises when thyroid function falls, and cholesterol falls when thyroid function rises. "In the 1930s, it was noticed that when the thyroid gland was removed, cholesterol immediately went up. If you gave a thyroid supplement, the cholesterol would decrease. Blood cholesterol was a mirror image of thyroid function," Peat said on *EastWest Healing*.

Active thyroid hormone (T3) drives the conversion of cholesterol into pregnenolone, progesterone, DHEA, bile acids, and other end products. When T3 is adequate, cholesterol is consumed as fast as it is synthesized. When T3 is low, whether from overt hypothyroidism, subclinical thyroid suppression, or the anti-thyroid effects of polyunsaturated fats, cholesterol piles up in the blood the way inventory piles up in a warehouse when the shipping department stops working. Peat's framework identifies numerous widespread suppressors of thyroid function: PUFA consumption, estrogen dominance, [[serotonin]] excess, [[endotoxin]] from a compromised gut, and chronic stress itself. Each impairs T4-to-T3 conversion or blocks thyroid hormone at the cellular level.

The therapeutic implication reverses the conventional approach. Instead of lowering cholesterol with drugs, restore thyroid function. "Thyroid was enough to prevent atherosclerosis, and at the same time, it lowered cholesterol," Peat said, summarizing the 1930s research that has been systematically ignored for almost a century.

Stress compounds the picture. "The pituitary senses something wrong and increases ACTH, which drives the adrenal glands to take up cholesterol and synthesize it into pregnenolone, progesterone, DHEA, and finally cortisol," Peat explained. If thyroid and vitamin A are adequate, cholesterol flows into the adrenals and ovaries and becomes hormones. If they are not, it accumulates. The body keeps producing more because it keeps sensing the need for protection, but the downstream pathway is blocked. Peat drew an analogy to blood pressure: "Similar to blood pressure, cholesterol rises when there is a problem to be soothed. When tissues are less efficient, they need more blood supply and care." Both are compensatory responses, not diseases.

## Low Cholesterol Kills

The most damaging consequence of the lipid hypothesis is the clinical drive to lower cholesterol as far as possible. "The lower the better" is, in Peat's analysis, a lethal misconception supported by research funded by industries that profit from cholesterol-lowering drugs and polyunsaturated oils.

Around 1985, a large study in Hungary showed that lowering cholesterol with drugs caused a massive increase in cancer mortality. "Hundreds of publications appeared in the U.S. saying that wasn't possible, because low cholesterol is good, the lower the better," Peat wrote. But the pattern from the Hungarian study turned out to be consistent with what is seen in any population with very low cholesterol, whether the levels are natural or drug-induced.

The Framingham heart study showed that people with cholesterol below 200 mg/dL scored lower on tests of "verbal fluency, attention/concentration, abstract reasoning, and a composite score measuring multiple cognitive domains" than those with higher cholesterol. A study of older women in care homes found that "the optimal cholesterol level for living a long time and keeping their mentality sound was 270 mg/dL," with mortality "5.2 times higher than the minimum" at the lowest cholesterol levels measured.

Peat linked the psychiatric mortality directly to neurosteroid depletion: "The increased mortality from accidents and suicide when cholesterol is lowered is reminiscent of the problems seen in progesterone deficiency, and it's very likely that a deficiency of the neurosteroids accounts for it." When cholesterol drops, the body cannot produce adequate pregnenolone, progesterone, and DHEA, the neurosteroids that regulate mood, learning, and impulse control.

Peat advised that cholesterol should not go below 160 mg/dL. Sometimes a person's cholesterol is too low not because of drugs but because of insufficient dietary substrate. "If you lack something needed to turn energy into cholesterol, such as sugar, or if you are overloaded with unsaturated fats, you might not produce enough cholesterol," he explained. "Sometimes a supplement of sugar is needed to bring up your production of cholesterol."

## Cholesterol in the Nucleus: Cell Division, Cancer, and Longevity

Cholesterol's structural role extends far beyond cell membranes. Peat emphasized that "cholesterol is found in the nucleus in the chromosomes, bound to DNA and in the nuclear matrix that governs the activation of genes, and in the mitotic spindle, which regulates separation of the chromosomes during cell division." This is not a peripheral detail. Without sufficient cholesterol, cells divide irregularly, producing daughter cells with abnormal numbers of chromosomes, a condition called aneuploidy. Aneuploidy is now recognized as an essential feature of [[cancer]] cells.

Statins, which inhibit cholesterol synthesis at the level of the cell, interfere with this process in every dividing cell. The connection between statin use and cancer risk follows directly from this biology.

On the longevity side, Peat cited a remarkable experiment with the roundworm *C. elegans*. Researchers added a gene that allowed the worm to synthesize its own cholesterol rather than depending on dietary sources. The result was a lifespan increase of up to 131%, "like increasing the human lifespan to about 175 years." These worms were also more resistant to radiation and heat stress, illustrating what Peat regarded as a general principle: organisms that can maintain higher cholesterol levels are more resilient and longer-lived.

## HDL and LDL: Both Protective

The conventional framing divides cholesterol into "good" (HDL) and "bad" (LDL). Peat rejected this dichotomy. "LDL is mostly the one that carries cholesterol into the places where it is needed, the brain, ovaries, testicles, and adrenal glands," he explained on the *EastWest Healing* episode. "HDL has some more specific antitoxic effects. Toxins will tend to increase HDL relative to LDL because of those antitoxic effects." The two forms represent different delivery and cleanup functions within a unified protective system, not a morality play of good versus bad.

## Oxidized Cholesterol: The Real Villain

If native cholesterol is protective, what accounts for its presence in atherosclerotic plaques? Peat identified the culprit as oxidized cholesterol, a chemically degraded form produced when cholesterol reacts with [[free-radicals|free radicals]], particularly those generated by the breakdown of polyunsaturated fats.

Around 1971, someone noticed that the commercial cholesterol used in feeding experiments was oxidized. "Comparing carefully prepared, unoxidized cholesterol with the oxidized degraded material, it was found that dietary cholesterol wasn't necessarily atherogenic," Peat wrote. The experiments that had appeared to indict cholesterol were actually showing the effects of rancid, degraded cholesterol dissolved in polyunsaturated oils that were themselves generating the free radicals that oxidize it.

The cooking implications are counterintuitive. "Steaming salmon produced several times as much oxidized cholesterol as frying it, because of the longer cooking time that allowed the polyunsaturated fatty acids to break down, producing toxins such as acrolein and free radicals that oxidize the cholesterol and other components of the fish," Peat wrote. "The toxic cholesterol content of the steamed salmon was much higher than that of beef cooked at a high temperature." The high PUFA content of fish makes it particularly vulnerable to generating toxic oxidized cholesterol during slow, moist cooking.

The real driver of cholesterol accumulation in arteries is the presence of oxidized polyunsaturated oils. "When oxidized polyunsaturated oils, such as corn oil or linoleic acid, are added to food, they appear in the blood lipids, where they accelerate the formation of cholesterol deposits in arteries," he wrote. The cholesterol in the arterial wall is there as a defensive patch. Sarah Murray distilled the mechanism for listeners: "Are you saying those oils are so toxic to the lining of our arteries that they cause a breakdown, which the body then tries to patch with cholesterol?" Peat answered: "Yes, that was worked out in the 1940s."

## The Statin Problem

Statins do not merely lower a number on a blood test. They inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, an enzyme in the mevalonate pathway that produces not only cholesterol but also [[coenzyme-q10|Coenzyme Q10]], dolichols, and other essential molecules. "Statins interfere with the whole chain of synthesis, including Coenzyme Q10 production," Peat said on *EastWest Healing*. "This is likely a factor in why they cause muscles to break down," a condition known as rhabdomyolysis. CoQ10 is essential for mitochondrial electron transport; depleting it impairs cellular energy production at the most fundamental level.

The neurological effects are equally concerning. "The tendency to become violent, suicidally depressed, or demented," Peat listed as consequences of statin-induced cholesterol lowering. "Cholesterol is an intrinsic part of chromosome and DNA function." Blocking its synthesis systemically is, in Peat's assessment, a form of metabolic sabotage. He cited the Hungarian study repeatedly: "Using a drug to lower cholesterol increased the death rate from just about every cause. Cancer, suicide, and even getting murdered were more probable when a person had their cholesterol artificially lowered."

## Low Thyroid, Low Cholesterol, and the Inflammatory Cascade

In his email correspondence, Peat connected low cholesterol and marginal thyroid function to the inflammatory cascade that drives degenerative disease. "If low cholesterol is combined with slightly low thyroid, the protective steroids aren't produced in normal amounts, and inflammatory processes develop," he wrote in [[cholesterol|an email exchange on cholesterol]]. "Connective tissue pain, waist fat, and constipation relate to the stress-inflammation processes, for example endotoxin slows the liver's detoxifying process, estrogen and serotonin signal defensive reactions that lead to cumulative problems."

The remedy follows the framework's logic: "Sweet fruits are anti-inflammatory and help to keep the liver functioning, including keeping cholesterol up and keeping estrogen and cortisol under control," Peat continued. "When estrogen is relatively high, tryptophan turns into serotonin and slows the thyroid, lowers the temperature." The solution is not a cholesterol drug but a metabolic correction: adequate thyroid, adequate sugar, adequate cholesterol production, and the elimination of the polyunsaturated fats and endotoxin that suppress the whole system.

Peat's broadest claim may be his most provocative: that cholesterol is essential to intelligence itself. "Simply injecting cholesterol into animals can improve their learning ability," he wrote. Cholesterol is a structural component of every synapse, every myelinated nerve fiber, every membrane involved in neurotransmitter release and reception. The neurosteroids produced from it regulate memory formation, mood, and neuroplasticity. "I suspect that the physiological meaning of cholesterol has to do with movement, stability, differentiation, memory, and sensitivity of the parts of the cells, that is, with everything physiological," Peat wrote. His final assessment of medicine's war on cholesterol was characteristically blunt: "To deliberately interfere with its synthesis, as contemporary medicine does, reveals a terrible arrogance."
