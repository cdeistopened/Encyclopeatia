# Methylglyoxal

Methylglyoxal (MGO, pyruvaldehyde, CH₃–CO–CHO) is a small reactive α-dicarbonyl produced as a side-stream of glucose metabolism and as a degradation product of triose phosphates, glycerol, and lipid peroxidation. It sits at one of the most consequential forks in Peat's framework: at homeostatic, low concentration in a healthy oxidizing cell it is one of [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Szent-Györgyi]]'s candidate "retine" molecules — electron-withdrawing dicarbonyls that hold the protein-water lattice in the differentiated, non-dividing state. At pathologically high concentration, in the lactate-flooded cell of a diabetic, a tumor, or a [[polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]]-loaded tissue, the same molecule becomes the dominant glycating agent driving the protein damage mainstream medicine attributes to "high blood sugar." Same chemistry, two contexts, opposite effects. It is the cleanest single illustration of why Peat refused to read biochemistry as a list of "toxic" and "protective" compounds and insisted on the *state of the cell* as the determinant.

The textbook story is monochrome: MGO is a toxic byproduct, glyoxalase I and II detoxify it, and elevated MGO in diabetics drives the AGEs that erode capillaries, lenses, kidneys, and nerves. Peat accepted the damage half and asked the harder question — why does the cell make the molecule at all, and what was Szent-Györgyi doing devoting his American decades to it? Across Peat's 2016 cancer essay and a *Generative Energy* #35 exchange with Danny Roddy, the answer is that methylglyoxal is one of a small family of dicarbonyls the cell's redox apparatus *uses* to organize itself — and its appearance as a glycator is what happens when the metabolic context that let it act catalytically has collapsed.

## Biochemistry: where it comes from, where it goes

Methylglyoxal forms by several routes whose relative weights determine whether it acts as catalyst or burden. The largest endogenous source is non-enzymatic phosphate loss from the triose intermediates of glycolysis — dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. About 0.1–0.4% of glycolytic flux escapes through this side-channel even in healthy cells; the fraction rises sharply when trioses accumulate because downstream glycolysis is backed up (as in [[lactic-acid|lactate]]-producing cells where pyruvate dehydrogenase is inhibited) or because [[fructose]] dumps trioses directly into the pool.

The second source Peat emphasized is glycerol from mobilized fatty acids. Danny Roddy raised it in [[35-co2-ketosis-and-mitochondria-pufa-sugar-iron-and-ages-progesterone-and-cell-stability|*Generative Energy* #35]]: "Isn't it the glycerol from the free fatty acids that is majorly forming the methylglyoxal, which is essential for the advanced glycation end products (AGEs) that are blamed on glucose?" Peat agreed: "Yeah. Especially in the presence of polyunsaturated fats, the methylglyoxal starts to break down, and then those fragments of linoleic or arachidonic acid amplify the so-called glycation. It's predominantly a PUFA deterioration that counts as glycation."

[[Lactic acid|Lactate]] is the third route. Both D-lactate (produced in larger amounts by diabetics and intestinal bacteria) and the L-lactate healthy tissue produces can be metabolized back to methylglyoxal. Peat made the chain explicit in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]] (2014): "Methylglyoxal can be formed from either form of lactate, and it is an important factor in the glycation of proteins. It can also be formed from MDA, a product of lipid peroxidation. Protein glycation is an important factor in diabetes and aging, but glucose, rather than lactate and polyunsaturated fats, is commonly said to be the cause." The "commonly said to be" is the Peatian move — the actual chemistry traces the carbonyl burden back to lactate and to PUFA breakdown products like MDA that themselves yield methylglyoxal.

Detoxification runs through the conserved glyoxalase system — glyoxalase I and II using glutathione as cofactor to convert methylglyoxal back to D-lactate. Its conservation from yeast to humans is itself evidence that the substrate is something the cell produces continuously and must continuously regulate — not an exotic toxin.

## Szent-Györgyi's retine/promine framework

From the late 1950s through the 1970s, [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Szent-Györgyi]] proposed cancer was a disturbance in the redox state of the cell's protein-water lattice — a deficit of small electron-withdrawing molecules that, in a healthy cell, hold protein-bound electrons in the oxidized configuration supporting differentiation and inhibiting division. He named the inhibitory class *retine* (from *retinere*, to hold back) and the stimulatory class *promine*. The proposal occupied his Woods Hole work in *The Living State* (1972) and *Electronic Biology and Cancer* (1976).

The candidate retine molecule was methylglyoxal. The reasoning was structural: two C=O groups separated by a single carbon make a strong electron-puller. Bound to a cellular protein it would withdraw electrons from the backbone in exactly the way the electron-donor-acceptor framework predicted, holding the lattice in the doped, conductive, differentiated state. Cells deficient in methylglyoxal would dwell in the reduced state division requires — the "primordial" proliferative state, the unicellular default. Complex multicellular organisms built an inhibitory apparatus on top of that default, of which methylglyoxal is one molecular component. Lose it — by hypoxia, lactate flooding, or failure of oxidative metabolism — and the cell reverts to dividing.

Peat compressed the framework in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism* (July 2016)]]: "In the 1950s and 1960s, Szent-Gyorgyi began talking openly about life processes in terms of a very precise balance between oxidation and reduction which kept systems of electrons in cells in a special free-radical-like state, oscillating between 'donor' and 'acceptor' molecules. He believed that a deficiency of the naturally occurring oxidative dicarbonyl molecule methylglyoxal might be a cause of cancer." Mainstream cancer biology read methylglyoxal as a glycating poison; the gene-centered consensus had no use for "small molecule that holds the protein lattice in the oxidized differentiated state."

The triangulation with W.F. Koch matters. Koch had used the more volatile dicarbonyl glyoxal (CHO–CHO) as one of his reagents in oxidation-restoration cancer therapy starting in 1916. When [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Szent-Györgyi]] arrived in the U.S. in 1947 and began his dicarbonyl program, he was extending Koch's empirical approach to a theoretical framework — though the FDA's campaign against Koch made acknowledging the lineage politically impossible. Peat made the connection direct in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]]: "Albert Szent-Györgyi respected Koch's work, and spent years investigating the involvement of the lactate metabolites, methylgyoxal and glyoxal, in cell physiology, but since the government's campaign against Koch was still active when Szent-Györgyi came to the U.S., he worked out many of the implications of Koch's work relating to cellular oxidation without mentioning his name." Szent-Györgyi's retine was a candidate chalone — the "restraining" tissue extract earlier researchers had pursued — with a specific molecular identity and an electronic mechanism.

## Pal 2016: experimental confirmation, with the load-bearing qualifier

The Szent-Györgyi framework would have remained historical curiosity if subsequent experimental work had not begun to confirm the central empirical claim. In April 2016, *Amino Acids* published Pal, Roy, and Ray's "Creatine supplementation with methylglyoxal: a potent therapy for cancer in experimental models" — one of a series from the Ray lab in Calcutta documenting selective cytotoxicity of methylglyoxal against cancer cells. Peat seized on it in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]]: "There is still interest in his theory, for example 'The anti-cancer effect of methylglyoxal is now well established in the literature' (Pal, et al., 2016), but that molecule can also be toxic, depending on the condition of the cell."

"Depending on the condition of the cell" is the load-bearing phrase. Pal and colleagues had shown methylglyoxal at doses lethal to a range of tumor cell lines in mice was tolerated by healthy tissues and could be combined with creatine and ascorbate to produce experimental cures of Ehrlich ascites carcinoma and sarcoma-180. The proposed mechanism — selective inhibition of mitochondrial respiratory complexes in tumor cells that have lost the flexibility to tolerate the dicarbonyl burden — recovers the framework's central prediction: a concentration window exists at which methylglyoxal is selectively toxic to undifferentiated dividing cells while tolerated by differentiated tissue. A parallel line showed sub-cytotoxic concentrations induce senescence in normal fibroblasts — pushing cells into the non-dividing state the framework predicted. Kill the dividing cell, retire the others into senescence: exactly what an endogenous chalone would do. Mainstream oncology has not adopted MGO therapy; the Pal results remain in the small-experimental-model literature. Peat's reading was optimistic, but the anti-cancer empirical claim from *Electronic Biology and Cancer* has held up across half a century of attempted falsification.

## The diabetic AGE-formation story: when the same molecule turns destructive

The opposite pole of the Janus is what mainstream biochemistry sees: the principal glycating agent in formation of advanced glycation end-products. In a tissue with elevated methylglyoxal — chronic hyperglycemia raising triose flux, lactate flooding from hypoxia, glycerol from PUFA-loaded adipose tissue — the dicarbonyl attacks arginine and lysine residues in long-lived proteins, forming MG-H1 (methylglyoxal-derived hydroimidazolone) and CEL (carboxyethyl-lysine) adducts. These are the molecular fingerprints of diabetic damage: thickened capillary basement membranes, opacified lenses, fibrosed kidneys, demyelinated nerves.

The conventional account names "hyperglycemia" as the cause. Peat's revision, developed across [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]], [[protective-co2-and-aging|*Protective CO2 and Aging*]], and *Generative Energy* #35, traces the carbonyl burden to its actual sources: PUFA breakdown products (especially MDA from linoleic acid peroxidation), glycerol released during lipolysis, and lactate accumulated under any condition that inhibits pyruvate oxidation. Glucose in a cell with intact oxidative metabolism is the *least* glycating fuel because it is rapidly cleared into the citric acid cycle. The methylglyoxal in diabetic blood is the chemical signature of *failed* glucose oxidation, not of glucose itself.

[[Carbon dioxide|CO₂]] is the protective factor most central to Peat's framework here. In [[protective-co2-and-aging|*Protective CO2 and Aging*]]: "A prolonged decrease in carbon dioxide can increase the susceptibility of proteins to glycation (the addition of aldehydes, from polyunsaturated fat peroxidation or methylglyoxal from lactate metabolism, to amino groups), and a similar process is likely to contribute to the methylation of histones, a process that increases with aging." CO₂ acts as a competitive Lewis acid at the same amino groups MGO would attack, occupying lysine ε-amines as carbamate and shielding them from carbonyl adduction. A high-CO₂ cell is a cell with proteins masked against glycation. Peat made the redox conditional explicit in *Generative Energy* #35: "By shifting the redox in that direction, I think it intensifies that methylglyoxal metabolism. Once you get that shift towards lactate, you're shifting against carbon dioxide, and carbon dioxide is a direct blocker of protein glycation." Same molecule, two cells: in a CO₂-rich oxidative cell, regulator; in a lactate-flooded reductive cell, glycator.

The [[lipid-peroxidation|lipoxidation]] amplification is the second mechanism pushing MGO from regulator to wrecker. The dicarbonyl alone produces modest glycation; in the presence of polyunsaturated fats it breaks down to reactive fragments of linoleic and arachidonic acid that accelerate and amplify protein damage. This is Peat's empirical basis for reframing "glycation" as predominantly *lipoxidation* — damage attributed to sugar is driven by the lipid component, with methylglyoxal as one intermediate carrying lipid-derived carbonyls onto protein amines. Li et al. (2008), cited in *Protective CO2 and Aging*, showed both glyoxal and methylglyoxal form DNA adducts with 2′-deoxyguanosine — adding genetic instability to the protein damage. The framework is recursive: lactate shift produces methylglyoxal; without CO₂ protection and with PUFA amplification, methylglyoxal modifies proteins and DNA; the damage further degrades oxidative capacity; which produces more lactate. The diabetic spiral.

## Manuka honey and the UMF/MGO claim

The one place methylglyoxal has entered popular awareness is manuka honey — the dark honey produced by bees foraging on the manuka tree (*Leptospermum scoparium*) in New Zealand. Its antibacterial activity, distinct from the hydrogen peroxide–mediated activity of ordinary honey, was traced in the late 2000s to unusually high methylglyoxal content: 100–700 mg/kg in active manuka versus under 10 mg/kg in most other honeys. The MGO arises non-enzymatically from dihydroxyacetone in the flower nectar. The "Unique Manuka Factor" (UMF) and "MGO" ratings on commercial labels both refer to this content.

High-MGO manuka is therapeutically active against MRSA, *H. pylori*, and burns and ulcers in topical wound-care applications, well-supported in clinical trials. The systemic-consumption story is murkier: ingesting concentrated methylglyoxal in a [[honey]] base is a different proposition from generating it endogenously in an oxidizing tissue. Peat was characteristically non-committal. Asked directly ([[sugar|sugar emails]]): "I haven't had any experience with manuka. Some honey can be allergenic, so it's good to look for a mild one; white sugar is probably similar, with less allergy risk." The reply reads as deliberate refusal to recruit Szent-Györgyi's framework into commercial positioning. The honeys his framework prefers are mild light ones — chosen for fructose-glucose ratio and trace minerals, not the dicarbonyl spike. The popular framing of manuka as a methylglyoxal-delivery system has the irony of selling the very molecule diabetic nephropathy literature is trying to clear. Whether oral MGO in a fructose matrix behaves more like Szent-Györgyi's retine or like the diabetic glycator is empirically unresolved; the Pal protocol used intraperitoneal injection with creatine and ascorbate — not oral honey.

## Counter-arguments

- **Mainstream view: methylglyoxal is purely a toxic byproduct.** Supported by the glyoxalase system's existence and demonstrated AGE chemistry, the dominant position is that MGO is a glycating poison the cell continuously detoxifies and elevated levels are exclusively pathological. The retine framework is historical curiosity; Pal's anti-cancer results have not been replicated at scale or translated into accepted therapy. A reasonable mainstream reading: MGO is bad, glyoxalase is good, the only therapeutic role is reducing concentration.

- **The dual-nature framing risks special pleading.** "Toxic at high concentration, protective at low" is, cynically, the structure of any defense of any compound. Without a quantitative dose-response and clinically validated concentration window, the position devolves into claiming whatever concentration is observed must have been appropriate. The actual MGO dose-response across tissues, redox states, and pathologies has not been mapped with the precision the framework requires.

- **Glyoxalase activity as a tumor adaptation, not protection.** *Elevated* glyoxalase I activity is a feature of many aggressive tumors — cancer cells appear to *need* high glyoxalase to clear MGO generated by their high glycolytic flux. If methylglyoxal selectively kills tumor cells, why have tumors evolved high glyoxalase as survival mechanism? One reconciliation: tumors generate so much MGO from aerobic glycolysis that even elevated glyoxalase keeps cells near a toxic threshold, and exogenous MGO tips them over. Another: the framework is wrong in its strong form. Empirically undecided.

- **MGO in diabetic complications is well-supported.** The role of methylglyoxal in AGE formation in diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy is among the more solid findings in modern AGE biology. Peat's revision of "hyperglycemia" to "lactate plus PUFA breakdown plus glycerol" as primary upstream drivers is reasonable but has not displaced the conventional account in clinical diabetology.

- **The Koch–Szent-Györgyi lineage as suppressed-medicine narrative.** Peat's framing sits inside his account of mid-twentieth-century cancer research as a politically suppressed tradition. The historical claim is partly defensible (the FDA's campaign against Koch was real; Szent-Györgyi's later work was marginalized) but invites motivated reasoning. The MGO hypothesis must stand on its own evidence, not on the political martyrology.

- **Carnosine and aminoguanidine as opposing paradigm.** A separate research tradition pursues MGO scavengers — carnosine, aminoguanidine, metformin (with incidental scavenging activity) — as anti-AGE agents. The framework conflicts: one school wants more MGO at the right concentration for cancer therapy, the other wants less across the board for diabetic complications. Resolution probably runs through the tissue-state and concentration-window arguments dual-nature requires.

## Data gaps

- **Clinical dose-response has not been mapped.** Szent-Györgyi's framework predicts a concentration window inside which MGO acts as retine-like regulator and outside which it acts as glycator. That window — bounds, tissue variation, redox-state dependence — has not been measured. Without that map, "homeostatic versus pathological" is a phrase, not a clinical parameter.

- **PUFA-amplification chemistry needs quantitative work.** The claim that protein damage attributed to glycation is predominantly PUFA-driven lipoxidation with MGO as intermediate is mechanistically plausible but unworked as a balance sheet. How much of AGE burden in a diabetic kidney is glucose-derived versus lipid-derived versus lactate-derived has not been apportioned by stable-isotope tracing in human tissue.

- **Koch–Szent-Györgyi clinical record is incomplete.** Szent-Györgyi's American decades produced theoretical synthesis but few clinical results; the Cancer Research Foundation funding never produced the case-series-with-outcomes data that would have settled the retine hypothesis. Pal's animal work fills part of the gap but not the human-clinical part.

- **Manuka honey is empirically unresolved.** Topical antibacterial activity is established; systemic effects of ingested manuka methylglyoxal on cancer risk, diabetic complications, or gut and liver redox have not been studied at scale. Whether oral MGO behaves anything like Pal's parenteral protocol is unknown.

- **Glyoxalase modulation therapy is one-sided.** A small literature explores pharmacological induction of glyoxalase I (via Nrf2 activators) for clearing MGO in diabetes. The opposite strategy — suppressing glyoxalase to allow MGO to reach retine-like concentration in tumor tissue — has been proposed but barely investigated.

- **Methylglyoxal in the brain.** Dicarbonyl chemistry in brain tissue — relevant to Alzheimer's, where AGE deposits are part of the pathology — has not been integrated with Peat's framework. The lactate-flooded post-hypoxic neuron is exactly the cell type Peat's framework predicts as a glycation site; whether brain dicarbonyl burden is reducible by thyroid, sugar, and CO₂ interventions is an open clinical question.

- **The boundary between methylglyoxal and glyoxal.** Koch used glyoxal; Szent-Györgyi worked on methylglyoxal; Peat discussed them as a family. The differential chemistry — glyoxal more volatile, faster-reacting, used as an injected reagent; MGO the endogenous product — deserves separate treatment Peat did not provide.
