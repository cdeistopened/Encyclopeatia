# Histamine

Histamine belongs to [[estrogen]]'s inflammatory cascade. The hub article on estrogen lays out the full shock physiology: a single large dose of estrogen produces the same syndrome as traumatic shock, with water leaving the bloodstream, veins relaxing, blood pressure collapsing, tissues swelling. Histamine is one of the two downstream amines (alongside [[serotonin]]) that estrogen mobilizes to accomplish those effects. Ray Peat's treatment of histamine is consistent across decades. It is not simply an allergy molecule, and the parasympathetic "aura of benevolence" that surrounds it is misleading. Histamine is a stress mediator, a mast-cell product that estrogen is uniquely powerful at releasing, and a proximate cause of the vascular leakage, tissue edema, and fibrotic remodeling that define most degenerative diseases. Antihistamines, properly chosen, turn out to be among the most broadly protective drugs in medicine.

## An Early Candidate for the Parasympathetic Transmitter

The historical framing matters because it explains why the medical culture still treats histamine as essentially benign. Before acetylcholine was identified as the main parasympathetic transmitter, histamine was a leading candidate. Peat traced this in [[autonomic-systems|"Autonomic Systems"]]: "Histamine was an early candidate, before acetylcholine was discovered to be the main parasympathetic agent. This view of histamine was fostered by the older idea of 'trophic nerves,' which easily became identified with the parasympathetic system." The parasympathetic nervous system carries the connotation of rest, digestion, and growth; anything grouped with it inherits that reputation. "Nitric oxide and histamine are both very important factors in degenerative inflammatory diseases," Peat continued, "but their association with the parasympathetic nervous system has given them an aura of benevolence."

The reclassification Peat urged was straightforward. Histamine is not a transmitter of rest and repair. It is a stress-induced substance that activates glycolysis at the expense of oxidative metabolism. "Acetylcholine, like histamine and serotonin, activates glycolysis, the conversion of glucose to lactic acid, which provides energy in the absence of oxygen." The conversion of glucose to [[lactic-acid]] is the metabolic signature of tissues that cannot complete the oxidation of fuel. When the autonomic transmitters accumulate, Peat warned, the results are not restorative: "Too much serotonin, histamine, acetylcholine, and polyunsaturated fatty acids, like too much adrenalin, can cause spasms of the coronary arteries, along with disturbances of mitochondrial respiration. In stress, these substances are almost sure to be present in excess." The resulting "generalized vasoconstriction and systemic alkalosis" are, in his framing, "characteristic of many kinds of stress, including cancer." Histamine participates in the shift toward Warburg-effect metabolism that Peat traced through [[estrogen]], [[PUFA]], and the stress hormones.

## Estrogen Is the Primary Releaser

Of all the agents that cause mast cells to release histamine, Peat identified estrogen as the most potent. The statement in "Autonomic Systems" is unambiguous: "Estrogen is the most potent of these hormones in causing the cells to release histamine." This is the mechanism behind a list of conditions that track with estrogen exposure: premenstrual flushing, pregnancy rhinitis, hot flashes, migraines that worsen at ovulation, and the midcycle histamine surge that clinicians have measured but rarely connected to its cause.

Estrogen and histamine are mutually reinforcing in a way that parallels the estrogen-serotonin loop covered in [[serotonin]]. "Serotonin and histamine are increased by estrogen, and their activation mimics the effects of estrogen," Peat wrote in [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|"Serotonin, Depression, and Aggression: The Problem of Brain Energy"]]. The two amines are not merely correlated with estrogen; their activation reproduces estrogen's effects downstream, which is why women present with the long list of inflammatory-neurological conditions that track with estrogen exposure.

Ovulation provides the cleanest demonstration that mast cells are built into normal reproductive physiology. "The mast cells are needed for the follicles to rupture, so their suppression prevents ovulation," Peat observed, describing how sympathetic overgrowth in the polycystic ovary suppresses mast cell activity as "a protective adaptation against excessive stimulation." The polycystic ovary is a tissue that has adapted to chronic estrogen and inflammation by suppressing the very cells that would allow it to ovulate. Infertility in PCOS and infertility driven by chronic inflammation share a mast-cell mechanism.

Multiple sclerosis fits the same pattern. "Estrogen activates mast cells to release histamine and serotonin, and activated mast cells can produce brain edema and demyelination," Peat wrote. The disproportionate incidence of MS in women during their reproductive years, and its association with the ratio of estrogen to progesterone, follows directly. [[Progesterone]] is the cyclic antagonist that interrupts the process during the luteal phase.

## Mast Cells, Tumors, and Cancer Therapy

The classical understanding of mast cells as allergic sentinels missed their involvement in cancer for decades. Peat made the connection directly: "Although mast cells have been known to be a common component of tumors for many years, it is only recently that antihistamines and other antiinflammatory drugs have been recognized as valuable therapies in cancer." Mast cells surround tumors. They release histamine, serotonin, and growth factors into the tumor environment. The inflammatory cloud they produce stimulates new blood vessel formation, which tumors require for growth beyond a few millimeters.

The capacity that serves wound healing in a normal animal serves tumor growth in a stressed one. Peat described the angiogenic logic in "Autonomic Systems": substances released by autonomic nerves "stimulate cell division, injuries which produce clots and vascular spasms will also stimulate the formation of new blood vessels." Histamine is not a discretionary participant in oncology; it is a growth factor that tumors exploit.

This frames Peat's standing objection to opiate pain management in cancer. In [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|"Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer"]], he wrote that opiates are "the most inappropriate means of pain control, since they increase the release of histamine, which synergizes with the tumor-derived factors to suppress immunity and stimulate tumor growth." The opiate prescription is mechanistically aligned with the tumor. [[Aspirin]] and antihistamines are the logical alternatives.

## Leakage, Hyperventilation, and Carbon Dioxide

The tissue-level action that defines histamine is increased vascular permeability. Blood vessels that should function as barriers develop leaks, and water, proteins, and even whole red blood cells pass into the surrounding tissues. Peat catalogued the triggers in [[fibrosis|"Fibrosis"]]: "Blood vessels leak under the influence of estrogen, irradiation, oxygen deprivation, vitamin E deficiency, free fatty acids, lactic acid, and various stress mediators, such as histamine and serotonin; particles as large as red blood cells can pass right through the endothelial cells." Histamine forces the endothelium into an oxidized, permeable state, and the tissues flood.

The CO2 connection is the most practically useful piece of the histamine story. [[Carbon-dioxide|Carbon dioxide]] stabilizes mast cells. Lose CO2, and mast cells release histamine. "Simply hyperventilating for a couple of minutes will increase leakage from the blood into the lungs; hyperventilation decreases carbon dioxide, and increases serotonin and histamine," Peat observed in [[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|"Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer"]]. The mechanism is an alkalinity effect. "In the case of mast cells, increasing alkalinity causes them to release histamine, but similar 'alkaline-induced exocytosis' seems to occur in all stressed tissues." Anything that lowers CO2 (panic, pain, altitude, fever, chronic stress, the shallow breathing patterns typical of anxiety) tips the intracellular environment toward alkalinity and opens the mast cell granules. This is why progesterone and thyroid hormone, both of which raise CO2 production, are anti-histamine at the root.

Peat's note in "Fibrosis" that "saturated fats, antihistamines, thyroid hormone, and other anti-inflammatory agents prevent or reverse fibrosis" places antihistamines inside a broader category of CO2-supportive, oxidation-supportive interventions. The fibrosis that characterizes late-stage organ disease is a tissue that has been leaking and remodeling for years. Blocking histamine does not merely palliate symptoms; it interrupts the cycle that builds the scar tissue.

## The Inflammatory Convergence

Histamine rarely acts alone. It appears in Peat's newsletters as one member of a standing list of mediators released together. "The inflammation-producing effects of lactate, serotonin, and endotoxin are overlapping, additive, and sometimes synergistic, along with histamine, nitric oxide, bradykinin, and the cytokines," he wrote in [[endotoxin-stress-depression|"Endotoxin, Stress, Depression"]]. CRH, the hypothalamic stress signal, directly "activates many inflammatory processes, including the release of histamine, cytokines, and nitric oxide" without needing to reach cortisol first.

The breadth of what stress turns on is itself the point. Peat described the converging enzymes in "Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer": COX, aromatase, and "enzymes that make histamine, serotonin and nitric oxide, the cytokines, and the stress-induced hormones of the pituitary and adrenal glands, are turned on in difficult situations, and have to be turned off when the threat has been overcome." Chronic low energy, chronic PUFA excess, chronic endotoxin exposure, and chronic hypothyroidism all keep the stress enzymes on permanently. The [[rosacea-inflammation-and-aging-the-inefficiency-of-stress|"Rosacea, Inflammation, and Aging"]] newsletter places histamine in the canonical excitotoxic cluster alongside lactate, glutamate, ammonium, nitric oxide, estrogen, PUFA, and endotoxin.

## The Gut, the Liver, and Dietary Histamine

Because the intestine is the largest reservoir of [[endotoxin]] in the body and the gut-liver axis is where dietary amines are processed, the histamine story cannot be separated from digestion. "As inflammatory mediators, serotonin and histamine are directly involved in asthma, hives, gastrointestinal damage from alcohol, nerve cell damage, edema, and shock," Peat wrote in "Serotonin, Depression, and Aggression." The overlap between hives and shock is not rhetorical. They are the same mechanism at different intensities.

Liver disease shifts the numbers sharply. "Liver disease can increase the brain's exposure to serotonin, ammonia, and histamine," Peat observed; "these three amines occur together and are neurotoxic." A liver that is inflamed, hypothyroid, or overwhelmed by endotoxin cannot clear histamine from the portal circulation, and what it fails to clear reaches the brain. This is why headaches, mental fog, and mood disturbance track so closely with digestive symptoms.

Dietary histamine follows the same logic. Fermented foods, aged cheese, wine, cured meats, and any meat that has sat long enough for bacterial decarboxylation to run generate large amounts of histamine directly. In [[food-junk-and-some-mystery-ailments-fatigue-alzheimers-colitis-immunodeficiency|"Food, Junk and Some Mystery Ailments"]], Peat tied this to the broader estrogen-endotoxin axis: "Estrogen is closely associated with endotoxinemia, and with histamine and nitric oxide formation, and with the whole range of inflammatory and 'autoimmune' diseases. Anything that irritates the bowel, leading to increased endotoxin absorption, contributes to the same cluster of metabolic consequences." The symptoms labeled "histamine intolerance" in popular functional-medicine literature are usually reflections of an underlying estrogen-endotoxin problem rather than a primary enzyme deficiency.

## Hypothyroidism and the Chronic Histamine State

Peat was consistent that hypothyroidism sits underneath most chronic histamine pathology. "In hypothyroidism, it is common for there to be an excess of adrenalin/noradrenalin, serotonin, histamine, and some of the pituitary hormones," he wrote in "Autonomic Systems." Correcting thyroid function immediately helps, but "especially when the energy deficiency has caused anatomical adjustments (redistribution of blood vessels and mast cells, for example) it's important to make the environment supportive in as many ways as possible." Thyroid hormone works against histamine through several routes: it increases CO2 production, supports liver clearance of histamine and estrogen, improves cellular energy, and reduces reflexive sympathetic activation.

The anatomical remodeling matters because it explains delayed recovery. Chronic hypothyroidism does not just raise histamine output from a fixed population of mast cells; it recruits more of them to stressed tissues. Reversing the chemistry is faster than reversing the remodeling, which is why people restoring thyroid function sometimes feel worse before they feel better.

## The Antihistamine Toolkit

Peat recommended a short list of antihistamines consistently. In an [[cyproheptadine|email exchange on cyproheptadine]], he listed the three he returned to most often: "Have you tried other types of antihistamine, such as ketotifen, diphenhydramine, or cyproheptadine?" Each has a different profile. Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) is the cheapest and most widely available and crosses the blood-brain barrier. Cyproheptadine has additional anti-serotonin activity and is particularly useful when the stress picture includes gut, mood, and inflammatory symptoms together. Ketotifen is a mast-cell stabilizer as well as an H1 blocker, which matters when the goal is to prevent release rather than block its effects.

For acute crises, the recommendation is simple. In [[the-dark-side-of-stress-learned-helplessness|"The Dark Side of Stress: Learned Helplessness"]], Peat wrote that "supplements of thyroid and progesterone are proven to be generally protective against the cholinergic threats, but there are many other factors that can be adjusted according to particular needs. Drugs such as atropine and antihistamines can be used in crisis situations." The same logic applies to the common problem of headaches. In an [[headaches-and-migraines|email on headaches and migraines]], Peat offered the practical combination: "Low blood sugar increases the absorption of toxin from the intestine as well as activating nerve reflexes and inflammation, so keeping the intestine active and clean with mild fiber, and blocking inflammation with aspirin and antihistamine, is usually helpful." Sugar plus aspirin plus antihistamine, addressed simultaneously at the gut and the head, is the protocol.

"Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer" places antihistamines inside a larger functional category. Salicylic acid and the structurally similar "antiinflammatories, local anesthetics, muscle relaxants, expectorants, and antihistamines" may "act as surrogates for the absent Mead acid family" and substitute for the anti-inflammatory lipids the body would make from saturated and monounsaturated fats if PUFA were not crowding them out.

[[Progesterone]] is the endogenous version of this protection. It raises CO2, stabilizes mast cells, opposes estrogen at the receptor and enzymatic level, and cyclically interrupts the estrogen-histamine loop in women of reproductive age. Peat's claim that "thyroid and progesterone are generally protective" is specific: these two molecules are the body's own antihistamine system, and pharmaceutical antihistamines are imperfect backups for a process thyroid and progesterone perform natively.

## On "Mast Cell Activation Syndrome"

The category currently marketed as MCAS (mast cell activation syndrome and its proliferating adjacent diagnoses) deserves to be read through Peat's framework. The observation that some people have chronically elevated histamine and react poorly to triggers others tolerate is real. The interpretation that they have a primary mast cell disorder requiring immunologic treatment is usually wrong. Almost every listed trigger is a stressor in Peat's sense: low blood sugar, infection, poor sleep, cold exposure, polyunsaturated oils, hyperventilation, fermented foods, estrogenic medications. Almost every reported symptom fits the histamine-serotonin-endotoxin picture.

The practical difference is consequential. The MCAS framing leads to mast-cell-directed drugs in isolation, restrictive elimination diets, and a chronic identity organized around fragility. The Peat framing leads to thyroid support, progesterone, CO2-restoring measures, aspirin, gelatin, raw carrot, elimination of PUFA, and antihistamines used strategically rather than continuously. Cyproheptadine is often the single most useful intervention because it blocks both histamine and serotonin. The goal is not to eliminate every trigger; it is to restore the metabolic state in which triggers do not produce disproportionate releases.
