# Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide is not metabolic waste in Ray Peat's framework; it is the single most reliable marker and mediator of healthy energy production, and the gas that makes oxidative life possible in the first place. Mainstream physiology treats CO2 as the exhaust left over after cells burn fuel, something the lungs exist primarily to expel. Peat inverted the picture. CO2 is the context in which every protein, every enzyme, and every mitochondrion operates correctly. Its absence deranges hormones, shuts down circulation, activates [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] production, and accelerates the chemistry of aging. "I suggest that methylation stabilizes and protects cells when growth and regeneration aren't possible (and that it's likely to increase when CO2 isn't available)," Peat wrote in his [[protective-co2-and-aging|"Protective CO2 and Aging" (2011)]] newsletter, locating CO2 at the junction between moment-to-moment metabolism and the epigenetic drift that accumulates over a lifetime. To understand Peat's framework without understanding CO2 is to miss the thing his other recommendations are ultimately aimed at producing.

## The Essential Gas

On a [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on altitude]], Andrew Murray asked how a metabolic waste product could possibly be more protective than oxygen. Peat's answer drew on a survey from 1940: "He found that they can't live more than one generation if they aren't exposed to adequate carbon dioxide. Even the obligate respiring, oxygen-dependent organisms need carbon dioxide. So he concluded that really it's more important as a life-supporting element than oxygen is." The early embryo made the point more starkly. Researchers culturing pre-implantation blastocysts on 5% CO2 and 20% oxygen were getting poor results. When they inverted the ratio, using 10-15% CO2 with only 5% oxygen, the embryos thrived. "Even at the crucial stage of early embryo development, carbon dioxide is the crucial factor, much more important than oxygen."

The geological argument pushed the point further back. "At the time of the Carboniferous period, geologically, the carbon dioxide was 12% or something really intense in the atmosphere," Peat told Danny Roddy in *Generative Energy* episode 26 on redox balance. "That really stimulated a great abundance of life." The name of the period is no accident: the explosion of plant and animal biomass that left the modern coal beds occurred under CO2 concentrations roughly thirty times today's ambient level.

## The Carbamino Effect

The molecular basis for CO2's protective action runs through its affinity for amine groups. Every protein in the body, with the exception of certain cyclic peptides, contains at least one amine, and usually several. "Any amino group in your body, whether it's on your DNA, your enzymes, or the so-called hormone receptors, these all contain amino groups," Peat explained on the altitude episode. "When there's enough carbon dioxide, it will stick to those groups. In the absence of carbon dioxide, other stuff will stick to those, such as glycation. Various free radical fragments of unsaturated fats will tend to stick to those and derange the hormones. Insulin, for example, is a different hormone in the presence of CO2 versus in the absence of CO2."

This reaction, the non-enzymatic binding of CO2 to amino groups to form carbamino compounds, is reversible, fast, and directly competitive with the slow, irreversible damage caused by glycation. In [[genes-carbon-dioxide-and-adaptation|"Genes, Carbon Dioxide, and Adaptation" (2012)]], Peat wrote that amine groups "react spontaneously with carbon dioxide, to form carbamino groups, and they can also react, nonenzymically, with sugars, in the reaction called glycation." CO2 on the amine means the glycation does not happen. CO2 off the amine means the sugars, aldehydes from [[pufa|PUFA]] peroxidation, and methylglyoxal from lactate metabolism all compete for the exposed site, and their reactions do not come undone.

The implication reorganizes what "diabetic damage" actually is. "You can't suffer the side effects of diabetes, for example, if your proteins are well protected." The complications blamed on high blood sugar turn out, in this framework, to be complications of low CO2.

## The Haldane-Bohr Effect

Textbook physiology gives the Bohr effect a sentence or two. Peat treated it as one of the central facts of metabolism. When oxygen binds hemoglobin in the lungs, the conformational change releases bound CO2. In the capillaries, the reverse is true: high tissue CO2 displaces oxygen from hemoglobin, delivering it exactly where it is needed. The paradox this resolves is that breathing more does not deliver more oxygen to cells. If anything, it delivers less. Hyperventilation blows off CO2, alkalinizes the blood, and locks oxygen onto hemoglobin at the very moment the tissues are trying to extract it.

The less familiar half of Peat's argument is that the effect is not confined to hemoglobin. "A few people who have tested other proteins find that the Haldane-Bohr effect applies to proteins in general. When there's a lot of carbon dioxide, it basically changes the pH or the isoelectric point of the protein, making it less accessible to oxygen. That in itself is a protection against the attack of oxygen against proteins." Hemoglobin is doing what every protein in the body does to a lesser degree.

In the heart this translates into a counterintuitive clinical rule: raising CO2 increases tissue oxygen. "It delivers the oxygen to the heart. But more important than that is that it delivers it in the optimum way. It retracts the electrons so the oxygen doesn't stick where it shouldn't. The electrons go directly to the oxygen down the electron transport chain, and the electrons are prevented from deviating and attacking the polyunsaturated fats." The damage that antioxidants are sold to prevent is damage that CO2 prevents upstream.

## CO2 Versus Lactic Acid

Nothing is more central to Peat's CO2 framework than its inverse relationship with [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]. CO2 is what fully oxidized glucose produces. Lactate is what partially oxidized glucose produces when the mitochondrial pathway is blocked or bypassed. A cell making CO2 is producing ATP cleanly. A cell making lactate is extracting a fraction of the available energy and dumping the rest as an acidic waste product.

In *Generative Energy* episode 36 on CO2 and mineral balance, Peat framed the interaction mechanistically: "Things that support oxidation, like thyroid hormone, are acting largely through forming carbon dioxide. One of the major functions of carbon dioxide is, in proportion to its increase, it suppresses formation of lactic acid as the alternative low-energy form of metabolism." The suppression runs in both directions. "Breathing too much oxygen displaces too much carbon dioxide, provoking an increase in lactic acid," Peat wrote in [[altitude-and-mortality|"Altitude and Mortality" (2006)]]. "Too much lactate displaces both oxygen and carbon dioxide. Lactate itself tends to suppress respiration." A system tipped toward lactate does not spontaneously tip back.

Otto Warburg's observation that cancer cells produce lactate even in the presence of oxygen sits at the heart of this picture. In [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging|"Lactate vs. CO2 in wounds, sickness, and aging"]], Peat drew the broader implication: lactate is not merely a marker of cancer metabolism, it is part of the mechanism, and the same holds for rosacea, arthritis, heart disease, diabetes, and neurological disease. Lactate "increases blood viscosity, mimics stress, causes inflammation, and contributes to shock." The therapeutic inversion follows directly: anything that raises CO2 lowers lactate, and anything that lowers lactate raises CO2. [[thyroid|Thyroid]] hormone, sugar, saturated fats, altitude, and bag breathing all push the balance in the same direction. Hyperventilation, hypothyroidism, stress, and PUFA push it the opposite way, producing what Peat called a state in which "hyperventilation imitates cancer metabolism."

## Altitude, Mitochondria, and the Lactate Paradox

The altitude data was some of the most persuasive evidence Peat marshaled. Mortality from heart disease and cancer decreases for every thousand feet of elevation gained, an effect the New Mexico studies showed cleanly across populations. The mechanism runs through CO2 retention. "Carbon dioxide, produced in the cells, releases oxygen into the tissues, relaxes blood vessels, prevents edema, eliminates ammonia, and increases the efficiency of oxidative metabolism."

Russian physiologists, following Yandell Henderson's early American work, pushed the experiments further. "If an animal was kept at high altitude for a few months, when it went back to low altitude, it lived the rest of its life retaining about twice as many mitochondria as it had at lower altitude. Meaning it was basically twice as efficient." This is the point of connection to Warburg's cancer work: it is [[mitochondria|mitochondrial]] failure that defines the cancer cell. The same Russian groups "produced cancers artificially in rats. The ones that were left at low altitude, 100% of them died. The others that were taken to 17,000 feet, 50% of them spontaneously threw off the cancer."

The "lactate paradox" described by exercise physiologists resolves in the same frame. Athletes at high altitude work hard without producing the lactate spikes they generate at sea level. "It's not really a paradox. It's that the oxygen is not at a high enough concentration to displace carbon dioxide. So the carbon dioxide is taking care of the lactic acid." The name is a confession that the mainstream picture cannot accommodate the finding.

## Calcium, Vasodilation, and Inflammation

CO2 keeps [[calcium]] in its ionized, extracellular state. "The CO2, one of its actions in the bloodstream is to form compounds, not exactly calcium bicarbonate, but complex ionic compounds, keeping calcium in an ionized state," Peat explained in *Generative Energy* 36. "When it's in this ionized, water-soluble state, it isn't in a state that has an affinity for cells." When CO2 falls, calcium floods into cells where its concentration should be kept a thousandfold lower than outside. The finger and toe cramping that follows acute hyperventilation is the immediate expression. The long-term expression is the calcification of arteries and soft tissues: "Lactic acid goes into the blood vessel at an abnormal concentration along with increased calcium, and together the chelated calcium and lactate combination with phosphate tends to form permanent crystallization inside the cell."

CO2 is also a powerful vasodilator. Low CO2 constricts blood vessels, cuts cerebral blood flow, leaks water out of capillaries, and provokes platelets to release serotonin and histamine. "Carbon dioxide at the proper concentration causes the platelets to retain their histamine, serotonin, and other inflammatory substances. When you hyperventilate, your platelets leak these and make your blood vessels leak, causing edema." The same effects occur when lactate rises for any reason, because lactate and low CO2 are effectively the same signal. CO2 also combines directly with ammonia from protein metabolism, turning off one of the upstream drivers of lactate formation.

The fetal environment, rich in CO2 and low in oxygen by adult standards, is the biological reference case Peat returned to. "Carbon dioxide activates the phagocyte process of various cells in your blood. That's a part of normal repair and regeneration. The baby gestating in the uterus is completely free of germs normally, but it still has very active phagocytes. Their activity is supported in proportion to the carbon dioxide tension." Scarless healing in the early fetus, he argued, is a CO2 phenomenon. The degenerative diseases of aging are diseases of progressive drift away from that starting point.

## Thyroid-Driven CO2 as the Marker of Metabolic Health

If there is a single throughline connecting Peat's CO2 work to the rest of his framework, it is that CO2 production is downstream of [[thyroid]] function and upstream of nearly everything else. A cell running on adequate T3 burns glucose all the way down to CO2 and water. A cell running on insufficient T3 stalls at lactate. Thyroid produces CO2. CO2 is the evidence that thyroid is working.

"Keeping your CO2 up steadily, year after year, gradually prevents the structural changes so that keeping yourself in the CO2/thyroid responsive state doesn't get harder with aging," Peat said. "If you slip back periodically, going out of the carbon dioxide state tends to allow collagen to increase, and lipofuscin and other esters of cholesterol with polyunsaturated fats (PUFA) increase as harmful baggage that builds up with age." The link to [[hypothyroidism]] appears in Peat's email correspondence as a routine diagnostic hint: CO2 and bicarbonate are "usually low with hypothyroidism, and CO2 is what regulates calcium."

Fuel source matters. "When you burn glucose, you get much more carbon dioxide per unit of oxygen used. So at high altitude, a person uses their oxygen more efficiently and they're more reluctant to burn their stored fats. Anything you do to increase the carbon dioxide will tend to protect you against mobilizing your fats and creating that diabetes-like state." This is the CO2 side of the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]: sugar oxidation yields more CO2 per oxygen than fat oxidation. [[coconut-oil|Coconut oil]] occupies its own place in this picture because its medium-chain fatty acids are oxidized faster and more completely than long-chain PUFA, and more complete oxidation produces more CO2. Sugar, thyroid, [[progesterone]], and coconut oil all belong to the set of interventions whose ultimate effect is to raise cellular CO2.

The connection to [[estrogen]] runs through the same channel. "Thyroid helps to lower the estrogen level. Estrogen and many of the related factors tend to cause you to hyperventilate, even to the point of having alkalosis." The characteristic lower CO2 tolerance of women compared to men traces to estrogen's respiratory drive, and that drive is one mechanism by which estrogen feeds the chronic [[aromatase]] cascade.

## Bag Breathing and External CO2

Peat's therapeutic recommendations followed directly from the physiology. If most people lose too much CO2 through subclinical hyperventilation, the simplest intervention is to breathe it back in. Hence [[bag-breathing|bag breathing]]. "I've seen people with high blood pressure bring their blood pressure down 30 points in a day or two just by bag breathing repeatedly." The protocol is minimal: breathe in and out of a sealed paper or plastic bag until it becomes uncomfortable, usually after a minute or two, then rest, then repeat two or three times during the day.

The adaptation is cumulative. "Each time you increase the CO2 to an uncomfortable level, it's suppressing the lactate a little bit and lowering the adrenaline, free fatty acids, and many things that cause you to hyperventilate. So you're basically curing your tendency to breathe too much by adapting to repeated exposures to extra CO2." In his emails, Peat suggested checking blood pressure and looking for pinkness under the nail beds as the easiest markers of a cumulative effect. Asked whether bag breathing could fully substitute for living at high altitude, he answered in one word: "No." The bag is a patch, not a cure, but it is extraordinarily safe and accessible.

External application works too. CO2 is absorbed through the skin against a concentration gradient because the gas is more soluble in tissue than in the surrounding air. A giant plastic bag filled with CO2 from a tank, stepped into at room temperature, produces immediate warmth and skin flushing. Carbonated springs operate on the same principle. CO2 therapy was standard medical practice into the mid-twentieth century: Yandell Henderson's 1940 *Cyclopedia of Medicine* entry documented its use for anesthesia recovery, shock prevention, newborn resuscitation, and heart disease, and Henderson assumed it would become as common as oxygen therapy. As Peat observed in "Genes, Carbon Dioxide, and Adaptation," "The fact that carbon dioxide therapy is extremely safe has led to the official doctrine that it can't be effective." The treatment quietly disappeared from practice, except in the ventilator literature where the rediscovery of "permissive hypercapnia" as protection against oxygen damage during mechanical ventilation represented a small return to the original principle.

## CO2 and the Redox State

A subtler dimension of Peat's CO2 work concerns its role in tuning the NAD+/NADH ratio. "The essential electron-moving cofactor, NAD and NADH, in the presence of carbon dioxide, is more oxidized, meaning that the oxygen is doing its work better in the presence of CO2," Peat explained. Remove CO2 and the pathway sticks. Electrons back up. NADH accumulates relative to NAD. Electrons escape the respiratory chain and attack polyunsaturated fats and genetic material.

In Gilbert Ling's language, which Peat adopted in *Generative Energy* 26, CO2 acts as a "cardinal adsorbent" that pulls electrons out of the protein matrix, keeping the cytoplasm in its structured, energized state. "The retraction of the electrons shifts the whole electronic state of the cell, making electrons scarcer. That ends up shifting the ratio between NAD and NADH towards the oxidized NAD side." The anti-swelling effect Peat repeatedly described, in which CO2 lets cells release excess water, follows from the same mechanism. Peat speculated that the positioning of carbonic anhydrase inside mitochondria next to ATP synthesis is not incidental: "I think its purpose there is partly to turn the newly formed carbon dioxide into carbonic acid, which then leaves, taking away water and creating ATP by dehydrating the phosphates." If the reading is correct, CO2 generation is not a side effect of ATP synthesis. It is one of the forces driving it.

## The Late-Career Shift

Danny Roddy observed on *Generative Energy* 26 that CO2 became a more central theme in Peat's work in the early 2000s than it had been earlier. Peat dated the shift to the late 1990s and credited Buteyko. "I had read some of Buteyko's articles in 1969 or so, but I didn't really think very much of it except it seemed valid." What changed was not the physiology but the emphasis. CO2 had always been in Peat's framework as a consequence of oxidative metabolism. The shift was to treat it as a therapeutic lever in its own right, worth targeting directly through bag breathing, altitude, carbonated baths, and the dietary patterns that maximize glucose oxidation.

Thyroid, sugar, saturated fats, progesterone, and [[sleep|restful sleep]] all produce CO2 as a downstream consequence. "In restful sleep, the oxygen tension is frequently low enough, and the carbon dioxide tension high enough, to trigger the multiplication of stem cells and mitochondria." The deep sleep that feels repairing is a nightly return toward the high-CO2 state of the fetus. What Peat added in his later work was the recognition that the CO2 is not merely an indicator of the other things working; it is one of the things that makes the other things work. The protection runs through the gas.

Proteins work correctly in CO2's presence. Calcium sits where it belongs. Blood vessels dilate appropriately. Mitochondria multiply under its influence. Phagocytes clean up damaged tissue in proportion to its concentration. Oxygen reaches the cells that need it and does not attack the lipids it should not touch. Lactate stays low. Hormones bind where they should and do not glycate where they should not. Remove CO2 from any of these systems and they start producing the pathologies that modern medicine catalogs as separate diseases. Restore it, and the pathologies retreat together. "The same simple metabolic therapies, such as thyroid, progesterone, magnesium, and carbon dioxide, are appropriate for a great range of seemingly different diseases."
