# Sodium

Few nutritional recommendations have caused more harm than the universal advice to restrict salt. In Ray Peat's framework, sodium is not a toxin to be minimized but a metabolic essential that supports [[thyroid]] function, opposes [[stress]] hormones, and helps maintain the blood volume and tissue hydration that efficient [[metabolism]] requires. The people with the highest blood pressure, researcher David McCarron found, ate the *least* salt—a finding that inverts the conventional wisdom entirely.

## The Aldosterone Problem

Salt restriction activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system—the body's emergency response to perceived sodium deficiency. Aldosterone, a mineralocorticoid hormone, forces the kidneys to retain sodium at the cost of excreting [[potassium]] and [[magnesium]]. It also promotes [[inflammation]], water retention, and the very hypertension that salt restriction was supposed to prevent.

The irony is physiologically precise. When salt intake drops, the body compensates by raising aldosterone. Aldosterone retains sodium by increasing blood pressure. The hypertension attributed to salt is actually caused by the hormonal response to salt restriction. As Peat explained, people with "inflammatory high blood pressure symptoms" often improve with more salt, not less.

The [[calcium]]-[[parathyroid hormone]] connection compounds the problem. Calcium restriction raises PTH, which increases aldosterone production independent of sodium intake. The standard low-salt, low-fat diet—often also low in calcium—activates multiple hormonal systems that raise blood pressure while the patient is congratulated for following medical advice.

## Thyroid and Sodium Retention

[[Hypothyroidism]] causes sodium wasting. When thyroid function is low, the kidneys excrete sodium excessively, reducing blood volume and impairing tissue perfusion. As Peat described, "Low thyroid women lose sodium easily. Thyroid causes sodium retention, which binds water and keeps blood volume up, perfusing kidneys properly so they stop signaling to raise blood pressure."

The mechanism creates a counterintuitive intervention: adequate salt intake can lower blood pressure by restoring blood volume, allowing the kidneys to stop activating the stress hormones that were compensating for hypovolemia. The edematous hypothyroid patient often needs more sodium, not less—combined with thyroid support to address the underlying cause.

## Sodium and Cellular Energy

Sodium has direct effects on mitochondrial energy production. As Peat explained in discussions of blood pressure and metabolism, salt helps optimize sugar metabolism and energizes the kidneys. Both sodium chloride (table salt) and sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) function as mild diuretics by supporting cellular energy rather than suppressing it.

The contrast with pharmaceutical diuretics is stark. Loop diuretics and thiazides force fluid loss by poisoning kidney function, depleting [[magnesium]] and potassium in the process. Sodium, when combined with adequate thyroid function, supports the kidney's natural ability to regulate fluid balance—addressing edema by restoring energy rather than by impairing function.

For people whose gut is irritated by chloride, Peat recommended baking soda in water as a gentler sodium source. The bicarbonate also provides the CO2-related benefits of supporting oxidative metabolism and opposing the lactic acid accumulation that characterizes metabolic stress.

## Blood Pressure: The Real Mechanism

The conventional story—sodium raises blood pressure through fluid retention—ignores the hormonal reality. Blood pressure regulation involves the interplay of aldosterone, [[adrenaline]], [[cortisol]], PTH, and thyroid status. Sodium intake is one variable among many, and restricting it activates compensatory mechanisms that often make hypertension worse.

Peat's practical recommendation was simple: salt food to taste. The body's sodium appetite, when not distorted by chronic restriction, accurately reflects physiological need. Craving salt is not a moral failing to be overcome but a signal to be respected—particularly in hypothyroid individuals whose sodium wasting creates genuine deficiency.

Combined with adequate [[calcium]] (to suppress PTH), [[magnesium]] (depleted by aldosterone), and thyroid support (to restore normal sodium handling), liberal salt intake supports rather than undermines cardiovascular health.
