# Iodine

Iodine is the substrate the [[thyroid]] gland pulls out of the bloodstream to build the hormone that runs oxidative metabolism. Four iodine atoms become thyroxine, three become T3, and the whole cellular energy apparatus rides on those atoms being present in the right quantity at the right moment. This is the simple biochemical fact that has generated more overreach, more supplementation enthusiasm, and more quiet thyroid damage than almost any other nutrient in the alternative health world. Ray Peat's position on iodine was unusual among thyroid-focused writers of his era in that he was not enthusiastic about it. He recognized its necessity, recommended a handful of ordinary food sources, and spent the rest of his correspondence warning people away from the milligram-range doses that the "Iodine Project" crowd was advocating. "Lugol's solution is sometimes helpful for an inflammation, but it's risky when there might be a thyroid problem," he wrote in a direct email reply on the topic. "Short term use of iodide is safe at a few milligrams per day, but chronic intake of even one mg per day increases the risk of thyroiditis." The caution is the whole article in one paragraph. The rest is the reasoning.

## The Substrate, Not the Solution

Iodine deficiency is real, it produces a recognizable disease, and the disease has a name that most people still associate vaguely with some other era. Goiter, the visible swelling of the thyroid gland as it strains to capture iodine from an inadequate blood supply, was endemic across much of the American interior in the early twentieth century. The landlocked states of the Great Lakes region became known as "the goiter belt" precisely because the soils there were leached of iodine and the diets rarely included enough seafood to compensate. The introduction of iodized salt in 1924 solved the problem at the level of public health, and Peat considered it solved. Writing in his November 1993 *Townsend Letter* essay [["Thyroid: Misconceptions"|Thyroid: Misconceptions]], he put it in stark terms: "Iodine deficiency used to cause goiters in the United States, but now it is almost impossible to have an iodine deficiency in this country. Iodine supplements can suppress the formation of thyroid hormone, producing classical signs of hypothyroidism."

Both halves of that sentence matter, and they matter together. The first half closes the door on the primary motive for supplementation: if you live in the United States, eat a normal mixed diet, and put iodized salt on your food, you are not iodine deficient. The second half opens a different door, one most iodine enthusiasts did not want to walk through. Supplementing past the point of adequacy does not produce better thyroid function. It produces worse thyroid function, indistinguishable in its clinical signs from the deficiency the supplement was meant to correct.

The mechanism behind this second effect is old, well-documented, and named for the men who described it. The Wolff-Chaikoff effect refers to the acute suppression of thyroid hormone synthesis that occurs when iodide concentrations in the gland rise above a certain threshold. A healthy thyroid confronted with an iodine flood temporarily shuts down its own organification machinery, the coupling of iodide to tyrosine residues that produces thyroxine and T3, as a defensive reflex against runaway hormone production. In a normal gland the suppression is transient. In a gland that is already inflamed, already attacked by antibodies, or already operating near the edge of its capacity, the defensive shutdown can become permanent. The iodine that was supposed to rescue the thyroid instead finishes what autoimmune disease had started.

Peat saw this happen often enough in his correspondence that the cautionary version of the message became a fixture. The risk scaled with duration and dose. "A few milligrams per day" for a short course could be therapeutic for an inflammatory condition; "chronic intake of even one mg per day" tipped the balance toward [[hashimotos|thyroiditis]]. The arithmetic here is worth pausing on. The recommended daily allowance for iodine in the United States is 150 micrograms. The megadose protocols popularized by David Brownstein and Guy Abraham in the 2000s were recommending 12.5 milligrams, fifty milligrams, and sometimes a hundred milligrams per day, amounts several hundred to a thousand times the RDA. Peat's upper limit for chronic intake was under 1,000 micrograms, a floor the Brownstein protocols cleared on the first pill.

## The Goiter Belt and Its Complications

The American goiter belt disappeared after iodization, but the international picture is less tidy. Peat cited with interest an Austrian study, [["Endemic goiter in Austria. Is iodine deficiency the primary cause of goiter?"|Grubeck-Loebenstein et al. (1982)]], that investigated goiter in a population where iodized salt had been mandatory for decades. The researchers found that about half the patients with ongoing goiter were still excreting inadequate iodine, but the other half were not. In the iodine-sufficient half, "other factors enhancing goiter development seem to assume particular importance," the authors concluded. The phrase is careful medical language for an unsettled question, and Peat used the study to make a point he returned to repeatedly: goiter is a symptom, not a diagnosis. A swelling thyroid can mean iodine deficiency, or it can mean the gland is straining to do its job under the influence of estrogen, stress, or goitrogens in the food supply. Reaching for iodine as the default treatment will rescue half the cases and aggravate the other half.

The goitrogens Peat worried about most were not the crucifers that health magazines traditionally warn against. They were the soy isoflavones. A Japanese study he cited repeatedly, [["The effects on the thyroid gland of soybeans administered experimentally in healthy subjects"|Ishizuki et al. (1991)]], gave thirty grams of soybeans a day to thirty-seven healthy adults with no history of thyroid disease. After three months, half the subjects in the older age groups developed hypometabolic symptoms, and goiters appeared in the same subjects. TSH rose significantly. Inorganic iodide levels fell during the soybean administration, and the goiters receded a month after the soy was withdrawn. The takeaway Peat drew was that modern goiter has as much to do with estrogenic food compounds as with iodine itself. Adding iodine to a soy-eating population treats the symptom while leaving the cause in place.

This is where Peat's framing diverged most sharply from the iodine enthusiasts. They saw goiter as prima facie evidence of deficiency and treated it with megadoses. Peat saw goiter as evidence of a gland under stress from multiple directions, only one of which was iodine supply, and he treated the other directions with equal seriousness. The [[estrogen]] pressure on the thyroid, the [[PUFA]] interference with thyroid hormone transport, the stress-induced cortisol dominance that suppresses T4-to-T3 conversion in the liver, these were the things that turned a marginally iodine-sufficient person into a patient with a visible goiter. Flooding the system with iodide did nothing about any of them.

## Seafood as the Sane Delivery System

Peat's actual dietary recommendation for iodine was simple enough that it never made it into the headlines of the supplement wars. In an email reply filed under [["diet-general"|Diet General]], he wrote: "Seafood, especially oysters, shrimp, squid, etc., would provide the iodine and selenium you need for good thyroid regulation." The pairing with selenium is not incidental. Selenium is the cofactor for the deiodinase enzymes that convert T4 to active T3 and, separately, for the glutathione peroxidase that protects the thyroid gland from the oxidative byproducts of its own hormone synthesis. Iodine without selenium is an incomplete nutrient. Shellfish provides both in their natural proportions, along with the copper and zinc that round out the thyroid's trace-element requirements.

The emphasis on marine animals over seaweed was deliberate. Kelp, dulse, and nori are marketed as iodine superfoods, and in terms of raw iodine content they are: a single gram of some kelp preparations can deliver ten or twenty times the RDA. This is precisely the problem. Seaweed is a convenient way to land a milligram-range dose in a single serving, and Peat treated it with the same suspicion he applied to Lugol's drops. The ocean concentrates iodine; seaweed further concentrates what the ocean already contains; and the resulting delivery system makes it almost impossible to stay inside the therapeutic window that Peat considered safe for chronic use. Oysters, by contrast, deliver iodine in milligram fractions rather than milligram multiples. A half dozen oysters a week meets the thyroid's needs without risking the Wolff-Chaikoff shutdown.

Iodine also arrives in the American diet through channels most people do not notice. Dairy processing uses iodine-containing sanitizers that leave residues in milk. Commercial bread once used iodate dough conditioners, a practice Peat noted in his article [["Thyroid Therapies, Confusion and Fraud"|Thyroid Therapies, Confusion and Fraud]], where he described how the practice had saturated the thyroids of bread-eating Americans to the point that radioactive iodine uptake tests sometimes failed because the gland could not absorb any more. "If a person had been eating iodine-rich food (and iodine was used in bread as a preservative / dough conditioner, and was present in other foods as an accidental contaminant), they would already be over-saturated with iodine, and the gland would fail to concentrate the iodine." The observation undermines the premise of most iodine supplementation. The typical American was not deficient. The typical American's gland was already holding all the iodine it could use and was about to be asked to hold more.

## The Protein-Bound Iodine Scandal

Peat's longest engagement with iodine was not about dosing at all. It was about what happens when a whole branch of medicine mistakes a blood measurement for a disease. The full account lives in [[hypothyroidism]], which owns the diagnostic scandal, but the iodine thread is central enough to mention here. In the late 1940s, the medical profession replaced basal metabolic rate as the primary diagnostic test for hypothyroidism with a new measurement called protein-bound iodine, or PBI. The test measured the iodine in the patient's blood, on the assumption that blood iodine was a proxy for thyroxine and that thyroxine was a proxy for thyroid function. Each link in that chain of assumptions was false, but the test was cheap, reproducible, and quantitative, and American endocrinology adopted it with enthusiasm. The patient population identified as hypothyroid collapsed from roughly forty percent under the old BMR criteria to about five percent under PBI. The other thirty-five percent were reclassified as suffering from "sloth and gluttony" or "genetic susceptibility."

The downstream damage was substantial, and some of it landed directly on the pharmacy shelf. Peat described in [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud]] how the PBI fixation corrupted the supply of desiccated thyroid itself. "The practice of measuring serum iodine, and equating it with 'thyroxine the thyroid hormone,' led to the practice of examining only the iodine content of the putative glandular material that was offered for sale as thyroid USP. This led to the substitution of materials such as iodinated casein for desiccated thyroid in the products sold as thyroid USP. The US FDA refused to take action, because they held that a material's iodine content was enough to identify it as 'thyroid USP.'" Iodinated casein is a milk protein with iodine atoms chemically attached to its tyrosine residues. It is not thyroid hormone. It passes through the body without producing the metabolic effects of real thyroid, and patients who had been stable on true desiccated thyroid began reporting that their "new" medication was not working. The FDA did nothing because the iodine content, the only thing being measured, was correct.

This is the inversion Peat wanted people to grasp. Iodine is a component of the thyroid hormone molecule, but iodine in a bottle is not thyroid function, and iodine in the blood is not a diagnosis. The substance is the raw material; the gland is what matters.

## Hashimoto's, Thyroiditis, and the Question of Cause

The most consequential of Peat's iodine warnings concerned autoimmune thyroiditis. The Wolff-Chaikoff effect describes iodine's acute suppression of hormone synthesis, but the chronic effect of high-dose iodine in susceptible people is something worse. Excess iodine can inflame the gland directly and provoke the antibody response that characterizes Hashimoto's disease. Peat was blunt about what this meant for supplementation protocols. "Chronic intake of even one mg per day increases the risk of thyroiditis." Not the severity. The risk itself. People who had no measurable antibodies before starting high-dose iodine could develop them after. People with borderline Hashimoto's could see their antibody titers climb. The supplement was not rescuing an autoimmune process; it was feeding one.

Peat's broader view of Hashimoto's, laid out in [["Thyroid: Misconceptions"|Thyroid: Misconceptions]], treated it as a downstream consequence of estrogen dominance and unopposed cortisol rather than as a primary autoimmune disease. "Thyroid cells contain estrogen 'receptors,' and estrogen inhibits thyroid secretion, and excess estrogen is involved in most 'autoimmune' disease. Supplemental thyroid so easily normalizes estrogen and the immune system that it, with progesterone and pregnenolone and DHEA, should be considered as the cure for autoimmune diseases, including thyroiditis, at least until someone finds an 'autoimmune' disease that doesn't respond to a balancing of these hormones." The treatment program for Hashimoto's, in other words, was to oppose estrogen, support the steroid pipeline, and provide the thyroid hormone the gland was struggling to make. Iodine was not on the list. It was, if anything, on the list of things that might have helped cause the problem in the first place.

## Iodolipids and the Autoregulation Story

One reason iodine enthusiasts could point to biochemistry in their defense is that the thyroid does synthesize interesting iodine-containing signaling molecules beyond T3 and T4. Peat cited the work of Dugrillon and colleagues on [[iodolactones-iodoaldehydes|iodolipid mediators of thyroid autoregulation]], which identified compounds like alpha-iodohexadecanal that are formed when thyroid cells iodinate polyunsaturated fatty acids. The iodolipids appear to participate in the gland's autoregulation, damping hormone synthesis when iodine is abundant. This is the molecular level of the Wolff-Chaikoff effect.

The detail Peat found most worth underlining was what the thyroid was iodinating. Polyunsaturated fatty acids. The reason the gland can form these iodolipid mediators at all is that its membranes contain enough [[PUFA]] to be available as substrates. In an organism whose diet is low in PUFA and high in saturated fats, the thyroid's iodolipid chemistry looks different, and by Peat's reading, probably works better. This is the sort of observation that makes Peat's iodine writing harder to map onto the supplementation debate. The mainstream asks whether you need more iodine or less. Peat asked what kind of fats the iodine is combining with inside the gland.

## Practical Guidelines

Peat's practical position on iodine can be compressed to a handful of instructions. Eat seafood once or twice a week, preferably shellfish, for iodine with selenium in natural proportions. Use iodized salt; it is adequate for the purpose it was designed to serve. Avoid kelp, dulse, and seaweed products as daily foods. Do not take Lugol's solution or iodine pills on any ongoing basis unless a specific, time-limited therapeutic reason exists. If a goiter is present, investigate estrogen, soy, PUFA, and stress before reaching for iodine. If Hashimoto's is present, treat the hormonal and inflammatory drivers and supplement thyroid directly rather than pushing more iodine into an already inflamed gland.

The quiet dietary target is about 150 to 200 micrograms per day, the amount that iodized salt and a mixed diet already deliver. "Short term use of iodide is safe at a few milligrams per day," Peat allowed, reserving higher doses for acute situations like an inflammation that happened to respond to topical or oral iodide, "but chronic intake of even one mg per day increases the risk of thyroiditis." The gap between 200 micrograms and 1,000 micrograms is the window in which Peat thought people were safe. The megadose protocols live several orders of magnitude above that window, in territory where the evidence for harm outruns the evidence for benefit.

Iodine was used historically in atherosclerosis, and Peat acknowledged in a brief [["Iodine Injections"|Townsend Letter exchange from October 1986]] that the practice had a real pharmacology behind it. That same history is worth remembering because it frames iodine correctly. Iodine is a drug with a narrow therapeutic window. It is not a vitamin in the sense that [[vitamin-a|vitamin A]] is a vitamin, where deficiency is the main problem and excess is rare. Iodine deficiency is rare, excess is common in the alternative health world, and the damage from excess is the damage Peat spent his limited patience trying to prevent.
