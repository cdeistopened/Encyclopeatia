# Iodine

Iodine occupies a paradoxical position in Ray Peat's framework—essential for [[thyroid]] hormone synthesis yet potentially dangerous when supplemented in excess. The enthusiasm for high-dose iodine supplementation in alternative health circles runs counter to Peat's caution about chronic intake above a few milligrams, which increases the risk of thyroiditis and autoimmune thyroid problems.

As Peat wrote in email exchanges, "Short term use of iodide is safe at a few milligrams per day, but chronic intake of even one mg. per day increases the risk of thyroiditis."

## The Thyroid Connection

The thyroid gland requires iodine to synthesize T4 and [[T3]]—the hormones that regulate oxidative [[metabolism]] throughout the body. Iodine deficiency causes goiter (thyroid enlargement) and [[hypothyroidism]]. This basic physiology drives the addition of iodine to salt and the medical recommendation for adequate iodine intake.

But the relationship is not linear. More iodine does not mean more thyroid hormone or better thyroid function. Above certain thresholds, iodine can trigger inflammation in thyroid tissue, particularly in people with existing thyroid vulnerabilities or autoimmune tendencies.

## Cautious Supplementation

Peat's position on iodine supplementation was notably conservative compared to practitioners who recommend milligram doses daily. His concern centered on the inflammatory potential of excess iodine and its ability to trigger autoimmune responses.

"Lugol's solution is sometimes helpful for an inflammation, but it's risky when there might be a thyroid problem," he wrote. The therapeutic window is narrow—iodine can help acute situations but chronic high-dose use creates its own problems.

## Dietary Sources

Rather than supplements, Peat recommended obtaining iodine from food—particularly seafood. In email exchanges about diet, he noted: "Seafood, especially oysters, shrimp, squid, etc., would provide the iodine and [[selenium]] you need for good thyroid regulation."

The food-based approach provides iodine in smaller, more physiological amounts alongside the other nutrients (particularly selenium) that support healthy thyroid function. Selenium is required for the conversion of T4 to T3 and helps protect the thyroid from oxidative damage that iodine excess might cause.

## Context Matters

Iodine requirements depend on overall metabolic context. Someone with healthy thyroid function eating adequate seafood likely needs no supplementation. Someone with goiter from severe iodine deficiency might benefit from modest supplementation. Someone taking large doses based on alternative health recommendations may be creating the thyroid inflammation they're trying to prevent.

The pattern reflects Peat's broader principle: nutrients work in context, and isolated high-dose supplementation often backfires. Thyroid health depends on the interplay of iodine, selenium, vitamin A, [[copper]], and the absence of thyroid-suppressing factors like [[PUFA]] and excess [[estrogen]]—not on maximizing any single nutrient.
