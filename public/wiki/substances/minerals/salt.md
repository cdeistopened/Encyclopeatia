# Salt

Mainstream medicine tells you to cut salt. Ray Peat said the opposite, and for half a century he argued that salt restriction was one of the most destructive dietary interventions ever promoted. Where public health authorities treat sodium as a driver of [[hypertension]], heart disease, and stroke, Peat treated it as thermogenic, protective, and essential to the same oxidative machinery that also depends on [[thyroid]] and [[progesterone]]. "Protein, salt, thyroid, and progesterone happen to be thermogenic, increasing heat production and stabilizing body temperature at a higher level," he wrote in his newsletter [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity"]]. "Prolactin and estrogen lower the temperature set-point." The implication is that a low-salt diet pushes physiology in the same direction as [[estrogen]] excess and [[hypothyroidism]]: toward reduced energy production, increased stress hormones, and the degenerative cascade that Peat spent his career mapping. Salt was not a hazard to be minimized but a substrate for metabolism, a stabilizer of blood volume, and, in Peat's clinical experience, one of the most reliable interventions for [[sleep]], edema, and the symptoms of aging.

## How a Cliché Became Policy

In the 1950s, the pharmaceutical industry introduced a new class of diuretics to replace the old mercury compounds, marketing them for [[hypertension]], heart failure, pregnancy edema, and obesity. Because these drugs worked by flushing sodium through the kidneys, their promotion came packaged with a simple instruction: restrict dietary salt to make the drug more effective. Walter Kempner's low-salt rice diet, published around the same time, gave the campaign scientific cover. "Dietary salt restriction has become a cultural cliché, largely as a consequence of the belief that sodium causes edema and hypertension," Peat wrote in [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|his core article on salt]]. Salt restriction combined with salt-losing diuresis was particularly harmful during pregnancy, he noted, "but that combination became standard medical practice for many years, damaging millions of babies."

The evidence, when Peat examined it, ran the other way. A review of roughly 100 studies by Alderman in 2004 found that salt restriction lowers blood pressure "a few points," but that the reduction did not correlate with better health. In one 3,000-person study over four years, people who ate less salt had higher mortality. An extra few grams of salt per day was associated with a 36 percent reduction in coronary events (Alderman, 1995). A larger 22-year study of more than 11,000 people again showed an inverse relationship between salt intake and mortality (Alderman, 1997). The foundation of the low-salt consensus was thinner than the consensus admitted.

## Blood Volume and the Albumin Trick

Peat's positive case for salt begins with blood volume. Sodium ions attach to the negative charges on serum albumin, and the complex attracts water, exerting what physiologists call oncotic pressure. When sodium is adequate, this pressure pulls excess extracellular fluid back into circulation, maintaining blood volume while preventing edema. When sodium is low, the albumin itself easily leaves the bloodstream along with the water, and the system collapses in both directions at once: reduced blood volume in the vessels, swelling in the tissues.

Tom Brewer, an obstetrician who devoted his career to prenatal nutrition, was one of Peat's primary sources on this mechanism. Brewer argued that adequate protein, calories, and salt were essential to preventing [[preeclampsia]], and he was largely responsible for the gradual abandonment of the low-salt-plus-diuretic treatment for pregnant women. In [[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|"When Energy Fails"]], Peat followed Brewer's logic to its conclusion: "The pregnant woman's blood volume expands, to permit the supply of energy to match the needs of the embryo. If the blood volume doesn't increase, or if it decreases, as in pregnancy toxemia, her blood pressure will increase." The blood pressure of preeclampsia is not an independent pathology. It is a consequence of failing blood volume, which is itself a consequence of inadequate sodium.

The same pattern holds outside pregnancy. "Plasma volume is usually lower in patients with essential hypertension than in normal subjects," Peat noted, citing Tarazi (1976). Essential hypertension shares the underlying machinery of preeclampsia: reduced blood volume, edema, and elevated [[aldosterone]]. Salt restriction worsens every step of that cascade.

## The Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone Trap

When sodium intake drops, the body does not simply tolerate the loss. It activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), a stress cascade Peat identified as central to "hypertension, kidney disease, heart failure, fibrosis of the heart, and other problems." The kidneys, sensing reduced blood flow, release renin. Renin triggers angiotensin production. Angiotensin raises blood pressure and stimulates the adrenal glands to produce aldosterone, which tells the kidneys to retain sodium at the expense of potassium. Salt restriction provokes exactly the hormonal response it was supposed to treat.

Aldosterone is particularly damaging. It drives the production of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), also known as vascular permeability factor, which makes capillaries leaky and promotes new blood vessel growth. "A restriction of salt in the diet causes more aldosterone to be produced, and increased salt in the diet causes aldosterone to decrease," Peat wrote. Beyond its vascular effects, aldosterone inhibits energy metabolism, increases insulin resistance, and promotes proinflammatory substances in fat cells. Its secretion rises during the night and is exaggerated in depressed and stressed people. "During aging, salt restriction can produce an exaggerated nocturnal rise in aldosterone," Peat noted in [[water-swelling-tension-pain-fatigue-aging|"Water, Swelling, Tension, Pain, Fatigue, Aging"]], connecting low-salt diets directly to the nocturnal catabolic state that accelerates bone loss.

Progesterone reinforces salt's protective circuit. Hans Selye demonstrated that progesterone normalizes sodium retention even in the absence of aldosterone, after the adrenal glands have been removed. "The synthetic antialdosterone drugs are simply poor imitations of progesterone," Peat wrote. It is estrogen, not progesterone, that is responsible for dangerous sodium loss.

## Stress Hormones and the Adrenaline Connection

One of Peat's most clinically actionable observations about salt is that restriction increases adrenaline. The sympathetic nervous system, already overactive in aging, becomes more so when sodium is low. Chronically elevated adrenaline contributes to capillary leakage, insulin resistance, vascular stiffness, and degenerative changes in the brain (Griffith and Sutin, 1996).

This explains salt's most reliable subjective effect: its ability to restore sleep. "The increase of adrenalin caused by salt restriction has many harmful effects, including insomnia," Peat wrote. "Many old people have noticed that a low sodium diet disturbs their sleep, and that eating their usual amount of salt restores their ability to sleep." In [[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|"When Energy Fails"]], he described the clinical pattern that became one of his most repeated practical recommendations: "I mentioned this to several people with sleep problems, and regardless of age, their sleep improved when they ate as much salt as they wanted. One study showed that most old people on a low sodium diet suffered from insomnia, and had unusually high adrenalin. When they ate a normal amount of salt, their adrenalin was normalized, and they slept better."

The bedtime pinch of salt, occasionally recommended in Peat-influenced circles, is the practical expression of this physiology. If the nocturnal rise in adrenaline, [[cortisol]], and aldosterone depends in part on sodium deficiency, adding salt before bed addresses the upstream cause of broken sleep rather than medicating the symptoms.

Salt also lowers [[serotonin]]. "When sodium intake is restricted, there is a sharp increase in serotonin secretion," Peat wrote in [[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|"Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging"]]. "Increasing sodium lowers both serotonin and adrenalin." The triad of adrenaline, cortisol, and serotonin, three of the hormones Peat most consistently identified as drivers of aging, all respond to sodium status.

## The Thermogenic Function

Salt's deepest role in Peat's framework is as a driver of cellular energy production. The continuous inward movement of sodium ions into resting cells activates sodium-potassium ATPase, the enzyme that breaks down ATP into ADP and phosphate. This breakdown signals the mitochondria to consume fuel and oxygen to regenerate ATP. More sodium means more energy consumption and more carbon dioxide production. Salt is a throttle on oxidative metabolism.

The experimental evidence is direct. When mice are chilled, they spontaneously prefer slightly salty water and it increases their heat production (Dejima, 1996). When rats are given 0.9 percent sodium chloride solution with their food, their heat production increases and their body fat decreases (Bryant, 1984). These regulatory effects depend on brain centers sensitive to the ratio between sodium and [[calcium]]. Decreasing sodium, or increasing calcium, shifts metabolism away from thermogenesis toward storage.

Peat summarized the integration in [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity"]]: "A lack of sodium slows metabolism, lowers carbon dioxide production, and creates inflammation, stress and degeneration. Rephrasing it, sodium stimulates energy metabolism, increases carbon dioxide production, and protects against inflammation and other maladaptive stress reactions."

[[Cortisol]] opposes this circuit. It blocks the thermogenic effects of sodium while increasing intracellular calcium, a combination that damages cells and accelerates degeneration. A low-sodium diet "accelerates the decrease in heat production that normally occurs with aging, lowering the metabolic rate of brown fat and body temperature, and increasing the fat content of the body" (Xavier, 2003). Aging and salt restriction act on the same machinery in the same direction.

## Hypotonic Body Fluids, Estrogen, and Prolactin

When estrogen is high, the osmolarity of blood plasma drops (Courtar 2007; Stachenfeld 1999). Peat returned to this point repeatedly because it explains a phenomenon conventional physiology cannot: women who carefully restrict salt still develop cyclic edema. Estrogen causes water retention independently of sodium. The body fluid becomes hypo-osmotic. Water, no longer held in the bloodstream by albumin and its associated sodium, enters the cells and causes them to swell.

Cell swelling is a more dangerous form of edema than the extracellular kind. It excites cells and can kill them if they cannot produce enough energy to restore their normal volume. It is the mechanism of brain injury and stroke, of traumatic tissue damage, and of any condition in which cells cannot meet their energy needs. In [[the-edema-of-stress|"The Edema of Stress"]], Peat described how he first explained this to women suffering from cyclic edema: "Seeing that salt restriction never helped women with cyclic edema, I explained the mechanisms of water regulation, first to just one woman. Within a month, she was salting her food to taste, and having no trouble with edema. Since then (1978) I have explained this to many women, and it always works for estrogen-induced cyclic edema, and also for the edema of pregnancy induced by excess estrogen."

[[Hypothyroidism]] produces the same picture through a different door. Hypothyroid people typically retain water while losing salt, because hypothyroidism raises the estrogen-to-progesterone ratio and elevates [[prolactin]]. In [[sharks-salmon-and-osmotic-therapies|"Sharks, Salmon & Osmotic Therapies"]], Peat drew an analogy to the salmon returning to freshwater, swelling as its cells lose the capacity to maintain their normal osmotic state. "The hypothyroid state is analogous to the salmon that has returned to the river." High prolactin and low thyroid conspire to make the body's fluids too dilute, and the cells too weak to resist swelling.

The practical consequence is that salt restriction, often prescribed to women precisely because they retain water premenstrually, makes the problem worse. Their salt craving is a physiological signal pointing at the correct intervention. "Premenstrual salt cravings are the result of the estrogen-disturbed water balance, and salt-restriction for PMS is as inappropriate as it is for pre-eclampsia or toxemia of pregnancy," Peat wrote. The craving is diagnostic.

## Hypertonic Sodium as Therapy

The therapeutic use of hypertonic saline, solutions containing two to ten times the normal sodium concentration, illustrates salt's pharmacological rather than nutritional role. "Hypertonic solutions are being used to resuscitate people and animals after injury," Peat wrote. "Rather than just increasing blood volume to restore circulation, the hypertonic sodium restores cellular energy production, increasing oxygen consumption and heat production while reducing free radical production, improves the contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, and reduces inflammation, vascular permeability, and edema."

Seawater, which is hypertonic to human tissues, has historically been used to treat wounds, and Mangete (1993) found that even more concentrated salt solutions accelerate wound healing. Hypertonic saline was used as a vaginal douche for salpingitis, gonorrhea, and cervical erosions, and as an abdominal irrigation in acute infections. [[sharks-salmon-and-osmotic-therapies|"Sharks, Salmon & Osmotic Therapies"]] also recorded Peat's observation that in the 1920s, researchers found hypertonicity inhibits cell division while hypotonicity stimulates it. Estrogen, he wrote, "mimics the shock phase of the stress reaction." The osmotic state of a cell's environment is directly tied to its proliferative behavior, linking estrogen-induced hypotonicity to the broader estrogen-cancer connection.

## Salt, Bone, and the Phosphate Relationship

Salt's effects on bone are counterintuitive. Mainstream nutrition treats sodium as a risk factor for bone loss on the theory that it increases urinary calcium excretion, but Peat pointed in the opposite direction. "Bone loss occurs almost entirely during the night, and the nocturnal rise in cortisol and prolactin has strongly catabolic effects," he wrote in [[water-swelling-tension-pain-fatigue-aging|"Water, Swelling, Tension"]]. "Increased salt in the diet appears to improve some aspects of calcium metabolism, such as reducing parathyroid hormone and increasing ionized calcium, when the diet is deficient in calcium."

The mechanism runs through the stress hormones that drive nocturnal bone resorption. Salt lowers adrenaline, cortisol, and aldosterone, three of the catabolic signals that rise during sleep. It also lowers [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]] and raises ionized calcium, both of which favor bone accretion over resorption. A low-salt diet exaggerates the nocturnal rise in aldosterone and cortisol, which means it exaggerates the nocturnal bone loss that adds up to osteoporosis.

The relationship to [[phosphate]] follows the same logic. Phosphate retention, which Peat identified as a central driver of aging, is managed by the same stress-hormone system salt regulates. When sodium is adequate and parathyroid hormone is low, phosphate handling improves. When sodium is restricted, PTH rises, and phosphate accumulates in soft tissues where it accelerates calcification. Salt protects the mineral balance that keeps minerals in bone rather than soft tissue.

## Salt Appetite as Physiological Wisdom

Peat's practical guidance on salt intake was unusually simple: trust the body's own signal. "Salt appetite is usually a good indicator of need," he wrote in an email exchange. "Appetite is usually a good guide to the amount needed." The counterpart, about the upper limit, was equally spare: "Too much salt can injure the stomach."

People working hard in summer heat can lose so much salt through sweat that they need salt tablets to avoid fainting, their hair crusted with crystals by the end of the day. People on low-salt diets adapt within a couple of days and can sweat copiously without losing much salt at all. The body adjusts its retention to compensate for intake. The standard physiology lab experiment, in which students drink a liter of saline and measure the resulting reduction in urine output, mistakes this adjustment process for a chronic effect.

Women's cyclic salt cravings are particularly instructive. "Many young women periodically crave salt and sugar, especially around ovulation and premenstrually, when estrogen is high," Peat wrote. "Physiologically, this is similar to the food cravings of pregnancy." The craving emerges precisely when estrogen is disturbing water balance and sodium loss is accelerating. Honoring the craving, rather than restricting salt and adding a diuretic, reliably prevents the edema that conventional obstetrics once tried to treat with the very drugs that caused it.

## Sea Salt vs. Table Salt

Peat had no patience for the purity politics of salt. "A friend had the celtic salt analyzed, and found it was high in toxic heavy metals," he wrote in one email exchange. "The pure white common salt is best." His brand recommendations were characteristically blunt: "I usually use either La Baleine or Morton's canning and pickling salt. Some sea salt is refined, by sequential evaporation, until it's very pure; either kind of pure white salt without additives is good."

Gray and pink salts, sold at premium prices on the theory that color indicates mineral richness, may also indicate heavy metal contamination. Pure white salt, whether from sea evaporation or ancient mineral deposits, contains what matters: sodium chloride without contaminants. Peat did not object to iodized salt either; iodine deficiency is its own problem, and [[iodine]] added to salt is a reasonable public health measure for the many people who do not eat seafood. For individuals whose gut is irritated by the chloride component, Peat suggested an alternative: "Baking soda in water is helpful for some people."

## Practical Notes

Peat's guidance on salt was not a recommendation to shovel it in. It was a recommendation to stop restricting it. Salt to taste. Respect cravings. Add a pinch before bed if sleep is broken. Use it liberally during hot weather or heavy sweat loss. Increase it during the premenstrual week. Do not fear it during pregnancy. For women with cyclic edema, salt to taste tends to resolve the problem within a cycle or two, and sleep usually improves as a side effect. For essential hypertension, the conventional wisdom is precisely wrong: adequate salt, combined with thyroid support and stress management, tends to lower rather than raise blood pressure through its effects on blood volume and the RAAS system.

The upper limit is set by the stomach. In fifty years of clinical observation and correspondence with thousands of people, Peat did not encounter salt excess as a common problem. Salt restriction, on the other hand, was the default cause of several categories of pathology that improved reliably when restriction was lifted.
