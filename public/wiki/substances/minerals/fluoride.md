# Fluoride

Ray Peat's writing on fluoride spans almost his entire public career. The earliest known engagement is a 1973 newspaper letter ([[fluoride-pollution|*Fluoride Pollution*]]) asking why legislators wanted to add fluoride to drinking water when food in some regions already contained ten times the water concentration. The latest is a 2019 radio appearance in which he was still warning callers about the same chemical, still recommending water filters, and still pointing out that one glass of fluoridated water was sufficient to neutralize a day's dose of T3. Across the intervening forty-six years the position never moved. Fluoride is an industrial-waste byproduct that was rebranded as a public-health intervention through funded science, mass advertising, and the prestige of dental and medical authority — and the biochemistry that makes it useful as an industrial pollutant control (it precipitates calcium, binds iodine, displaces aluminum from solution) is exactly the biochemistry that makes it harmful in the human body. Peat read fluoride as the cleanest case study in [[regulatory-capture]] available to the nutritional literature: a five-decade demonstration of how a waste stream becomes a "nutrient" when the political economy lines up.

This page concentrates on what Peat actually wrote. The hub mechanisms — how thyroid hormone is built and what blocks it, how bone remodels, how parathyroid hormone drives mineral redistribution — live at [[thyroid]], [[osteoporosis]], and [[parathyroid-hormone]]. The political-economy mechanism by which industry-friendly findings became public-health consensus lives at [[regulatory-capture]]. This article handles the substance itself: where it comes from, how it harms the body, what the historical record shows about how it got into the water supply, and what Peat recommended doing about it.

## Chemistry and Sources

Fluoride is the anion of fluorine, the most reactive element on the periodic table. In nature it occurs in trace amounts in groundwater (typically below 0.5 parts per million), in tea leaves (which concentrate it from soil), in some seafood, and in bones — animal bones included, which is why Peat noted in an email reply on [[gelatin]] that "the only analysis of gelatin that I have seen showed very little fluoride. Since most of the fluoride in an animal is concentrated in the bones, and gelatin is made from skin, it probably doesn't contain much."

The fluoride in the modern environment, however, is not the trace natural background. It is the byproduct of two heavy industries: aluminum smelting and phosphate fertilizer manufacturing. The smelting of bauxite into aluminum releases hydrogen fluoride and silicon tetrafluoride as airborne pollutants that historically devastated downwind agriculture and livestock. The manufacture of superphosphate fertilizer, in which sulfuric acid is reacted with phosphate rock, releases fluosilicic acid as a scrubbed off-gas. Both processes generate fluoride waste in quantities that, before water fluoridation, had to be disposed of at significant cost.

The compound now added to most American municipal water systems is hydrofluorosilicic acid (H₂SiF₆), the scrubbed waste from phosphate fertilizer plants. In the [[2015-12-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-nitric-oxide-nitrates-nitrites-and-fluoride|December 2015 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode dedicated to fluoride]], host Andrew Murray summarized the supply chain plainly: "The fluoride put in our drinking water is not a pharmaceutical-grade additive; it is an industrial waste byproduct. With increasing aluminum production in the early 20th century, manufacturers needed somewhere to put the fluoride waste. They commissioned research to support adding fluoride to the water supply. The main fluoride chemical added to water today is hydrofluorosilicic acid, a byproduct from the phosphate fertilizer industry."

Beyond municipal water, fluoride enters the body through:

- **Toothpaste** — sodium fluoride or stannous fluoride at 1,000–1,500 ppm, dramatically higher than the 0.7–1.0 ppm in fluoridated water.
- **Tea** — black, green, and oolong teas concentrate fluoride from soil. A heavy tea drinker can exceed several milligrams per day from this source alone, which is why Peat treated tea as a sometimes-yes but rarely-encouraged beverage and why high tea consumption in fluoride-rich regions of China and India has produced documented skeletal fluorosis.
- **Processed foods and beverages** — anything reconstituted with municipal water (sodas, juices, soups, beer) carries the water's fluoride load. Peat's 1973 [[fluoride-pollution|letter]] flagged this point: "in some regions the fluoride concentration in food is 10 times higher than in the water."
- **Pharmaceuticals** — many drugs are fluorinated to alter metabolism, including the fluoroquinolone antibiotics (Cipro, Levaquin), SSRIs (Prozac, Paxil — fluoxetine literally means "fluorine added to oxetine"), corticosteroids, anesthetics (halothane, sevoflurane), and some statins. The fluorine atom is usually carbon-bonded and not directly ionic, but metabolism releases some as free fluoride.
- **Non-stick cookware** — Teflon (polytetrafluoroethylene) sheds fluoride and other fluorinated compounds at high temperatures.
- **Aluminum smelter emissions** — airborne hydrogen fluoride and silicon tetrafluoride in the vicinity of smelters, the original waste stream that motivated the industry's search for an alternative disposal route.

The cumulative load is the point. Even a person who drinks unfluoridated bottled water still acquires fluoride from toothpaste, tea, restaurant food, processed beverages, and pharmaceuticals. The 0.7 ppm "optimal" figure that defenders cite refers only to the water concentration; the total daily intake in a typical fluoridated city in the United States runs several milligrams.

## How Fluoride Harms

Peat's mechanistic objection to fluoride was concentrated on three tissues: the thyroid, the skeleton, and the pineal gland. Each is documented in his own writing and corroborated by his repeated personal experiments on the question of T3 inactivation.

**Thyroid: iodine displacement and T3 neutralization.** Fluoride and iodine are both halogens, and fluoride is small enough and reactive enough to substitute for iodine at thyroid receptor sites and in the thyroid hormone molecule itself. Peat's most concrete statement of the mechanism appeared in the [[2011-06-03-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-milk-calcium-and-hormones|*EastWest Healing* milk-calcium-and-hormones episode]] and was repeated almost verbatim in the [[2025-11-28-eastwest-healing-milk-calcium-and-hormones|2025 version]] of the same show: "I avoid fluoridated water carefully. During a stay in San Francisco, I wasn't aware the water was fluoridated and I started getting extreme hypothyroid symptoms. The water had enough fluoride to totally destroy the thyroid supplement I was taking. It just takes one fluoride atom to ruin the T3 molecule. It is a thyroid toxin." The same anecdote appeared in the [[2018-01-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-female-hormones-progesterone|January 2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on female hormones and progesterone]]: "When I spent time in San Francisco, I was using a daily supplement of T3 (Cytomel). Within a few days of drinking the fluoridated water, I developed a migraine, which made me assume it was a sudden crash of my thyroid function. If you take T3 with fluoridated water, the fluoride in the stomach will totally knock out the dose. As long as it is circulating as ionic fluoride, I think it will knock out any T3 it runs into."

The reproducibility of the personal experiment is itself the evidence. Peat had a sensitive readout — symptomatic hypothyroidism within days of switching water supplies, recovery within days of switching back — and the readout fired every time he ran the experiment. In the [[2019-09-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fascinating-insights-into-mr-thyroid|September 2019 *One Radio Network* interview]] he summarized the rule of thumb that emerged: "A glass of fluoridated water is enough to knock out a day's portion of the active thyroid hormone." The full thyroid mechanism — how iodine is captured, organified, and built into T4 and T3 — lives at [[thyroid]] and [[iodine]]. The relevant point for this article is that fluoride disrupts the chain at multiple steps: by competing with iodide uptake at the sodium-iodide symporter, by displacing iodine from organified tyrosine residues, and by directly degrading circulating T3 in the gut and bloodstream.

**Bone: brittleness despite increased density.** Fluoride deposits in bone matrix, replacing the hydroxyl groups in hydroxyapatite to form fluorapatite. The result is a denser, harder, *more brittle* crystal. In the 1980s and early 1990s, fluoride was actually prescribed as a treatment for osteoporosis in doses of 20–80 mg per day, on the strength of DEXA scans showing increased bone mineral density. The trials terminated when fracture rates in the treatment arm *increased* despite the higher density readings. Peat's reference to this episode appeared in the 2006 newsletter [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*]]: "Other women are told to reduce their protein consumption, or to take fluoride or whatever drug has been most recently promoted ... Too much fluoride clearly increases the risk of bone fractures, and the side effects of other drugs haven't been properly studied in humans, while they often have dangerous effects in animals."

The fluoride-as-osteoporosis-treatment episode is one of the clearest demonstrations of why DEXA scanning is a misleading proxy for actual bone strength — the topic treated at length in [[osteoporosis]]. A bone matrix can be denser on a radiograph and weaker in the hand. Mineral packing is not the operational definition of bone health; collagen renewal and crystalline integrity are. Fluoride poisoned the renewal process while increasing the mineral signal, producing exactly the inversion that the mineral-centric framework could not detect until femurs began snapping. The episode also forecasts the same pattern with bisphosphonates a decade later — drugs that suppress osteoclast activity, raise DEXA readings, and produce atypical femoral fractures from accumulated microdamage in the unrenewed matrix.

**Pineal: calcification and melatonin suppression.** The pineal gland concentrates fluoride more than any other soft tissue. Jennifer Luke's 2001 dissertation work at the University of Surrey, published in *Caries Research*, documented pineal fluoride concentrations several times higher than those in cortical bone. The pineal calcifies progressively with age in fluoridated populations and, in animal studies, fluoride-exposed pineal glands secrete reduced melatonin. Peat did not write a dedicated treatment of the pineal-fluoride connection, but the implication threads through his framework: the pineal coordinates circadian timing of [[progesterone]], [[prolactin]], and metabolic rate, and anything that suppresses its function — bright nighttime light, serotonin-elevating stress, fluoride deposition — degrades the same hormonal current that runs sleep, reproductive cycling, and aging.

**Dental fluorosis: the visible warning sign.** The condition that fluoridation was supposed to prevent is dental caries. The condition it reliably produces is dental fluorosis — mottling, opacity, and brown staining of tooth enamel from fluoride incorporation during enamel formation in childhood. The CDC reports dental fluorosis in roughly 40% of American adolescents, with severe cases concentrated in fluoridated areas and in children who swallow fluoride toothpaste. Peat treated fluorosis as the visible canary in the bloodstream: a tissue that displays its fluoride load on the front of every smile, signaling the heavier deposits in the bones, pineal, and thyroid that no one is examining. The "decay reduction" claim that justified fluoridation is itself thin. In the [[2015-12-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-nitric-oxide-nitrates-nitrites-and-fluoride|December 2015 episode]], Peat described his read of the early trials: "I read a few studies in the 1970s in which they compared villages that had high fluoride or zero fluoride added to the water. It happens that the teeth were healthier in the village *without* the fluoride. Some of the early studies were done in Montana towns where the water was naturally mineralized. They were getting trace minerals natural in the mountain water — magnesium, molybdenum, and others — that helped the teeth mature properly. They didn't control for that; they blamed the fluoride content for the good dental health."

The recent meta-analyses confirm the suspicion. The Cochrane review of 2015 found that the studies underpinning water fluoridation were almost entirely conducted before 1975, used weak methodology, and could not be replicated under modern trial standards. The decay reduction in fluoridated areas, where it exists at all, is small and shrinking as the rest of the developed world — most of which does not fluoridate — has converged on similar dental outcomes through diet, hygiene, and the topical action of toothpaste at the surface of the tooth.

## The Fluoridation History

The political-economy story of how fluoride entered the water supply is the load-bearing case study in [[regulatory-capture]] applied to nutrition. The compressed version belongs here; the longer treatment of the regulatory mechanism lives on the dedicated page.

The principal scientific architect of water fluoridation was Harold Hodge, a toxicologist at the University of Rochester. Hodge was also the chief of toxicology for the Manhattan Project, where fluoride was both a critical industrial reagent (used in uranium enrichment) and a major occupational hazard. Manhattan Project records, declassified in the 1990s, show that worker fluoride exposures in the uranium-processing facilities at Oak Ridge and Hanford produced documented bone, joint, and dental injuries — injuries that the project's medical division had a legal incentive to minimize. Hodge moved directly from Manhattan Project toxicology into a leading role on the Public Health Service's water-fluoridation studies in Newburgh and Kingston, New York, in 1945. The same toxicologist who had been arguing for the safety of high-dose fluoride exposure in classified industrial settings was now the lead investigator on the trials that would establish fluoride as a public-health additive.

The corporate side of the network was anchored by the Kettering Laboratory at the University of Cincinnati, founded and largely funded by industries with significant fluoride emission liability — the aluminum industry (Alcoa, Reynolds), the steel industry, and the phosphate fertilizer industry. The Kettering Laboratory's published research consistently downplayed fluoride toxicity and emphasized its potential dental benefits, throughout the same decades when its industrial sponsors were being sued by downwind farmers whose livestock were dying of fluorosis. The classical pattern of [[regulatory-capture]] — the regulator (the Public Health Service) drawing its expertise from researchers (Hodge, the Kettering Laboratory) whose career incentives ran with the regulated industry — was operating in plain view.

The Public Health Service endorsed water fluoridation in 1950 on the basis of the Newburgh-Kingston results, which were still in their early years and would not be completed until 1954. The endorsement was political, not scientific. By the time the trial data was actually available, fluoridation had been adopted by hundreds of municipalities, and the question had moved from "should we do this?" to "are you opposed to children's dental health?" Peat watched the same dynamic play out at the local level in Eugene, Oregon, where a 1976 fluoridation ballot measure prompted his [[find-the-facts|June 1977 letter to the *Register-Guard*]]: "If fluoridation proponents have such a high regard for the public that they will prescribe and administer mass medication for them, and such a high estimation of the public intelligence that they believe medical and dental issues should be resolved by a public vote, then it would seem that they should have enough respect for the public intelligence to participate in some form of a public debate or at least a presentation of the actual facts in support of their position." The letter is dated to the era when Eugene was debating its own fluoridation; the question was settled by ballot rather than by scientific argument, and the local dental establishment — which Peat noted "no dentist or physician in this area" was willing to publicly defend — produced no debate.

The dissenting researchers of the period paid for their dissent. John Yiamouyiannis, a biochemist who had worked for *Biological Abstracts* and was familiar with roughly 15,000 fluoride studies, became one of the most effective public opponents of fluoridation. He partnered with Dean Burk — one of the National Cancer Institute's most decorated cancer researchers and a longtime collaborator of Otto Warburg — to study cancer mortality in fluoridated versus unfluoridated American cities. Their analysis, published in 1977 with a follow-up in 1985, found elevated cancer mortality in fluoridated areas after controlling for age and sex distribution. Peat described the Yiamouyiannis-Burk work in the [[2015-12-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-nitric-oxide-nitrates-nitrites-and-fluoride|2015 episode]]: "I went to hear John Yiamouyiannis talk. He was debating a dentist who claimed to be an expert on fluoridation. Yiamouyiannis had worked for the abstracting service *Biological Abstracts* and was familiar with 15,000 studies on fluoride, so he could make any opponent look ridiculous. Yiamouyiannis and Dean Burk did a study in Los Angeles and found that cancer incidence increased after fluoride was added. Dean Burk was one of the best cancer researchers in the world — a colleague of Otto Warburg — but his reputation was damaged by getting involved with fluoride."

The career-cost mechanism is the closing piece of the regulatory-capture pattern. Hodge's career advanced from Manhattan Project toxicology to dean-level appointments. Burk's career, by contrast, was constrained for the rest of his life by the fluoride controversy, despite his decades of unimpeachable cancer research at NCI. Yiamouyiannis was systematically marginalized by the dental profession; Stephen Barrett of Quackwatch became a long-running attack vector against him and against fluoride critics generally. The asymmetry — promotion for those who supported fluoridation, professional damage for those who questioned it — is itself one of the most reliable signals that the regulatory consensus was being maintained by means other than evidence.

The European picture is the natural experiment that undercuts the American consensus. Most of continental Europe — Germany, France, the Netherlands, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland, Italy, Spain, Switzerland, Belgium, Austria — does not fluoridate municipal water. The Netherlands tried it and abandoned it on constitutional grounds (compulsory medication of a population) in the 1970s. Sweden's evaluation in the 1970s concluded the benefits were insufficient to justify the intervention. The dental outcomes in unfluoridated European countries, four to five decades after the divergence from American policy, are statistically indistinguishable from American outcomes. If fluoridation were producing the population-level dental benefit its defenders claim, the European data should show it. It does not.

## What Peat Recommended

The protocol is short. The longest part of it is filtering the water.

**Filter drinking water.** Reverse osmosis is the most reliable removal method; activated charcoal filters reduce but do not eliminate fluoride; standard refrigerator filters and Brita pitchers do not remove fluoride at all. The [[hypothyroid-recovery|hypothyroid recovery protocol]] lists fluoride explicitly as one of the eliminations: "Fluoride in drinking water. Competes directly with iodine. Peat said that 'a glass of fluoridated water is enough to knock out a day's portion of the active thyroid hormone.' Use filtered water or a reverse osmosis system."

**Avoid fluoride toothpaste.** Peat's position on toothpaste was nuanced. In the [[teeth-gums|teeth-and-gums email file]] he wrote: "I think daily use of fluoride is likely to be harmful to the gums, but applying it occasionally to the teeth can harden the enamel, improving its resistance to cavities. I think the alkaline effect of baking soda is helpful for thorough cleaning." The chronic daily use is the problem; an occasional topical application is not the central toxicity. The alternatives the community has converged on — baking soda, coconut oil (which Peat noted is antiseptic and useful for receding gums and sensitive teeth in the same email file), hydroxyapatite toothpaste, simple salt — all clean the teeth without delivering a daily fluoride dose to the gums and the digestive tract.

**Limit tea consumption.** A cup or two of tea is not a problem for most people; ten cups a day in a region with naturally fluoride-rich water can produce skeletal fluorosis without any municipal fluoridation at all. Peat did not recommend tea as a regular beverage in any case; coffee with milk and sugar, citrus juice, and milk were his standard daily fluids. Heavy tea drinkers should know the fluoride load they are accepting and balance it against the other inputs.

**Avoid fluoridated pharmaceuticals where alternatives exist.** This is not a categorical rule. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics (Cipro, Levaquin) and SSRI antidepressants (Prozac, Paxil) carry the highest fluoride loads per dose, and Peat treated both classes as toxic for other reasons as well — Cipro for its mitochondrial damage and tendon rupture profile, SSRIs for their serotonin-elevating mechanism and prolactin effects. Halothane and sevoflurane are the standard fluorinated anesthetics; alternatives exist for many procedures.

**Boron antagonism.** Where exposure history is significant — decades in a fluoridated city, joint pain consistent with skeletal fluorosis, suspected fluoride-driven hypothyroidism — boron is the trace mineral that competes with fluoride for binding sites and increases renal fluoride clearance. The dedicated treatment of boron supplementation lives at [[borax]]. The dose is on the order of 3–6 mg of elemental boron per day, achievable from dietary sources (raisins, prunes, certain fruits) or from a pinch of borax dissolved in water.

**Iodine: cautious.** The question of whether iodine supplementation can counter fluoride's thyroid suppression came up in the [[2018-01-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-female-hormones-progesterone|2018 progesterone episode]]: "On iodine, I know you've mentioned that even small amounts can be toxic. But outside the laboratory, does more iodine make sense in an environment where we are exposed to fluoridated water? Does it counteract the fluoride?" Peat: "I've never seen a test of that idea." His suspicion of milligram-range iodine dosing — documented at [[iodine]] — held even in the presence of fluoride exposure. The remedy for fluoride is not iodine; it is removing the fluoride.

## Tracking Signal

If fluoride load was a significant contributor to a person's metabolic state, removal often produces measurable changes within weeks to months. The cleanest readouts are:

- **Thyroid markers and symptoms.** TSH may fall; T3 may rise; basal temperature and morning pulse may climb into the metabolically healthy range. The change is most dramatic in people who, like Peat himself in San Francisco, were taking a thyroid supplement and watching it fail to work — removal of the fluoride load often restores responsiveness to a dose that previously did nothing.
- **Energy and sleep.** The pineal-melatonin pathway and the thyroid-energy pathway run on the same circadian and metabolic axes; sustained removal of fluoride exposure can produce slow improvements in both.
- **Joint and skeletal pain.** People with low-grade skeletal fluorosis — chronic stiffness, joint pain, fragility — sometimes report substantial improvement on filtered water plus boron over six to eighteen months.
- **Skin and dental sensitivity.** Some people report improvements in skin clarity and reduced tooth sensitivity within weeks of switching to filtered water and non-fluoride toothpaste.

The signal is rarely dramatic in isolation. Fluoride accumulates over decades and clears slowly; the removal benefits are cumulative and compounding rather than acute. The clearer experiment, the one Peat ran on himself repeatedly, is the acute fluoride load delivered with a known thyroid dose — a glass of fluoridated water alongside a Cytomel tablet, in a person who knows what a normal day on the dose feels like, will produce within hours the migraine and hypothyroid crash he described.

## Counter-arguments

The Centers for Disease Control listed water fluoridation among the "ten great public health achievements of the twentieth century" in 1999. The American Dental Association continues to endorse fluoridation and treats opposition as a fringe position. The defenders' case has four planks.

**The decay reduction claim.** The original studies in Newburgh-Kingston and Grand Rapids reported reductions in childhood caries of roughly 50–65% in fluoridated communities. The Cochrane review of 2015 found that most of these studies predated 1975, used weak methodology by modern standards, and did not control for the parallel improvements in oral hygiene, diet, and dental access that were occurring across the same decades. Where modern, well-controlled studies have been run, the dental benefit is small — single percentage points of cavity reduction, often statistically indistinguishable from zero — and is increasingly attributed to topical fluoride application (toothpaste, varnish) rather than systemic ingestion.

**The "optimal" 0.7 ppm claim.** The 0.7 ppm target adopted by the US Public Health Service in 2015 (down from the previous 0.7–1.2 ppm range) reflects an implicit concession that the higher concentration was producing too much dental fluorosis. The downward revision is not a vindication of the original program; it is a partial walkback. The same agencies that promoted the higher dose for decades have not explained why the new lower dose is safe when the previous higher dose was also called safe.

**The "swallowing toothpaste is the only problem" line.** This argument acknowledges that systemic fluoride above some threshold is harmful while insisting that the threshold is higher than typical intake. The argument presupposes that the threshold has been identified, that interindividual variation in fluoride metabolism is small, and that the cumulative dose from water plus toothpaste plus tea plus processed foods plus pharmaceuticals is below the threshold for everyone. None of these assumptions is well supported in the literature.

**The European outcomes argument as a non-randomized trial.** The most defensible counter to the European data is that European countries have stronger universal dental care, dietary differences, and demographic differences that could account for similar outcomes in the absence of fluoridation. This is true in principle; in practice, the variables proposed to explain the equivalence have not been quantified in a way that rescues the fluoridation hypothesis.

The strongest version of the defenders' case is that the historical fluoridation decision was made under genuine scientific uncertainty, that the dental benefit appeared real at the time, and that the more recent evidence of harm has been confounded by other industrial pollutants. The weakest version is the appeal to authority — the CDC says so, the ADA says so, therefore the question is settled — which is the version that dominates public-facing communication and which is the version Peat's framework was designed to resist.

## Data gaps

- **Population-level long-term thyroid effects.** The 2020 NIH-funded *JAMA Pediatrics* study by Green et al., linking prenatal fluoride exposure to lower childhood IQ in Canadian cohorts, opened a methodological door that earlier US studies had not walked through. Comparable studies of long-term adult thyroid markers in fluoridated versus unfluoridated populations have not been conducted at sufficient scale.
- **Individual fluoride metabolism variation.** Renal clearance of fluoride varies substantially between individuals, and people with impaired kidney function accumulate fluoride faster than the general population. The "optimal" dose framework assumes population uniformity that does not exist.
- **Fluoride-aluminum synergy.** Aluminum and fluoride form aluminofluoride complexes (AlF₄⁻) that activate G-protein-coupled receptors in ways neither element produces alone. The combined exposure — from aluminum cookware, aluminum-adjuvanted vaccines, antiperspirants, and fluoridated water — has not been adequately studied as a joint exposure.
- **Pineal and behavioral effects.** Beyond the Luke dissertation and a handful of animal studies, the pineal fluoride load and its consequences for melatonin, circadian rhythm, and mood remain under-investigated.
- **Reversibility.** The kinetics of skeletal fluoride clearance after exposure cessation, the role of boron supplementation in accelerating clearance, and the persistence of pineal calcifications all need more careful study than the current literature provides.
