# Magnesium

Magnesium occupies a peculiar position in Ray Peat's framework: a mineral he considered essential to nearly every aspect of cellular energy, nerve function, and stress resistance, yet one he almost never recommended supplementing directly. "I don't recommend chemical supplements of magnesium," he wrote in an [[magnesium|email on magnesium]], "because they all contain some manufacturing impurities that can cause bowel inflammation, such as hemorrhoids." The tension between magnesium's centrality to cellular life and the practical difficulty of supplementing it runs through decades of Peat's interviews and correspondence. His solution was characteristically metabolic rather than nutritional: fix the [[thyroid]], eat the right foods, and the body will retain the magnesium it needs. When supplementation was necessary, the skin rather than the gut was often the preferred route of entry.

## The ATP Connection: Why Magnesium Is Not Optional

The deepest layer of Peat's interest in magnesium lies at the level of cellular energy production. Most of the magnesium inside a cell is not floating free but bound to [[ATP]], the molecule that stores and transfers energy. This binding is not incidental; it is structural. As Peat explained on [[#36：-co2-and-mineral-balance-thyroid-magnesium-calcium-in-health-and-disease-current-events|*Generative Energy* #36, discussing CO2 and mineral balance]]: "ATP binds magnesium, so most of the magnesium in the cell is attached to the ATP molecule. With the lower energy state, less thyroid and less [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]], the ATP isn't reformed as fast, so you have more ADP which binds [[calcium]]. That's part of the cell increasing its affinity for calcium at the same time that calcium is losing its affinity for the blood solution."

The implications cascade through every cell in the body. When ATP production is robust, magnesium is bound, retained, and functional. When ATP production falters, magnesium leaks out of the cell, ADP accumulates, and calcium floods in to take its place. The shift from a magnesium-dominant intracellular environment to a calcium-dominant one is, in Peat's framework, the shift from healthy oxidative metabolism to the stress state that underlies [[inflammation]], calcification, and degeneration.

His newsletter [[calcium-and-disease-hypertension-organ-calcification-shock-vs-respiratory-energy|"Calcium and Disease" (2009)]] stated the mechanism precisely: "Much of the intracellular magnesium is complexed with ATP, and helps to stabilize that molecule. If cellular energy production is low, as in [[hypothyroidism]], cells tend to lose their magnesium very easily, shifting the balance toward the lower energy molecule, ADP, with the release of phosphate. ADP complexes with calcium, rather than magnesium, increasing the cell's calcium content."

The practical consequence is that magnesium deficiency is not simply a nutritional gap to be filled by swallowing a pill. It is a metabolic signal, a downstream indicator that cellular energy production has faltered. Supplementing magnesium without addressing the energy deficit is, in Peat's framework, treating the symptom while ignoring the cause.

On the same *Generative Energy* episode, [[georgi-dinkov|Georgi Dinkov]] asked whether high blood magnesium might paradoxically be a bad sign, since it would indicate cells leaking their contents. Peat confirmed: "Yeah, exactly like [[potassium]]. High potassium often means that your cells are dying and letting their potassium leak into the bloodstream. Same with magnesium." This observation undermines the standard clinical interpretation that "normal" serum magnesium means adequate magnesium status. The magnesium that matters is inside the cell, bound to ATP, and no standard blood test captures it accurately. Peat noted that erythrocyte magnesium tests, while better than serum tests, are still imperfect because red blood cells "are mainly glycolytic in their energy system, so they aren't very representative of the oxidative cells."

Dinkov raised one of the most striking implications of the ATP-magnesium relationship: a study showing that infusions of the magnesium salt of ATP caused tumors to regress in animals. Peat's response connected it directly to the calcium-magnesium balance: "The compound with magnesium probably is the right way to use ATP therapeutically because other forms will break down easily. Free ATP is very dangerous; it's one of the major danger signals or activators of inflammation. But when it's bound to magnesium, it is probably not toxic outside the cell and can get inside the cell easier." The tumor, starved of energy and flooded with calcium, responds to a direct supply of the magnesium-ATP complex that healthy cells produce on their own.

## Thyroid: The Master Switch for Magnesium Retention

If magnesium's fate inside the cell depends on ATP, then the organ that governs ATP production, the thyroid gland, becomes the master regulator of magnesium status. This was one of Peat's most consistent and practically important points.

"Getting enough [[sodium]] in the diet helps to retain magnesium," he wrote in his email exchanges, "but both of them are lost easily when thyroid function is low; when the thyroid status is good, the requirement for magnesium is easily met by ordinary foods." The statement reframes magnesium deficiency as, in most cases, a thyroid problem rather than a dietary problem.

The mechanism is direct: thyroid hormone increases cellular respiration, which increases ATP production, which increases the cell's capacity to bind and retain magnesium. A hypothyroid cell, producing less ATP, literally cannot hold onto its magnesium. The newsletter [[multiple-sclerosis-protein-fats-and-progesterone|"Multiple Sclerosis, Protein, Fats, and Progesterone" (2009)]] put it bluntly: "Magnesium is rapidly lost from cells in hypothyroidism."

This creates a dangerous feedback loop when someone begins thyroid supplementation. The newly activated cells start producing more ATP and demanding more magnesium, pulling it from the blood. If the body's magnesium reserves are already depleted from years of hypothyroidism, the sudden demand can create an acute deficiency in the organs that need magnesium most. Peat described the phenomenon on a [[2014-11-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-nitric-oxide|2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on nitric oxide]]: "Sometimes the product has changed; European forms of thyroid supplements seem unpredictable. But if it is the same product, usually a magnesium deficiency can cause exactly those symptoms. Thyroid makes your cells able to use magnesium. A big organ like your skeletal muscles and bones can take up so much from your blood that your brain and heart have trouble getting the magnesium they need to respond to the thyroid. Then you get an exaggerated stress and [[adrenaline]] reaction."

The image is vivid: skeletal muscle and bone, being the largest organs in the body, act as magnesium sponges when thyroid is introduced, leaving the brain and heart scrambling for what remains. The stress symptoms that result, including heart palpitations, anxiety, and insomnia, are often mistakenly attributed to "taking too much thyroid" when they actually indicate too little magnesium to support the new metabolic rate.

Peat's practical advice was to start thyroid supplementation slowly and ensure magnesium intake was adequate before and during the transition. On a [[2016-02-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-iodine-supplement-reactions-hormones-and-more|2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on iodine and supplement reactions]], he elaborated: "People who have trouble with thyroid hormone can be deficient in magnesium. Hypothyroidism makes tissues fail to retain magnesium. When you supplement thyroid, you suddenly experience an extreme magnesium deficiency in the heart and brain. Taking magnesium with the thyroid helps. Others, if deficient in adrenal or gonadal steroids, will suffer stress symptoms. Using a supplement like [[pregnenolone]] will make them tolerate the thyroid more easily."

He also noted that the dose of thyroid needed to be titrated with extreme care: "I've known people who stayed with 1 microgram doses of [[T3]] for a week or two. You have to make sure your whole diet is very good, including protein, sugar, calcium, and magnesium."

On [[2019-07-18-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-milk|a 2019 *One Radio Network* episode about milk]], he put the feedback loop in its starkest terms: "Increasing thyroid requires magnesium. Cells begin making more energy and need to retain magnesium because it binds to ATP. A low thyroid person is breaking down ATP because there isn't enough magnesium to stabilize it. If you don't have a good reserve of magnesium, increasing thyroid just increases stress."

## The Calcium-Magnesium Balance

Calcium and magnesium are not merely two minerals that happen to coexist in the body; they are metabolic antagonists whose balance determines the state of virtually every cell. The newsletter "Calcium and Disease" contains Peat's most comprehensive treatment of this relationship: "Magnesium and potassium are mainly intracellular ions, sodium and calcium are mainly extracellular ions. When cells are excited, stressed, or de-energized, they lose magnesium and potassium, and take up sodium and calcium. The mitochondria can bind a certain amount of calcium during stress, but accumulating calcium can reach a point at which it inactivates the mitochondria, forcing cells to increase their inefficient glycolytic energy production, producing an excess of [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]. Abnormal calcification begins in the mitochondria."

This passage maps the fundamental degenerative process in Peat's model: stress drives magnesium out and calcium in; calcium overload kills mitochondria; dead mitochondria force the cell into glycolysis; glycolysis produces lactic acid; lactic acid drives more stress. The calcification that conventional medicine treats as an inevitable consequence of aging is, in this framework, a consequence of inadequate respiratory energy and the resulting failure to maintain the correct mineral ratios.

Magnesium deficiency alone can trigger the inflammatory cascade. Peat cited the work of Selye's colleague G. Jasmin, who "showed that magnesium deficiency causes inflammation." The newsletter continued: "A deficiency of either calcium or magnesium can stimulate the parathyroid glands to produce more hormone ([[parathyroid-hormone|PTH]]), which increases calcium absorption, but also removes calcium from the bones." The result is paradoxical: the person who is deficient in calcium or magnesium ends up with too much calcium in the wrong places (soft tissues, arteries, joints) and too little in the right place (bones). [[Parathyroid-hormone|Parathyroid hormone]], driven up by the mineral deficiency, becomes "an important factor in causing cells to take up too much calcium, and its excess is associated with many inflammatory and degenerative diseases."

The practical conclusion was both simple and profound: "Simply eating an adequate amount of calcium and magnesium can alleviate many problems related to stress and aging that are considered serious, such as heart arrhythmia, pancreatitis, and tissue calcification." In the newsletter [[osteoporosis-harmful-calcification-and-nerve-muscle-malfunctions|"Osteoporosis, Harmful Calcification, and Nerve/Muscle Malfunctions" (2006)]], Peat noted that early calcification in the heart and kidneys often appears first in the mitochondria, "for example when magnesium is deficient."

The newsletter [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|"Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis" (1985)]] contained one of his clearest therapeutic summaries: "I think rational therapy for osteoporosis would include thyroid, [[vitamin-a|vitamin A]], bright light, and either pregnenolone or a mixture of [[progesterone]] and [[DHEA]], with a diet rich in protein and all the salts: calcium, magnesium, sodium, and potassium. A bias toward magnesium, rather than calcium, would protect against many conditions that occur in aging and stress." That final sentence is striking. While conventional advice for aging emphasizes calcium supplementation, Peat suggested that a magnesium bias is more protective, precisely because the aging body's problem is usually too much calcium in the wrong compartments rather than too little calcium overall.

## Nerve, Muscle, and Sleep

The calcium-magnesium balance plays out with particular intensity in excitable tissues: nerves and muscles. The newsletter on osteoporosis explained: "Free intracellular calcium is the trigger for muscle contraction (and magnesium is an important factor in relaxation.) Capillary tone, similarly, is increased by hyperventilation, and relaxed by carbon dioxide. The muscle-relaxing effect of carbon dioxide shows that the binding of intracellular calcium is promoted by carbon dioxide, as well as by ATP."

When magnesium is adequate and ATP production is strong, muscles contract and relax in their proper rhythm. When magnesium falls and calcium accumulates, muscles tend toward sustained contraction, cramping, and stiffness. The same newsletter connected this directly to common complaints: "Muscle stiffness, muscle soreness and weakness, and [[osteoporosis]] all seem to be consequences of inadequate respiration, allowing lactic acid to be produced instead of carbon dioxide. [[Insomnia]], hyperactivity, [[anxiety]], and many chronic brain conditions also show evidence of defective respiration."

The neurological dimension extends beyond muscle tension. In the "Calcium and Disease" newsletter, Peat wrote: "Increased intracellular calcium, in association with excess [[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]] and excitatory amino acids, is involved in several neurodegenerative diseases, including ALS, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's chorea, and [[epilepsy]]. Magnesium, nicotine, progesterone, and many other substances are known to protect against excitotoxic calcium overload, but there is no coherent effort in the health professions to make rational use of the available knowledge."

Magnesium's calming effect on nerves explains why it is popularly recommended for sleep and anxiety. A caller named Miranda on a [[2016-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidant-theory-and-the-continued-war-on-cancer|2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on antioxidant theory]] reported that magnesium glycinate "helps my nerves." Peat did not dispute the benefit but raised the characteristic complication: supplements can cause intestinal inflammation, and "intestinal irritation can cause insomnia." The very supplement taken to aid sleep may, through bowel irritation, prevent it.

## The Supplement Problem

Peat's skepticism toward magnesium supplements was not philosophical but empirical, rooted in his own experience and that of people he advised. "All of the magnesium supplements that I have tried caused allergy symptoms and bowel inflammation," he wrote, "but some people don't have a problem with magnesium carbonate or magnesium glycinate."

The core issue is manufacturing impurities. Even when the magnesium compound itself is benign, the industrial process of producing it introduces contaminants that irritate the intestinal lining. On a [[2020-03-16-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-what-is-a-virus-anyway|2020 *One Radio Network* episode]], when Patrick Timpone asked about magnesium carbonate, Peat advised caution: "Many products have impurities that cause bad headaches."

He evaluated the common supplement forms with his usual specificity. Magnesium carbonate was "a very good supplement, except that it can cause intestinal irritation." Both carbonate and glycinate have inherent benefits (carbonate provides alkalinity, glycine supports inhibitory neurotransmission), "but each of the compounds has its own impurities." Magnesium citrate was problematic for different reasons: it has "other effects on metabolism that could be harmful." Magnesium oxide he simply did not recommend because "it's very poorly absorbed." Magnesium malate he considered acceptable but cautioned to "watch for allergy reactions such as a headache."

The response to Miranda's question about belly fat while taking magnesium glycinate revealed how Peat thought about supplement reactions. Her "belly fat" gain was probably not fat at all: "Magnesium supplements can cause intestinal inflammation or irritation. I would guess that is happening with an increase of [[histamine]], nitric oxide, and [[endotoxin]] absorption. The 'fat' increase might be water retention caused by inflammation." The inflammatory response to the supplement triggers a chain of secondary effects, including histamine release, nitric oxide production, and increased absorption of bacterial toxins from the gut, all of which cause water retention that mimics fat gain.

Magnesium salicylate drew a more nuanced assessment: "I haven't tried magnesium salicylate, but most magnesium compounds have been seriously irritating to my intestine." He noted that it is "popular for arthritis" and would be "a good form of salicylate" if the magnesium component does not cause irritation, since it releases [[salicylic-acid|salicylic acid]] in the intestine and blood.

## Dietary Sources: The Preferred Path

Peat's preferred solution to magnesium needs was consistently dietary rather than supplemental. His most frequently recommended sources form a practical hierarchy.

**Cooked greens and their cooking water** stood at the top. "The things I most often recommend for magnesium are the water from boiling greens such as beet, chard, turnip and kale," he wrote. The technique is specific: boil the leaves briefly, and drink the green water. On the 2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on nitric oxide, he described the method: "If you want a really intense source, you can boil leaves like kale or beet greens for just two or three minutes. The green water that comes out is very concentrated in magnesium and calcium."

The volume needed is modest. On a [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on diabetes and nerves]], he quantified it: "If you eat cooked greens by the cups and quarts, it's an adequate source of protein. But if you aren't eating it as your main food for protein, then half a cup to a cup a day will provide your vitamin K and a generous amount of magnesium and calcium. Those are the nutrients that quickly cook out into the broth. If you drink only the water that you cooked a cup or so of leaves in, you get a supplement of magnesium and calcium."

He did note a limitation of leafy greens in the "Calcium and Disease" newsletter: "Leafy vegetables are a very rich source of magnesium, but they are also a potential source of large amounts of lead and other toxins." The cooking water concentrates minerals but also concentrates whatever contaminants the plant absorbed from the soil.

**[[Coffee]]** was the second most frequently mentioned source. It appears in virtually every list Peat gave of magnesium-rich foods. The newsletter "Multiple Sclerosis, Protein, Fats, and Progesterone" listed "coffee, fruits, some vegetables and meats" as magnesium sources alongside Epsom salt baths. The dual function of coffee as both a magnesium source and a metabolic stimulant made it a particularly efficient recommendation in Peat's framework.

**[[Chocolate]]** received brief but consistent endorsement: "Well cooked greens are very good sources, coffee and chocolate are, too."

**A diet of [[milk]] and fruit, or milk and meat** was Peat's baseline dietary recommendation for mineral balance generally, and magnesium specifically. The "Calcium and Disease" newsletter stated: "A diet of milk and fruit, or milk and meat, provides a nutritional balance with generous amounts of calcium and magnesium." On the 2016 episode on antioxidant theory, Peat told Miranda: "If your thyroid lets your cells retain magnesium, then 400mg per day is easy to get from foods: fruit, milk, cheese, [[eggs]], meat, and [[seafood]]."

**Fruit juices** also featured prominently. On the *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on nitric oxide, when asked directly for a good magnesium source, Peat answered: "Fruit juices and coffee."

The recurring message across all of these recommendations is that a metabolically sound diet, built around Peat's core foods, provides adequate magnesium without supplementation, provided the thyroid is functioning well enough to let cells retain it.

## Topical Absorption: The Bathing Solution

When magnesium intake beyond dietary sources was needed, Peat consistently pointed to the skin rather than the gut. Epsom salt baths (magnesium sulfate dissolved in bathwater) were his most frequently recommended supplementation method, often combined with [[baking-soda|baking soda]] to enhance absorption.

On a [[2012-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-acidity-x-alkalinity|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on acidity and alkalinity]], a caller reported taking baking soda baths for muscle soreness. Peat endorsed the practice and expanded it: "Magnesium sulfate (Epsom salts) and baking soda are all good. The baking soda helps you absorb magnesium if you have Epsom salts mixed with it." Sarah Murray suggested a practical ratio, one pound of each, and Peat agreed.

The mechanism involves carbon dioxide, one of Peat's most valued metabolic products. The baking soda in the bath generates CO2, which the skin absorbs. Peat explained the underlying physiology: "Experimenters in naturally carbonated mineral springs found that people were absorbing carbon dioxide through their skin. Even though their body contains a lot of carbon dioxide, the body has such an affinity for it that it will soak it up from the mineral water against the gradient. It's as if we had pumps in our skin."

On the 2020 *One Radio Network* episode, he connected the bath protocol directly to the inflammatory process: "Carbon dioxide is what should be produced in energy production. Bathing in carbon dioxide, using baking soda and Epsom salts in a bath, helps. The body absorbs the carbon dioxide, which turns off the inflammatory process and suppresses the wasteful production of lactic acid."

An ambitious caller on the [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on diabetes and nerves]] described an elaborate bath containing baking soda, Epsom salt, magnesium chloride, and aspirin/caffeine powder for knee pain. He asked Peat whether the baking soda creates a gradient that pulls substances through the skin. Peat's answer focused on the systemic benefits: the carbon dioxide from baking soda has "diuretic effects and some anti-inflammatory effects," and both [[caffeine]] and [[aspirin]] increase cellular respiration and suppress nitric oxide, providing "multi-levels of defense for the mitochondria." Caffeine penetrates the skin more readily than aspirin, but "some aspirin is absorbed" as well.

His email correspondence confirmed the principle concisely: "Either Mg chloride or Mg sulfate with baking soda can be absorbed through the skin."

## Magnesium and Stress

The relationship between magnesium and stress is bidirectional: stress depletes magnesium, and magnesium deficiency amplifies stress. Peat's framework treats this not as a vicious circle to be broken by supplementation alone, but as a reflection of the cell's energy state.

The "Calcium and Disease" newsletter addressed one of the most common sources of magnesium loss: low sodium intake. "Since a very low sodium diet increases the loss of magnesium, by increasing [[aldosterone]] synthesis, simply increasing the amount of sodium in the diet can help some people to balance their minerals and minimize stress." The conventional advice to restrict sodium, which Peat opposed on multiple grounds, has the unintended consequence of depleting magnesium through aldosterone-mediated excretion.

During severe stress, such as fasting, the alkaline minerals including magnesium play a protein-sparing role. On the 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on acidity and alkalinity, Peat explained: "When a person is fasting for several days, they will generally lose more protein than fat because the stress hormones rise. If you just drink the minerals, salt water, baking soda, potassium, some magnesium and calcium, any of the alkaline minerals will radically spare the amount of protein that you would be consuming and wasting." The kidneys destroy protein to generate ammonia for acid-base regulation; alkaline minerals reduce this burden.

Peat also cited the work of William Frederick Koch on the substitutability of alkaline minerals: "Working on the parathyroid hormone, he found that the alkaline minerals to a tremendous extent would substitute for each other. You can take out the parathyroid glands, which causes a calcium problem, but you could cure that with sodium or potassium or magnesium." This substitutability means that the other minerals, calcium, sodium, and potassium, have "antistress effects that spare magnesium." Good overall mineral nutrition reduces the specific demand for magnesium.

Magnesium also protects against hyperthermia, a specific danger in conditions where metabolic rate rises rapidly. The "Multiple Sclerosis" newsletter noted: "Increased salt is thermogenic, but increased magnesium is protective against hyperthermia." This protective effect makes magnesium particularly important during thyroid supplementation, illness, or any condition that elevates metabolic rate.

## The Therapeutic Constellation

Peat's recommendations for normalizing magnesium status always involved multiple interventions working together. The newsletter on osteoporosis summarized his comprehensive approach: "The combination of supplements of thyroid (emphasizing T3), magnesium, progesterone and pregnenolone can usually restore normal respiration, and it seems clear that this should normalize calcium metabolism, decreasing the calcification of soft tissues, increasing the calcification of bones, and improving the efficiency of muscles and nerves. (Magnesium, like carbonate, is a component of newly formed bone.)"

The parenthetical about bone formation is noteworthy. Magnesium is not merely a regulatory mineral that helps direct calcium to bones; it is itself a structural component of new bone. This means that magnesium deficiency impairs bone formation directly, independent of its effect on calcium metabolism.

In his email correspondence, Peat wrote: "I think it's best to use the foods with high magnesium content, and to start the thyroid slowly, allowing the tissues time to absorb magnesium; the other minerals, Ca, Na, and K, have antistress effects that spare magnesium." His newsletter "Multiple Sclerosis, Protein, Fats, and Progesterone" added another dimension: "In experiments that I have described in previous newsletters, increasing sodium, ATP, carbon dioxide, and progesterone, and increasing the ratio of magnesium to calcium, have been found to increase cellular energy and structure."

The consistent message is that magnesium cannot be addressed in isolation. It exists in a web of relationships with thyroid hormone, the other alkaline minerals, progesterone, pregnenolone, and CO2 production. Fixing any one element without attending to the others is likely to produce the paradoxical stress reactions that lead people to conclude they "can't tolerate" a given supplement.

## Dosing Notes

When oral supplementation was necessary, Peat provided specific guidance. The RDA of 420 mg for an adult was, in his view, adequate "with the average diet. Good thyroid function, and plenty of calcium, potassium, and sodium can decrease the amount of magnesium needed."

For acute supplementation when beginning thyroid support, his recommendation on the *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on nitric oxide was precise: "About 100 milligrams at a time. If you take one or two micrograms of Cytomel (T3), 100 milligrams of magnesium will be plenty for the first two or three hours of responding to that thyroid."

For short-term loading, on the 2019 *One Radio Network* episode about milk, he allowed higher doses: "In the first few days, a gram or two of magnesium carbonate is okay. But a good diet, coffee, milk, [[orange-juice|orange juice]], meat, and fish, are all good magnesium sources."

The subtext of all his dosing advice is that oral magnesium supplementation should be temporary, used to bridge a gap while thyroid function and dietary quality are improved. The long-term solution is always the metabolic one: a body that produces enough ATP to retain its own magnesium.
