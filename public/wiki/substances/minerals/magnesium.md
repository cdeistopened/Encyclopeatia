# Magnesium

Most discussions of magnesium treat it as one mineral among many—good for cramps, sleep, maybe heart health. In Ray Peat's bioenergetic framework, magnesium occupies a far more fundamental position: it is the mineral signature of a well-energized cell. Where magnesium dominates intracellularly, the cell has energy, structure, and readiness to work. Where [[calcium]] displaces it, the cell is stressed, inflamed, and sliding toward degeneration. The balance between these two minerals inside cells is not a detail of nutrition but a direct readout of metabolic health.

The mechanism is precise. As Peat explained in a [[2016-04-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-april-2016|2016 episode]], "Magnesium stabilizes the high-energy ATP molecule. Without [[thyroid]], you can't make ATP very quickly. If you have adequate thyroid producing oxidative energy and ATP, the cell retains magnesium. That complex holds the cell in a ready-to-work state with lots of reserve energy."

## The ATP-Magnesium Complex

The biochemistry is elegant. ATP—the universal energy currency of cells—binds magnesium. Most of the magnesium inside a healthy cell is attached to ATP molecules, forming the Mg-ATP complex that represents stored, immediately available energy. As Peat described in a conversation with Danny Roddy on [[carbon dioxide]] and mineral balance, "ATP binds magnesium, so most of the magnesium in the cell is attached to the ATP molecule. With the lower energy state—less thyroid and less [[carbon dioxide|CO2]]—the ATP isn't reformed as fast, so you have more ADP which binds calcium."

This single biochemical fact connects magnesium status to the entire bioenergetic framework. When energy production is high—supported by adequate thyroid, good nutrition, and low [[stress]]—cells maintain abundant ATP-magnesium complexes. The cell stays relaxed, responsive, and structurally stable. When energy production drops, ATP breaks down to ADP, magnesium is released, and calcium floods in to take its place. The cell shifts from its resting, energized state to an excited, stressed state—the same shift that characterizes [[inflammation]], muscle spasm, and ultimately tissue degeneration.

## Why Thyroid Comes First

Peat consistently argued that magnesium supplementation without adequate thyroid function is addressing a symptom rather than a cause. The cell loses magnesium because it cannot maintain ATP production, and it cannot maintain ATP production because thyroid hormone is insufficient. Giving magnesium to a hypothyroid person may temporarily improve symptoms, but the underlying energy deficit continues to drain magnesium from cells faster than supplements can replace it.

The reverse is also problematic. As Peat warned in a [[2019-07-18-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-thyroid-pufas-oj-and-sugar|2019 interview]], "Increasing thyroid requires magnesium. A low thyroid person is breaking down ATP because there isn't enough magnesium to stabilize it. If you don't have a good reserve of magnesium, increasing thyroid just increases stress."

This creates a practical sequencing problem. Thyroid hormone speeds up metabolism, increasing the demand for ATP, which increases the demand for magnesium. If magnesium reserves are depleted—as they often are after prolonged [[hypothyroidism]]—a sudden increase in thyroid can produce cramps, heart palpitations, and anxiety rather than the expected improvement in energy. Peat's practical advice was to build up magnesium reserves before or alongside thyroid supplementation.

## The Muscle Competition

The heart depends on magnesium for stable rhythm, just as skeletal muscles depend on it for smooth contraction and relaxation. When magnesium is scarce, these tissues compete. Peat described the danger in a discussion of thyroid and mineral balance: "Your biggest muscle cells retain magnesium and calcium in a balance. Thyroid is needed for the muscles to retain the proper amount of magnesium. If you suddenly increase thyroid when you've been deficient, your leg muscles will compete with your heart muscles" for available magnesium.

This competition explains why some people experience heart rhythm disturbances when starting thyroid supplementation—not because thyroid is harmful to the heart, but because increasing metabolic rate in a magnesium-depleted body forces the heart to compete with suddenly more active skeletal muscles for an insufficient supply of the mineral both require.

## Deficiency Symptoms

Because magnesium is central to the ATP energy system, its deficiency produces symptoms across virtually every organ system. As Peat stated, "A magnesium deficiency will cause seizures, insomnia, and inflammation." The seizure connection reflects magnesium's role in opposing calcium-driven excitation in neurons. The insomnia reflects the inability of neurons and muscles to return to their resting state. The inflammation reflects the cascade of calcium-activated enzymes—phospholipases, proteases, caspases—that become active when intracellular calcium rises unopposed.

Cramps, spasms, constipation, anxiety, elevated blood pressure, and irregular heartbeat are all consistent with the basic mechanism: insufficient ATP-magnesium complexes to maintain the relaxed, energized cellular state.

## Practical Considerations

Peat regarded food sources of magnesium—fruits, well-cooked greens, coffee, chocolate—as preferable to isolated supplements, though he acknowledged that modern diets and depleted soils often make supplementation necessary. The form matters less than the context: magnesium taken without attention to thyroid status, calcium balance, and overall energy production treats a number without addressing the system.

[[Carbon dioxide]] retention—supported by adequate thyroid function, bag breathing, and avoidance of hyperventilation—helps cells retain both magnesium and potassium. [[Progesterone]] supports magnesium retention by opposing [[estrogen]]'s tendency to promote calcium influx and cellular excitation. The mineral balance inside cells ultimately reflects the hormonal and metabolic balance of the whole organism.
