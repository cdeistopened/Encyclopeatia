# Calcium

The conventional story about calcium is that Americans do not get enough of it, which is why they develop osteoporosis. Ray Peat's framework reveals something more interesting: the problem is rarely insufficient calcium in the diet but rather what happens to calcium when the body's energy systems fail. In a well-functioning metabolism, calcium stays where it belongs—in bones and teeth, in tightly regulated intracellular pulses. When energy production falters, calcium migrates into soft tissues, arteries, and the interior of cells, producing the calcification that medicine then tries to treat as a separate disease.

The key to understanding calcium in Peat's work is the [[parathyroid hormone]] (PTH) system. When dietary calcium drops too low—or when [[phosphate]] rises too high relative to calcium—the parathyroid glands secrete PTH to pull calcium from bones into the bloodstream. This keeps serum calcium stable but at a terrible cost.

## Parathyroid Hormone: The Hidden Damage

PTH does far more than dissolve bone. As Peat described in discussions on calcium and hormones, PTH blocks oxygen utilization in all tissues, shifting cells toward [[lactic acid]] production. The lactic acid then dissolves more bone mineral, releasing more calcium into the blood in a self-reinforcing cycle. Peat compared PTH's effects to those of other stress mediators—[[prolactin]], [[serotonin]], [[estrogen]]—that share the pattern of impairing oxidative [[metabolism]] while promoting inflammation and tissue breakdown.

As Peat explained in a [[2019-10-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-brain-barriers|2019 episode]], prolactin weakens bones through similar mechanisms and rises in tandem with TSH when thyroid function is low. The connection between [[hypothyroidism]], elevated PTH, and bone loss is central to Peat's understanding of [[osteoporosis]]—not as a calcium deficiency disease but as a metabolic energy disease.

The night is when PTH does its worst damage. As Peat noted in his newsletter *Adaptogenic Milk*, nocturnal PTH surges—driven by the natural drop in blood calcium during sleep—contribute to increased blood pressure, disturbed sleep, and accelerated bone loss. Drinking [[milk]] before bed suppresses these surges by keeping calcium available through the night.

## The Calcium-Phosphate Ratio

Peat consistently emphasized the ratio of calcium to phosphate rather than the absolute amount of either mineral. Modern diets are heavily skewed toward phosphate—present in high concentrations in meat, grains, soft drinks, and processed foods—while calcium intake has declined. This imbalance drives chronic PTH elevation even when total calcium intake appears adequate.

A high calcium-to-phosphate ratio suppresses PTH, which in turn allows cells to use oxygen efficiently and keeps calcium deposited in bones rather than soft tissues. As Peat explained in a conversation with Georgi Dinkov, calcium accumulates inside de-energized cells precisely because those cells lack the ATP needed to pump calcium back out. PTH amplifies this problem by further impairing cellular energy production.

This explains the apparent paradox of calcification: the body has too much calcium in the wrong places and too little in the right places, simultaneously. The solution is not to restrict calcium but to restore the energy systems that maintain its proper distribution—adequate [[thyroid]], [[progesterone]], [[carbon dioxide]], and [[magnesium]].

## Why Milk Is Central

Peat recommended [[milk]] as the ideal calcium source because it provides calcium in a form that naturally balances phosphate. Two quarts of milk daily, combined with adequate [[vitamin D]] (Peat suggested maintaining blood levels around 50 ng/mL), effectively suppresses PTH and supports bone density without the complications of isolated calcium supplements.

The evidence supports the recommendation broadly. As Peat noted, milk drinkers tend to be the least obese—citing the Masai as an example of a population consuming large quantities of dairy with excellent metabolic health. The calcium in milk also suppresses the stress hormones that promote fat storage and [[inflammation]].

For supplementation beyond dairy, Peat recommended powdered eggshells as the best calcium supplement and oyster shell calcium as the second choice. He preferred these whole-food calcium sources over refined calcium carbonate or citrate supplements, which lack the trace minerals present in natural calcium sources.

## Calcium, Magnesium, and Energy

The relationship between calcium and [[magnesium]] is fundamental to cellular function. As Peat explained in his newsletter *Progesterone, Thyroid, Cancer*, progesterone, thyroid hormone, and carbon dioxide all protect cells by favoring intracellular magnesium over calcium. When energy fails, calcium floods into cells and displaces magnesium, activating inflammatory cascades and impairing mitochondrial function.

Adequate dietary magnesium is what Peat called "extremely important"—not as a calcium antagonist but as a partner in maintaining the proper intracellular mineral balance that healthy energy production requires.

## The Calcification Cascade

In Peat's framework, calcification is not a random consequence of aging but the final stage of a specific degenerative sequence. As he described in his newsletter *Estrogen, Calcium, Heavy Metals, and Nerve Degeneration*, the progression runs: [[inflammation]] → edema → fibrosis → calcification. Each stage represents a further deterioration of cellular energy and structure.

The calcification begins in [[mitochondria]]—the very organelles responsible for energy production—and progresses outward to connective tissue, arteries, and joints. [[Vitamin K]] plays a critical role by directing calcium toward bones and away from soft tissues. Combined with vitamin D, adequate calcium intake, and thyroid support, vitamin K helps maintain the proper calcium distribution that the body's energy systems would normally ensure on their own.
