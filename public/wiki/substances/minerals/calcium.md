# Calcium

Calcium is the mineral that mainstream medicine gets exactly backwards. The conventional story is simple: calcium builds bones, and too much of it causes dangerous calcification of arteries and soft tissues. Ray Peat spent decades arguing that this narrative inverts causality. Dietary calcium deficiency is what drives soft tissue calcification, because the body compensates for low dietary calcium by raising [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]] (PTH), which pulls calcium out of bones and deposits it precisely where it should not go. "Some people who know about the involvement of calcium in aging, stress, and degeneration suggest eating a low calcium diet," Peat wrote in his [[calcium-and-disease-hypertension-organ-calcification-shock-vs-respiratory-energy|"Calcium and Disease" (2009)]] newsletter, "but since we all have skeletons, dietary calcium restriction can't protect our cells, and in fact, it usually intensifies the process of calcification of the soft tissues." The solution to calcium-related disease is not less calcium but more of it, in the right form, in the right ratio to [[phosphate]], and supported by [[vitamin-d|vitamin D]], [[magnesium]], and adequate [[thyroid]] function. Peat himself drank up to a gallon of [[milk]] per day for over thirty-five years and recommended that others aim for at least 2,000 mg of dietary calcium daily, primarily from dairy.

## The Calcium Paradox

A calcium-deficient diet produces the very problems that people associate with calcium excess. This is not a minor irony but a central mechanism of degeneration.

When cells are stressed or dying, they lose [[magnesium]] and potassium while taking up sodium and calcium. The mitochondria can buffer some calcium during stress, but past a threshold the accumulated calcium inactivates them, forcing cells into glycolytic energy production and excess [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]. This intracellular calcium overload excites cells at the same time it inhibits their energy production, driving inflammation, [[fibrosis]], and eventually gross calcification.

Low dietary calcium makes this worse, not better. When the diet provides insufficient calcium, parathyroid hormone rises to maintain blood calcium levels by stripping it from bones. But PTH does far more than mobilize bone calcium. It inhibits cellular respiration, activates glycolysis, increases [[serotonin]] and [[histamine]] release, and promotes the inflammatory cascade that deposits calcium in soft tissues. As Peat explained on a [[2012-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-phosphate-and-calcium-metabolism|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on phosphate and calcium metabolism]]: "When you're low in calcium in your diet, you're increasing the parathyroid hormone to take calcium out of your bones to make up for what you're not eating. The parathyroid hormone releases serotonin and histamine, causing more inflammation and more calcification of the tissues: arteries, kidneys, brain cells, everywhere except the bones."

The result is the aging pattern Peat described as universal: bones thin while soft tissues calcify. "At the age of 90, the amount of calcium in the elastic layer of an artery is about 35 times greater than at the age of 20." This is not calcium excess. It is calcium in the wrong place, driven there by the hormonal response to calcium deficiency.

Adele Davis recognized this half a century earlier. Peat quoted her in a 2020 *Generative Energy* episode on calcium and phosphate: "It is extremely important to realize that calcium deposits in soft tissues become worse when the diet is low in calcium." His comment was characteristically blunt: "It's just horrifying to see how the medical journals now, at least 90% of the people talking about calcium, completely miss that information that she had back in 1960."

## Parathyroid Hormone: The Central Mechanism

PTH responds to calcium or magnesium deficiency by increasing calcium absorption and mobilizing it from bones. In doing so, it initiates a cascade of anti-metabolic effects. It inhibits cellular respiration and activates glycolysis, lowering ATP and shifting metabolism toward lactic acid production rather than [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]]. It causes diuresis, hypertension, increased cell division, and cartilage growth. "The antimetabolic actions of PTH mimic those seen in aging and diabetes," Peat wrote, "and surgical removal of the parathyroid glands has been known to eliminate diabetes."

PTH sits at the center of a feedback loop involving several of Peat's major stress mediators. [[Prolactin]], increased by [[estrogen]] or serotonin, causes the body to lose calcium from bones while stimulating PTH secretion. Prolactin acts on bone partly by increasing [[interleukin-6]] (IL-6) formation, and IL-6 in turn stimulates prolactin release. Serotonin and IL-6 stimulate each other, and PTH and serotonin each stimulate the other's release. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle that strips calcium from bone and deposits it in arteries, kidneys, and brain.

Peat framed this comprehensively on the [[2011-06-03-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-milk-calcium-and-hormones|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on milk, calcium and hormones]]: "Calcium is potentially the thing that causes all of the problems: allergies, cancer, heart attacks. But when calcium is regulated properly, it protects against all of those things." The regulation depends on keeping PTH low, and the first lever for doing that is eating enough calcium.

The clinical evidence from kidney dialysis patients reinforced his point. "Since they've been doing dialysis for kidney disease, we've gained better insight into how PTH works," he explained on the same show. "They found that if they remove the parathyroid gland entirely, they can solve many of the deadly effects of chronic kidney dialysis. Hypertension and insomnia, for example, are immediately corrected by removing the parathyroid gland." The range of conditions that improved after parathyroidectomy gave Peat a working catalog of everything dietary calcium could address by suppressing PTH without surgery.

## Carbon Dioxide: The Master Regulator

Behind dietary calcium and vitamin D, the deepest regulator of calcium distribution in Peat's framework is carbon dioxide. CO2 keeps calcium in its proper extracellular location through a mechanism he described in detail on a *Generative Energy* episode on CO2 and mineral balance: "CO2, one of its actions in the bloodstream is to form compounds, not exactly calcium bicarbonate, but complex ionic compounds, keeping calcium in an ionized state. When it's in this ionized, water-soluble state, it isn't in a state that has an affinity for cells."

The practical demonstration is hyperventilation. "If you hyperventilate for a minute or two, you'll notice that your fingers, hands, and feet tend to cramp up. Losing the calcium outside of cells in the water-soluble state, along with other changes happening with the loss of carbon dioxide, causes the calcium to go into cells instead of being held outside by the CO2."

This links calcium metabolism directly to [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]] and [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]]. When cells produce CO2 efficiently through mitochondrial respiration, the streaming flow of CO2 carries calcium out of the cell, keeping it in the extracellular space where it belongs. When oxidative metabolism fails and lactic acid production takes over, as happens in [[hypothyroidism]], calcium migrates into cells, causing excitation, inflammation, and eventual calcification. "Part of the inhibition is that carbon dioxide produced in the cell forces calcium to leave the cell," Peat explained on the EastWest Healing episode. "That relaxes the cell because calcium should be outside the cell."

The connection to bone is equally direct. In tissue culture experiments with mouse skull bone, PTH dissolves bone by causing it to produce lactic acid. "But if you increase the CO2, it directly starts forming crystals of calcium carbonate and restoring the mineral structure of the bone." Peat described respiration and carbon dioxide as "the basic antagonists of PTH." At birth, a baby has practically no PTH, probably because of the high intrauterine CO2 concentration. The metabolic lesson: anything that supports CO2 production, primarily thyroid hormone, supports proper calcium handling. Anything that impairs it shifts calcium into the degenerative pattern.

## The Calcium-to-Phosphorus Ratio

Dietary calcium cannot be understood in isolation from [[phosphate]]. The ratio between phosphate and calcium in the diet is the primary signal that activates or suppresses PTH. As Peat stated directly on the 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode: "The ratio between phosphate and calcium is the main thing that activates the PTH. Minimizing phosphate or increasing calcium is extremely important."

The problem is that the modern diet is heavily weighted toward phosphate. Grains, beans, and meats are all high in phosphate and low in calcium. Meat can run as high as 10 to 1 for phosphate over calcium. "A lot of people habitually eat a ratio of roughly five, six, or seven times as much phosphate as calcium," Peat observed. "You probably shouldn't exceed about two parts of phosphate for each calcium." In an [[calcium|email exchange on calcium]], he confirmed that a 2:1 calcium-to-phosphorus ratio is safe and that even 1:1 is probably acceptable, though the ideal has not been clearly defined.

Milk and cheese solve this ratio problem elegantly. Cow's milk has a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of about 1.3 to 1, because cows naturally prefer to eat leaves, which are the plant source of calcium with the least phosphate. "If you stick to a mostly milk and cheese-centered diet for your proteins, you'll stay close to a one-to-one ratio," Peat advised. Even when meat is consumed alongside dairy, the high calcium content of milk brings the overall ratio into an acceptable range.

The consequences of chronic phosphate excess extend well beyond PTH activation. In his [[phosphate-activation-and-aging|"Phosphate, Activation, and Aging" (2013)]] newsletter, Peat described research showing that "excess phosphate can increase inflammation, tissue atrophy, calcification of blood vessels, [[cancer]], dementia, and, in general, the processes of aging." He highlighted the *Klotho* mutant mouse, which aged rapidly and displayed most features of human aging, from respiratory failure to arterial hardening to [[osteoporosis]]. The Klotho gene product regulates phosphate metabolism, and its absence creates a model of accelerated aging driven largely by phosphate excess. The industrial food system worsens the problem: phosphate salts are now standard meat additives, pushing the dietary ratio further in the wrong direction without most people being aware of it.

## Dairy as the Ideal Calcium Source

Peat's calcium recommendation was inseparable from his recommendation of dairy. "Milk is, next to eggshells, our best source of calcium," he stated on the EastWest Healing episode. A quart provides roughly 1,200 to 1,250 mg of calcium against just under 1,000 mg of phosphate. Three pints daily yields about 2,000 mg, his baseline recommendation.

Peat's case for dairy went beyond its mineral profile. Milk's calcium inhibits fatty acid synthase (the enzyme involved in fat storage) while simultaneously activating uncoupling proteins in the mitochondria that burn calories and protect against free radical oxidation. "Milk is the only food that does both of those things simultaneously," he said. In his [[milk-in-context-allergies-ecology-and-some-myths|"Milk in Context" (2011)]] newsletter, he connected this to the metabolic syndrome: "When there is adequate calcium, vitamin D, and magnesium in the diet, PTH is kept to a minimum. When PTH is kept low, cells increase their formation of the uncoupling proteins." The metabolic syndrome, involving [[diabetes]], hypertension, and [[obesity]], is associated with high PTH.

Peat also cited evidence linking milk to protection against [[dementia]] (through uncoupling proteins) and cancer (through fatty acid synthase inhibition). His own practice was extreme by conventional standards: up to a gallon of milk per day for thirty-five years, reducing to two or three quarts when he could get large quantities of [[oranges|orange juice]]. The Maasai and other cattle-raising peoples, he noted, often consume 5,000 mg of calcium daily from milk.

In an [[parathyroid-hormone-pth|email on parathyroid hormone]], Peat reported: "I regularly use at least two quarts of milk per day, in the past I have averaged a gallon a day, high calcium intake helps to compensate for low vitamin D, but both vitamin D and calcium in the diet tend to lower parathyroid hormone, and the serum calcium level." More dietary calcium produces lower serum calcium because PTH suppression stops the body from pulling excess calcium into the bloodstream from bone.

## Eggshell Calcium

For those unable to consume sufficient dairy, Peat's preferred supplemental calcium was powdered eggshell. "Eggshells are the purest form of calcium carbonate," he stated on the EastWest Healing episode. "They are even cleaner than oyster shells."

The preparation is straightforward: boil eggs to remove additives from the shells, then pulverize the shells into a fine powder. A quarter to a half teaspoon provides substantial calcium. Sarah Murray, the KMUD interviewer, offered a practical formulation on the 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode: "If you take a quarter teaspoon three times a day with meals, that's about 2,000 milligrams."

The purity advantage matters. Peat noted that "chemical analyses comparing eggshells to commercial supplements find that the lowest concentration of toxic heavy metals is in eggshells. Oyster shells are the next cleanest. Old cows used for bone meal often have high lead content." In email correspondence, he was equally direct: "Powdered eggshells are the best calcium supplement, oyster shells are the next best."

Beyond purity, the form of calcium matters pharmacologically. Calcium carbonate, the form found in eggshells, releases carbon dioxide as it is metabolized. This is significant because CO2 stimulates bone formation. Calcium citrate, by contrast, which the supplement industry heavily markets as the most absorbable form, actually causes calcium to be lost in the urine. "Citric acid itself causes you to lose calcium in your urine," Peat explained on the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hair loss, inflammation, and osteoporosis]]. "Calcium carbonate isn't quite as soluble, but eventually, it has a chance to absorb. It is a very effective and safe supplement because the carbon dioxide is the form that stimulates bone formation rather than breaking it down."

Among plant sources, turnip greens have about 10 times as much calcium as phosphate, but the cabbage family is anti-thyroid and all leaves carry unsaturated fats that can interfere with protein digestion. "Otherwise, milk and cheese are pretty much the alternative sources, unless you want to grind up eggshells," Peat concluded.

## Calcium and Blood Pressure

One of the most practically significant applications of Peat's calcium framework is its relationship to hypertension. For decades, the public health message has been that sodium causes high blood pressure. Peat cited the work of David McCarron, who noticed that the government's own data on diet and hypertension showed the opposite pattern. "The people who ate the most salt had the lowest blood pressure, and those who ate the least salt had the highest pressure," Peat wrote in the "Calcium and Disease" newsletter. "He showed that a calcium deficiency, rather than a sodium excess, was the most likely nutritional explanation for hypertension."

The mechanism connects directly to PTH and arterial tone. "The tone of the small arteries is very responsive to calcium," Peat explained on the 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode. "If you're low in calcium, your blood pressure goes up. For about 30 years, David McCarron has been saying it's not sodium that causes high blood pressure, it's calcium deficiency. Eating extra calcium can often cure hypertension."

Population-level data supports this. Mortality rates from arteriosclerotic heart disease and cancer are lower in regions with hard water, which often contains large amounts of calcium or magnesium. The protective factor is not sodium's absence but calcium's presence.

## Osteoporosis and Bone Formation

Peat's view of osteoporosis inverts the conventional treatment approach. Standard medical practice prescribes [[estrogen]] replacement and bisphosphonate drugs; Peat considered both counterproductive. His approach centered on suppressing PTH through dietary calcium and vitamin D, supporting thyroid function, and providing the metabolic conditions for CO2-driven bone mineralization. "Everyone's bones get thinner with aging, which corresponds to the loss of muscle tissue and the calcification of soft tissues," he said on the 2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on osteoporosis. "All of that is under the control of energy metabolism."

Most bone calcium loss occurs during the night, when PTH cycles upward alongside prolactin, [[cortisol]], and other stress hormones. [[Aspirin]] emerged as an underappreciated tool for bone health: "People seldom talk about it, but aspirin improves calcium retention in bones," Peat noted, adding that [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] should accompany it. "Vitamin K protects your arteries from calcification and allows the bone to use carbon dioxide to bind calcium into the bone."

[[Progesterone]] plays a parallel protective role, increasing CO2 content of the tissues and inhibiting the inflammatory mediators that drive both calcification and osteoporosis. Even [[baking-soda|baking soda]] contributes by helping the kidneys excrete excess phosphate while directing calcium into bones. Peat also noted that SSRI antidepressants cause osteoporosis through serotonin's interference with bone metabolism, another confirmation that the stress mediators driving soft tissue calcification are the same ones stripping calcium from the skeleton.

## Practical Recommendations

Peat's practical calcium recommendations can be distilled from his consistent advice across decades of interviews and correspondence:

**Target intake:** At least 2,000 mg of calcium per day. This can be achieved through approximately three pints of milk, or two glasses of milk combined with eggshell powder, or a combination of dairy and boiled greens.

**Preferred sources:** Milk and cheese first. Eggshell powder for supplementation. Oyster shell as second choice. Boiled leafy greens (turnip greens especially) as a plant option, with awareness of their anti-thyroid and unsaturated fat content.

**Avoid:** Calcium citrate and calcium lactate supplements, which activate calcium loss through the urine. Bone meal supplements, which carry heavy metal contamination risk from old animals.

**Ratio management:** Keep phosphate intake low relative to calcium. Limit grains, beans, and high-phosphate meats to not more than 8 ounces a day when relying on dairy for the calcium-to-phosphorus balance.

**Supporting nutrients:** Vitamin D and magnesium work alongside calcium to suppress PTH. Vitamin K directs calcium into bones and away from arteries. Aspirin inhibits PTH actions and supports bone calcium retention. Adequate protein (at least 100 grams daily for active people) prevents the PTH elevation associated with low-protein diets.

**The paradox of high blood calcium:** A blood calcium level of 11, 12, or 13 (normal is 9-10) often indicates PTH excess driven by insufficient dietary calcium or excessive phosphate. "By getting them to eat more calcium, and maybe take Vitamin D and K, they can very quickly get their calcium down to normal," Peat explained. The counterintuitive prescription of more dietary calcium to lower blood calcium levels is one of the clearest practical expressions of his framework.
