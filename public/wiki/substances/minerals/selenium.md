# Selenium

Selenium is one of the few minerals whose biological role is so narrow and so non-negotiable that the body builds an exception to its own genetic code to handle it. The amino acid selenocysteine — the active form of selenium in human enzymes — is encoded by a stop codon read as an amino acid only in the presence of specific machinery. The cell goes to that trouble for about twenty-five selenoproteins, but two of them carry almost all of the metabolic weight that mattered to Ray Peat: the deiodinase enzymes that convert thyroxine to active T3, and the glutathione peroxidases that defuse the lipid peroxides produced by the thyroid gland's own iodination chemistry. "If your liver isn't getting enough glucose or selenium, it is unable to convert thyroxine into T3," Peat told Josh Rubin in a [[2011-02-22-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-thyroid|February 2011 East-West Healing interview]]. That single sentence captures why selenium matters in his framework. Without it, the thyroid axis stalls at the wrong hormone.

What it does not capture, and what most contemporary functional medicine misses, is the second half of the picture: the narrow window between adequacy and toxicity, the modest amount most American diets already supply, and the unimpressive evidence base for the supplement doses being recommended for autoimmune thyroid disease. Peat's selenium message is structurally identical to his iodine message and his vitamin D message. The nutrient is essential. The deficiency is real in specific geographies. The supplement is reasonable in narrow circumstances. The dose-response curve turns toxic faster than enthusiasts admit, and the answer is almost always one weekly meal of seafood rather than a daily pill from the health food store.

## The Two Enzymes That Justify the Trouble

The thyroid gland produces mostly thyroxine, T4, a storage hormone that does very little until peripheral tissues convert it into T3. Two-thirds of that conversion happens in the [[liver]] under conditions Peat outlined in nearly every interview that touched on hypothyroidism: adequate glucose, adequate selenium, and a liver not too busy clearing [[estrogen]] to do its other jobs. "The liver has to convert T4 to T3 for it to be effective. It needs glucose and selenium to make the conversion," he wrote in his [[thyroid|thyroid email correspondence]]. "Adequate protein, at least 80 grams per day, is necessary. Sea food, once a week will provide selenium, two quarts of milk and a quart of orange juice would provide many of the other essential nutrients."

The conversion enzyme is iodothyronine deiodinase. There are three of them, each containing a selenocysteine residue at the catalytic site, each clipping an iodine atom off a thyroid hormone molecule to change its shape and biological meaning. Type 1 deiodinase, abundant in the liver and kidney, produces the bulk of circulating T3. Type 2, expressed in the brain, pituitary, brown fat, and skeletal muscle, generates T3 locally for tissues that demand a tight setpoint. Type 3, in the placenta and developing brain, runs in reverse, inactivating thyroid hormone where development requires lower activity. All three depend on selenium. A selenium-deficient body can have normal TSH, normal T4, and still run hypothyroid at the cellular level because the conversion step has quietly failed. Peat called this out as one of the principal reasons "normal labs" do not rule out hypothyroidism. "If you were deficient in selenium, the correcting effect would be quick," he wrote, "but if there was a problem with intestinal flora, that would have to be taken care of before conversion was good" ([[thyroid|thyroid email]]).

The second enzyme is glutathione peroxidase. The thyroid gland generates hydrogen peroxide as a normal byproduct of iodine organification — the same chemistry that builds thyroid hormone also produces a steady stream of oxidative species that would damage the gland if left uncontrolled. Glutathione peroxidase uses [[glutathione]] and a selenocysteine residue to reduce hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides back to water and stable alcohols. The thyroid is, by tissue weight, one of the most selenium-dense organs in the body, and it is dense for exactly this reason. In a selenium-replete person eating a low-PUFA diet, the gland's antioxidant defenses keep pace with its own oxidative chemistry. In a person who is selenium-deficient, PUFA-loaded, and chronically iodine-flooded, the defenses fail, lipid peroxides accumulate, follicular cells are damaged, and the conditions for chronic thyroiditis are established. The whole mechanism is summarized inside [[hashimotos]]; the point in this article is that selenium sits at the chokepoint.

A third role bridges these two. Selenium-containing thioredoxin reductase recycles thioredoxin, the cell's main protein-disulfide manager, and contributes to keeping the cytoplasm in the reduced state required for normal enzyme function. Peat returned to this idea in his discussion of [[antioxidants]] with Andrew Murray on a [[2012-10-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidants|2012 KMUD episode]]: "Selenium activates the thyroid. By making the cell use oxygen and consuming all the potentially harmful electrons, the thyroid functions as an antioxidant. Even though its function is to increase oxidation, it prevents the random, harmful type of oxidative damage." The phrasing is dense and characteristic. Selenium does not work as an antioxidant by sponging up free radicals. It works by enabling the thyroid to keep [[oxidative-metabolism]] running smoothly, which is what prevents free radicals from being generated in the first place.

## Dietary Sources: The Weekly Seafood Meal

The Peat-shaped diet covers selenium adequately through a single weekly meal of seafood, augmented by ordinary intake of eggs, dairy, and well-raised meat. He came back to this prescription repeatedly. "Sea food, once a week will provide selenium," he wrote in the same [[thyroid|thyroid email]]. In a January 2019 [[2019-01-24-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fats-and-questions|One Radio Network interview]], he made the specifics explicit: "The low-fat fish and the shellfish. Shellfish are great sources of copper. Oysters are high in iron, but squid is very good; it's low in iron and high in copper, selenium, and iodine. Cod and sole are low-fat fish that are safe."

The pattern is consistent across his correspondence. Six medium oysters supply roughly 150 to 200 micrograms of selenium along with [[zinc]], [[copper]], [[iodine]], and B12 — a single serving covers a full day's needs three times over without isolating the mineral from its biological context. Squid is rich and low in iron. Sardines, anchovies, cod, and sole all deliver useful amounts. Shrimp is on his list. Tuna is selenium-rich but Peat warned against it because of mercury accumulation and PUFA content; the same reservation applies to most large predatory fish. Brazil nuts are famously concentrated, sometimes containing the entire RDA in a single nut, but Peat almost never recommended them as a primary source, and the variability between batches is large enough that a person aiming for a steady intake cannot rely on a fixed nut-count. Eggs supply useful selenium when the hens have grazed on selenium-replete soil. Beef and pork are moderate sources. Cow's milk is a low but reliable source. Liver, kidney, and other organ meats are good sources in proportion to the soil the animal grazed on.

The repeating logic in Peat's recommendations is that selenium travels with other things one wants. The seafood that delivers it also delivers iodine, the trace minerals deficient in inland soils, and the cholesterol-rich, low-PUFA animal protein the rest of his framework leans on. There is no need to think about selenium as a separable supplement when the diet is built around the foods that contain it. "Seafood, especially oysters, shrimp, squid, etc., would provide the iodine and selenium you need for good thyroid regulation," he wrote in an email about a low-carbohydrate diet inquiry ([[thyroid|ALL-EMAILS line 1083]]). The advice was always to fix the diet, not to add a pill.

## Soil Variation, Keshan Disease, and the Geography of Deficiency

The selenium content of plants tracks the selenium content of the soil they grew in, and soil selenium varies wildly by region. The mineral originates in volcanic activity and disperses into bedrock over geological timescales; some soils are rich in it (the high plains of the western United States, parts of Venezuela, much of Japan's coastal agriculture), and some are profoundly depleted (large swaths of China, Finland before remediation, parts of New Zealand, the southeastern United States in certain bands). The classic illustration of dietary insufficiency is Keshan disease, a juvenile cardiomyopathy first identified in selenium-poor counties of northeast China in the 1930s. Children in those areas died of heart failure at high rates; supplementing selenium in the local food supply eliminated the disease. This is the floor — the dose at which absence produces a recognizable, geographically clustered, dose-responsive pathology.

Peat acknowledged regional variation. "Things grown inland depend on whatever is in the soil, so they are often deficient in selenium and copper," he told Andrew Murray on a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|November 2011 KMUD episode]]. "The good thing about anything growing in the ocean is that it has access to selenium, iodine, and other trace minerals." In an [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|Eluv interview]] he made the same point about whole-diet selenium being regionally dependent: "Then there are just the regionally deficient things such as iodine and selenium; if you eat some kind of seafood, then you're getting the basic things for anti-stress foods."

In his [["Supplements" email file|email-supplements]] he was blunter about the deficiencies he considered most widespread in the American diet. "Most supplements contain enough impurities to eventually cause problems. Thyroid and aspirin are among the safest, and the most likely to be valuable indefinitely. It depends on where you live, but vitamin D3, vitamin K, and selenium deficiencies are extremely widespread." That last sentence is the closest Peat came to endorsing selenium as a default supplement, and the qualifier — "it depends on where you live" — does most of the work. For a person eating seafood weekly from any modern food supply, the deficiency is unlikely. For a person on a tightly inland, low-seafood, plant-heavy diet, it is possible.

## The Toxicity Threshold

Selenium has one of the narrowest therapeutic windows of any nutritionally essential mineral. The RDA is 55 micrograms per day for adults. A typical American intake, even without targeted supplementation, runs between 90 and 130 micrograms. The European Food Safety Authority sets the tolerable upper intake at 300 micrograms per day for adults; the US Institute of Medicine sets it at 400. Above 400 micrograms per day, sustained for weeks to months, selenosis appears. The symptoms include garlic-smelling breath, brittle nails with white lateral streaks or true onycholysis, hair loss in patches, peripheral neuropathy, fatigue, irritability, and gastrointestinal upset. Severe cases produce hepatic dysfunction and, rarely, cardiac symptoms. The 2008 American Brazil-nut supplement contamination incident, in which one product was mislabeled and delivered roughly 200 times the intended dose, sent over two hundred users to clinicians with the full selenosis syndrome.

Peat did not write often about selenosis at the published-essay level, but his preference for food sources over isolated supplements reflects the underlying caution. The arithmetic deserves stating because it is rarely stated cleanly in the autoimmune literature. A 200 microgram selenium tablet plus a typical American diet of 100 to 130 micrograms places daily intake right at the EFSA upper limit. Add a Brazil nut a day — one nut can carry 70 to 90 micrograms, sometimes more — and intake crosses 400. The Hashimoto's protocols that pair "200 mcg selenium" with "two Brazil nuts daily" are, on the wrong batch of nuts, frank toxicity protocols. Peat's rule of thumb — one seafood meal a week, no targeted pill in most cases — has the structural advantage of being uncrossable. It is very hard to develop selenosis from oysters.

## The Hashimoto's Selenium Pitch — and What the Evidence Actually Shows

For roughly two decades, functional and integrative medicine has promoted selenium supplementation as a cornerstone of [[hashimotos|Hashimoto's]] management. The pitch usually runs as follows: selenium-dependent glutathione peroxidase defends the gland from oxidative damage; selenium-dependent deiodinases run the conversion; supplementing 200 micrograms of selenomethionine per day reduces anti-TPO antibody titers and slows progression. Several randomized trials in Italian and Greek cohorts, conducted from the early 2000s onward, did show statistically significant TPO antibody reductions over six to twelve months — typically in the range of 20 to 40 percent. The original studies came out of populations with documented marginal selenium status. The protocol spread to North America, where soils and diets generally provide substantially more selenium, without much attention to whether the cohorts being treated resembled the cohorts in the trials.

Peat's framework accommodates the Italian results without endorsing the North American protocol. If the gland is under chronic oxidative stress, [[glutathione-peroxidase|glutathione-peroxidase]] activity is rate-limited by selenium availability, and antioxidant capacity is genuinely impaired by deficiency, then in a deficient cohort, supplementing should reduce the rate of follicular damage and the steady-state production of cleanup antibodies. The mechanism is straightforward. The empirical question is whether the cohort is deficient in the first place. Inland Italians and Greeks in those trial populations very probably were. American Hashimoto's patients eating a normal mixed diet plus iodized salt plus occasional seafood very probably are not. In the [[hashimotos]] article the protocol detail is given: 100 to 200 micrograms per day of selenomethionine, for a defined trial period of six to twelve months, with antibody monitoring, in a patient with documented or suspected deficiency — and oysters or sardines or a sardine-and-eggs week first. The functional medicine default — 200 micrograms forever, two Brazil nuts daily, in every Hashimoto's patient regardless of dietary baseline — is exactly the failure mode Peat's caution was designed to prevent.

There is a further wrinkle the selenium-for-Hashimoto's literature usually skirts. The same trials that report TPO antibody reduction generally do not report changes in clinical thyroid status, conversion efficiency, or symptom burden. Lower antibody titers in a patient still hypothyroid by every other measure are not a therapeutic endpoint. Peat's framework holds that the antibodies are downstream cleanup signals; lowering them by improving the gland's antioxidant defense is consistent with that view, but lowering them while the gland's underlying injury continues — iodine excess, estrogen dominance, gut [[endotoxin]], unrelieved [[cortisol]], persistent [[pufa|PUFA]] in membranes — does not constitute recovery. The selenium pill became popular because it was easy to prescribe and easy to verify. The harder upstream work, of the kind described in [[hypothyroid-recovery]], is what produces the actual clinical change.

## When Supplementation Makes Sense

In Peat's correspondence, selenium supplementation surfaces as an option in a few narrow circumstances. A patient with documented low serum selenium, especially one eating an inland, low-seafood, plant-heavy diet. A patient with active Hashimoto's flare, in whom a 6 to 12 month trial of 100 to 200 micrograms of selenomethionine is reasonable while the dietary and hormonal pillars of recovery are being rebuilt. A patient with rapidly progressing thyroid dysfunction in whom the antioxidant defense of the gland is plausibly rate-limiting. A patient with stress-related deficiency syndromes in which selenium and the B vitamins are reasonable bridge support. "If you are getting enough of the major nutrients, including protein, calcium, and sugar," Peat wrote in his [[supplements|supplements email]], "it's possible that you have a specific stress-related deficiency, for example of B6, niacinamide, or selenium."

Outside those circumstances, his answer is the same answer he gave to nearly every nutrient question. Eat better food. One seafood meal a week, [[eggs]], dairy, well-raised animal protein, a daily raw carrot, sunlight, sleep. If the diet is in place, the selenium question generally answers itself.

A few specific notes on form. Selenomethionine, the form found in food and in most well-made supplements, is well absorbed and incorporated into general protein turnover, which buffers it against acute toxicity but also takes longer to reach steady-state tissue levels. Sodium selenite, the inorganic form, is more rapidly bioavailable but less buffered against overdose. Selenocysteine itself is the active form in enzymes but is not generally available as a supplement; the body synthesizes it from selenomethionine or selenite. Peat's preference, where supplementation was warranted, was selenomethionine — the food form, the safer-margined form. He did not write at length about the question. His preferred selenium delivery was always a plate of oysters.

## Selenium and the PUFA Problem

One context in which Peat raised selenium often was the catastrophic combination of PUFA-rich diet and selenium deficiency. In his April 1996 newsletter [["BSE, Mad Cow, Scrapie, Etc.: Stimulated Amyloid Degeneration"|bse-mad-cow-scrapie-etc-stimulated-amyloid-degeneration-and-the-toxic-fats]], he reframed crazy chick disease and BSE-spectrum neurodegeneration as PUFA pathologies that veterinarians had misattributed to selenium deficiency: "Veterinarians are usually taught that it is caused by a selenium deficiency, but it is actually the result of an excess of PUFA in the diet, and is exacerbated by increased iron or other oxidants, and prevented by increased vitamin E, selenium, or substitution of saturated fats for the unsaturated." The cleaner formulation: selenium is the lid on a problem PUFA produces. Remove the PUFA and the lid does not have to work as hard.

He returned to the point in his [[1995-cholesterol-longevity-newsletter|"Cholesterol, Longevity, Intelligence, and Health" newsletter]]: "People who eat seafood get much more selenium in their diet than people who eat nothing from the sea, and selenium is one of the extremely protective nutrients that prevent atherosclerosis in animal experiments with excess cholesterol." And in [["Age Pigment: Cause and Effect of Aging and Stress"|age-pigment-cause-and-effect-of-aging-and-stress]] he listed selenium deficiency alongside PUFA excess and iron excess as one of the three manipulable accelerants of lipofuscin formation, the brown pigment that accumulates in aging cells when polyunsaturated fats and iron react under inadequate antioxidant defense.

The recurring frame: selenium's protective role is most visible against the background of PUFA exposure. In a diet built on saturated fats, ripe fruit, dairy, and gelatin, the selenium requirement is real but unspectacular. In a diet built on seed oils and inland grains, the deficiency surfaces faster and the consequences are larger.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream nutritional position on selenium is moderate and reasonable. The RDA is set at 55 micrograms per day on solid trial data. The tolerable upper intake is set at 400 micrograms per day with adequate margin. Most American intake falls comfortably between these bounds. The medical position on Hashimoto's selenium supplementation is mixed: the 2018 Cochrane review found the evidence "insufficient to inform decision-making," while several endocrine societies decline to recommend supplementation outside documented deficiency. Peat's caution and the mainstream's caution agree more than they disagree.

**The pro-supplement functional medicine position.** Practitioners who recommend selenium for every Hashimoto's patient point to the consistent antibody-reduction signal in randomized trials and argue that even in non-deficient patients, additional selenium improves glutathione peroxidase activity in tissues where local supply may be marginal. The position is not unreasonable for a defined trial period at a defined dose; it becomes unreasonable when the protocol becomes indefinite, when Brazil nuts are added on top, and when no antibody monitoring is built in. The risk of selenosis at the doses functional medicine recommends, in patients eating typical American diets, is real if small.

**The high-dose autoimmune position.** Some integrative practitioners recommend 400 to 600 micrograms per day for active Hashimoto's, citing the upper limit of trial doses and arguing that gland-specific antioxidant demand exceeds systemic demand. There is no good evidence for sustained high doses, and the toxicity threshold sits squarely within this range. Peat would treat this as the same overreach he attributed to high-dose iodine.

**The whole-food skepticism position.** A separate critique, with which Peat partially sympathized, holds that isolated selenium supplements are inferior to food sources because food delivers selenium along with the cofactors it travels with biologically — vitamin E, glutathione precursors, zinc, copper. Selenomethionine in a pill does not act in vivo the way selenocysteine-in-context does in oyster tissue. The empirical case for this is weak in the published literature but mechanistically plausible, and the practical implication — eat seafood, don't isolate the mineral — is exactly Peat's default.

**The "ignore the soil" position.** Some American nutritionists treat selenium adequacy as essentially universal in the modern food supply because long-distance distribution mixes selenium-rich and selenium-poor agricultural inputs at the grocery store. The argument has merit for the median consumer but fails for tightly local diets, organic-only diets restricted to a deficient region, and populations eating recently-grown produce from depleted soils. Geographic variation is still real for the margins.

## Data gaps

The dose-response curve between selenium intake and Hashimoto's outcomes has been characterized for TPO antibody titers but not for clinical thyroid status, long-term progression to overt hypothyroidism, or symptom burden. The relevant trial would compare selenium supplementation against full Peat-style upstream intervention — iodine restriction, anti-estrogen support, gut work, PUFA reduction, [[t3|T3]] supplementation where indicated — and that trial has not been done.

The interaction between selenium status and iodine tolerance is qualitatively described — selenium-replete patients tolerate higher iodine intakes — but not quantitatively. The exact threshold at which selenium supplementation rescues iodine sensitivity is unknown.

Selenium dosing in pregnancy and lactation is poorly characterized. The placenta concentrates selenium, the developing brain depends on deiodinase activity, and severe maternal deficiency is associated with adverse outcomes, but the supplemental dose range that is both safe and sufficient is not well-defined. Peat did not address this in detail.

The form-versus-form question — selenomethionine versus sodium selenite versus selenium yeast versus methylselenocysteine — is debated in the supplement literature without a clear winner. The food-form preference Peat held is intuitively defensible but not rigorously demonstrated in head-to-head trials.

The biomarker problem is unresolved. Serum selenium reflects recent intake; whole-blood selenium reflects medium-term status; selenoprotein P or glutathione peroxidase activity reflects functional sufficiency. Most clinical practice uses serum selenium and interprets it loosely. A patient with adequate serum selenium can still be functionally selenium-limited at the level of specific enzymes.

The pediatric data is thin. Selenium requirements for children, the appropriate supplemental dose in pediatric autoimmune thyroid disease, and the developmental implications of mild deficiency are not well characterized.

The interaction between selenium and the [[gut-microbiome|gut microbiome]] is emerging and unresolved. Some intestinal bacteria synthesize selenocompounds, some compete for dietary selenium, and the conversion of dietary selenomethionine to bioavailable forms depends partly on gut function. Peat noted intestinal flora as a modifier of thyroid conversion in general but did not address selenium-specific gut interactions.
