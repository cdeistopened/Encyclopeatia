# Vitamin A

Vitamin A occupies a unique position in Ray Peat's framework: it is the essential cofactor that allows [[thyroid]] hormone to convert [[cholesterol]] into protective steroid hormones. Without adequate vitamin A, the entire steroid cascade—[[pregnenolone]], [[progesterone]], [[DHEA]], [[testosterone]]—stalls regardless of how much cholesterol or thyroid hormone is available. As Peat stated in a [[2012-11-23-world-puja-world-puja-november-2012|2012 interview]], "Vitamin A is the main cofactor for the thyroid to be able to turn cholesterol into those hormones."

The relationship is so intimate that the body transports vitamin A and thyroid hormone on the same protein. As Peat explained in a [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 episode]], "Vitamin A and thyroid work so closely together biologically that the protein transporting them is a single protein called transthyretin"—literally the retinol-thyroid transport molecule. This is not a coincidence but a reflection of how deeply these two substances are intertwined in the body's energy and hormone production systems.

## The Cholesterol-to-Hormone Gateway

The practical consequence of vitamin A's role is enormous. When thyroid hormone and vitamin A nutrition are adequate, as Peat described in an interview on cholesterol and saturated fats, "the amount of progesterone coming out of an ovary or adrenal gland exactly corresponded to the amount of cholesterol going in. A very big portion of cholesterol is turned into protective hormones."

When either vitamin A or thyroid is deficient, cholesterol accumulates rather than converting into hormones. This is one mechanism Peat identified behind the age-related pattern of rising cholesterol alongside declining protective steroids. Medicine interprets the rising cholesterol as a disease requiring statin drugs; Peat saw it as a symptom of inadequate vitamin A and thyroid—the conversion machinery is broken, so the raw material piles up.

As he wrote in his newsletter *Aging Ovaries, Not the Eggs*, "cholesterol, vitamin A and efficient cellular respiration are essential factors for synthesizing progesterone. Accumulated [[iron]], unopposed [[estrogen]], and impaired use of cholesterol and oxygen are factors known to contribute to the widespread and variable damage" that characterizes reproductive and metabolic aging.

## Retinol vs. Carotene

Peat drew a sharp distinction between preformed vitamin A (retinol, found in animal foods) and beta-carotene (found in plants). Carotene is not vitamin A—it is a precursor that the body must convert, and this conversion depends on thyroid function and is highly inefficient in many people.

Worse, Peat argued that carotene has biological effects opposite to retinol. As he explained in a [[2019-05-21-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-may-2019|2019 interview]], "You want natural animal vitamin A, such as in eggs and liver. It is needed for making muscle protein and steroid hormones like progesterone and testosterone." He warned that carotene "turns off your metabolism and blocks testosterone production"—acting as an anti-thyroid, anti-androgenic substance rather than a vitamin A source.

This distinction explains why populations consuming large amounts of orange and yellow vegetables but little animal food can develop functional vitamin A deficiency despite apparently high "vitamin A" intake on paper. The carotene accumulates in tissues (producing the characteristic yellowish skin tint) without converting to the retinol the body actually needs.

## Vitamin A and Estrogen Opposition

Vitamin A supports the liver's ability to inactivate [[estrogen]], placing it within Peat's network of anti-estrogenic substances alongside [[progesterone]], [[vitamin E]], and [[aspirin]]. As Peat discussed in a [[2008-09-11-politics-and-science-politics-and-science-september-2008|2008 episode]], thyroid hormone activates liver metabolism to destroy estrogen, and this process requires vitamin A. When vitamin A is deficient, the liver's capacity to clear estrogen diminishes, allowing estrogen to accumulate and exert its inflammatory, anti-metabolic effects.

The connection to [[cancer]] follows directly. Peat's newsletters described how radiation destroys vitamin A in tissues in a manner similar to how estrogen acts—both create conditions of vitamin A deficiency that promote the shift from differentiated, specialized cell function toward the primitive proliferative state characteristic of malignancy.

## Food Sources

Peat recommended liver as the richest source of preformed vitamin A, followed by eggs, butter, and cream. These animal foods provide retinol in its immediately usable form, bypassing the conversion problems associated with carotene.

He cautioned against relying on supplements for very high doses, since vitamin A in excess can be toxic—particularly in the absence of adequate [[vitamin D]], which works synergistically with vitamin A. The fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and [[vitamin K|K]] function as a complex, and imbalances between them can be as problematic as deficiencies.

For most people, Peat's practical recommendation was regular consumption of liver (once or twice weekly), daily eggs, and liberal use of butter—providing vitamin A in the context of the saturated fats and other fat-soluble vitamins that support its proper utilization.
