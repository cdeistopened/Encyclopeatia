# Vitamin A

Vitamin A in Ray Peat's framework is not a single nutrient with a single deficiency disease but a steroid-adjacent hormone required for building every other steroid the body makes. Cholesterol cannot become pregnenolone without it; pregnenolone cannot become [[progesterone]], [[testosterone]], or DHEA in normal quantities when it is scarce; the [[thyroid]] hormone that regulates all of this cannot even be transported through the blood without sharing a carrier protein with it. Peat learned most of what he knew about the vitamin the hard way, treating his own intractable acne and ingrown whiskers with doses that would send a mainstream nutritionist into cardiac arrest. "I found that I had an extremely high vitamin A requirement, increased by stress or bright light, and that it related to thyroid function," he wrote in one of his detailed [[vitamin-a|email responses on vitamin A]]. "Usually, thyroid and vitamin A are the supplements that stop acne." The story that emerges across his newsletters, articles, and correspondence is not the story of a vitamin. It is the story of a small molecule that sits at the intersection of steroid synthesis, skin biology, cell differentiation, immunity, and the transport machinery that moves thyroid hormone around the body.

## Retinol, Not Carotene

Peat drew a sharp line between preformed vitamin A, retinol, which is found in animal foods, and its plant-based precursor, beta-carotene, which the body must convert to the active form. The line matters because, in his view, the conversion is unreliable in precisely the people who most need vitamin A. "I avoid carotene, because it blocks thyroid and steroid production," he wrote plainly in [[vitamin-a|one email exchange]]. "I use halibut liver oil-derived vitamin A, or retinyl palmitate."

The mechanism is a loop. Converting carotene to retinol requires thyroid hormone and adequate vitamin B12. When [[hypothyroidism|thyroid function]] is low, the conversion slows. Carotene accumulates in the tissues, including the calluses on the palms and soles, producing the faint orange tint Peat treated as a hypothyroid signal. "I found that when my need for vitamin A began to decrease I tended to accumulate carotene in my calluses; that happens when the thyroid function is lower, reducing the need for vitamin A." The carotene is itself antithyroid, so the deficiency it signals is the same deficiency that keeps it from being converted. Carotenemia, in this framework, is not a harmless cosmetic curiosity but a feedback loop pointing back at the thyroid.

The practical implication is counterintuitive for anyone raised on the food pyramid. Carrots, sweet potatoes, and dark leafy greens are not reliable sources of vitamin A for a person with a compromised metabolism. The animal foods Peat favored are. "Cream, butter, eggs, and liver are good sources of vitamin A," he wrote in the same email. "When people supplement thyroid and eat liver once or twice a week, their acne and dandruff (and many other problems) usually clear up very quickly." Liver was the food he mentioned most often, and the one that produced the most consistent clinical results.

## The Steroid Connection

The single most important thing Peat said about vitamin A is that it is a raw material for steroid synthesis. In his article [[cholesterol-longevity-intelligence-and-health|"Cholesterol, Longevity, Intelligence, and Health"]], he wrote that "thyroid hormone and vitamin A are used in the process of converting cholesterol into pregnenolone, the immediate precursor of progesterone and DHEA. Anything that interfered with these processes would be disastrous for the organism. The supply of cholesterol, thyroid and vitamin A must always be adequate for the production of steroid hormones and bile salts."

This puts vitamin A upstream of nearly every protective hormone in the body. Pregnenolone becomes progesterone, which opposes [[estrogen]] and stabilizes the thymus. It becomes DHEA, which has its own anti-estrogenic and anti-inflammatory effects. When vitamin A is scarce, the entire pregnenolone pipeline narrows, and the cell has less material with which to mount a hormonal response to stress. This is why Peat's "acne and dandruff" pairing of thyroid plus vitamin A produced such broad effects: the patients were not merely correcting a skin problem but restoring the synthetic capacity of every tissue that makes steroids. The skin is a major site of steroid metabolism, which is why the symptoms that first signal vitamin A deficiency tend to appear there.

In [[repairing-immunodeficiencies|"Repairing Immunodeficiencies"]], Peat tied the pipeline to the immune system. "If thyroid and vitamin A can't be used efficiently to form steroids, a steroid imbalance is likely. Unopposed cortisol is immunosuppressive in several ways, including thymic hypoplasia, depression of the histaminolytic activity and mono-oxygenase activity of the liver, contributing to chronic allergies." Vitamin A deficiency, in other words, is a cortisol-permissive state. Without the raw material to produce the steroids that oppose cortisol, the immune system drifts into the catabolic, allergic, and immunosuppressed profile that defines chronic illness.

Pregnenolone and progesterone return the favor. "Pregnenolone and progesterone have a vitamin A-sparing effect," Peat wrote, "besides their direct protective action for the thymus." The relationship runs both directions. Adequate steroids reduce the rate at which vitamin A is consumed, and adequate vitamin A permits the steroids to be made in the first place.

## The Transthyretin Bottleneck

One of Peat's most distinctive contributions to the vitamin A conversation is his focus on transport. Thyroid hormone and vitamin A share a carrier protein in the blood called transthyretin, also known as prealbumin. This is not an accident of biology. The protein exists because both molecules need to be moved quickly from one tissue to another, and both can bind firmly to transthyretin because of their highly unsaturated structure.

The highly unsaturated structure is also the problem. In [[oils-in-context|"Oils in Context"]], Peat explained: "Transthyretin (also called prealbumin) is important as a carrier of the thyroid hormone and vitamin A. The unsaturation of vitamin A and of thyroxin allow them to bind firmly with transthyretin and certain other proteins, but the unsaturated fatty acids are able to displace them, with an efficiency that increases with the number of double bonds, from linoleic (with two double bonds) through DHA (with six double bonds)." Polyunsaturated fatty acids from vegetable oils compete for the same binding sites. They bump thyroid hormone and vitamin A off the carrier, leaving the cells that needed them without their signal.

This reframes the story of the American diet. The seed oils that dominate the modern food supply do not simply contribute unwanted calories or oxidize into damaging byproducts. They actively interfere with the delivery of thyroid hormone and vitamin A to the tissues, producing a functional deficiency of both even in people whose intake looks adequate on paper. "While the competition by PUFA for protein binding sites blocks the effects of thyroid hormone and vitamin A, the action of PUFA on the sex steroid binding protein (SBP, or SSBG, for sex steroid binding globulin) increases the activity of estrogen." The same dietary shift that starves cells of vitamin A makes them hyper-responsive to estrogen.

Transthyretin itself collapses under stress. In [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|"TSH, Temperature, Pulse Rate, and Other Indicators in Hypothyroidism"]], Peat wrote that "transthyretin, which carries both vitamin A and thyroid hormones, is sharply decreased by stress, and should probably be regularly measured as part of the thyroid examination." The stressed patient has less of the protein that carries both, combined with more of the PUFA that displaces them. Two vectors of functional deficiency, operating simultaneously, on a system that cannot signal without the carrier.

## Skin, Cell Differentiation, and the Estrogen Antagonism

Peat's clinical interest in vitamin A started with his own skin. It expanded because the mechanism turned out to touch nearly every surface in the body. In [[blocking-tissue-destruction|"Blocking Tissue Destruction"]], he laid out what vitamin A does for tissue: "A deficiency of vitamin A causes excessive release of the lysosomal enzymes, acid hydrolases, resulting in tissue catabolism. Also, vitamin A is necessary for the proper differentiation of cells in skin and other membranes. A deficiency tends to cause an increased rate of cell division, with the production of abnormal cells, and a substitution of keratinized cells for other types."

Keratinization, the piling up of horny, dead-cell material on a surface, is the signature of vitamin A deficiency. It is also the signature of [[estrogen]] excess. "Vitamin A's effect on the skin opposes that of estrogen," Peat wrote. "Estrogen also promotes keratinization and speeds cell division. A deficiency of vitamin A can cause leukoplakia in the mouth and on the cervix of the uterus; although this is considered 'pre-cancerous,' I have found it to be very easily reversible." Vitamin A and estrogen are pulling on the same cellular steering wheel from opposite directions. When vitamin A is adequate, cells differentiate properly, division slows, and the tissue matures into its specialized form. When estrogen dominates, cells stay in an immature, proliferative, keratin-producing mode that looks like chronic low-grade injury.

The acne Peat treated in himself is the visible version of this. "Since vitamin A is highly unsaturated, in excess it suppresses the thyroid, so it has to be balanced with the thyroid; the combination is effective for increasing progesterone and decreasing estrogen, slowing the turnover of skin cells, and making the skin cells function longer before flaking off. Plugged pores, combined with a local shift toward synthesizing inflammatory substances, foster bacterial infection. Bright light stimulates the production of steroids, and consumes vitamin A very quickly, but when the balance is right, the acne clears up in just a day or two." The acne patient is a miniature tutorial in the whole system. Thyroid, vitamin A, progesterone, estrogen, light exposure, inflammation, and skin turnover all converge on a few square inches of facial skin.

Peat's broader reading of keratin itself was striking: "I suspect that the intracellular fiber, keratin, is produced when a cell can't afford to do anything more complex. Adequate vitamin A speeds protein synthesis, and allows it to be used more efficiently." Keratinization is a cellular surrender, the default response of a tissue that has run out of metabolic resources. Vitamin A is part of what keeps cells from defaulting.

## Toxicity and the Thyroid Balance

The counterweight to all of this is that vitamin A is genuinely toxic in excess. Peat did not deny this, but he reframed what the toxicity actually is. In his [[vitamin-a-toxicity|"Vitamin A Toxicity" (Townsend Letter for Doctors, October 1985)]] article, he wrote: "I think the symptoms associated with moderate amounts of vitamin A in susceptible people are the result of temporary suppression of thyroid function, which can result from an excess of any unsaturated oil. Vitamin E, which spares vitamin A, has a powerful effect in preventing toxicity from large doses of vitamin A. In traditional diets, people who ate fish livers also ate fish thyroids."

The reframing identifies thyroid suppression as the mechanism, makes the problem a hormonal imbalance rather than a direct poisoning, and explains why traditional peoples who ate fish liver did so in combination with fish thyroid. It also identifies [[vitamin-e]] as the specific protector, since vitamin E spares vitamin A from oxidative destruction.

Peat's own requirement shifted dramatically with circumstances. "For several years, when I had an extremely high metabolic rate, I needed 100,000 units per day during sunny weather to prevent acne and ingrown whiskers, but when I moved to a cloudy climate, suddenly that much was too much, and suppressed my thyroid. The average person is likely to be hypothyroid, and to need only 5,000 units per day." Metabolic rate, sun exposure, and stress moved his requirement across a twenty-fold range. The fixed RDA is, in this view, a statistical fiction masking enormous individual variability.

At extreme doses, toxicity is mostly rancidity. "The toxic effects of extremely big doses, such as 500,000 to a million i.u., seem to be from either oxidative processes (rancidity) that are prevented by adequate vitamin E, or by antithyroid effects." Vitamin A is a delicate, highly unsaturated molecule; in an inadequately protected body, it oxidizes into fragments that produce symptoms indistinguishable from deficiency. "Vitamin A oxidizes easily and an excess can create symptoms of a deficiency, so vitamin E is the most important thing for correcting it."

Peat warned sharply against cod liver oil as a vehicle. In [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration"]], he noted: "Between the first and second world wars, cod liver oil was recommended as a vitamin supplement, at first as a source of vitamin A, and later as a source of vitamins A and D. But in the late 1940s, experimenters used it as the main fat in dogs' diet, and found that they all died from cancer, while the dogs on a standard diet had only a 5% cancer mortality." Cod liver oil is a PUFA-dominated fat carrying a protective vitamin. The PUFA cancels the protection, and then some. This is why Peat preferred halibut liver oil or isolated retinyl palmitate for supplementation.

## Immunity, Fertility, and Cancer Prevention

Vitamin A's role in the steroid pipeline makes it a quiet factor in several conditions where it is not traditionally considered the main issue. In [[repairing-immunodeficiencies|"Repairing Immunodeficiencies"]], Peat listed it alongside the other anti-estrogen steroid-supporting factors: "Vitamin A, thyroid, progesterone, and the related steroid, dehydroepiandrosterone, all oppose estrogen, which has several immunosuppressive effects, including a cortisol-like thymic atrophy, hypoactivity of T cells, and reduced production of gamma-interferon and interleukin-2, reduced natural killer cell activity, and it probably has a role in the development of some auto-immune diseases." The vitamin A deficient person is simultaneously cortisol-dominant, estrogen-dominant, and immunosuppressed because all three conditions share the same metabolic root.

For prostate cancer, Peat was specific about where vitamin A fit in the prevention protocol. In his [[prostate-cancer|"Prostate Cancer"]] newsletter, he wrote that "thyroid supplementation, adequate animal protein, trace minerals, and vitamin A are the first things to consider in the prevention of prostate hypertrophy and cancer. Nutritional and endocrine support can be combined with rational anticancer treatments, since there is really no sharp line between different approaches that are aimed at achieving endocrine and immunological balance, without harming anything." The list is a pregnenolone-availability list. Protein provides the substrate for transport proteins; minerals support the enzymes; thyroid drives the rate; vitamin A is the cofactor that lets cholesterol become pregnenolone in the first place.

Vitamin A also figures in Peat's discussion of fertility through its role in steroid synthesis and its opposition to estrogen. The mechanisms that clear acne and reverse leukoplakia support the hormonal environment in which conception and gestation can proceed normally. A vitamin A deficient woman is a progesterone-deficient woman, whether the lab reads her progesterone as low or not.

In [[iron-s-dangers|"Iron's Dangers"]], Peat included vitamin A in his list of endogenous antioxidants alongside thyroxine, uric acid, biliverdin, selenium, iodine, sodium, magnesium, and lithium. In his view, both vitamin A and vitamin E are antioxidants and both are hormonally active; neither role can be severed from the other.

## Practical Guidelines

Peat's practical recommendations cluster around a few principles. The target is individual, not institutional. The average hypothyroid American probably needs no more than 5,000 IU per day, but anyone with elevated metabolism, stress, acne, or heavy sun exposure may need ten to twenty times that much. The form matters: retinyl palmitate or halibut liver oil, not cod liver oil, not carotene supplements. The partnership with vitamin E is non-negotiable. "About 100 i.u. of vitamin E would help to keep the vitamin A from being wasted by oxidation, and possibly could reduce your requirement for it."

Food first. Liver once or twice a week, supplemented by eggs, cream, and butter, meets most people's needs without the variability of a supplement. The calluses on the palms and soles serve as a tissue-level indicator of carotene accumulation and, by extension, of relative thyroid function. When the tissues are saturated with vitamin A and the thyroid is functioning, carotene is converted efficiently and the tissues stay clear. Supplementation should respect the thyroid balance, more in bright, sunny, high-metabolism conditions, less in cloudy, sedentary, hypothyroid ones. The thyroid itself can be supplemented in parallel, and the two together work better than either alone.

One final caution. A research abstract Peat cited noted that retinoid treatment through breastmilk can produce "hormonal imprinting" in offspring, causing "lifelong alterations at receptorial level." This reinforces caution about pharmaceutical retinoid derivatives (Accutane, tretinoin) in women of childbearing age, and Peat's general preference for nutritional over pharmaceutical sources of the vitamin.
