# Niacin

Niacin (vitamin B3) has two forms with very different effects in Ray Peat's framework. Nicotinic acid—the flushing form—increases histamine, [[serotonin]], and prostaglandins, making it inflammatory. Niacinamide—the non-flushing form—is the cellular active form that inhibits PARP, preserves NAD, and provides anti-aging benefits. This distinction matters enormously: one form promotes the inflammatory mediators Peat considered central to disease, while the other opposes them.

As Peat explained in a [[2014-01-01-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-patrick-timpone-dr-peat-answers-questions-regarding-health-die|2014 episode]], "Niacin sometimes refers to nicotinic acid. Nicotinic acid happens to increase your histamine, serotonin, and prostaglandins, all of which are inflammatory. I don't think it's good for anyone to take nicotinic acid other than as occurs in food. Niacinamide is a form of that same vitamin (B3) that is active in cells and doesn't release those inflammatory materials."

## PARP Inhibition

Niacinamide's primary therapeutic mechanism in Peat's framework is PARP inhibition. PARP (poly-ADP-ribose polymerase) is an enzyme that consumes NAD during DNA repair. In chronic stress and aging, PARP overactivity depletes NAD, reducing the cell's energy capacity.

In a [[2014-10-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-longevity|2014 episode on longevity]], Peat explained: "There are drugs that block that enzyme, which is called PARP. PARP-inhibiting enzymes can save cells from dying unnecessarily or let them go ahead and die. Niacinamide happens to be a very safe way to inhibit that enzyme. At the same time, it is the precursor for making NAD, so you're plugging the drain and filling up the tank at the same time."

This dual action—providing the raw material for NAD while preventing its wasteful consumption—makes niacinamide uniquely protective against cellular aging.

## The Tryptophan Connection

The body can synthesize niacin from [[tryptophan]], but this pathway is vulnerable to disruption. As Peat noted, "We can normally make that from proteins—converting tryptophan to niacin—but if we're deficient in protein or have a vitamin B6 deficiency, that conversion doesn't happen."

[[PUFA]] and [[estrogen]] divert tryptophan metabolism away from niacin toward serotonin and toxic metabolites like quinolinic acid. This means people with metabolic dysfunction may need dietary or supplemental niacin even if their protein intake would theoretically supply enough tryptophan.

## Form Matters

The emphasis on niacinamide rather than nicotinic acid reflects Peat's understanding of [[inflammation]]. Nicotinic acid causes flushing precisely because it triggers histamine release—the same inflammatory cascade that contributes to allergies, asthma, and other conditions.

Many commercial B-vitamin supplements contain nicotinic acid because it's cheaper. Peat's advice: seek out niacinamide specifically, or obtain niacin from food sources where it occurs in its natural bound forms.
