# Vitamin C

Vitamin C is the most thoroughly misframed nutrient of the twentieth century. The molecule isolated from adrenal cortex in 1928 and from paprika in 1932 by [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Albert Szent-Györgyi]] — who was awarded the 1937 Nobel Prize for that work and for his reconstruction of the dicarboxylic acid sequence of the citric acid cycle — was, in its discoverer's own framework, a catalyst at the level of cellular respiration. The popular nutrition science of the postwar decades stripped this framing away and recast ascorbic acid as a "scavenging antioxidant," the same way it recast [[vitamin-e]] from an anti-estrogen into a passive guardian of seed oils. Linus Pauling then built a megadose tradition on top of the antioxidant frame, Robert Cathcart added a bowel-tolerance dosing protocol, and the synthetic-ascorbic-acid supplement industry — running on a corn-derived production process — became a global enterprise selling the most familiar bottle in any health-food store.

Peat read the whole tradition against Szent-Györgyi. The ratio of *dehydroascorbate* (the oxidized form) to *ascorbate* (the reduced form) reports the redox state of the cytoplasm. A healthy cell running full [[oxidative-metabolism]] sits with dehydroascorbate predominant, because the electrons ascorbate donates are being picked up and used by an actively respiring downstream chain. A stressed cell, a cancer cell, a hypoxic cell accumulates the reduced ascorbate form because the working electron acceptors downstream are missing. Vitamin C is a diagnostic, not a treatment. Pouring more ascorbate into a system already swimming in the reduced form does not flip the redox switch in the right direction; in some cases, especially in the presence of [[PUFA]] and unbound [[iron]], it makes things worse. The clinical bottom line is consistent with the [[anti-stress-diet]]: get vitamin C from orange juice, cabbage, and potato along with their natural bioflavonoid and mineral context; treat isolated synthetic ascorbic acid with skepticism, especially in megadose form.

## Biochemistry: The Ascorbate-Dehydroascorbate Cycle

Ascorbic acid is a six-carbon lactone — a sugar acid, structurally related to glucose — whose two enediol hydroxyl groups make it an unusually good electron donor at biological pH. The molecule donates one electron at a time. The first donation produces *semidehydroascorbate*, a relatively stable free-radical intermediate; the second produces *dehydroascorbate*, a tricarbonyl compound. Dehydroascorbate is itself reducible back to ascorbate by NADH and glutathione, completing the cycle. The redox couple ascorbate/dehydroascorbate is, in this sense, one of the cell's small-molecule electron-transfer pairs, parallel to NAD+/NADH and glutathione/GSSG.

The framing that distinguishes Peat's reading from textbook biochemistry is the claim that the *ratio* of the two forms is the diagnostic quantity. "The ordinary ascorbic acid (which contains one carbonyl group, activated by its conjugation with double bonded carbons) is, in the cell, oxidized to dehydroascorbate, a tricarbonyl, with a relatively stable free radical intermediate state, semidehydroascorbate. The oxidized form greatly predominates in healthy cells, but in cancer cells the reduced form is increased. This corresponds to the generally less oxidized state of cancer cells, or of normal cells that are in an excessively excited state, relative to the availability of oxygen," Peat wrote in his [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism* (July 2016)]]. This is Szent-Györgyi's framework, restated for a clinical reader.

In a healthy respiring cell, downstream electron acceptors — molecular oxygen, the respiratory chain, the protein-lattice "Hopkins electron pool" — pull electrons through the cytoplasm fast enough that small-molecule donors like ascorbate spend most of their time in the oxidized form. A cell in which dehydroascorbate accumulates is using oxygen well. A cell in which ascorbate accumulates has a downstream chain that has stalled — mitochondria that are not pulling electrons fast enough — and, by [[gilbert-ling|Ling's]] cardinal-adsorbant logic, has shifted toward the reduced, swollen, divisible state characteristic of [[cancer]] and stress. The tricarbonyl form of vitamin C is itself an electron-withdrawing molecule in the same class as [[methylglyoxal]], the dicarbonyl Szent-Györgyi identified as a candidate restraint on cell division.

Peat made the framing explicit in his late cancer writing: "Many of the flavonoids are known as 'antioxidants,' but, like ascorbic acid/dehydroascorbate, inside the functioning cell they become beneficial oxidative catalysts. In excessive doses they might act as reducing agents. Ascorbic acid itself in some situations increases mTOR" ([[carcinogenic-metabolism|*Carcinogenic Metabolism*, Q1 2022]]). A substance that is "an antioxidant" in a test tube can be a *pro-oxidative catalyst* inside a healthy cell. The categorical label does not survive the move from chemistry to biology.

## Food Sources: Cabbage, Orange Juice, Potato

Peat's recommendations for vitamin C were food-first and conservative. The three foods he cited most often — cabbage, orange juice, and potato — supply ascorbate at modest concentrations alongside the natural context the redox-indicator framework requires: sugars that support [[oxidative-metabolism]] downstream, [[bioflavonoids]] that Szent-Györgyi originally classed with vitamin C as "vitamin P," and the mineral cofactors the chemistry needs. The list extends naturally to acerola, rosehip, guava, and to liver and adrenal glands, but the three Peat returned to are the foods of the [[anti-stress-diet]] — accessible year-round, low in PUFA, high in supportive minerals.

[[orange-juice|Orange juice]] is the canonical case. Peat in *Sugar Issues* described the contrast directly: "When people were given a 300 calorie drink containing glucose, or fructose, or orange juice, those receiving the glucose had a large increase in oxidative and inflammatory stress (reactive oxygen species, and NF-kappaB binding), and those changes were absent in those receiving the fructose or orange juice (Ghanim, et al., 2007)." A drink of pure glucose at the same caloric load *increases* oxidative stress; the same calories from orange juice *do not*. The natural matrix of fructose, sucrose, ascorbate, bioflavonoids, magnesium, and potassium is the medium in which the ascorbate participates correctly. Stripping ascorbate out of that matrix and presenting it as crystalline powder is a different intervention from drinking the juice. A quart of OJ supplies roughly 250 mg of ascorbate along with its bioflavonoid and sugar context — well above the RDA and below the megadose threshold where contamination concerns become serious.

[[Potatoes]] are the unappreciated source. A well-cooked potato supplies meaningful ascorbate alongside potassium and easily digested starch. Cabbage and other [[cruciferous vegetables]] — well-cooked to neutralize their raw-state goitrogens — supply ascorbate along with sulfur compounds and fiber. The food-first hierarchy is the same as the rest of Peat's framework: the substance in question is rarely the limiting factor in isolation; the deficit is usually downstream metabolic capacity, and the supportive matrix matters as much as the headline nutrient.

## Bioflavonoids: Szent-Györgyi's Vitamin P

Szent-Györgyi's original characterization of vitamin C included a class of accompanying molecules he called "vitamin P" or "citrin," extracted from lemon and paprika juice along with ascorbate. The vitamin P fraction prevented purpura — subcutaneous capillary bleeding — at concentrations where pure ascorbate did not. By 1938 he had decided citrin (which he renamed *bioflavonoid*) was not strictly a vitamin and was "more like a drug, substituting for a natural regulatory factor that was missing." Peat developed this in his progesterone work: "Later research has confirmed that view, showing that the bioflavonoids inhibit the enzyme hyaluronidase, which degrades the ground substance of connective tissues." Bioflavonoids preserve capillary integrity by inhibiting hyaluronidase — the connective-tissue-degrading enzyme "activated by shock, and also by [[estrogen]]" — not by acting as antioxidants.

The natural matrix of vitamin C is therefore therapeutically distinct from isolated ascorbate. Citrus juice supplies hesperidin, quercetin, naringenin, and rutin; cabbage and potato supply other flavonoid classes. The clinical anti-purpura, capillary-strengthening effect Szent-Györgyi originally attributed to "vitamin C" was in significant part the bioflavonoid effect, and crystalline ascorbic acid alone does not reproduce it. This is the framing that justifies Peat's caution about high-dose supplemental ascorbate alongside his generous food-source recommendation: the supplement supplies the indicator without the regulatory drug.

## The Mainstream Framing: Pauling, Cathcart, IV Vitamin C

The dominant late-twentieth-century story about vitamin C descends from [[linus-pauling|Linus Pauling's]] 1970 book *Vitamin C and the Common Cold* and his subsequent collaborations with the Scottish surgeon [[ewan-cameron|Ewan Cameron]] on terminal cancer. Pauling argued, from a mix of evolutionary reasoning and Hoffer-Osmond's orthomolecular psychiatry, that the human RDA was wildly low — that most mammals synthesize ten to twenty grams of ascorbate per day, that humans lost the GULO gene for that synthesis somewhere on the primate line, and that the appropriate replacement dose should reflect species-normal production rather than the antiscorbutic minimum.

[[robert-cathcart|Robert Cathcart]] developed this into a clinical dosing protocol. He observed that the oral dose tolerated before diarrhea — "bowel tolerance" — rose dramatically during acute illness, from 4–10 grams in health to 100–200 grams in acute viral pneumonia or sepsis. Cathcart inferred that the gap reflected real tissue demand and dosed to 75–90% of bowel tolerance.

Intravenous vitamin C, pursued by Cameron and Pauling for terminal cancer and revived in the 2000s by Mark Levine at the NIH and the Riordan Clinic, sidesteps the oral absorption ceiling. IV ascorbate at 25–100 grams achieves plasma concentrations 50–100× higher than oral can produce — concentrations at which ascorbate behaves as a pro-oxidant, generating hydrogen peroxide selectively in catalase-poor tumor cells. Paul Marik's late-2010s sepsis protocol (IV ascorbate, hydrocortisone, thiamine) generated brief enthusiasm but was largely deflated by the CITRIS-ALI, VITAMINS, and LOVIT trials. [[liposomal-vitamin-c|Liposomal vitamin C]] claims oral-IV equivalence on pharmacokinetic data that are mixed at best.

These traditions share a frame: vitamin C is an antioxidant, more is better up to bowel tolerance, demand rises during stress, supplementation should be aggressive in acute illness. Peat did not deny that ascorbate sometimes helps in acute settings; he denied the framework that explains why.

## The Peat Reframe: Indicator, Not Treatment

If dehydroascorbate predominates in healthy cells and ascorbate accumulates in stress, the rational clinical question is not "how do I push more ascorbate into the patient" but "why is the downstream chain that would cycle ascorbate back to dehydroascorbate not working." Lactate is the parallel case: lactate is not the disease, it is the signal that oxidative metabolism has stalled. Treating the signal — buffering lactate, drinking gram doses of ascorbate — without restoring the metabolic capacity that produced the abnormal ratio is treating the thermometer instead of the fever.

Vitamin C supplementation is therefore rarely the bottleneck in a Peat-style intervention. Restoring thyroid function, eliminating PUFA, supplying sugar and CO2-producing carbohydrate, ensuring magnesium and copper sufficiency — these restore the downstream chain that lets dietary ascorbate cycle properly. Adding crystalline ascorbic acid to a still-hypothyroid, still-PUFA-burdened body does not fix the underlying redox imbalance and, in some configurations, makes it worse.

Peat's most direct statement of caution about supplemental ascorbic acid is the email exchange [[vitamin-c|"Vitamin C"]]:

> The trace impurities in synthetic ascorbic acid can increase free radical production, and quite a few people have allergic reactions to it. The situation isn't as clear with citric acid, but I think manufacturing impurities could account for some of the effects I see. For many years, I have been seeing more symptoms relieved by stopping all the chemical supplements, than by using them.

Synthetic ascorbic acid is produced through the [[reichstein-process|Reichstein process]] — sorbitol fermented from corn glucose, oxidized through several steps, and crystallized. The corn feedstock is now overwhelmingly genetically modified, and the process produces low levels of D-ascorbate enantiomer, oxidation byproducts, and residual catalysts. Whether these specifically produce the allergic and pro-oxidant responses Peat describes is not well documented in the published literature; his clinical observation that stopping the supplement relieved more symptoms than starting it was empiricism rather than pharmacology.

The same framing explains rising bowel tolerance in acute illness. A body under viral or bacterial stress has shifted toward the reduced state because oxidative metabolism is suppressed by [[endotoxin]], [[serotonin]], and inflammatory cytokines. The "demand" Cathcart saw was the body absorbing ascorbate into a system that could not cycle it. Whether the absorbed dose then helped or harmed depended on context: with bioflavonoids and sugar present, it could function as Szent-Györgyi's oxidative catalyst; with unbound iron and PUFA, it generated the carbonyl damage the alloxan-diabetes result demonstrated.

## The Iron Warning: Ascorbate as Mobilizer

Vitamin C is one of the most potent dietary enhancers of non-heme iron absorption — recommended as a public-health intervention in anemic populations and printed on iron-fortified cereal boxes. In Peat's framework, where adult [[iron]] excess — not deficiency — is the dominant concern after age 30, that effect inverts the public-health recommendation. From [[irons-dangers|*Iron's Dangers* (2006)]]:

> Vitamin C stimulates the absorption of iron, so it might be a good idea to avoid drinking orange juice at the same meal with iron-rich foods. A deficiency of copper causes our tissues to retain an excess of iron, so foods such as shrimp and oysters which contain abundant copper should be used regularly.

The practical consequence is to separate vitamin C from iron-rich meals — red meat, liver, iron-fortified grains. Beyond mobilization, ascorbate in the presence of free iron is a Fenton substrate: it reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺, and ferrous iron reduces hydrogen peroxide to the hydroxyl radical, the most damaging ROS in biology. This is the chemistry behind IV oncology pro-oxidant dosing — and at chronic lower doses in the presence of accumulated tissue iron, it contributes to the protein-carbonyl damage Peat highlighted from Miyata et al. 1998: "In vitro incubation of proteins with ascorbic acid accelerated the production of protein carbonyls as well as CML and pentosidine, and incubation with arachidonate accelerated the production of protein carbonyls as well as CML, MDA, and HNE. By contrast, incubation of proteins with glucose resulted in the production of CML and pentosidine, but not protein carbonyls" ([[suitable-fats-unsuitable-fats|*Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats*]]).

Ascorbic acid and arachidonate, *together*, generated protein carbonyl damage in vitro; glucose did not. Vitamin C in the presence of PUFA is the chemistry of advanced glycation and lipoxidation end-products — the damage mainstream nutrition science misattributes to dietary sugar.

## The Alloxan Diabetes Result

The most striking experimental result Peat cited against the antioxidant framing comes from Lazarow's lab. Alloxan is a uric-acid analog that destroys pancreatic beta cells through free-radical generation — the classic type 1 diabetes induction model. Sulfhydryl compounds (cysteine, glutathione, thioglycolic acid) protect beta cells when administered before alloxan. Naive antioxidant theory predicts ascorbate should also protect. From Peat's bracketed commentary on [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context*]]:

> While certain doses of cysteine, glutathione, and thioglycolic acid completely prevented alloxan diabetes, it was interesting that all of the rats receiving ascorbic acid became diabetic. To me, this argues for the free radical cause of diabetes, rather than just the sulfhydryl oxidation. Lazarow suggested that succinic dehydrogenase, and various other sulfhydryl enzymes, including those involved in fatty acid oxidation, might be involved.

*All* the ascorbate-treated rats developed diabetes. Peat read this as confirming that vitamin C, far from scavenging free radicals, was implicated in *generating* the damage alloxan produced — consistent with the iron-Fenton mechanism (alloxan generates superoxide, ascorbate accelerates conversion to hydroxyl radical, beta cells die). The result has not been replicated as a standard demonstration, but it cleanly inverts the antioxidant framing.

## Clinical: Collagen, Infection, Cancer Adjunct

Vitamin C is a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases — the enzymes that hydroxylate the proline and lysine residues in nascent [[collagen]] chains, allowing the triple helix to crosslink. Ascorbate deficiency produces scurvy: failed wound healing, capillary fragility, gum disease, joint pain, death. The antiscorbutic dose is small (historically 10 mg/day, modern RDA 75–90 mg); the collagen-synthesis enzymes saturate at quite low ascorbate concentrations. Food-source vitamin C in any reasonable diet supplies more than enough.

For infection and immune function, ascorbate is one of many catalytic small molecules supporting the phagocyte respiratory burst. Peat treated food-source ascorbate as sufficient and was unimpressed by the megadose-during-acute-illness tradition. His instinct in acute viral illness was sugar, salt, gelatin, sleep, and bag-breathing — restoring the metabolic capacity that lets ascorbate cycle — rather than gram doses of crystalline powder.

For cancer, the IV vitamin C pharmacological rationale (pro-oxidant tissue concentrations selectively damaging catalase-poor tumor cells) Peat did not deny but did not emphasize. His cancer framework — derived from Warburg, Szent-Györgyi, and Koch — was thyroid, CO2, sugar, and PUFA elimination, with [[methylglyoxal]] and the [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]] system as the load-bearing interventions. For sepsis, the [[marik-sepsis-protocol|Marik protocol]] has not survived randomized trials; the framing fits the redox-indicator picture badly.

## PUFA and the Carbonyl Damage Pathway

The most consequential downstream effect of vitamin C status — independent of dose — is its interaction with PUFA. The Miyata 1998 result is one of several showing that ascorbate plus arachidonate, incubated with protein, generates carbonyl damage that neither generates alone. The mechanism is Fenton chemistry plus [[lipid-peroxidation|lipid peroxidation]]: ascorbate reduces tissue iron, iron catalyzes peroxidation through PUFA-rich membranes, and the carbonyl breakdown products (4-HNE, MDA) modify nearby proteins.

Peat cited a parallel result in [[the-great-fish-oil-experiment|*The Great Fish Oil Experiment*]]: "Dietary docosahexaenoic acid-induced production of tissue lipid peroxides is not suppressed by higher intake of ascorbic acid in genetically scorbutic Osteogenic Disorder Shionogi/Shi-od/od rats." The scorbutic-rat model maps dietary dose cleanly onto tissue status, and the experiment failed to confirm the protection naive antioxidant theory predicted.

The clinical consequence is the iron warning generalized: the protective use of vitamin C requires a body with low PUFA load. In a body saturated with stored arachidonate and DHA, ascorbate becomes a Fenton-reaction substrate that accelerates damage rather than preventing it. Chain-breaker function and substrate context cannot be separated — the same logic that runs through the [[vitamin-e]] page.

## Counter-arguments

- **The Pauling-Cameron cancer studies.** Pauling and Cameron's 1976 and 1978 papers reported substantial survival benefit from 10 g/day IV and oral ascorbate in terminal cancer patients at Vale of Leven Hospital. The Mayo Clinic replications by Charles Moertel in 1979 and 1985 — using oral doses only — found no benefit. Pauling argued the Mayo trials used the wrong dosing route, population, and endpoints; Moertel argued the Vale of Leven studies were uncontrolled and selection-biased. Modern IV vitamin C oncology research has partially vindicated Pauling on the pharmacokinetic point (IV achieves pro-oxidant tissue concentrations oral cannot) but has not produced the survival benefits Cameron originally claimed. The mainstream oncology view in 2026 is that high-dose IV vitamin C is an adjunct of uncertain benefit — neither standard care nor disproven.

- **The Marik sepsis protocol.** Paul Marik's 2017 retrospective case series showed dramatic mortality reduction in septic shock with IV ascorbate, hydrocortisone, and thiamine. CITRIS-ALI (2019), VITAMINS (2020), ACTS (2021), and LOVIT (2022) failed to confirm and one suggested possible harm. Mainstream sepsis guidelines no longer recommend the protocol — a paradigm case of a promising open-label signal not surviving randomized confirmation.

- **The megadose tradition for colds.** Hemilä's Cochrane meta-analyses show modest reduction in cold duration with ≥1 g/day prophylactic dosing, no effect on incidence in general populations, possibly larger effect in subjects under heavy physical stress. The effect is real but modest, and food-source vitamin C supplies the relevant doses. The megadose-during-acute-illness intervention has weaker evidence and competes with bowel-tolerance side effects.

- **The mainstream RDA versus Linus Pauling Institute.** The 75–90 mg adult RDA was set against scurvy prevention and tissue-saturation pharmacokinetics. The Linus Pauling Institute argues 200–500 mg/day is the appropriate replacement for lost endogenous synthesis. The dispute is partly empirical (what plasma level is "saturated") and partly framework-dependent (saturation for what purpose). Peat's position sits closer to the conservative end but rejects the framework on which both sides agree — that more is monotonically better up to saturation.

- **The IV-vitamin-C pro-oxidant mechanism in cancer.** The pharmacological case (plasma concentrations only achievable IV generate selective hydrogen peroxide damage in catalase-poor tumor tissues) is supported by reasonable preclinical and phase I/II data (Levine at NIH; *Cancer Cell* 2017). Whether the mechanism translates to clinical survival benefit in any specific tumor type is an active research question. Peat acknowledged the pharmacological possibility without making it central to his cancer framework, which stayed with the Warburg/Szent-Györgyi metabolic reframe.

- **Liposomal vitamin C.** Marketing claims of IV-equivalent oral plasma levels rest on a mix of industry-funded and academic data with mixed quality. Better-controlled studies show modest improvement over standard oral dosing — not IV equivalence. The marketing has outrun the evidence.

- **The endogenous-synthesis evolutionary argument.** Pauling's claim that humans should "replace" the lost GULO-mediated synthesis with 10 g/day rests on extrapolation from goat and rat rates. Alternative readings (Drouin, Godin, and Pagé 2011) propose that the loss of GULO was selected for in primates whose frugivorous diet provided enough ascorbate that endogenous synthesis became a metabolic luxury, and that the appropriate intake is the dietary intake of a frugivorous primate — perhaps 200–500 mg/day from fruit — not 10 g/day from powder.

- **Bowel tolerance as physiological signal.** Cathcart's reading — that rising bowel tolerance during illness reflects rising tissue demand — is one possible interpretation. The alternative reading is that bowel tolerance reflects the inflamed bowel's increased absorption capacity rather than the tissue's increased capacity to use the absorbed dose.

- **Vitamin C as a respiratory catalyst.** Szent-Györgyi's early claim has been partially folded into modern biochemistry (vitamin C is a cofactor for several hydroxylases and a redox-active small molecule), but the strong form — that the dehydroascorbate/ascorbate ratio is the diagnostic redox marker of the cell — is not standard. Modern redox biology focuses on glutathione and NADPH ratios; ascorbate is treated as one component among many. Whether Szent-Györgyi's framing is correct and prematurely abandoned, or whether modern redox biology's deprioritization is better-grounded, is contested.

## Data gaps

- **Synthetic-ascorbate contamination profile.** Peat's clinical observation that "trace impurities in synthetic ascorbic acid can increase free radical production" is consistent with Reichstein-process byproducts, but the specific identities and concentrations in current pharmaceutical-grade vitamin C are not well characterized. Whether the corn feedstock contributes contamination (Bt proteins, residual aflatoxin, glyphosate) or whether the chemistry removes those species is not clear. The supplement industry has not been transparent about lot-level analytical data.

- **Quantitative dehydroascorbate/ascorbate ratios in tissue.** The Szent-Györgyi/Peat claim that the oxidized form predominates in health and the reduced form in cancer needs quantitative tissue measurements across disease states. Historical measurements were difficult because dehydroascorbate is unstable. Modern mass-spectrometry methods could close this gap and have only partially done so.

- **Iron mobilization by food-source vitamin C.** The non-heme iron absorption enhancement is established for supplemental ascorbate at >100 mg per meal; whether 25–50 mg from a typical orange juice serving produces clinically meaningful iron loading in iron-replete adults over decades is less well characterized.

- **Alloxan replication.** The Lazarow alloxan-diabetes result deserves modern replication with clear dose-response data. The historical record is thin on dose, route, and timing.

- **The bioflavonoid mechanism for capillary integrity.** Szent-Györgyi's anti-purpura observation is real and the hyaluronidase-inhibition mechanism Peat invoked is plausible. Quantitative dose-response, the specific bioflavonoid species responsible, and the ascorbate interaction are less well characterized than the basic phenomenon.

- **IV vitamin C oncology trials.** The pharmacological case is plausible; the clinical efficacy data are still phase I/II and patient-selection-dependent.

- **The carbonyl-damage pathway in vivo.** The Miyata et al. 1998 in vitro result that ascorbate + PUFA produces protein carbonyl damage that neither produces alone is consistent with Fenton chemistry but has not been developed into a quantitative in vivo model. The relative contribution to disease of ascorbate-driven versus other carbonyl sources is not well measured.

- **Peat's clinical population.** His observation that stopping the supplements relieved more symptoms than starting them reflects a self-selected population — correspondents to a famous bioenergetic writer, many already taking large doses. Whether the same observation would hold in a general population is unclear.

- **Liposomal-vitamin-C pharmacokinetics.** Independent rigorous PK comparisons of liposomal versus standard oral versus IV are sparse.
