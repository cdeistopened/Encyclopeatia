# Vitamin E

Vitamin E was one of Ray Peat's most consistently recommended supplements, and one of his sharpest case studies in the corruption of science by commercial interests. The compound identified in the 1920s and 1930s as an anti-estrogen, anti-clotting, fertility-restoring factor had been systematically redefined by the estrogen and seed oil industries into a mere guardian of polyunsaturated fats against oxidation. The suppression was so thorough that, as Peat noted in his dedicated newsletter [[vitamin-e-estrogen-antagonist-energy-promoter-and-anti-inflammatory|"Vitamin E" (2007)]], "as recently as the 1970s (and maybe later), university professors of dietetics were flatly saying 'no one needs vitamin E.'" Meanwhile, M.K. Horwitt, the man influential in designating vitamin E as a simple antioxidant, was privately taking 200 mg daily "plus [[aspirin]]." He apparently did not have much confidence in the ideas he advocated publicly.

## The Shute Family and the Anti-Estrogen Discovery

The modern history of therapeutic vitamin E begins with a Canadian physician named R.J. Shute and his sons, Wilfred and Evan. In 1933, Shute recognized that many pregnancies were complicated by circulatory crises, including "cyclic bleeding, thrombosis, stroke, and hypertension," particularly among poorly nourished women. Vitamin E had already been found to improve [[fertility]] in both male and female animals and to prevent intrauterine death, earning the name "antisterility vitamin." The Shutes suspected it could prevent miscarriages in human women, and their results confirmed the hypothesis.

What made the Shutes' work transformative, in Peat's view, was their explanation for why it worked. Animal research in the 1930s was demonstrating that [[estrogen]] had toxic effects including "infertility or intrauterine death, connective tissue abnormalities, and excessive blood clotting." The Shutes proposed that vitamin E achieved its effects by opposing estrogen directly. As Peat summarized in a [[2012-10-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidants|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on antioxidants]], they "found that Vitamin E not only made infertile women more fertile, but they knew that these women with excess estrogen often suffered from blood clot diseases, venous clots, embolisms in the lung, and strokes. They found that Vitamin E not only made them fertile by antagonizing the estrogen, but it prevented the clotting problems. It showed very clearly its anti-clotting effect was part of its anti-estrogen effect."

Other researchers, observing that [[progesterone]] also protected against estrogen's toxic effects, described vitamin E as a "progesterone-sparing agent," since so many of its anti-estrogenic effects resembled those of progesterone. The Shutes soon expanded their clinical work beyond obstetrics. Blood clots, phlebitis, hypertension, heart disease, and diabetes all responded well to treatment with large doses. Peat returned to the Shutes' legacy throughout his career, telling Georgi Dinkov in a [[64-life-energy-estrogen-fibrosis-calcification-process-theology-altruism-with-ray-peat|*Generative Energy* episode on estrogen and fibrosis]] that "it turns out that estrogen is a basic pro-inflammatory activator, and vitamin E is a very essential anti-estrogen and anti-inflammatory factor."

## The Industry Suppression

"In the 1940s, it had already become clear to the estrogen industry that vitamin E research was impinging on its vital interests," Peat wrote. The suppression proceeded on two fronts simultaneously. The estrogen industry branded estrogen "the female hormone," created "physician-shills" to promote it, and ensured that "very little research showing the curative effects of vitamin E in human diseases was allowed to be published."

The seed oil industry operated in parallel. As Peat explained in a [[2010-11-18-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-science-behind-the-dangers-of-polyunsaturated-fats|2010 *EastWest Healing* episode on polyunsaturated fats]], the agricultural industry had turned to corn and soybean diets for fattening animals, only to find that the animals "were suffering from degenerative brain diseases and testicular degeneration." Adding vitamin E protected against these effects. "This was a very sudden shift away from Vitamin E as the anti-estrogen, and it started being called the antioxidant protective against the breakdown of unsaturated fats."

The redefinition served both industries. In the 1940s, the official measure of vitamin E activity was changed from preventing embryonic death and tissue degeneration to preventing the oxidation of unsaturated oils. As Peat put it in his [[2020-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vibrations-frequencies-part-2|2020 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on vibrations and frequencies]], "the anti-estrogen effect was identical to the anti-PUFA effect. But both the seed oil industry and the estrogen industry found it convenient to sell the idea that Vitamin E is merely an antioxidant."

## Chain Breaker, Not Just Antioxidant

Peat did not reject the term "antioxidant" because vitamin E lacks antioxidant properties. He rejected it because the label conceals more than it reveals. In his [[irons-dangers|"Iron's Dangers" (2006)]] newsletter, he offered the alternative terminology: "Vitamin E and [[vitamin-c|vitamin C]] are known as antioxidants, because they stop the harmful free-radical chain reactions which often involve oxygen, but they do not inhibit normal oxidation processes in cells. 'Chain breaker' would be a more suitable term."

The distinction matters. A "chain breaker" interrupts the cascade of [[lipid-peroxidation|lipid peroxidation]], the process in which one oxidized [[PUFA]] molecule damages its neighbor, propagating damage through cell membranes. This is not the same as generally suppressing oxidation. In Peat's framework, healthy cells require vigorous [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]], the efficient burning of glucose to produce [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and energy. Vitamin E protects this process by stopping the chain reactions that would otherwise destroy the mitochondrial membranes where respiration occurs.

The practical consequence is that vitamin E's protective role is proportional to the body's burden of polyunsaturated fatty acids. Peat wrote in his newsletter that "since the requirement for vitamin E decreases as the consumption of unsaturated fats decreases, the requirement, if any, would be very small if we didn't eat significant quantities of those fats." This insight led him to his characteristic hierarchy of interventions: eliminating PUFA from the diet is more protective than any amount of vitamin E supplementation. "Vitamin E supplementation is seldom as effective as the absence of the toxic oils."

## Vitamin E Versus Estrogen: A Point-by-Point Opposition

Peat's newsletter devoted a long passage to the systematic antagonism between vitamin E and estrogen. The list reads like a metabolic mirror image. Estrogen causes capillaries to become leaky; vitamin E does the opposite. Estrogen increases platelet aggregation; vitamin E decreases it. Estrogen increases prostaglandin synthesis; vitamin E decreases it. Estrogen increases the activity of the inflammatory enzymes COX and LOX; vitamin E decreases their activity. Estrogen releases enzymes from [[lysosomes]]; vitamin E inhibits their release. Estrogen often increases intracellular [[calcium]] and protein kinase C; vitamin E has "generally opposite effects."

The polyunsaturated fatty acids and their derivatives, the [[prostaglandins]], act as "effectors, or amplifiers, of estrogen's actions." This means that vitamin E's protection against PUFA is itself partly an anti-estrogenic mechanism. "If vitamin E is acting as a protectant against the polyunsaturated fatty acids, that in itself would account for at least some of its antiestrogenic effects," Peat wrote.

Beyond these downstream antagonisms, vitamin E acts on the supply side. It "inhibits lipolysis, lowering the concentration of [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] (the opposite of estrogen's effect), and it also binds to, and inactivates, free fatty acids." Peat speculated that its long saturated carbon chain might allow it to serve as a substitute for the omega-9 fats from which [[mead-acid|Mead acid]] is formed, the same protective lipid that aspirin may also mimic.

In a [[2016-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidant-theory-and-the-continued-war-on-cancer|2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on antioxidant theory and cancer]], Peat described the cellular consequence of losing this protection: "If you are deficient in Vitamin E and C, the electrons predominate and shift over to reducing the sulfur groups all through the cell into the sulfhydryl form, promoting inflammation."

## Fibrosis, Clotting, and the Shutes' Unfinished Work

The connection between vitamin E and [[fibrosis]] runs through the clotting cascade. In his [[fibrosis|"Fibrosis" (2001)]] newsletter, Peat described how "fibrin clots stimulate the growth of fibroblasts," and how leaky blood vessels "can lead to tissue fibrosis." Vascular leakiness is caused by "estrogen, irradiation, oxygen deprivation, vitamin E deficiency, free fatty acids, [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]], and various stress mediators, such as [[histamine]] and [[serotonin]]."

The Shutes had shown that vitamin E "activates the proteolytic, fibrinolytic, enzymes that remove blood clots." But the research was gradually buried. Peat suspected why: "their observations showing that vitamin E, [[thyroid]], and progesterone protect against heart disease by opposing the clotting effects of estrogen (and unsaturated fats) came too close to saying that the problem can be solved without the help of the great drug industry."

At the cellular level, vitamin E deficiency means the cell "loses molecules such as ATP and proteins, that diffuse out into the extracellular spaces." This leaky, edematous state characterizes [[hypothyroidism]] and chronic energy deficit. Peat reported seeing "keloids regress quickly following the application of vitamin E with antiestrogenic hormones."

## Lipofuscin: The Visible Measure of PUFA Damage

[[Lipofuscin]], the brown "age pigment" that accumulates in the brain, heart, and skin with aging, served as Peat's most vivid illustration of what vitamin E deficiency looks like at the cellular level. In his [[age-pigment-cause-and-effect-of-aging-and-stress|"Age Pigment" (2013)]] newsletter, he wrote that lipofuscin "is produced by the over-feeding of unsaturated fats (such as fish oils) and the underfeeding of vitamin E, relative to the amount of unsaturated oils. Ultraviolet light, iron, and aluminum promote its formation, apparently by the production of free radicals."

Age spots visible on the skin indicate systemic damage. "If you can see them on the skin, you're getting them in your brain and other organs," Peat told listeners in a [[2012-10-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidants|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on antioxidants]]. "If they're fairly new, Vitamin E, progesterone, and even a tiny bit of vodka can help to remove them." In his newsletter, he elaborated: "I have often seen topically applied vitamin E with progesterone remove age spots from the skin. In younger (age 45 to 55) women who have developed the spots while using estrogen, the spots sometimes disappear when they stop using estrogen. In much older people, the use of vitamin E and progesterone has sometimes only caused the spots to slowly become lighter, during several months."

## The Quinone Experiment: Electronic Activation

One of Peat's most distinctive contributions to vitamin E research came from a laboratory accident in 1971. Using paper chromatography to compare liver lipids with vitamin E and various quinones, he mixed the two substances and noticed something unexpected. "I happened to mix the vitamin E with one of the quinones, and noticed that it turned almost black," he recounted in an [[vitamin-e|email exchange on vitamin E]]. "All of the quinones had the same effect." When the solvent separated them on the chromatographic paper, "their original lighter colors reappeared." The dark color was characteristic of charge-transfer bonds, in which electrons are delocalized between molecules.

Peat connected this to Albert Szent-Gyorgyi's observation about the dark color of healthy liver tissue, "a color that can't be extracted as a pigment." If vitamin E could form charge-transfer complexes with [[coenzyme-q10|ubiquinone]], it might prevent the "leakage" of electrons in the respiratory chain that generates toxic free radicals. "If it can prevent the 'leakage' of electrons, then this in itself would improve respiratory efficiency."

Years later, he tried to replicate the experiment with commercial ubiquinone capsules and failed. The quinone was dissolved in vegetable oil, and he realized that "a solvent containing double bonds (e.g., soy oil or other oil containing PUFA) would very likely prevent the close association between vitamin E and ubiquinone which is necessary for charge-transfer to occur." If unsaturated oils interfere with this electronic interaction, "then this could explain, at least partly, their toxicity for mitochondria." He told listeners on the [[2020-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vibrations-frequencies-part-2|2020 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] that the quinone reaction had been "very vivid" in an old 1960s sample of Sigma Chemical Co. vitamin E, but could not be reproduced in newer products.

## Peat's Dissertation and Soderwall's Fertility Research

Peat's personal connection to vitamin E research ran through his thesis adviser at the University of Oregon, Arnold Soderwall, whom he described as "a major figure in Vitamin E research." Soderwall had shown that hamsters remained fertile much longer when given increasingly large doses of vitamin E as they aged. As Peat explained in the 2020 interview, "by increasing the dose of Vitamin E steadily with aging, he kept the animals from miscarrying well into old middle age."

Peat's own experiments extended this line of investigation. "I continued that research and found that it was partly acting by way of improving the production of progesterone as well as lowering the effect of estrogen." In his newsletter, he described the specific finding: "vitamin E increased the amount of oxygen in the uterus, correcting an oxygen deficiency produced either by supplemental estrogen or by old age. Progesterone has similar effects on the delivery of oxygen to the uterus."

This discovery led directly to Peat's most practical contribution to vitamin E's therapeutic use: the progesterone-vitamin E solution.

## Progest-E: The Progesterone-Vitamin E Solution

In 1971, Peat discovered that "vitamin E and progesterone work together to sustain efficient production and use of biological energy." Around 1980, he found that vitamin E is "a uniquely powerful, stable, and biologically compatible solvent for progesterone." He patented the formulation in 1984 and sold it as Progest-E.

The practical significance was immense. Progesterone taken in crystalline or micronized form is rapidly metabolized by the bowel and liver, with "less than 5% absorption" typical. Dissolved in vitamin E, the solution "permits otherwise impossibly high doses to be given, increasing by as much as 2000% progesterone's already dramatic effects" in conditions including epilepsy, habitual miscarriages, auto-immune diseases, and cancer. As Peat explained in his [[origins-of-progesterone-therapy|"Origins of Progesterone Therapy" (1992)]] newsletter, the vitamin E vehicle allows progesterone to be distributed via chylomicrons to all tissues, "allowing it to escape the tendency of the liver to convert it rapidly to an excretory form."

He applied the same principle to [[DHEA]], finding that dissolving it in vitamin E "makes it very quick-acting and controllable; it circulates and distributes without affecting your liver." When steroids are taken in crystalline form, "your liver gets the first opportunity to metabolize it, and that's when it most easily turns into estrogen."

## The Quality Problem

Peat grew increasingly concerned about commercial vitamin E quality. The old Sigma product from the 1960s was "semi-solid, like crystallized honey," viscous and amber. Modern products were often thin, liquid, and diluted with soybean oil. "Even 10 or 20 years ago, the majority of it was highly diluted with soybean oil, highly unsaturated," he told listeners in 2020. The supplement sold to protect against PUFA damage was being delivered in a PUFA vehicle.

The deterioration traced to corporate changes. "When ADM bought Distillation Products from Eastman several years ago, the composition of their vitamin E products went through several changes," Peat noted in an [[acne|email exchange on acne]]. "Since then research results haven't been as consistent as they were 40 to 60 years ago." Some products now "contain significant amounts of PUFA, and lack some of the substances such as octacosanol that were in traditional products."

For evaluating quality, Peat offered a rule of thumb: "If the potency of a vitamin E product is around 1000 i.u. per milliliter, the amount of soy oil isn't a concern, but if it's only about 100 i.u./ml, then there's enough oil to matter." The "thickest, darkest, vitamin E is likely to be the cleanest." He himself eventually stopped supplementing, "partly because of the new manufacturing methods, that were associated for several years with adding soy oil to the product."

## Mixed Tocopherols Versus D-Alpha

From the 1940s onward, d-alpha tocopherol was designated the standard measure of vitamin E potency because it was the most potent antioxidant of the tocopherol family. Peat saw this as another consequence of the industry-driven redefinition. "D-alpha tocopherol was identified as the standard because it was the most antioxidant. But it turns out that the gamma and others have very distinct, more important biological activities, including opposition to estrogen."

Research cited in Peat's newsletter supported the practical difference. A study on cultured heart cells found that a mixed tocopherol preparation "was much superior to alpha-tocopherol" in protecting cells from injury. The authors concluded that the "lack of efficacy of commercial tocopherol preparations in clinical trials may reflect absence of gamma- and delta-tocopherols."

When asked directly about his preference, Peat was clear: "In similar milligram amounts, I would prefer gamma." He recommended "the mixture of the non-racemic natural forms," specifying that "the mixture as it occurs in nature is much better than any of the synthetic D,L-alpha that has been sold for a long time because it's cheap to make."

Sarah Murray summarized the practical point on air: "If people are going to supplement, especially if they are eating PUFA fats (pork, chicken, fish, vegetable oils), it is important to get a mixed tocopherol, not just the d-alpha tocopherol."

## Iron, Aging, and the Increasing Need

Vitamin E's requirement increases with age because stored [[iron]] accumulates over a lifetime. "Iron destroys vitamin E, so vitamin E should be taken as a supplement," Peat wrote in "Iron's Dangers." "It shouldn't be taken at the same time as the iron-contaminated food, because iron reacts with it in the stomach."

Hans Selye demonstrated the practical consequences: "he could experimentally produce a form of scleroderma (hardening of the skin) in animals by administering large doses of iron, followed by a minor stress. He could prevent the development of the condition by giving the animals large doses of vitamin E." Peat noted that "because of those changes, we need more vitamin E as we age," recommending approximately "100 mg per day" as a baseline, qualified by context: "your vitamin E requirement increases with your iron consumption." Iron in foods like eggs, chocolate, and liver "won't interfere" with vitamin E taken at a different time; the concern is simultaneous ingestion.

## Seizures, Brain Protection, and Respiratory Support

"Vitamin E has been proven to reduce the incidence of seizures in animal experiments," Peat said in his 2016 interview. "Stress in a brain cell lowers oxygen availability and triggers that reductive process, causing lipid peroxidation. If your tissues are saturated with Vitamin E, that's much less likely to happen." The mechanism extends to any tissue under metabolic stress: when cells cannot maintain oxidative energy production, they shift to a reduced state that generates toxic free radicals from PUFA in their membranes. Vitamin E interrupts this cascade, parallel to thyroid hormone's support of mitochondrial respiration.

Peat placed vitamin E alongside [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] and coenzyme Q10 in the energy-delivery chain: "Along with Vitamin C and Vitamin K, it functions with Coenzyme Q10 to deliver energy." He noted a practical caveat: "I wouldn't mix them, even in your stomach. I think it's good to take them at different times because I've seen a reaction, at least in the presence of light, where Vitamin E reacts with Vitamin K."

## Interactions with Vitamin A

[[Vitamin-a|Vitamin A]] "oxidizes easily and an excess can create symptoms of a deficiency, so vitamin E is the most important thing for correcting it," Peat wrote. The toxic effects of very large doses of vitamin A "seem to be from either oxidative processes (rancidity) that are prevented by adequate vitamin E, or by antithyroid effects." Conversely, "excess vitamin A, like PUFA, interferes with thyroid hormone transport, so it's important to balance the two." Beef liver provides both vitamins in their natural ratio: "it just takes an occasional meal to meet those requirements generously."

## Practical Dosing

Peat's dosing recommendations trended downward over his career as product quality declined. His newsletter-era guidance was approximately 100 units per day. In a later interview on [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-lipofuscin|*Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on lipofuscin]], he recommended "something like 20 or 30 milligrams." In his email exchanges, he explained the shift: vitamin E "can still be very protective against lipid peroxidation and inflammation, but the products have been changing frequently in the last 15 years, so I think it's good to be cautious and use minimal doses."

Some people experienced digestive irritation from the viscous oil. Peat attributed this to physical properties rather than toxicity: "I think it's the viscous, oily quality that is irritating," and recommended taking it with food. When a caller worried that 400 IU of alpha-tocopherol with 300 mg of mixed tocopherols might be excessive, Peat doubted it: "the effect of Vitamin E on many cell processes is anti-inflammatory. It inhibits prostaglandin formation, similar to aspirin." On safety, he was unequivocal: "Pure vitamin E doesn't have any toxic effects, except when it's enough to irritate the intestine, probably because of viscosity."

## Coconut Oil as a Complement

Peat placed [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]] and vitamin E together as a practical anti-aging pair. "A supplement of vitamin E and coconut oil should have a significant effect on the rate of aging," he wrote: "the effect of coconut oil is to reduce the need for vitamin E, to act as a dilutional chain-breaker in free radical propagation, and to provide energy at a high level to repair cell damage." Since coconut oil is highly saturated, it displaces PUFA from tissues over time, reducing the substrate for lipid peroxidation and consequently reducing the demand for vitamin E as a chain-breaking protector.

## The Broader Pattern

Keeping the diet free of polyunsaturated fats, "to create something like the 'deficiency' state that is so protective (against cancer, trauma, poison, shock, inflammation, infection, etc.) in the animal experiments, seems preferable to trying to saturate ourselves with antioxidants," Peat wrote. The ideal is a body with so little PUFA that it barely needs the chain breaker at all. But given modern diets saturated with seed oils, supplemental vitamin E remains a practical necessity alongside aspirin, progesterone, thyroid, and coconut oil.

"I think it's important that we don't allow the drug publicists to obscure the broad importance of substances such as aspirin, vitamin E, progesterone, and thyroid," he wrote. "For 60 years, a myth that was created to sell estrogen has harmed both science and the health of many people."
