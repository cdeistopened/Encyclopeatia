# Vitamin D

Vitamin D is not really a vitamin—it is a steroid hormone synthesized in the skin from [[cholesterol]] under the influence of sunlight. This distinction matters in Ray Peat's framework because it places vitamin D within the same family of protective steroids that includes [[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], and [[DHEA]]. Its most critical function is not the familiar role in bone health but its suppression of [[parathyroid hormone]] (PTH)—the stress hormone that Peat identified as a central driver of [[aging]], calcification, and metabolic disease.

As Peat explained in a [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-july-2011|2011 episode]], the vitamin D receptor exists in every cell in the body, and the natural inhibitors of PTH are vitamin D and [[calcium]]. When either is deficient, PTH rises, triggering the destructive cascade of bone dissolution, soft tissue calcification, and impaired oxidative [[metabolism]] that Peat connected to virtually every degenerative condition.

## The PTH Suppression System

Vitamin D's primary metabolic role is maintaining the calcium economy in a way that keeps PTH suppressed. As Peat described in a [[2019-12-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-december-2019|2019 interview]], "Vitamin D helps shift the ratio in your body towards calcium and away from [[phosphate]]. Vitamin D, or high calcium intake, turns off the parathyroid hormone."

This suppression has far-reaching consequences. PTH does not merely regulate minerals—it blocks oxygen utilization in tissues, promotes [[lactic acid]] production, activates inflammatory mediators including [[serotonin]] and histamine, and collaborates with aldosterone to suppress energy production. As Peat discussed, vitamin D deficiency activates an entire stress cascade: rising PTH leads to increased [[endotoxin]] absorption, elevated aldosterone, higher [[estrogen]], and progressive metabolic deterioration.

The connection between vitamin D deficiency and disease is therefore not mysterious—it reflects the metabolic damage caused by chronic PTH elevation. Conditions associated with low vitamin D—[[osteoporosis]], [[cancer]], autoimmune disease, depression, fatty liver—are all conditions associated with elevated PTH and impaired energy production.

## Vitamin D and Calcium Together

Peat consistently emphasized that vitamin D and calcium work as a unit. Neither alone is sufficient to suppress PTH optimally. High calcium intake can compensate somewhat for low vitamin D, and adequate vitamin D can compensate somewhat for lower calcium—but both together provide the strongest suppression of PTH and its metabolic consequences.

This explains a clinical paradox: some people with adequate vitamin D levels still have elevated PTH and its associated problems. As Peat noted in email exchanges, "elevated PTH causes generalized inflammation even with good vitamin D if the calcium-to-phosphate ratio is off." The modern diet's excess of phosphate from meat, grains, and processed foods can override vitamin D's protective effect unless calcium intake is also high.

Peat personally consumed over two quarts of [[milk]] daily, which he described as the most practical way to maintain the high calcium intake that keeps PTH suppressed regardless of vitamin D fluctuations.

## Dosing Considerations

Peat's vitamin D recommendations were moderate compared to some alternative health advocates. In email exchanges on PTH, he recommended "2,000 to 6,000 IU vitamin D3 daily in winter," with the dose adjusted based on blood levels and individual response. In a [[2021-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2021|2021 episode]], he suggested approximately 5,000 IU per day for adults as a reasonable winter dose.

He noted that 10,000 IU daily for a couple of winter months is safe for most people, but preferred keeping the dose lower while increasing calcium and [[vitamin K]] intake—addressing the same PTH problem from multiple angles rather than relying on high-dose vitamin D alone.

Peat was cautious about very high vitamin D supplementation because vitamin D, like all steroid hormones, has dose-dependent effects that can become counterproductive at extremes. Excessive vitamin D can promote calcium absorption beyond what the body can properly distribute, potentially contributing to the soft tissue calcification that moderate vitamin D helps prevent. The key is maintaining blood levels that suppress PTH—around 40-60 ng/mL—without pushing into supraphysiological ranges.

## Sunlight as the Ideal Source

Peat regarded sunlight as the optimal source of vitamin D, with the caveat that sun exposure must be managed to avoid the [[lipid peroxidation]] damage that ultraviolet radiation causes in PUFA-rich skin. A person whose tissues are low in polyunsaturated fats tolerates sun exposure far better than one whose skin contains high levels of linoleic acid and DHA—the very fats that undergo peroxidation under UV light.

This creates a practical hierarchy: reduce tissue PUFA first, then increase moderate sun exposure, and supplement with oral vitamin D3 during winter months or when sun exposure is insufficient. The combination of low PUFA, adequate sun, and supplemental D3 maintains the hormonal environment that keeps PTH suppressed and calcium properly distributed.
