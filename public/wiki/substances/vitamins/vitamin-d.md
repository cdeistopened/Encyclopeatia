# Vitamin D

Vitamin D sits near the center of Ray Peat's nutritional recommendations not because of the brittle-bone story that made it famous, but because of what it does to parathyroid hormone, the inflammatory signal that ties [[thyroid]] and [[estrogen]] into the same degenerative pattern he spent his career mapping. "Vitamin D is probably the single most important thing for Americans," he said on a [[2021-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2021-kmud|2021 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]]. "Considering the average American diet, the majority of people are far below the optimal level." The statement is striking from a man generally skeptical of supplement-heavy approaches. What made vitamin D the exception was a specific mechanism he kept circling back to: it suppresses parathyroid hormone, and a suppressed PTH is one of the fastest ways to calm the inflammatory fire that drives heart disease, [[calcium]] misplacement, insulin resistance, and the whole spectrum of diseases of stress.

## A Pro-Hormone, Not a Vitamin

The name is misleading. Vitamin D is a steroid hormone the body synthesizes from [[cholesterol]] when ultraviolet light strikes the skin, travels to the liver for one hydroxylation, then to the kidneys for another, and finally binds receptors throughout the body to regulate gene transcription. The rickets framework that named it a vitamin in the 1920s captured its ability to prevent a bone disease in children but obscured almost everything else about it.

The critical distinction inside the vitamin D system is between cholecalciferol (D3), the form made in skin or taken as a supplement, and 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (calcitriol), the "activated" form produced in the kidneys under stress. As Peat explained in a [[36-co2-and-mineral-balance-thyroid-magnesium-calcium-in-health-and-disease-current-events|*Generative Energy* episode on CO2 and mineral balance]], the two do nearly opposite things: "When I talk about vitamin D, I mean cholecalciferol, definitely not the 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol. That activates the so-called vitamin D receptor, which is activated by high PTH and does the dirty work of high PTH, such as turning on lactic acid production." Both are colloquially called "vitamin D," which has caused confusion in the medical literature, where some researchers treat calcitriol as the gold standard and therefore report harm from what is actually a stress-amplifying metabolite, not the benign pro-hormone Peat recommended.

## The Parathyroid Hormone Connection

Peat's most important claim about vitamin D is that its central function is not calcium absorption for bones but suppression of parathyroid hormone. PTH is released when serum calcium drops, and its job in an emergency is to pull calcium out of bones and into the blood. In chronic deficiency, it stays elevated and behaves as a pro-inflammatory agent. "I think its main function, not the calcium bone-building function, is that it reduces the parathyroid hormone," Peat told listeners in a [[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|2019 *One Radio Network* interview]].

What PTH does when elevated reads like a catalog of Peat's central pathologies. It activates mast cells to release histamine and serotonin. It increases inflammatory cytokines, tumor necrosis factor, and nitric oxide. It raises intracellular calcium, lowers cellular energy production, and shifts metabolism toward glycolysis and lactate. In his newsletter ["Milk in Context: Allergies, Ecology, and Some Myths"](milk-in-context-allergies-ecology-and-some-myths), he wrote that "multiple sclerosis relapses consistently occur at times of high PTH, and remissions consistently occur at times of low PTH. PTH increases the activity of nitric oxide synthase, and nitric oxide is a factor in the vascular leakiness that is so important in MS."

The practical corollary is that vitamin D, calcium, and [[magnesium]] work together to hold PTH down. "When there is adequate calcium, vitamin D, and magnesium in the diet, PTH is kept to a minimum. When PTH is kept low, cells increase their formation of the uncoupling proteins, that cause mitochondria to use energy at a higher rate." It is a metabolic upgrade achieved not by stimulating anything, but by removing a chronic low-grade alarm signal.

## The Calcification Paradox

The most counterintuitive turn in Peat's framework is the calcification paradox. Mainstream caution about vitamin D comes from old experiments in which gigantic doses produced hypercalcemia and soft-tissue calcification. Peat inverted the logic. "It's true that if you take a million units or so, after a while you'll start getting hypercalcemia, calcification of soft tissues," he said in the 2021 episode. "But the most common cause of calcification of the soft tissues is actually from a *deficiency* of vitamin D."

The mechanism runs through PTH. In aging and kidney failure, calcification of kidney tissue and blood vessels becomes the lethal problem, and the same patients have extreme vitamin D deficiency and an excess of parathyroid hormone. Removing the parathyroid glands surgically can alleviate some of the calcification symptoms. "Just supplementing a moderate amount of vitamin D, like 5,000 units a day for an adult, can be enough. If you have calcium in your diet, it can be enough to lower the parathyroid hormone. Lowering the parathyroid hormone makes you put your calcium into the bones where it should go, keeping it out of the soft tissues."

In the [[2016-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vitamin-d|2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on vitamin D]], Peat listed the full spectrum of damage: "atrial fibrillation, enlargement of the ventricle, calcification of the valves, hardening of the arteries, calcification of the coronary arteries, and even calcification to the extent of what they call a 'heart stone,' a huge mass of calcium." These are commonly treated as inevitable features of aging. In Peat's frame, they are the downstream consequences of a chronically elevated parathyroid hormone that adequate vitamin D would have suppressed.

## The 1950s Suppression and the Vitamin A Scare

Like [[vitamin-e]], vitamin D had a history of being suppressed just as its therapeutic potential was becoming clear. In the 2016 episode, Peat described the 1950s public health shift: "People started thinking about nutrition for themselves, Adele Davis's books came out, for example, and the medical establishment reacted to discourage the public from using their own therapy. There were stories about how horribly toxic Vitamin A was, claiming it would destroy your brain and make your skin fall off, with very little evidence backing it up."

Similar scare stories surrounded vitamin D. "There were similar animal experiments with gigantic doses of Vitamin D. Supposedly, there was a case of a baby whose mother took a million or so units of Vitamin D while pregnant, and it supposedly caused the bones to thicken so much that there was no room for the brain to develop. Despite reading Adele Davis, I was directed away from Vitamin D because there was such a taboo about taking anything more than 200 or 300 units per day." A related wrinkle: during the 1940s and 1950s, supplemental vitamin D was synthetic, created by ultraviolet irradiation of fungus. Research around 1970 claimed this synthetic form might be contributing to arterial hardening in young people. "Milk additives switched over to Vitamin D3 right after those articles came out." The early "vitamin D causes calcification" literature was partly tracking the use of wrong or degraded molecules, not the cholecalciferol sunlight produces.

## D3 Versus D2

The distinction between cholecalciferol (D3) and ergocalciferol (D2) is the second place Peat insisted on form specificity. D3 is what human skin makes from cholesterol in response to UV. D2 is the form made in fungi and yeasts through irradiation. "D3 is the normal one that we make when we are in the sunlight," Peat said in the 2021 episode. "It works the same chemically as the one we make. Some products on the market are vitamin D2; that is metabolized slightly differently and doesn't have all the good protective effects of vitamin D3." And on calcitriol: "You don't want the triol, some people confuse that with vitamin D3. You want cholecalciferol." Mushrooms are an edge case, since sun-exposed mushrooms contain small amounts of D2. A powdered mushroom study "actually depressed Vitamin D3 and increased Vitamin D2," suggesting D2 can displace D3 in serum assays without providing the same protection.

## Sunlight, Age, and the Cholesterol Bottleneck

Peat preferred sunlight to any supplement. Skin can produce far more vitamin D than any reasonable dose, and full-spectrum sunlight includes visible red and orange wavelengths that penetrate deeply into tissue and quench the free radical damage the ultraviolet causes. "The red light acts as anti-free radical protection," he said in 2021. "It effectively quenches excited electrons and reverses the effect of the free radical damage done by ultraviolet light. A tanning bed, which is only UV and some visible light, will produce more aging effects than sunlight of the same tanning effect."

But sunlight is not reliably sufficient. The limits are latitude, season, time of day, pigmentation, and age. "From 10:00 AM to 2:00 PM in the summer at high latitudes, it only takes 15 or 20 minutes to get enough," he said in 2016, but outside that window UVB is too low. Dark skin is a major modifier. "In Mexico, for example, women with dark skin who are outside all day, but only exposing their face and hands, tend to be deficient in Vitamin D, even at high altitude and in brilliant sunlight."

Aging is the third major limit, and the mechanism is a cholesterol deficiency in the skin. In his 2019 interview, Peat explained: "The older you get, the less cholesterol there is in your skin. It's basically a cholesterol deficiency in the skin that makes older people need much more sunlight." In a [[2018-11-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-skin-cancer|2018 episode on skin cancer]], he put numbers on it: "Over the age of 60 or 70, it takes about twice as much sunlight exposure to produce the same amount of vitamin D. Old skin has only about half as much cholesterol as young skin." The decline is systemic; adrenals, liver, skin, and brain all lose roughly 50% of their cholesterol-producing machinery by old age. Peat's practical compensation was to rub cholesterol onto the skin and to avoid PUFA seed oils topically, since polyunsaturated fats inhibit the HMG-CoA reductase enzyme that makes cholesterol. "If you are going to use any oil on the skin, it should be cocoa butter, cow butter, or anything highly saturated, not any of the PUFA seed oils."

## Vitamin D and Thyroid Are Functionally Parallel

One of Peat's most compressed statements appears in the 2016 episode: "Functionally, Vitamin D and thyroid are parallel. You can't quite separate them." The parallel runs through their shared anti-inflammatory mechanism. Both suppress the signaling molecules that activate mast cells, release cytokines, and drive the degenerative processes of stress and aging. "TSH itself directly activates and causes tissue to release inflammatory cytokines and interleukins. Parathyroid hormone does that as well. By taking Vitamin D, increasing calcium intake, or decreasing phosphate relative to calcium, you will lower both TSH and parathyroid hormone."

The parallelism also explains clinical overlaps. Low thyroid tends to coincide with low vitamin D, and [[hypothyroidism]] increases the need for supplementation. "If your mother was deficient in Vitamin D or calcium, that sets up a methylation pattern in offspring that can affect their sensitivity to Vitamin D." Deficiency in one generation can install epigenetic suppression of the vitamin D receptor in the next.

The liver is a prerequisite for both systems. It performs the first hydroxylation of cholecalciferol to 25-OH-cholecalciferol, the form measured in standard blood tests. Peat wrote in an email: "I've known several liver-avoiders whose serum 25-OH-Vitamin D stays low despite big doses, and I suspect their general nutrition is responsible. It's reasonable to feed their liver optimally." A sluggish liver impairs both thyroid and vitamin D activation simultaneously.

## Phosphate, Milk, and the Calcium Ratio

Calcium and phosphate are in constant equilibrium, and a chronic excess of phosphate is functionally equivalent to vitamin D deficiency. "The excess phosphate, and the reaction of the parathyroid hormone to that high phosphate intake, is exactly the same as a deficiency of Vitamin D," Peat said in 2016. "Too much phosphate and too little Vitamin D results in what amounts to an early stage of chronic kidney degeneration." PTH and phosphate are classified as "uremic toxins" in late-stage kidney disease, but Peat's point was that the same process starts decades earlier in anyone whose diet is dominated by meat, nuts, grains, and beans.

[[Milk]] solves the problem twice over. It delivers a high calcium-to-phosphate ratio and contains casein and whey peptides that are anti-inflammatory on their own. "These peptides and proteins of milk, casein, and whey contain anti-inflammatory substances intrinsically, but they also lower parathyroid hormone simply because of the calcium." A person drinking two to four quarts of vitamin-D-fortified milk makes it nearly impossible for PTH to stay elevated, independent of any supplement regimen.

Sugar creates an unexpected bypass. Peat described an old experiment: "They put one group on a starchy diet and the other group with only sugar as the carbohydrate, both with a vitamin D deficiency. The ones on the starchy diet had very weak, atrophied bones. But the sugar diet built strong bones despite the deficiency in vitamin D. Sugar was lowering phosphate, and apparently, that accounted for the sugar making up for a vitamin D deficiency." Anything that lowers serum phosphate has a partially vitamin-D-sparing effect.

## Klotho, the Anti-Aging Protein

In the early 2010s, Peat began connecting vitamin D to a protein called Klotho. Klotho was discovered in a mutant mouse that aged extremely fast: osteoporosis, dementia, wrinkles, and gray hair within weeks. The missing gene regulates calcium metabolism and has a direct anti-parathyroid hormone effect. "The Vitamin D that we take, or get from sunlight, works to reinforce Klotho, the anti-aging protein," Peat said in 2019. "At the same time, it lowers the parathyroid hormone." Klotho also suppresses the formation of the activated calcitriol that Peat warned against, so "taking vitamin D and calcium has a similar effect as that anti-aging protein Klotho." The whole system matters: calcium adequate, phosphate low, PTH suppressed, calcitriol held down, Klotho reinforced.

## Blood Levels, Dosing, and the PTH Diagnostic

Peat's target for serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D was the upper half of the normal range. "I think the upper end of the scale is right. Maybe 50 to 80 on the 25-hydroxy test." In an email, he sharpened the criterion: "I think 50 ng/ml is a good goal. The point at which it lowers parathyroid hormone would be the right amount." The second formulation matters more than the first. The real diagnostic is not a number but a functional test: supplement to the point where PTH comes down.

His dosing guidance fell into a narrow range. The winter minimum for someone without sunlight was around 2,000 IU. A common working dose was 5,000 IU. The safe upper daily limit was around 10,000 IU. "If you figure that being in a bathing suit in the sun for 20 or 30 minutes, enough to just start turning pink, can make 10,000 or 15,000 units, then 5,000 or 10,000 units is never going to be harmful. I've never heard of 10,000 a day being harmful." For a few winter months, 10,000 IU daily was within his comfort zone, "but it's better to increase calcium and [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]], keeping the vitamin D a little lower unless you have the blood level checked occasionally."

The "informed recommendation" he cited was around 2,000 IU per day. "I use Carlson's, and I think most of the informed people are recommending about 2,000 units per day. John Cannell's site, 'the vitamin D council,' has a newsletter." This places Peat closer to the moderate end of the high-dose movement than to the maximalist end. He was not recommending 20,000 or 50,000 IU daily, and he was emphatic that higher doses should be paired with blood testing and adequate calcium and vitamin K.

For winter, he layered several approaches. "In addition to a few quarts of milk with added vitamin D, and eating liver, I rub several drops of the vitamin D oil onto my skin, and occasionally use an ultraviolet light." Topical application requires about ten times the oral dose because skin turnover and washing remove a significant fraction. "Some people with sensitive, thin skin might absorb as much as 30 or 40% of what they put on." His personal topical dose was "about 20,000 iu."

## Deficiency Symptoms and the Disease Spectrum

Peat described rapid responses to correcting severe deficiency. In the 2016 episode, he recounted a woman in her sixties with a 25-hydroxy level of 7 or 8 ng/mL: "She said she was so weak she could hardly walk; her muscles were simply very weak and shrunken. Within a week of taking a moderate dose, I think it was 5,000 units a day, she could walk around." At intermediate levels of 15 to 25 ng/mL, he saw "gradations of that weakness, depression, shrinking muscles, insensitivity to insulin, and a tendency to have high blood sugar. These are the most common diseases of stress."

The full disease spectrum he associated with low vitamin D is wide: anxiety, depression, muscle weakness, insulin resistance, atrial fibrillation, arterial and valvular calcification, scleroderma, multiple sclerosis, tuberculosis, HIV progression, and malaria morbidity. "Just looking at people when they are brought into the hospital, the higher the Vitamin D when they come in, the more likely they are to go out alive." Prion diseases are a distinctive entry on the list: "The prion diseases are probably influenced because Vitamin D prevents the polymerization of the prion protein, which is involved in scrapie, Mad Cow Disease, Alzheimer's, and probably several other degenerative brain diseases."

The direction of causality is important in Peat's frame, because mainstream medicine often treats low vitamin D as a consequence of illness rather than a cause. "I think the low level of Vitamin D and calcium is probably the cause of the sick liver and kidneys, rather than being a product. Supplementing with calcium and Vitamin D will often correct the liver and kidney problem. It isn't a matter of curing the disease so they can activate Vitamin D; it's a matter of getting the Vitamin D and calcium into the system."

## Immunity, Obesity, and Topical Protection

Peat placed vitamin D at the top of the immune nutrient hierarchy for Americans: "Vitamin D is the most important immune-supporting nutrient for Americans. In the world in general, vitamin A and zinc are rivals for protection of the membranes and direct immunity to germs." The mechanism runs partly through a parallel cholesterol metabolite: "There is another cholesterol metabolite, 25-hydroxycholesterol, which is very similar in function to 25-hydroxyvitamin D. They both activate the immune system."

On topical vitamin D after sun exposure, Peat cited an experiment on hairless mice: "The topical vitamin D prevented the UV carcinogenesis by treating them after the exposure." The broader goal was not to cure cancer but to stop making cancers. "Autopsies on middle-aged people killed in accidents found that everyone by the age of 50 has cancer somewhere in their body. Yet most people don't die of cancer. The goal is to stop making cancers. I think that is the function of applying things such as progesterone, vitamin D, [[cholesterol]], and maybe mevalonic acid."

Vitamin D and calcium together also suppress fat formation. "The fatty liver, for example, is corrected by both Vitamin D and calcium in animal studies. A high calcium intake by itself, with a normal amount of Vitamin D, is very effective at preventing overweight." The mechanism loops back to PTH: suppression raises uncoupling protein expression, which burns energy as heat rather than storing it as fat.

In his newsletter ["Cholesterol, Longevity, Intelligence, and Health"](cholesterol-longevity-intelligence-and-health), Peat placed vitamin D alongside cholesterol, vitamin E, and progesterone as "structural antioxidants, that prevent oxidation partly by stabilizing molecular structures." The protective effects scale with consistent, long-term presence in tissues, not with weekly megadoses. Daily or near-daily supplementation during low-sun months reflects this: steady, moderate input outperforms occasional heroic doses.

## Practical Summary

Sunlight is best but insufficient for most Americans most of the time, and it becomes increasingly insufficient with age because of declining skin cholesterol. Supplementation should be D3 (cholecalciferol), not D2 or calcitriol, at 2,000 to 5,000 IU daily for maintenance and up to 10,000 IU briefly when blood levels demand correction. The target serum level is 50 to 80 ng/mL of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, but the functional diagnostic is whether PTH has come down. Calcium (Peat aimed for around 2,500 mg/day), magnesium, and some vitamin K should accompany higher-dose vitamin D. Phosphate intake should stay moderate relative to calcium, which in practice means dairy and fruit should dominate over meat, nuts, grains, and beans. Liver and thyroid should be supported, since both are prerequisites for activating the hormone system. Topical application requires about ten times the oral dose.

Peat was not a vitamin D maximalist. He rejected the dosing extremes of both the mainstream (200 to 600 IU as adequate) and the fringe (50,000 IU daily indefinitely). His position was that most Americans live in a state of chronic low-grade PTH elevation that a moderate, consistent dose of D3 plus adequate calcium would resolve, and that this resolution would lift the inflammatory ceiling on a long list of degenerative conditions the medical system treats as unrelated to nutrition. The most important number was not the vitamin D level but the parathyroid hormone level.
