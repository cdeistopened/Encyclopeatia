# Vitamin K

Vitamin K is the nutrient that resolves the central paradox of [[calcium]] metabolism: how to drive calcium into bones while pulling it out of arteries. The letter "K" stands for koagulation, the Danish word under which the vitamin was discovered in the 1930s, and that naming accident became one of the most consequential public-health failures of the twentieth century. Asked on a [[2014-01-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-questions-and-answers|2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor* Q&A]] why doctors are so against the vitamin, Ray Peat replied that medical school teaches "that 'K' stands for 'clot' and that taking it increases blood clotting. However, the clotting system is incredibly complicated. Two essential factors, Protein C and Protein S, prevent abnormal coagulation, and you cannot make them without Vitamin K." A vitamin branded as the cause of clots turns out to be required to prevent them, to rebuild osteoporotic bone, to decalcify arteries, to lower blood pressure, and to make high-dose [[aspirin]] safe.

## The Calcification Paradox

The central confusion in mainstream nutrition is the belief that eating calcium causes arterial hardening. Peat spent decades arguing the opposite. On a [[2012-07-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-blood-pressure-regulation-heart-failure-and-muscle-atrophy|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on blood pressure]], he put the case briefly: "Eating enough calcium is protective of the circulatory system and prevents hardening of the arteries. The parathyroid hormone rises when you don't eat enough calcium or eat too much phosphorus. Vitamin K is also very powerful at preventing calcification of the arteries."

Two nutrients do the same job by different mechanisms. Adequate dietary calcium suppresses parathyroid hormone, the signal that dissolves bone and dumps minerals into the bloodstream. Vitamin K directs whatever calcium is present into the tissue where it belongs. The misplacement of calcium is not a problem of excess intake but of mislocation. Asked on KMUD whether arterial calcification can be reversed, Peat pointed to the Japanese osteoporosis literature: "The Japanese have done studies using vitamin K, very high doses of it, to effectively rebuild osteoporotic bones while taking calcium out of arteries in animal studies. Vitamin K works on cellular energy and specifically on the handling of carbon dioxide, which lets the proteins handle calcium."

The mechanism runs through [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and a protein called osteocalcin. In his 2012 newsletter [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|"Osteoporosis, Aging, Tissue Renewal, and Product Science"]], Peat described how new bone mineral begins as calcium carbonate and is gradually converted to calcium phosphate. "Besides forming part of the mineral, carbon dioxide is also incorporated into a protein (in a process requiring vitamin K), in a process that causes this protein, osteocalcin, to bind calcium. The osteocalcin protein is firmly bonded to a collagen molecule." Without vitamin K, the carboxylation reaction that activates osteocalcin cannot occur. The protein is made but inert. Calcium has no address to be delivered to and ends up in the places it should not go.

The same mechanism explains why vitamin K deficiency is not a clotting disorder but a misdirection disorder. In his 2015 newsletter [[heart-and-hormones|"Heart and Hormones"]], Peat wrote that "the calcification of blood vessels caused by phosphate excess and vitamin K deficiency involves the expression of a protein which has its proper place in the skeleton. The replacement of heart muscle by fibrous connective tissue and even bone is a basic biological problem of differentiation." Ectopic calcification is bone tissue growing in the wrong place. Vitamin K is what prevents that misplacement.

## K1 and K2: The Family of Menaquinones

The vitamin K family divides into two branches. K1 (phylloquinone) is the plant form, concentrated in [[kale]] and other green leaves. K2 (the menaquinones, abbreviated MK-4 through MK-13) is the bacterial and animal form, made by gut flora and stored in tissues including [[liver]] and egg yolks. Peat's preference between the two was modest but clear. In an email exchange, he wrote: "K1 is probably a little less active than K2." Both work; K2 works somewhat better.

Among K2 variants, the most frequently debated distinction is between MK-4 (the short-chain form found in animal products) and MK-7 (the longer-chain form produced by bacterial fermentation, including the Japanese food natto). Peat was dismissive of the urgency around the distinction. Asked directly about MK-4 versus MK-7, he replied: "Taking the vitamin with a meal, it will absorb slowly and steadily, and I don't think will make much difference."

What matters more than the specific menaquinone isomer is the vehicle. Vitamin K is fat-soluble, and commercial K2 is typically suspended in an oil that can cause problems. One of Peat's email correspondents reported severe abdominal pain, nausea, and lower-right-side cramping after 2 mg of oral K2. Peat's response was personal: "I had a similar experience, and thought it might be the excipient oil, so I've used it only on my skin since then." Topical application bypasses the gut entirely. It is a workable route for people who cannot tolerate the oral form, and an echo of the same logic he applied to [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]] and [[progesterone]]: the delivery vehicle matters as much as the molecule.

## The Osteocalcin Mechanism in Detail

The 2012 osteoporosis newsletter contains the densest single passage Peat wrote on the cellular chemistry of bone maintenance. It links vitamin K to two other substances in his therapeutic repertoire: [[aspirin]] and [[sodium-bicarbonate|sodium bicarbonate]].

"While lactic acidosis causes bone loss, acidosis caused by increased carbonic acid doesn't; low bicarbonate in the body fluids seems to remove carbonate from the bone, and also mineral phosphates. The parathyroid hormone, which removes calcium from bone, causes [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] to be formed by bone cells. Lactic acid produced by intense exercise causes calcium loss from bone, and sodium bicarbonate increases calcium retention by bone. Vitamin K2 blocks the removal of calcium from bone caused by parathyroid hormone and prostaglandin E2, by completely blocking their stimulation of lactic acid production by bone tissues. Aspirin, which, like vitamin K, supports cell respiration and inhibits lactic acid formation, also favors bone calcification. Vitamin K2 stimulates the formation of two important bone proteins, osteocalcin and osteonectin, and reduces the activity of [[estrogen]] by oxidizing estradiol."

Parathyroid hormone tells bone to give up calcium by commanding bone cells to produce lactic acid, which does the actual mineral dissolving. Block the lactic acid production, and you block the bone loss even with parathyroid hormone present. Vitamin K works at three levels simultaneously: it blocks the parathyroid-driven lactic acid signal, it activates the carbon-dioxide-binding proteins that hold calcium in bone, and it oxidizes estradiol. Aspirin runs alongside it on the first two mechanisms. Sodium bicarbonate supports the third leg. The three substances form a small, cheap, well-understood osteoporosis protocol that does not appear in any bisphosphonate brochure.

On the 2011 KMUD episode [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|"Hair Loss, Inflammation, Osteoporosis"]], Peat tied these threads together: "People seldom talk about it, but aspirin improves calcium retention in bones. It isn't as profitable as osteoporosis drugs like bisphosphonates, but it is very safe as long as you take Vitamin K with it. Vitamin K protects your arteries from calcification and allows the bone to use carbon dioxide to bind calcium into the bone."

## Hypertension: The 50 mg Protocol

One of the most striking anecdotes in Peat's vitamin K corpus concerns blood pressure. On the 2014 KMUD episode [[2014-12-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-you-are-what-you-eat-2014|"You Are What You Eat"]], a caller asked about hypertension. After running through his standard list of interventions (potassium, magnesium, calcium), Peat added: "One other nutrient that can powerfully lower blood pressure is vitamin K. I know a doctor whose blood pressure had been 240 over 140, and within two or three weeks of using a lot of vitamin K, his pressure came down to 140 over 70." When Andrew Murray asked what counted as "a lot," Peat replied: "That doctor who got such extreme results was taking 40 or 50 milligrams per day." On a different episode, recounting the same story, he reported a second patient whose 220/70 dropped to 140/70 in "two or three weeks."

The mechanism is not mysterious. If vascular walls are accumulating calcium, they become stiffer, raising systolic pressure. If vitamin K directs that calcium out of the arterial wall, the walls regain compliance and pressure falls. In "Heart and Hormones," Peat noted that "a considerable amount of research showed that vitamin K is effective for treating hypertension, but again, most doctors warn against its use, because of its reputation as a clot forming vitamin. Recently, the value of the 'blood thinner' warfarin, a vitamin K antagonist, has been questioned for people with heart failure."

The warfarin irony is worth dwelling on. For decades, the standard anticoagulant has been a drug whose mechanism is to block vitamin K, the very nutrient that directs calcium out of blood vessels and into bone. A population treated with warfarin for years can be expected to develop the two conditions vitamin K prevents: arterial calcification and osteoporosis. In the 2014 Q&A episode, a caller on Pradaxa asked whether vitamin K was dangerous. Peat's answer: "I have never heard of that. It is good to have your blood clotting system checked periodically if you are taking a Warfarin-type drug, but Vitamin K helps regulate calcium in all systems. It puts it into your bones and keeps it from going into your arteries." That sentence is the article in miniature. Vitamin K does not promote clots. Vitamin K regulates calcium. Everything else is downstream.

## The Aspirin Companion Rule

The single most practical protocol in Peat's vitamin K writing concerns aspirin. He recommended aspirin for cancer prevention, arthritis, inflammation, and bone maintenance. The one consistent caveat was vitamin K. In his email correspondence on aspirin, he put the rule plainly: "Aspirin makes you need more vitamin K, even when you aren't using much. People who use aspirin for arthritis or cancer often take several grams a day. 7000 mg is a lot, and it's very important to take vitamin K with aspirin."

The exact ratio was worked out on air by Sarah Johanneson Murray, Peat's longtime KMUD co-host. On the [[2012-10-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidants|October 2012 episode on antioxidants]] and again on the [[2021-08-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-august-2021-kmud|August 2021 broadcast]], she stated the formula: "It is only safe to use more than a baby aspirin a day if you are taking Vitamin K. For every aspirin (325mg) you take, you need 1 milligram (1000 micrograms) of Vitamin K2 to keep yourself from bleeding excessively." One aspirin equals one milligram of K2. A therapeutic anti-inflammatory dose of two or three grams per day implies six to nine milligrams of K2. Peat agreed with the threshold in an email: "Vitamin K is good for protecting muscles and bones, so 500 micrograms to one milligram is good anyway, and it would be protective against even more aspirin." A baby aspirin (81 mg) does not require supplementary K. Above that, the ratio becomes a hard rule.

The pairing turns up again in the context of cancer. Asked about using aspirin for cancer prevention, Peat wrote: "I think a little aspirin, regularly if not daily, is good prevention, if you are sure to get enough vitamin K, to prevent excess bleeding. Taking some at bedtime can be very helpful for sleeping; sometimes I take about 500 mg at night." A question about testicular cancer surgery drew a similar combined protocol: "If vitamin K is used generously, aspirin wouldn't be likely to cause a bleeding problem."

The aspirin-K rule has a folk-medicine fallback for people without access to supplements. When asked how to protect against the bleeding effects of aspirin using only food, Peat replied that "25 g of spinach and 5-10 g of beef liver" daily "is likely to be enough." That is a small handful of greens and a bite-sized piece of liver.

## Food Sources: Kale and Liver

Peat's food recommendations for vitamin K were simple and consistent across a decade of interviews. On the 2011 osteoporosis episode, Andrew Murray asked the obvious question: "What are food sources for Vitamin K?" Peat: "Kale is the richest vegetable, and liver is a very rich source. A serving of well-cooked kale every other day should provide enough."

The "well-cooked" qualifier matters. Raw cruciferous leaves are rich in goitrogens and difficult to digest; well-cooked kale retains most of the K content while removing the [[thyroid]]-suppressing compounds. On the 2012 phosphate and calcium episode, Peat added: "I take liver once or twice a month at least." Monthly liver is enough for maintenance. The 2006 newsletter [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|"Bone Density: First Do No Harm"]] placed K among the fat-soluble bone nutrients and named the same food: "For vitamins, especially K, occasional liver can be helpful."

For people eating for therapy rather than maintenance, food alone is usually insufficient. Sarah Murray made the distinction explicit: "Kale is king, but it may not be in high enough doses if you want to use aspirin to block inflammation in doses of 1 to 3 grams a day. For high doses, it's not safe unless you have Vitamin K2."

## Dosing

Peat's dosing recommendations for vitamin K were unusually wide and context-dependent. The baseline from his emails is 500 micrograms to 1 milligram. For general arterial protection, he recommended more. On the [[2012-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-phosphate-and-calcium-metabolism|2012 episode on phosphate and calcium metabolism]], a caller asked how much to take: "Vitamin K, you can get a high potency formula of a mixture of K1 and K2. With that, I think from 1 to 10 milligrams per day is in the safe range as well as probably being therapeutic for the arteries."

That range, 1 to 10 mg, covers the large majority of his practical recommendations. For osteoporosis, hypertension, or aspirin-pairing, somewhere in that window is the working zone. For extreme cases, as in the doctor whose blood pressure normalized on the Japanese protocol, he reported people using "40 or 50 milligrams per day." That is the upper ceiling, and he treated it as a temporary therapeutic dose rather than a maintenance level. The supplement form was specified by Sarah Murray on the 2012 episode: "A brand that's pretty pure is Thorne Research Vitamin K liquid; one drop is 1 milligram. So we're talking about one to ten drops."

Safety at these doses is not a serious concern. Peat never identified a toxic threshold for vitamin K in healthy people. The cautions were the excipient oil irritation (resolved by topical application) and the warning to people on warfarin, who need to know that changing their vitamin K intake will require their drug dose to be re-titrated.

## Respiration, Estrogen, and the Broader Protective Role

Peat placed vitamin K in a small cluster of nutrients that support oxidative respiration. On the 2014 Q&A episode, he described it this way: "Vitamin D and Vitamin K help regulate calcium and magnesium. A big dose of Vitamin K, now available in oily form, can correct osteoporosis and take calcium out of the blood vessels, which tends to correct hypertension. By regulating energy metabolism, it might even help prevent episodes of low blood pressure. It works with Coenzyme Q10 to maintain oxidative metabolism."

The coenzyme Q10 connection is the same electronic logic Peat applied to vitamin E. The quinone structures of K, Q10, and the tocopherols are all involved in electron transport at the cellular level. In a vitamin K deficiency, the related carboxylation and electron-transfer functions stall; the tissues fall into the lower-energy, higher-stress state he associated with aging and degeneration. On the 2020 KMUD episode [[2020-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vibrations-frequencies-part-2|"Vibrations/Frequencies Part 2"]], he grouped K with the general protective nutrients: "Vitamin K is another generally protective factor. Calcium, sodium, and magnesium also have a basic anti-inflammatory function when you get them through good food."

The 2012 osteoporosis newsletter made the anti-estrogenic mechanism explicit: vitamin K2 "reduces the activity of estrogen by oxidizing estradiol." Estradiol, the most active circulating estrogen, is detoxified in large part by oxidation to weaker metabolites. Vitamin K participates in that process, joining [[progesterone]], [[vitamin-e|vitamin E]], and [[thyroid]] hormone as substances that accelerate estradiol clearance. This is one of the quieter reasons Peat recommended the vitamin so broadly: in a framework where excess [[estrogen]] is a root cause of degeneration, any reliable estradiol-oxidizer is useful.

One practical caveat: vitamin E "reacts with Vitamin K" in the presence of light, and Peat recommended not mixing the two in the same dose. Take them at different times of day. The precaution matters less for someone getting both from food than for someone stacking capsules.

## The Silent Deletion

The vitamin's name carries its own error. A student taught that K stands for koagulation remembers that K promotes clotting even after learning the more nuanced biochemistry. The reflex is stronger than the later correction. Patients on warfarin are told to avoid vitamin K. Patients with heart disease are told to avoid vitamin K. Patients on aspirin are rarely told anything about it at all. The nutritional context has been silently deleted from mainstream cardiology, and the result is an elderly population with both osteoporosis and arterial calcification, the two signatures of exactly the deficiency nobody is screening for.

Peat's recommendations on the vitamin never changed across forty years, because the physiology did not change. Vitamin K puts calcium in bones and keeps it out of arteries. Kale and liver cover the baseline. One to ten milligrams per day of supplement covers the therapeutic range. Fifty milligrams per day, for a few weeks, is what some people have used to reverse a decade of vascular damage.
