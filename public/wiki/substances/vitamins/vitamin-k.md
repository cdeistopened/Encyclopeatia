# Vitamin K

Vitamin K solves a problem that most people do not know they have: the misdirection of [[calcium]]. In Ray Peat's framework, the degenerative diseases of aging—arterial calcification, kidney stones, joint deposits, pineal calcification—coexist with bone loss not because of too much calcium but because calcium is going to the wrong places. Vitamin K activates the proteins that direct calcium into bones and keep it out of soft tissues, making it an essential complement to [[vitamin D]], calcium, and [[aspirin]] in Peat's approach to metabolic health.

As Peat summarized in a [[2014-01-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-january-2014|2014 episode]], "Vitamin K helps regulate calcium in all systems. It puts it into your bones and keeps it from going into your arteries."

## The Carbon Dioxide Connection

Vitamin K's mechanism operates through [[carbon dioxide]]. Bone mineralization depends on CO2 to bind calcium into the hydroxyapatite crystal structure that gives bone its strength. Vitamin K activates osteocalcin—the protein in bone that captures calcium—through a carboxylation reaction that requires CO2 as a substrate.

As Peat explained in a [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-july-2011|2011 episode]], "Vitamin K protects your arteries from calcification and allows the bone to use carbon dioxide to bind calcium into the bone." This connection places vitamin K within the bioenergetic framework: efficient oxidative [[metabolism]] produces CO2, CO2 supports bone mineralization, and vitamin K activates the proteins that make this possible. Poor metabolism produces less CO2 and more [[lactic acid]]—and lactic acid actively dissolves bone rather than building it.

The Japanese have explored this connection clinically. As Peat described in a [[2012-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2012|2012 episode]], "The Japanese have done studies using vitamin K—very high doses of it—to effectively rebuild osteoporotic bones while taking calcium out of arteries." The dual action—strengthening bone while decalcifying arteries—reflects vitamin K's fundamental role in directing calcium to its proper locations.

## The Aspirin Partnership

Vitamin K's most important practical application in Peat's recommendations is as a companion to [[aspirin]]. Aspirin inhibits prostaglandin synthesis and reduces [[inflammation]], but it also affects blood clotting—a property that becomes problematic at higher therapeutic doses without vitamin K to maintain coagulation balance.

The protocol is specific. As discussed in KMUD episodes, the recommendation is one milligram (1,000 micrograms) of vitamin K2 per standard 325mg aspirin tablet when using aspirin regularly. Baby aspirin (81mg) generally does not require supplemental K. Peat noted in email exchanges that "Vitamin K is good for protecting muscles and bones, so 500 micrograms to one milligram is good anyway, and it would be protective against even more aspirin."

The partnership is synergistic rather than merely compensatory. Aspirin prevents the inflammatory mediators—[[prostaglandins]], [[serotonin]], TSH, cytokines—that "break down healthy bone structure and at the same time put calcium into your arteries, brain, and pineal gland." Vitamin K then directs the calcium freed from inflammatory deposits back into bone. Together, they address both the cause of calcium misdirection (inflammation) and the mechanism of proper redistribution (K-dependent carboxylation).

## Depletion and Sources

Vitamin K is produced by intestinal bacteria, which means antibiotic use can create acute deficiency. As Peat cautioned in email exchanges, "It's important to get some vitamin K1 or K2 when you use an antibiotic." The combination of antibiotics and aspirin is particularly risky without vitamin K supplementation, since both reduce clotting capacity through different mechanisms.

Peat recommended food sources first: cooked kale and other dark greens for K1, liver and certain cheeses for K2. As he noted, "Eating lots of cooked greens, such as kale, and liver are the best sources. Some types of cheese are also good sources of vitamin K."

For supplementation, Peat mentioned that he personally experienced gastrointestinal discomfort from oral K2 supplements, suggesting topical application as an alternative for sensitive individuals. The K2 form (specifically MK-4, the animal-derived form) is generally preferred over K1 for its superior tissue distribution and longer biological activity.

## Heart Rhythm Stabilization

Beyond bone and vascular health, vitamin K contributes to cardiac stability. As Peat noted, "Vitamins D and K, and calcium are important for stabilizing the heart rhythm." This connects to the broader framework where adequate mineral balance and energy production—supported by the fat-soluble vitamin complex of A, D, E, and K—maintain the electrical stability of cardiac tissue that [[progesterone]] and [[magnesium]] also support.
