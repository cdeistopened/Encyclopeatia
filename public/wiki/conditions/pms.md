# PMS

In the consulting room of mainstream gynecology, premenstrual syndrome is a strange beast. It is the most common cyclical complaint in industrialized medicine, affecting some version of "most reproductive-age women" depending on which line one draws, and yet its official mechanism remains agnostic. Modern reviews describe it as a "sensitivity" to normal ovarian hormones, a problem of altered GABA-A subunit composition, or a serotonergic dysregulation triggered by ordinary luteal-phase steroid shifts. The drugs the field prescribes — SSRIs (often given only during the luteal phase), oral contraceptives that flatten the cycle entirely, and increasingly GnRH agonists that abolish ovarian function — reflect this confusion. The treatments do not target a known mechanism. They target the patient.

Ray Peat spent fifty years arguing that the confusion is unnecessary. PMS is not a sensitivity to normal hormones. It is the expected behavior of a body whose hormones are no longer normal — an endocrine state in which estrogen, prolactin, aldosterone, serotonin, and cortisol are doing what they always do under stress, while the substance that should restrain them, [[progesterone]], has collapsed below the threshold needed to oppose them. The luteal phase is the diagnostic window. It is the second half of the cycle, the two weeks between ovulation and menstruation, when the corpus luteum should be producing roughly 20 to 30 mg of progesterone per day. When it produces less — because thyroid function is low, because of PUFA-loaded membranes, because chronic stress is shunting pregnenolone toward cortisol, because the diet is short on protein or vitamin A or salt — the ratio of estrogen to progesterone inverts. Every symptom on the long PMS checklist follows from that inversion.

This article concentrates on what is distinctive about Peat's reading: the luteal-phase E:P collapse mechanism, the [[katharina-dalton|Katharina Dalton]] lineage from which Peat's progesterone protocols descend, the aldosterone-and-water-retention story (which is why salt-restriction and diuretics worsen rather than relieve cyclic edema), the cycle-phase eating and progesterone dosing playbook, and the framing question that organizes the rest: PMS is not psychological. The foundational pharmacology of [[estrogen]], [[progesterone]], [[prolactin]], [[serotonin]], and the broader story of [[thyroid|hypothyroidism]] is referenced through hub links rather than restated here.

## The cycle and its phases

A normal cycle is a 28-day rhythm. Days 1-14 are the follicular phase: estrogen rises to its mid-cycle peak before ovulation, while progesterone stays low. Ovulation occurs around day 14, and the follicle collapses into the corpus luteum — a temporary endocrine organ whose dedicated function is producing progesterone. Through the luteal phase (days 14-28), progesterone rises to its monthly peak (15-30 ng/mL serum), with a smaller secondary estrogen rise. If conception does not occur, the corpus luteum involutes around day 26-28, both hormones drop sharply, and menstruation begins.

The premenstrual symptom window — the last four to ten days before menstruation — sits squarely inside the luteal phase. That is a specific physiological claim. PMS is not a "sensitivity to estrogen," which is highest mid-cycle when most women feel best, and it is not a "sensitivity to progesterone," which Peat insisted is the substance that fixes the syndrome rather than the substance that causes it. PMS is the surface expression of a luteal phase in which progesterone is not high enough to do the work it is supposed to do — and the longer the luteal phase, the more cumulative the harm of inadequate opposition.

Peat described the windows of vulnerability in his [[progesterone-summaries|*Progesterone Summaries* (2012)]] newsletter: "Symptoms in cycling women are most common around ovulation and in the premenstrual week, when the estrogen/progesterone ratio is normally highest. At puberty, in the early twenties and in the late thirties and menopause are the ages when the ratio is most often disturbed — and these are also the ages when thyroid disorders are commonest in women." The convergence of the two — a hormonal ratio biased toward estrogen, and a thyroid function biased low — is the substrate from which PMS emerges. The two reinforce each other. Low thyroid impairs the liver's ability to detoxify estrogen and impairs progesterone synthesis. Excess estrogen impairs thyroid hormone release and conversion of T4 to T3. The system spirals.

## The mechanism: E:P collapse and what follows from it

The simplest formulation Peat gave was that "an excessive estrogen/progesterone ratio is more generally involved in producing or aggravating symptoms than either a simple excess of estrogen or a deficiency of progesterone" ([[progesterone-summaries|*Progesterone Summaries*]]). PMS is the ratio disease. From that single relationship — too much estrogen relative to the progesterone available to oppose it — every other branch of the symptom tree descends.

**Water retention and bloating.** Estrogen causes the cell to retain water by raising intracellular pH, increasing the cell's negative charge, and increasing the affinity of cytoplasmic proteins for water. In [[water-swelling-tension-pain-fatigue-aging|"Water: swelling, tension, pain, fatigue, aging"]], Peat made the mechanism explicit: "Estrogen itself causes sodium loss, reduced osmolarity, and increased capillary leakiness." When water leaks out of the bloodstream into tissues, the swelling is felt as bloating in the abdomen, fullness and tenderness in the breasts (mastalgia), tightness around the eyes, and the characteristic premenstrual weight gain of three to five pounds within a few days. None of this water is "fluid imbalance" in the casual sense. It is estrogen-driven cellular hydration that progesterone normally reverses by causing the same cells to release water and re-establish the proper salt-to-water ratio. The same newsletter named the protective substance directly: "Progesterone, which helps to maintain blood volume (partly by preventing vascular leakiness, preventing excessive sodium loss and by supporting albumin synthesis) antagonizes aldosterone."

**Aldosterone as the water amplifier.** Mainstream advice — restrict salt to reduce premenstrual edema — relies on the assumption that water follows sodium. Peat insisted this gets the relationship backward in the context of a hyper-estrogenic state. Estrogen elevates aldosterone, the adrenal hormone that signals the kidney to retain sodium at the expense of potassium and magnesium. Low salt intake, far from reducing edema, raises aldosterone further (because aldosterone secretion is the body's response to perceived sodium deficiency), which raises prolactin, serotonin, and cortisol, and worsens every downstream symptom. Peat described the inflection point in his [[the-edema-of-stress|*Edema of Stress*]] newsletter: "Seeing that salt restriction never helped women with cyclic edema, I explained the mechanisms of water regulation, first to just one woman. Within a month, she was salting her food to taste (i.e., salting it heavily in the premenstrual week), and having no trouble with edema. Since then (1978) I have explained this to many women, and it always works for estrogen-induced cyclic edema." The first patient who tried it, as he recounted in his [[2011-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-water-retention-and-salt|2011 interview on water retention]], "had a total avoidance of premenstrual water retention the very first month."

**Prolactin and mood.** Estrogen also drives prolactin release from the pituitary. Prolactin is the lactation hormone, but in the non-lactating woman it does several things implicated directly in PMS: it raises aldosterone (compounding the salt-and-water disturbance), promotes water uptake by cells, contributes to breast tenderness, and exerts a depressing effect on dopamine. The breast tenderness that women report in the four to seven days before menstruation tracks prolactin elevation more reliably than it tracks any other hormone. The conventional explanation — that "estrogen sensitizes breast tissue" — is consistent with Peat's reading but misses the upstream lever. Lowering estrogen and raising progesterone lowers prolactin, and the tenderness resolves.

**Serotonin and the mood-anxiety-sugar-craving triad.** Estrogen is one of the more powerful natural inducers of serotonin synthesis, particularly in the gut, where 95% of the body's serotonin is made. Peat treated serotonin not as the conventional "neurotransmitter of happiness" but as a stress and inflammation signal whose elevation produces exactly the symptom cluster women report premenstrually: irritability, anxiety, depression, brain fog, carbohydrate cravings, irritable bowel symptoms, sleep disturbance, and headaches. In his [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|*Serotonin, Depression, and Aggression: The Problem of Brain Energy* (2012)]] newsletter, he wrote: "Irritable bowel syndrome, another kind of 'movement disorder,' can be treated effectively with anti-serotonin agents. This syndrome is very common in women, with premenstrual exacerbations, when estrogen is highest." The carbohydrate cravings that drug companies have framed as "sensitivity to mood symptoms" are, in Peat's reading, a metabolic intelligence — the cell asking for the substrate that will restore oxidative metabolism and lower the stress signal.

**Catamenial epilepsy as the extreme case.** The most striking evidence that PMS is an endocrine syndrome and not a psychological one is that the same hormonal pattern, taken to its extreme, produces seizures. In his [[2018-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-1|2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* interview on female hormones]], Peat made the bridge explicit: "Katharina Dalton was one of the early people working on that. She started treating PMS with progesterone in the 1940s. There isn't a terrible difference between catamenial epilepsy and PMS." Catamenial epilepsy is epilepsy whose seizures cluster premenstrually. Estrogen is excitotoxic; progesterone (via its metabolite [[allopregnanolone]]) is the most potent positive modulator of the GABA-A receptor in human biology. When the ratio inverts severely enough, the seizure threshold drops below baseline and the patient seizes. When it inverts less severely, she gets a migraine, an anxiety attack, or a depressive episode. The continuity from "psychological" to "epileptic" is a continuity of dose, not a categorical difference of mechanism. Peat's [[epilepsy-and-progesterone|*Epilepsy and Progesterone*]] article documents this in detail; the same hormones, in the same ratios, sit behind both.

**Migraine.** Premenstrual migraine is the same story compressed into one tissue: estrogen-driven hypoxia, cerebral vascular water retention, and serotonin release from platelets — the established triggers. The carrot-fiber observation in [[natural-estrogens|*Natural Estrogens*]] linked it to measurable estrogen: "Several women who suffered from premenstrual symptoms, including migraine, had their serum estrogen measured before and after the carrot diet, and they found that the carrot lowered their estrogen within a few days, as it relieved their symptoms."

**Cortisol and energy collapse.** Behind every elevation of estrogen, prolactin, aldosterone, and serotonin sits an elevation of cortisol. When progesterone is inadequate, the adrenal cortex uses pregnenolone — progesterone's own precursor — to make cortisol instead. The premenstrual sleep disturbance, fatigue, and the "out-of-energy" feeling conventional medicine reads as depression is, in this frame, a cell being run on cortisol instead of progesterone. Mechanism detail on the [[progesterone]] hub.

## The Katharina Dalton lineage

Peat did not invent the progesterone protocol for PMS. He inherited it. The British physician [[katharina-dalton|Katharina Dalton]] (1916-2004) was the first clinician to treat premenstrual syndrome systematically with progesterone, beginning in the 1940s and continuing for the next half-century. Dalton coined the term "premenstrual syndrome" in a 1953 *British Medical Journal* paper with Raymond Greene. Her clinical practice and her book *The Premenstrual Syndrome and Progesterone Therapy* (1977) are the source of the dosing principles Peat absorbed and modified.

Peat opened the very first paper he wrote on the transdermal route, [[transdermal-progesterone-for-premenstrual-syndrome|*Transdermal Progesterone for Premenstrual Syndrome*]] (Townsend Letter for Doctors, November/December 1983), with the Dalton citation: "For many years, Katharina Dalton has studied the use of progesterone therapy for the premenstrual syndrome. A typical patient may require ten or more progesterone injections per month, more or less permanently. While this is feasible (at least in some countries) it is not comfortable or convenient, in some cases leads to serious reactions at the injection sites, and in the United States would be too expensive for general use." Dalton's protocol worked — she had spent thirty years documenting that it did — but its delivery (intramuscular injection or suppository) made it inaccessible. Peat's contribution was procedural: he developed a transdermal vehicle (progesterone dissolved in vegetable oil and a lotion base) that allowed women to self-administer effective doses without injections. He reported on two hundred women treated this way: "We have used transdermal progesterone therapy in two hundred women suffering from the full range of premenstrual symptoms, including migraine, acne, depression, mastalgia, edema, and lethargy, and we found that nearly all of the women, applying the lotion themselves, are able to find the appropriate dosage for controlling their symptoms."

Dalton's clinical practice yielded a second observation central to Peat's later thinking. She followed the children of women she had treated with progesterone during pregnancy (originally for toxemia, not PMS). As Peat recounted on the [[progesterone]] hub, "her patients' babies, who usually came from working-class backgrounds with average IQs of 90, tended to average 130 IQ after progesterone treatment. The intelligence of the babies corresponded to the amount of progesterone she gave the mothers." A 40-point dose-dependent IQ shift. Dalton's PMS work and brain-development work converged on the same hormone — the substance that fixed PMS, prevented toxemia, and built fetal intelligence — left to a small group of clinicians on the margins while estrogen was rebranded as the "female hormone" and sold at scale.

## Protocols

Peat's PMS protocol stack is a layered approach. The progesterone is rarely a stand-alone fix. It works best when the underlying conditions that produce estrogen excess have already been addressed.

**1. Diet — year-round, not cycle-phase.** Peat did not prescribe a "luteal-phase diet." He prescribed the same nutrient-dense diet across the cycle and treated PMS as a symptom of failing on it:

- **Protein, 70-100+ g/day, including eggs.** In [[progesterone-summaries|*Progesterone Summaries*]] Peat wrote: "With a diet high in protein (e.g., at least 70-100 grams per day, including eggs) and vitamin A (not carotene), I have found that the dose of progesterone can be reduced each month." Protein insufficiency cripples the liver's ability to conjugate and excrete estrogen, raising the estrogen burden the corpus luteum's progesterone has to oppose. Eggs supply choline, vitamin A, and cholesterol — the precursor pool for steroid synthesis.
- **Adequate sugar (fruit, milk, honey, well-cooked starches), avoiding chronic low-carbohydrate eating.** Cortisol rises during the night and during food deprivation; the rise is what triggers conversion of pregnenolone (the precursor of progesterone) into cortisol instead. Eating sugar with salt before bed restrains the nocturnal cortisol rise and protects the next day's progesterone.
- **Low-PUFA.** Polyunsaturated fats — vegetable oils, fish oil, nuts, seeds, the [[pufa|whole class]] — are antithyroid and synergize with estrogen. PUFA-loaded membranes lower the threshold for every stress response in the body. Peat: "Unsaturated fat is antithyroid and synergizes with estrogen."
- **Daily raw carrot.** The fiber of a raw, peeled carrot binds bacterial endotoxin and estrogen circulating through the bile, preventing their reabsorption. Peat tracked serum estrogen drops within days of beginning the carrot, with corresponding symptom relief — the carrot-fiber observation in *Natural Estrogens* is the cleanest demonstration that "PMS symptoms" track measurable estrogen, not introspective mood states.
- **Calcium-rich (dairy, well-cooked greens).** Calcium supports thyroid function and progesterone production through multiple pathways; milk is also a good source of accessible cholesterol and the lactose that helps maintain stable blood sugar.

**2. Salt — more, not less, during the premenstrual week.** This is the single recommendation that diverges most sharply from mainstream advice. The conventional gynecology of the 1970s told women with cyclic edema to restrict salt; Peat told them to eat more, "to taste, even salting it heavily in the premenstrual week." The mechanism is the aldosterone story above. Restricting salt raises aldosterone, which retains sodium but at the cost of dumping potassium and magnesium, raising prolactin, and increasing serotonin synthesis. Eating to one's salt craving lowers aldosterone, restores potassium and magnesium balance, and — counterintuitively — acts as a diuretic by suppressing the hormonal axis that was retaining the water in the first place. Peat's clinical observation, repeated across patient populations from the 1970s onward, was that this single change "always works for estrogen-induced cyclic edema."

**3. Thyroid — find and correct the latent hypothyroidism.** "Premenstrual stress suggests that the thyroid function is low, at least during that time," Peat wrote to a correspondent in his [[menses|*Menses* email exchange]]. The diagnostic tools Peat preferred — morning oral temperature (target 98.0-98.6°F on waking, rising to 98.6 by mid-morning), resting pulse (target 75-90 BPM), Achilles reflex time, and the global symptom picture — are more sensitive to clinically relevant hypothyroidism than the standard TSH screen. When low thyroid is found, supplementation (typically a T4/T3 combination, dosed conservatively) restores the substrate for progesterone synthesis and normalizes estrogen detoxification.

**4. Progesterone dosing.** Peat's [[progesterone-summaries|*Progesterone Summaries*]] gives the operational instructions:

- **Timing.** "The normal pattern of progesterone secretion during the month is for the ovaries to produce a large amount in the 2nd two weeks of the menstrual cycle, (i.e., day 14 through day 28) beginning at ovulation and ending around the beginning of menstruation, and then to produce little for the following two weeks. An average person produces about 30 milligrams daily during the 2nd two weeks." Supplementation mimics this — days 14 through 28, then off — to keep the cycle going. Taking progesterone in the first half of the cycle can disrupt ovulation.
- **Dose.** "Three to four drops, or about 10 to 15 milligrams of progesterone, is often enough to bring the progesterone level up to normal. That amount can be taken days 14 through 28 of the menstrual cycle; this amount may be repeated once or twice during the day as needed to alleviate symptoms."
- **Vehicle.** Vitamin E. "When dissolved in vitamin E, progesterone begins entering the blood stream almost as soon as it contacts any membrane, such as the lips, tongue, gums, or palate, but when it is swallowed, it continues to be absorbed as part of the digestive process." Topical (transdermal, throat or inner arm) and oral (a few drops on the tongue, gums, or lips) both work; vitamin E is the vehicle that allows progesterone to bypass the liver's first-pass clearance long enough for it to act systemically.
- **Self-titration.** "We have used transdermal progesterone therapy in two hundred women… nearly all of the women, applying the lotion themselves, are able to find the appropriate dosage for controlling their symptoms" ([[transdermal-progesterone-for-premenstrual-syndrome|*Transdermal Progesterone for PMS*]]). The dose is what works. Some women need 5 mg, some 100 mg. Peat warned about under-application: "We have learned, when some women said the progesterone had no effect, that they were applying it as sparingly as they would a rare perfume, just touching it to their wrists."
- **No need to keep dosing indefinitely.** "Progesterone stimulates the ovaries and adrenals to produce progesterone, and it also activates the thyroid, so one dose can sometimes have prolonged effects. It shouldn't be necessary to keep using progesterone indefinitely, unless the ovaries have been removed."

**5. Adjuncts.**

- **Vitamin B6.** Pyridoxine reduces estrogen activity; Dalton was a strong advocate, and trials find moderate doses (50-100 mg/day) effective.
- **Magnesium.** Aldosterone wastes magnesium; deficiency contributes to cramps, vascular tension, anxiety, and headaches. Replenish via diet and a small supplemental dose.
- **[[aspirin|Aspirin]].** Inhibits aromatase and prostaglandins (the dysmenorrhea mechanism). Peat preferred aspirin over selective NSAIDs — broader anti-estrogenic effect, gentler profile at modest doses.
- **Pregnenolone.** "Pregnenolone doesn't have the direct hormonal effects, but it's the precursor, and by stopping exaggerated stress reactions it is likely to help" ([[menses|*Menses* email]]). A gentler upstream lever when stress dominates.

**6. Light, sleep, stress.** "Spending a summer in Alaska, with an unusually long day, may relieve the symptoms of a chronic sufferer. Dark cloudy winters in England or the Pacific Northwest are powerful stressors, and cause lower production of progesterone in women, and testosterone in men" ([[progesterone-summaries|*Progesterone Summaries*]]). Bright morning light lowers melatonin and aldosterone, the nocturnal stress cluster that aggravates PMS.

## Counter-arguments

The Peat framework on PMS does not occupy the mainstream position. The most important counter-arguments to take seriously:

**SSRIs are FDA-approved for PMDD and reduce symptoms in placebo-controlled trials.** Premenstrual dysphoric disorder ([[pmdd|PMDD]]) is the DSM-coded severe subtype of PMS. Fluoxetine (Prozac), sertraline (Zoloft), and paroxetine (Paxil) are licensed for PMDD; meta-analyses show effect sizes around 0.4-0.6 SD, which is clinically meaningful. Peat acknowledged the data and offered the mechanistic re-reading rather than denying the effect. In his [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression-the-problem-of-brain-energy|2012 *Serotonin* newsletter]] he noted: "One of the many actions of the 'SSRI' (such as fluoxetine, Prozac), which aren't related to their effect on serotonin, is to increase the concentration of allopregnanolone in the brain, imitating the action of increased progesterone. Following this discovery, Lilly got Prozac approved as a treatment for premenstrual syndrome. Since the production of allopregnanolone and progesterone depends on the availability of pregnenolone and cholesterol, a low cholesterol level would be one of the factors making this an inappropriate way to treat PMS." The SSRIs work for PMS, on Peat's reading, *because* they raise allopregnanolone (progesterone's metabolite) — i.e., they do the same thing oral progesterone does, indirectly, with a worse side-effect profile and the secondary harm of raising serotonin. The mainstream view inverts cause and effect: it treats the rise in allopregnanolone as a side effect of the "real" mechanism (serotonergic), when in Peat's framework the rise in allopregnanolone is the actual mechanism and the elevated serotonin is a separate, undesirable consequence.

**Oral contraceptives can suppress PMS by suppressing the cycle.** Combination OCPs (especially newer formulations with drospirenone, an aldosterone antagonist) reduce PMS symptoms in trials by abolishing the natural luteal phase entirely. The mainstream gynecologic position treats this as evidence that "the cycle itself" is the disease and the cycle's elimination is the cure. Peat rejected this framing categorically — the synthetic estrogens and progestins in OCPs are not the same molecules as endogenous estradiol and progesterone, they are pharmacologically more dangerous (with documented effects on thrombosis, stroke, breast cancer, depression, libido, and long-term fertility), and they suppress endogenous progesterone production through pituitary feedback, making the underlying deficiency worse. Drospirenone's modest anti-aldosterone effect is the right direction, but the package it comes in is wrong.

**"PMS is psychosomatic / sociocultural / overdiagnosed."** A persistent strain in academic gynecology and psychiatry, dating to the 1960s and resurfacing periodically, holds that PMS is a cultural construct — a label applied to ordinary cyclical fluctuations that women in non-Western cultures do not pathologize. The strain has empirical support insofar as PMS prevalence varies by population, and it has been used to argue against "medicalizing" the cycle. Peat's response was that the variation by population is itself a clue: the populations with the lowest PMS prevalence are also those with the most adequate protein, the lowest PUFA intake, the highest sunlight exposure, the least chronic stress, and the most preserved thyroid function. The variation is real and the mechanism is endocrine, not interpretive. Calling PMS a cultural construct does not make the elevated aldosterone or the depressed progesterone go away.

**Modern hormone-cycle research emphasizes "altered sensitivity" rather than "altered levels."** Studies measuring serum progesterone and estradiol across the cycle in PMS and non-PMS women have often (not always) found similar absolute levels. This is the empirical basis for the "sensitivity" hypothesis: the same hormones in different women produce different symptoms. Peat addressed this directly through the [[tissue-bound-estrogen-in-aging|tissue-vs-serum estrogen]] distinction. Serum estrogen is a poor proxy for tissue estrogen — the ratio of tissue to plasma estradiol can range from 1.5 to over 20, and the highest tissue concentrations occur exactly when serum concentrations are low. Studies that measure serum levels and find them "normal" in PMS are measuring the wrong compartment. The same is true for progesterone: serum levels can look normal while the tissues are functionally progesterone-deficient because of [[cortisol]] competition for the same receptors. The "sensitivity" the mainstream literature describes is, in Peat's reading, a difference in tissue-bound estrogen and in the cortisol/progesterone ratio at the receptor — which is exactly the difference his protocols correct.

**GnRH agonists "prove" the ovarian origin and justify chemical menopause.** A small literature supports GnRH agonist therapy (Lupron and analogues) for severe PMDD that resists other treatments. The argument is that abolishing ovarian cyclicity eliminates the syndrome, which "proves" ovarian hormones cause it. The mechanism Peat would object to is again inverted: ovarian estrogen and progesterone are both abolished by GnRH agonist therapy, including the protective progesterone the patient was already short on. The treatment imposes a state of induced menopause — with the documented harms to bone, cardiovascular function, mood, and cognition that follow — to suppress a syndrome that adequate progesterone alone can correct. The aggressive use of GnRH agonists for PMS is, in this reading, the reductio of the field's confusion about which of the two hormones is the problem.

## Data gaps

- **No large-scale randomized trial of Peat's full protocol exists.** Dalton's progesterone work was clinical and observational; Peat's was the same. The serious test would be a randomized comparison of (high-dose oral progesterone + thyroid + adequate protein + salt + low PUFA + B6) vs. (SSRI + OCP) over 12 cycles, with both subjective symptom scoring and objective markers (aldosterone, prolactin, thyroid panel, tissue estrogen where available). It has not been done. The pharmaceutical infrastructure that would fund it does not benefit from progesterone, which is not patentable. Independent foundations have not stepped in.
- **The role of [[allopregnanolone]] is more complex than a single signaling story.** A subset of the literature (Bäckström and colleagues) finds that allopregnanolone produces dysphoria in some women rather than calm — an inverted GABA-A response that the mainstream literature uses to argue that "progesterone causes PMDD." Peat responded to this directly in the [[2018-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-progesterone-vs-estrogen-listener-questions-part-1|2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] by pointing out the confusion of "depression" with "biological depression" (suppression of inflammatory excitation, which is therapeutic) — but the receptor-subunit story underlying the paradoxical responses is not fully worked out. Some women do report initial worsening on progesterone; whether this is the "estrogen-being-released-from-tissues" effect Peat described, a dose-response inversion, or a subset of GABA-A receptor configurations where allopregnanolone genuinely produces dysphoria is unresolved.
- **Cycle-phase eating remains underspecified.** Peat treated diet as roughly constant across the cycle. Some practitioners working in his tradition (and adjacent traditions) recommend more carbohydrate in the luteal phase to match the higher metabolic demand and the cortisol-suppression need. Whether this represents a real refinement or a placebo on top of the underlying protocol is unclear.
- **The interaction with hormonal contraceptive history is poorly characterized.** Women on combination OCPs for a decade or more have suppressed endogenous progesterone production for the duration; recovery time and prognosis for spontaneous cycling after discontinuation are not documented in any single source. Anecdotally, recovery can take months to years and may require thyroid and progesterone support throughout.
- **The role of gut endotoxin in PMS specifically is suggestive but not pinned down.** The general claim — gut-derived endotoxin elevates serotonin and is a tractable lever — is supported across other [[anxiety-depression|conditions]], but the specific contribution of LPS to luteal-phase symptoms has not been measured.
