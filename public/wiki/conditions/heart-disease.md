# Heart Disease

The dominant medical framework of the last sixty years treats coronary heart disease as a plumbing problem: dietary saturated fat raises serum cholesterol, cholesterol accumulates in arterial walls, plaque narrows the lumen, and eventually a clot occludes a vessel and kills a patch of myocardium. Every step of this chain has been monetized — low-fat margarine, polyunsaturated cooking oils, statins, antihypertensives, bypass surgery, stents. Ray Peat spent his career arguing that the chain is not merely incomplete but inverted at almost every joint. Heart disease, in his framework, is a thyroid disease. The cholesterol elevation that mainstream cardiology treats as a cause is, in his reading, a symptom of inadequate [[hypothyroidism|thyroid function]] — the diagnostic sign that physicians used to read in the 1930s before Synthroid and the Protein Bound Iodine test displaced clinical judgment. The arterial damage that the cholesterol hypothesis blames on saturated fat is, in Peat's reading, driven by [[pufa|polyunsaturated fat]] peroxidation in vessel walls already weakened by the metabolic and hormonal sequelae of low thyroid. And the statins that have become the most-prescribed class of drug in medical history deplete, in the same enzymatic stroke, the molecule the body raised to defend itself.

The hub link this article rests on is [[broda-barnes]]. Barnes was the Fort Collins physician whose decades of clinical practice produced what Peat described as the most extraordinary heart-disease statistic in twentieth-century medicine: across more than thirty years of treating thyroid patients with desiccated [[thyroid]] dosed to restore normal body temperature, not a single one of them died of heart disease. Peat cited this figure in dozens of interviews. "Broda Barnes, a pioneer in thyroid therapy, found that low thyroid regions had high rates of cancer and heart disease. He put his patients on thyroid supplements and in 30 years had no patient die of heart disease," he told listeners on [[2019-03-19-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-health-of-the-human-body|2019 *One Radio Network* on the health of the human body]]. The endocrine establishment did not test Barnes's claim. It changed the diagnostic criteria for thyroid disease and made the population from which he had drawn his evidence statistically invisible.

This article reconstructs Peat's case. The mechanism section traces the path from low thyroid through compensatory adrenaline, lactic acid, free fatty acid mobilization, vascular permeability, and oxidized PUFA in the arterial wall. The inversion section explains why the lipid hypothesis is, in Peat's reading, "little more than advertising copy" for the seed oil industry. The statins section catalogues the collateral damage from inhibiting the mevalonate pathway. The protocols section translates the framework into action. The counter-arguments and data gaps sections give the mainstream rebuttal its full weight.

## Mechanism: Heart Disease as Thyroid Disease

The clearest single statement of Peat's framework on the heart appears in his 2006 newsletter [[progesterone-not-estrogen-is-the-coronary-protection-factor-of-women|*Progesterone, Not Estrogen, is the Coronary Protection Factor of Women*]], where he wrote: "Broda Barnes was right when he said that the 'riddle of heart attacks' was solved when he demonstrated that hypothyroidism caused heart attacks, and that they were prevented by correcting hypothyroidism. He also observed that correcting hypothyroidism prevented the degenerative conditions (including heart disease) that so often occur in diabetics." The next paragraph in the same newsletter sketches the cellular chain that connects low thyroid to coronary pathology:

> "In hypothyroidism and diabetes, respiration is impaired, and lactic acid is formed even at rest, and relatively little carbon dioxide is produced. To compensate for the metabolic inefficiency of hypothyroidism, adrenalin and noradrenalin are secreted in very large amounts. Adrenalin causes free fatty acids to circulate at much higher levels, and the lactic acid, adrenalin, and free fatty acids all stimulate hyperventilation. The already deficient carbon dioxide is reduced even more, producing respiratory alkalosis. Free fatty acids, especially unsaturated fats, increase permeability of blood vessels, allowing proteins and fats to enter the endothelium and smooth muscle cells of the blood vessels. Lactic acid itself promotes an inflammatory state, and in combination with reduced CO2 and respiratory alkalosis, contributes to the hyponatremia (sodium deficiency) that is characteristic of hypothyroidism."

Every step in this chain is mechanically specified. The hypothyroid cell cannot complete the [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidation]] of glucose; it produces [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] instead of [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]]. The adrenal medulla compensates by secreting adrenaline, which forces hepatic glycogen out and mobilizes free fatty acids from adipose tissue. Those fatty acids, biased toward [[pufa|polyunsaturated]] forms after decades of seed oil consumption, increase the permeability of vessel walls. Proteins and fats enter the endothelium. The lactic acid and depleted CO2 produce alkalosis, hyponatremia, and edema. The combination — a cell membrane more permeable than it should be, in a tissue compartment biased toward lactic acid and free fatty acids, in a body whose protective hormones (progesterone, T3, CO2) are depleted — is the molecular precondition of atherosclerosis.

The cholesterol elevation that mainstream cardiology treats as the disease is one of the most reliable early warnings that this state has begun. As Peat wrote in [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud|"Thyroid Therapies: Confusion and Fraud" (2007)]], "by the mid-1930s it was generally known that hypothyroidism causes the cholesterol level in the blood to increase; hypercholesterolemia was a diagnostic sign of hypothyroidism. Administering a thyroid supplement, blood cholesterol came down to normal exactly as the basal metabolic rate came up to the normal rate." The thyroid-cholesterol mirror is treated in detail on the [[cholesterol]] page; the relevant point here is that the same hypothyroid state that floods vessels with free fatty acids also blocks the enzymatic conversion of cholesterol into pregnenolone, [[progesterone]], DHEA, and the other protective steroids. Cholesterol piles up in the blood because thyroid hormone is not present to drive its conversion. Statin medication for elevated cholesterol therefore corrects neither cause: it does not raise thyroid function and does not reduce PUFA-driven vascular permeability. It depletes the substrate the body needs to make its own protective hormones while leaving the upstream defect untouched.

The second mechanistic arm is hormonal. Peat returned across decades to Hans Selye's 1940s demonstrations that estrogen produces shock and that progesterone protects against stress-related pathologies. In the same 2006 newsletter, he listed estrogen's cardiovascular effects: "Estrogen itself intensifies all of these changes of hypothyroidism, increasing permeability and edema, and decreasing the force of the heart-beat, impairing the diastolic relaxation. Besides its direct actions, and synergism with hypothyroidism, estrogen also chronically increases growth hormone, which causes chronic exposure of the blood vessels to higher levels of free fatty acids (with a bias toward unsaturated fatty acids), and promotes edema and vascular leakage." Estrogen and hypothyroidism are not independent risk factors. They are a feedback loop: estrogen suppresses thyroid at the gland and at the [[liver]]; hypothyroidism weakens the liver's capacity to clear estrogen; the resulting hyperestrogenism amplifies every vascular harm of low thyroid.

[[Progesterone]] is the protective counterforce. Peat wrote in [[progesterone-not-estrogen-is-the-coronary-protection-factor-of-women|*Progesterone, Not Estrogen*]]: "Progesterone's effects are antagonistic to estrogen's. Progesterone decreases the formation of nitric oxide, decreasing edema; it strengthens the heart beat, by improving venous return and increasing stroke volume, but at the same time it reduces peripheral resistance by relaxing arteries (by inhibiting calcium entry but also by other effects, and independently of the endothelium) and decreasing edematous swelling." The hormone women lose at menopause is not estrogen but progesterone; women begin to die of heart attacks at rapidly increasing rates after the corpus luteum ceases to produce it, and men who have heart attacks have higher estrogen levels than men of the same age who have not. "During the years that men are beginning to have a considerable risk of heart attacks, with declining thyroid function indicated by lower T3, their testosterone and progesterone are declining, while their estrogen is rising. Men who have heart attacks have much higher levels of estrogen than men at the same age who haven't had a heart attack," Peat noted in the same newsletter.

The third arm is the heart muscle itself. In his 2015 newsletter [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]], Peat described the metabolic shift in failing myocardium: "Stress, in the form of pressure-overload, or overactivity of the renin-angiotensin system and sympathetic nervous system or adrenergic chemicals, or a failure of energy caused by diabetes, insulin deficiency, or hypothyroidism, causes a shift of energy production from the oxidation of glucose to the oxidation of fatty acids, with the release, rather than oxidation, of the lactic acid produced from glucose. This sequence, from reduced efficiency of energy production to heart failure, can be opposed by agents that reduce the availability of fatty acids and promote the oxidation of glucose. Niacinamide inhibits the release of free fatty acids from the tissues, and thyroid sustains the oxidation of glucose." The failing heart is not, in this view, a pump that has worn out. It is a tissue that has been forced — by hypothyroidism, by chronic adrenergic drive, by [[insulin]] dysregulation, by accumulated PUFA in mitochondrial cardiolipin — to switch from the clean oxidation of sugar to the dirty oxidation of fat, with lactic acid as a chronic byproduct.

The same newsletter explains diastolic failure in terms of calcium binding. Thyroid hormone regulates the synthesis of phospholamban and calsequestrin, the proteins that bind cytosolic calcium and allow the cardiomyocyte to fully relax between contractions. "When the cell is energized, by the mitochondria working with thyroid, oxygen, and sugar, these proteins rapidly change their form, binding calcium and removing it from the contractile system, allowing the cell to relax, to be fully prepared for the next contraction. Heart failure is described as 'diastolic failure' when the muscle isn't able to fully relax. In an early stage, this is just a waterlogged, fatigued condition, but when continued, the metabolic changes lead to fibrosis and even to calcification of the heart muscle," Peat wrote in [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]]. The lengthened QT interval that prefigures arrhythmia and sudden death is, on this view, the electrocardiographic signature of the same failure of relaxation that the Achilles tendon reflex test reveals in hypothyroid skeletal muscle.

The Szent-Györgyi staircase experiment, which Peat returned to repeatedly, made the hormonal asymmetry visible. As described in [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]]: "Szent-Györgyi found that estrogen treatment decreased the staircase effect, while progesterone treatment increased the staircase. He described the staircase as a situation in which function (the rate of contraction) builds structure (the size of the contraction). Progesterone allowed 'structure' to be built by the contraction, and estrogen prevented that." In standard cardiac physiology, a heart that beats faster also beats more strongly — function builds structure. In shock and in heart failure, the staircase collapses: pulse rate rises but stroke volume does not. Estrogen produces this decoupling experimentally. Progesterone restores it. The framework in which estrogen is "cardioprotective" — which still appears in mainstream cardiology textbooks — is, in Peat's reading, exactly upside down.

## The Mainstream Framework Being Inverted

The standard explanation of coronary heart disease was assembled between roughly 1950 and 1970 around what is now called the lipid hypothesis. Saturated fat raises serum cholesterol; cholesterol accumulates in arterial intima; an inflamed plaque ruptures and forms a thrombus; the thrombus occludes a coronary artery; the downstream myocardium infarcts. The prescriptions follow mechanically: eat less saturated fat and more polyunsaturated vegetable oil, lower serum cholesterol with statins, reduce blood pressure with various antihypertensives, and prescribe aspirin to prevent the final clot. The framework is taught in every medical school in the world.

Peat's case against this story is partly historical, partly biochemical, and partly commercial. The historical layer he traced repeatedly. In [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud|"Thyroid Therapies: Confusion and Fraud"]] he placed the relevant decade: "The biology of atherosclerotic heart disease was basically solved before the second world war." Barnes had done his PhD work at Chicago a few years after Otto Warburg in Germany had identified a respiratory defect at the center of cancer biology. Barnes removed the thyroid glands of rabbits and watched them develop atherosclerosis exactly as hypothyroid humans did. By the mid-1930s the cholesterol-thyroid mirror was established medical knowledge; hypercholesterolemia was diagnosed as hypothyroidism and treated by replacing the missing hormone. Then, in the late 1940s, the cholesterol hypothesis was reconstructed as a story about dietary fat, the Protein Bound Iodine test redefined thyroid prevalence from 40% to 5%, and Barnes's framework — never refuted — was rendered invisible.

The commercial layer Peat traced in [[progesterone-not-estrogen-is-the-coronary-protection-factor-of-women|*Progesterone, Not Estrogen*]]: "While estrogen was being offered as the hormone that protects against heart attacks, the liquid vegetable oils were being advertised as the food that would prevent heart attacks. Just a few years after the estrogen industry suffered the setbacks of the DES and heart attack publicity, the oil industry cancelled some tests of the 'heart protective diet,' because it was causing both more heart attacks and more cancer deaths. Somehow, these two fetid streams converged: Estrogen, like the unsaturated oils, lowered the amount of cholesterol in the blood, and an excess of blood cholesterol was said to cause heart attacks. (And, more recently, the estrogenic effects of the seed oils are claimed to offer protection against cancer.) The ability to lower the cholesterol 'risk factor' for heart attacks became a cultural icon, so that the contribution of estrogen and unsaturated oils to the pathologies of clotting could be ignored. Likewise, the contribution of unsaturated fats' lipid peroxidation to the development of atherosclerotic plaques was simply ignored."

The biochemical layer is the strongest. The cholesterol that mainstream pathology finds in atherosclerotic plaque is not native cholesterol but oxidized cholesterol — a chemically degraded form produced when free radicals attack the cholesterol molecule. The free radicals come predominantly from the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fats. The full mechanism is treated on the [[cholesterol]] page. The summary version is that the cholesterol in the arterial wall is there as a defensive patch over endothelium damaged by oxidized PUFA, and the cholesterol elevation in serum is the body's protective response to a stressed circulatory system that has lost the protective steroids cholesterol would normally become. Treating either reading as evidence of a cholesterol-driven disease confuses the firefighter with the fire.

The trial evidence that the saturated-fat–cholesterol–heart-disease chain depends on is, on close examination, far weaker than its cultural dominance suggests. The Swedish physician Uffe Ravnskov spent decades systematically dismantling it, and Peat cited his work as a more rigorous source than any establishment review. Studies that replace saturated animal fats with polyunsaturated vegetable oils have, in several large trials, produced increased — not decreased — total mortality, with the increase concentrated in cancer rather than appearing as a decrease in cardiovascular deaths. The Sydney Diet Heart Study, the Minnesota Coronary Experiment, and reanalyses of the Anti-Coronary Club data have all shown patterns inconsistent with the lipid hypothesis. The historical settlement was political and commercial; it has been maintained by institutional inertia rather than by reproducible biological evidence.

## The Statins Critique

Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme of the mevalonate pathway. The mevalonate pathway makes cholesterol. But it also makes [[coenzyme-q10|Coenzyme Q10]], dolichols (required for glycoprotein synthesis), isoprenoids (required for the prenylation of small GTPases involved in cell signaling), and several other molecules whose names appear in basic biochemistry textbooks but rarely in the patient information sheets that come with prescription statins. Peat treated statin therapy as a case study in how the lipid hypothesis became self-justifying: a drug developed to lower a number on a blood test ended up depleting a half-dozen substances the body actually needs, and the resulting harms were either misattributed or absorbed as acceptable trade-offs.

CoQ10 depletion was the harm Peat returned to most often. CoQ10 is the molecule that carries electrons between Complexes I/II and Complex III of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Without it, oxidative phosphorylation slows. Without it, the same myocardium that the statin is supposedly protecting cannot produce ATP efficiently. Statin-induced rhabdomyolysis — skeletal muscle dissolving into the bloodstream and clogging the kidneys — is the acute presentation of this mechanism. The chronic presentation, less dramatic but probably more widespread, is the diffuse muscle weakness, fatigue, and exercise intolerance that statin patients commonly report and that their physicians commonly dismiss.

The neurological harms are at least as well-documented. Cholesterol is a structural component of every synapse, every myelinated nerve fiber, and every neurosteroid that the brain manufactures locally to regulate mood, memory, and impulse control. Statins cross the blood-brain barrier (some preferentially) and inhibit cholesterol synthesis in the central nervous system. The early statin trials reported elevated rates of accidents, suicides, and violent deaths in the treatment arms — a pattern that the trial designers explained away as random noise but that has resurfaced in subsequent meta-analyses and in the Hungarian cholesterol-lowering data Peat cited repeatedly. The Framingham heart study, the same dataset that helped establish the lipid hypothesis, also showed that people with cholesterol below 200 mg/dL scored lower than higher-cholesterol controls on every cognitive domain measured. The "lower is better" doctrine, in Peat's reading, runs directly against the neuropsychological evidence.

The carcinogenicity of statins is the most contentious of these claims, but it has a clear mechanistic rationale in Peat's framework. Cholesterol is bound to chromosomes and to the mitotic spindle; it is required for orderly cell division; cells that divide with insufficient cholesterol produce aneuploid daughter cells; aneuploidy is a defining feature of cancer cells. The cancer signals in early statin trials were repeatedly attributed to chance; subsequent long-term data have shown enough additional signal that even mainstream review papers now acknowledge an open question. Peat's framework predicts the signal. Mainstream cardiology, having staked its credibility on cholesterol lowering, has been institutionally unable to take it seriously.

There is also the question of what the statin trials actually measured. Even on their own terms, the absolute reductions in cardiovascular mortality from statin therapy in primary prevention trials are small — a number-needed-to-treat in the hundreds to prevent a single event, with little or no all-cause mortality benefit in lower-risk populations. The relative-risk framing under which statins are commonly prescribed converts these modest absolute numbers into impressive-sounding percentages. In secondary prevention (patients who have already had a cardiac event), the numbers are stronger, but the mechanism may be pleiotropic — statins have mild anti-inflammatory and anticoagulant effects that operate independently of cholesterol lowering. The drug may "work" in a narrow sense without confirming the lipid hypothesis it was designed to act on.

The combination — small absolute benefit, large absolute harm in some populations, depletion of substances the body cannot replace pharmacologically — places statins, in Peat's framework, among the most consequential iatrogenic harms of contemporary medicine. The reframe is not that statins do nothing. It is that the doctrine under which they are prescribed (cholesterol causes heart disease; lower cholesterol prevents it) is inverted, and the molecule that the statin actually depletes (the body's master structural antioxidant) is one that the failing heart needs more of, not less.

## Protocols: What to Do Instead

The framework's prescriptions follow from its mechanism. The hub is [[thyroid]] supplementation, particularly with attention to the active hormone T3 rather than synthetic T4 alone. Many of the metabolic and vascular changes that drive heart disease respond within weeks to the restoration of body temperature, pulse rate, and metabolic rate that adequate thyroid produces. The dosing details, monitoring protocols, and individual variation are treated in [[hypothyroidism]]; this section assumes that page as the operating reference and notes only the cardiology-specific cautions.

The first caution is the starting dose. Peat repeatedly warned that introducing thyroid hormone abruptly in a person who has been hypothyroid for years can transiently strain the heart by drawing magnesium from cardiac muscle into the large skeletal muscles that suddenly have more thyroid activity to use it. "It's very important to make sure your diet includes plenty of magnesium when you're starting to use thyroid," he advised on [[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|2019 *One Radio Network* on vitamin D and thyroid]]. Starting at small doses (15-30 mg of desiccated thyroid, or 1-3 mcg of T3 with meals), monitoring temperature and pulse, and ensuring adequate dietary magnesium from green vegetables, coffee, chocolate, and the saltwater of mineral-dense broths converts a theoretical risk into a manageable one.

[[Progesterone]] is the second hub. Peat treated progesterone supplementation as a near-universal protective measure for women approaching or past menopause, and as a useful adjunct for any person with stress-pattern hypothyroidism in which adrenaline and cortisol are elevated. Progesterone strengthens the diastolic relaxation, improves stroke volume, reduces vascular permeability, reduces edema, and antagonizes estrogen at every relevant site. The vitamin-E-based oral progesterone formulations (Progest-E and analogs) provide rapid sublingual absorption and avoid the complications of transdermal carriers. Doses ranged in Peat's correspondence from 10 to 100 mg as a starting point; the heart-specific cases (women with elevated blood pressure, palpitations, edema, or post-menopausal coronary symptoms) often used the upper end.

[[Aspirin]] is the third pillar. Aspirin's antithrombotic effect is the one mainstream cardiology has retained from the broader picture, but in Peat's framework it is the smallest of the molecule's protective actions. Aspirin inhibits aromatase, reduces estrogen production, blocks the conversion of [[pufa|arachidonic acid]] to inflammatory prostaglandins, suppresses the release of free fatty acids from triglyceride stores, and shifts mitochondrial metabolism from fatty acid oxidation toward glucose oxidation — the same direction that thyroid and CO2 push. The cardiovascular dose Peat described in his correspondence is comparable to the standard low-dose aspirin used in cardiology (81-325 mg/day), but with vitamin K supplementation to prevent the bleeding-tendency side effects.

Dietary changes follow from the mechanism. Eliminating [[pufa|polyunsaturated fats]] — corn, soy, safflower, sunflower, canola, fish, and the polyunsaturated nuts and seeds — removes the substrate from which oxidized LDL, inflammatory prostaglandins, and PUFA-amplified estrogen effects are generated. The substitution targets are saturated animal fats (butter, ghee, beef tallow), [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], and small amounts of olive oil. The half-life of stored PUFA in body fat is on the order of four years, so the change is structural and takes years to complete; in the interim, supplementing vitamin E protects the stored PUFA from peroxidation during fasting and stress.

Sugar — specifically the fruit sugars of orange juice, ripe fruit, milk, and white sugar — supports the liver's conversion of T4 to T3 and prevents the nocturnal stress cascade that drives the morning surge of adrenaline and cortisol. The pattern Peat described repeatedly is that hypothyroid people whose liver glycogen is chronically depleted wake at 2-3 AM with palpitations because their adrenal medulla has been called on to maintain blood sugar; adding a small carbohydrate snack before bed (a glass of milk with sugar, a small serving of ice cream) often eliminates the pattern within days. Salt supports adrenal function, reduces the renin-angiotensin activation that drives both hypertension and fibrosis, and corrects the hyponatremia that hypothyroidism produces. The fear of dietary salt in cardiology rests on epidemiological associations Peat considered statistically weak and mechanistically backwards.

[[Carbon-dioxide|Carbon dioxide]] is itself therapeutic. The simplest method is bag breathing. As Peat described in [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]]: "Another technique that is easy to use to lower blood pressure and improve heart rhythm is to breathe into a paper bag for a minute or two at a time, to increase the carbon dioxide content of the blood. This has a vasodilating effect, reducing the force required to circulate the blood, and reduces anxiety. Rhubarb and emodin (a chemical found in rhubarb and cascara) have been found to have heart protective actions." Higher altitude, when geographically accessible, produces sustained elevations in CO2 retention and is associated in epidemiological data with lower coronary mortality.

Magnesium supplementation deserves its own mention. The cardiologists' fear of magnesium derives from a single class of patients (those in renal failure who cannot excrete it) and has produced a population-wide deficiency in everyone else. Magnesium glycinate, magnesium chloride, or topical magnesium oil at doses of 200-600 mg per day relaxes vascular smooth muscle, improves diastolic function, reduces arrhythmia, and supports the calcium-binding proteins that allow myocardial relaxation. Combined with the other elements of this protocol, it addresses an enormous range of "essential" hypertension and palpitation without lowering thyroid function or interfering with sleep.

Niacinamide (the amide form of vitamin B3) inhibits the release of free fatty acids from adipose tissue and shifts cardiac metabolism toward glucose oxidation. Peat cited it explicitly in [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]] as one of the practical interventions in heart failure: "Niacinamide inhibits the release of free fatty acids from the tissues, and thyroid sustains the oxidation of glucose." Doses of 100-500 mg, taken several times per day with meals, complement the dietary PUFA reduction by preventing the mobilization of already-stored PUFA during stress.

Pregnenolone, DHEA, and small amounts of testosterone (where indicated) round out the steroid protocol. Cholesterol is the substrate; thyroid and vitamin A are the conversion catalysts; the resulting steroids — pregnenolone first, then progesterone and DHEA, then the downstream sex steroids and cortisol — are the protective molecules whose deficiency the elevated cholesterol was signaling. Supplementing the downstream products bypasses the conversion bottleneck while the thyroid-vitamin-A axis is being restored.

What is conspicuously absent from this protocol is the centerpiece of modern cardiology. There is no statin. There is no low-fat diet. There is no fish oil. There is no LDL-lowering target. Peat regarded each of these as actively harmful: statins for the mevalonate-pathway depletion described above, the low-fat-high-PUFA diet for everything documented on the [[pufa]] page, fish oil for the immunosuppression and lipid-peroxidation cascade documented on the [[fish-oil]] page, and the LDL-lowering target for the cancer, dementia, and accident-mortality signals that emerge whenever cholesterol is pushed below approximately 160 mg/dL.

## Counter-arguments

Mainstream cardiology has not stood still since the 1960s, and the framework Peat critiqued has refined itself in directions he did not engage with at length. The counter-arguments below are the strongest versions of the rebuttals that working cardiologists would offer, presented in their best light so that the framework's claims can be evaluated against them.

**Mendelian randomization and LDL.** The most serious modern challenge to the "cholesterol is just a symptom" position comes from Mendelian randomization studies of natural genetic variants that lower LDL cholesterol lifelong. People who inherit loss-of-function variants in PCSK9, NPC1L1, HMGCR, or LDLR genes have lower LDL from birth and lower lifetime cardiovascular event rates roughly proportional to the magnitude of the LDL reduction. These genetic experiments are not confounded by diet, statins, or industry funding in the same way the dietary trials are. The simplest reading is that LDL itself, integrated across decades, contributes causally to atherogenesis — not as the sole cause, but as a contributing factor independent of thyroid status. Peat's framework would presumably respond that these genetic variants also affect cholesterol-derived signaling lipids and that the LDL number is a proxy for something else. But the Mendelian data is the strongest evidence the lipid hypothesis has, and it is genuinely difficult to dismiss.

**Apolipoprotein B as a refined risk marker.** Modern lipidology has largely moved past total cholesterol and HDL/LDL ratios to ApoB-containing particle counts as the operative risk variable. ApoB is the structural protein of LDL, IDL, VLDL, and [[lipoprotein-a|Lp(a)]] particles, and elevated ApoB correlates with cardiovascular risk more tightly than any of the older lipid measures. The argument is that it is not cholesterol as a chemical but the number of atherogenic particles infiltrating the arterial intima that matters. This refinement does not directly contradict Peat's mechanism (PUFA-driven vascular permeability still admits whatever lipoproteins are circulating), but it does push back on the idea that "cholesterol" is purely a defensive patch.

**The Lp(a) signal.** [[Lipoprotein-a|Lipoprotein(a)]] is a particle that resembles LDL but carries an additional protein (apolipoprotein(a)) that strongly resembles plasminogen and inhibits clot lysis. Elevated Lp(a) is largely genetic, not lifestyle-driven, and is one of the strongest single risk factors for premature coronary disease. The mechanism (impaired clot lysis combined with intimal lipid deposition) is well characterized at a molecular level. Peat's framework has relatively little to say about Lp(a); the existence of a strongly genetic, mechanistically clear lipid risk factor pushes back on the strong form of "lipids don't cause heart disease."

**Statin trials in secondary prevention.** The strongest evidence for statin benefit is in patients who have already had a coronary event. In these populations, the absolute reductions in recurrent events and cardiovascular mortality are larger and more consistent than in primary prevention. The mechanism is debated — pleiotropic anti-inflammatory effects, plaque stabilization, modest LDL lowering — but the trial evidence is harder to wave off as the artifact of industry funding alone.

**Inflammation as a parallel axis.** The hsCRP-guided JUPITER trial and subsequent work on IL-6 and IL-1β pathways (canakinumab in CANTOS) suggest that vascular inflammation is a distinct and modifiable axis of atherosclerotic risk, partially independent of cholesterol. Peat's framework actually accommodates this well — endotoxin, PUFA-derived prostaglandins, and estrogen all drive vascular inflammation in his model — but it suggests that "thyroid plus protective steroids" may be an incomplete protocol if it does not also address gut-derived endotoxemia and the chronic low-grade inflammation of subclinical infection.

**The Inuit and Mediterranean data.** Early epidemiological data on Inuit populations was used to support omega-3 fish oil supplementation. Peat aggressively dismantled this case (see [[fish-oil]]). But the Mediterranean diet evidence (PREDIMED, Lyon Diet Heart Study) is harder to dismiss, and the protective components are debated: olive oil, fish, wine, vegetables, low red meat, social meals, and seasonality all confound. The Mediterranean evidence is not strongly anti-Peat (olive oil is monounsaturated, not polyunsaturated; the diet is not low-fat), but it does suggest that dietary patterns matter at a population level in ways the Peat framework does not always cleanly predict.

**Heart-failure pharmacology actually works.** Modern heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is treated with beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors / ARBs, MRA antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone), and SGLT2 inhibitors. Multiple large randomized trials show substantial mortality reductions from these agents in HFrEF. Peat's framework prescribes thyroid, progesterone, and metabolic substrate optimization, but it does not engage in detail with why guideline-directed medical therapy works as well as it does in advanced heart failure. A working cardiologist would point out that "give T3, niacinamide, and salt" is not a tested protocol in HFrEF and that there is now thirty years of mortality data behind the conventional regimen.

**Selection bias in Barnes's data.** The most repeated single statistic in Peat's heart-disease framework is Barnes's "no patient died of heart disease in 30 years." This figure is real, but the cohort was self-selected (patients who sought out a thyroid-focused physician in a small Colorado town), the diagnostic criteria for thyroid status were Barnes's own, and the comparison was not against a matched control. Barnes published the figure in his own books rather than in peer-reviewed cardiology journals. The framework would be substantially stronger if a contemporary clinical research network attempted to replicate the finding in a registered cohort.

## Data gaps

Several questions in the framework are unsettled, and intellectual honesty requires their being named.

**T3 dosing in established CAD.** The framework's case for thyroid supplementation in heart disease prevention is well developed. The case for T3 supplementation in patients with established coronary artery disease is thinner. There is a small literature on T3 use in cardiac surgery (heart transplant donors, certain forms of cardiogenic shock), which Peat referenced in [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]]: "Heart transplant surgeons have discovered that administering T3 to the brain-dead heart donor before removing the heart improves its viability and function in the recipient." But the question of how aggressively to dose T3 in a patient with a 70% LAD stenosis and stable angina is not directly addressed in Peat's writing, and the question of whether thyroid replacement in such a patient could provoke an acute ischemic event by raising metabolic demand faster than the stenotic vessel can supply blood is real. The conservative approach (low starting dose, slow titration, magnesium supplementation, continuous symptom monitoring) Peat described is reasonable but has not been formally tested against guideline-directed medical therapy.

**Atrial fibrillation.** The framework predicts that AF is downstream of hypothyroidism, hyperestrogenism, and PUFA-driven inflammation, and that magnesium, taurine, thyroid, and progesterone should reduce its incidence. The clinical evidence supporting this prediction is sparse. Modern AF management (rate or rhythm control with appropriate anticoagulation) has substantial trial evidence behind it. Whether Peat's metabolic protocol can replace, complement, or only marginally improve on standard care has not been tested.

**Sex differences in the late-life risk pattern.** The framework's prediction that women have low heart attack rates before menopause because of progesterone, and increasing rates after because of progesterone loss, is partially supported by the epidemiology. But the actual sex-stratified data shows that women's cardiovascular mortality rises gradually with age and is dominated by heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) rather than acute myocardial infarction. The framework predicts HFpEF reasonably well (diastolic failure, edema, low CO2), but the specifics of why women's risk profile differs from men's at every decade are more complex than "loss of progesterone" alone explains.

**Genetic contribution.** Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) produces premature coronary disease in carriers of LDLR or PCSK9 loss-of-function variants. These patients do not have hypothyroidism, do not consume excessive PUFA in many cases, and develop atherosclerosis in childhood. The framework's response (genetic LDL elevation overwhelms even adequate thyroid status; statin therapy may be appropriate in this specific population) is reasonable but is a concession that lipids do, in fact, matter in some patients.

**Endotoxin and gut barrier.** Peat's later work increasingly emphasized endotoxin from the gut as a driver of inflammation, including vascular inflammation. The protocol implications (carrot salad, charcoal, antibiotics in severe cases, sugar to feed beneficial flora) are present in his correspondence but were never integrated into a unified gut-cardiovascular protocol. This is a gap that subsequent practitioners have begun to fill but that remains under-specified in Peat's own writing.

**Coronary microvascular disease.** Modern cardiology increasingly recognizes that the symptoms of ischemic heart disease in women (and in some men) frequently arise not from large-vessel atherosclerosis but from microvascular dysfunction at the level of arterioles too small to be seen on angiography. Peat's framework predicts this — PUFA-driven permeability, low CO2, hypothyroid edema, and adrenergic vasoconstriction all act preferentially on small vessels — but the specific therapeutic implications for diagnosed coronary microvascular disease have not been worked out in his corpus.

**Lp(a) and Apo(a).** The framework does not really engage with [[lipoprotein-a|Lp(a)]]. This is a known gap. A particle whose plasma concentration is set largely by a single gene and whose elevation is among the strongest predictors of premature coronary disease deserves more than the silence Peat's writing gives it.
