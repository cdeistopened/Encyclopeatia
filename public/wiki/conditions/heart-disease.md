# Heart Disease

The cholesterol theory of heart disease has dominated cardiology for decades, but Ray Peat considered it one of the most damaging misconceptions in modern medicine. In his framework, [[cholesterol]] does not cause heart disease—it rises as a protective response when [[thyroid]] function is too low to convert cholesterol into the steroid hormones the body needs. The real causes of heart disease are the same factors that drive all degenerative conditions: impaired energy production, [[PUFA]] accumulation, [[estrogen]] dominance, and chronic [[stress]].

[[Broda Barnes]] demonstrated this clinically over thirty years of practice. As Peat recounted in a [[2019-03-19-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-march-2019|2019 interview]], Barnes found that regions with low thyroid function had high rates of both cancer and heart disease, and that "in thirty years of thyroid therapy, none of his patients died of heart disease." The average dose was two grains of Armour Thyroid—a modest intervention with extraordinary cardiovascular results that mainstream cardiology has never seriously investigated.

## Cholesterol: Symptom, Not Cause

The relationship between thyroid and cholesterol is direct and dramatic. As Peat explained in a [[2012-08-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hot-flashes-night-sweats|2012 episode]], "Thyroid and cholesterol are mirror images. Removing the thyroid causes an immediate cholesterol rise; supplementation drops 450-500 cholesterol to normal in a week." This was established as early as 1993 in rabbit experiments showing that thyroid hormone prevented cholesterol-induced circulatory disease.

The cholesterol that damages arteries is not native cholesterol but oxidized cholesterol—produced when [[PUFA]] in lipoproteins undergo [[lipid peroxidation]]. As Peat noted in a [[2008-12-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cholesterol-is-an-important-molecule|2008 episode on cholesterol]], the original experiments used toxic oxidized cholesterol to produce atherosclerosis in animals. Native, unoxidized cholesterol does not damage blood vessels—it is a structural component of every cell membrane and the precursor to all protective steroid hormones.

Lowering cholesterol with statins, in Peat's view, attacks the body's protective response while ignoring the underlying metabolic failure. When cholesterol rises with age, the problem is inadequate conversion—insufficient thyroid and [[vitamin A]] to transform cholesterol into [[pregnenolone]], [[progesterone]], and [[DHEA]]. The brain, which has the highest cholesterol concentration of any organ, suffers most from artificial cholesterol reduction.

## Progesterone: The Heart's Protector

[[Progesterone]]'s heart-protective effects extend beyond its role as an anti-estrogen. As Peat described in a [[2013-05-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-heart-i|2013 episode on heart health]], "Progesterone not only has anti-seizure effects, but it has heart-protective effects—steadying the heart rhythm the way digitalis does and blocking aldosterone, preventing fibrosis of the heart."

The rhythm-stabilizing mechanism operates through the QT interval. [[Estrogen]] prolongs the QT interval—the time the heart takes to reset between beats—making arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death more likely. Progesterone shortens it, accelerating the heart's return to its resting, energized state. Research Peat cited concluded that "estrogen causes sudden cardiac death, and progesterone prevents it."

## PUFA and Cardiac Lipid Peroxidation

The heart is continuously supplied with fatty acids for fuel, making it particularly vulnerable to the effects of stored [[PUFA]]. As Peat discussed in his newsletter *The Generality of Adaptogens*, stress and catecholamine release drive fatty acid oxidation in the heart, and when those fatty acids are polyunsaturated, the result is lipid peroxidation that directly damages cardiac tissue.

[[Vitamin E]], [[coconut oil]], and other protective substances act as cardiac adaptogens precisely because they decrease lipid peroxidation in heart tissue. The Shute family's clinical work with vitamin E—preventing heart attacks and strokes through its anti-estrogenic and chain-breaking antioxidant effects—fits this mechanism perfectly.

[[Magnesium]] deficiency compounds the problem by destabilizing the heart's electrical system. When cellular ATP drops—from hypothyroidism, PUFA exposure, or chronic stress—magnesium is lost from cardiac cells and [[calcium]] floods in, promoting the hyperexcitability that underlies arrhythmias and ultimately cardiac failure.

## The Thyroid Solution

If heart disease is fundamentally a metabolic energy problem, then restoring metabolic rate is the primary intervention. Thyroid supplementation converts excess cholesterol into protective hormones, improves the heart's ability to oxidize fuel efficiently, and opposes the estrogen dominance that promotes cardiac inflammation and fibrosis.

[[Aspirin]] reduces the inflammatory prostaglandins produced from PUFA in cardiac tissue. Adequate [[calcium]] and [[vitamin D]] suppress [[parathyroid hormone]], which—like estrogen—impairs cardiac energy production. And reducing dietary PUFA eliminates the substrate for the lipid peroxidation that damages the heart under any metabolic stress.

Barnes's clinical record—thirty years without a cardiac death among thyroid-treated patients—suggests what is possible when the metabolic approach replaces the cholesterol-lowering approach. The absence of follow-up on this finding reflects the institutional priorities Peat criticized throughout his career.
