# Inflammation

In conventional medicine, inflammation was long viewed as simply a healing mechanism—the body's necessary response to injury or infection. Ray Peat inverts this understanding. In his framework, inflammation is not so much a curative process as it is the core mechanism of degeneration itself. What we call "disease" is often just inflammation given enough time.

"About 50 years ago, when I was in school, they were teaching that inflammation is part of the curative healing process—we needed it to kill germs and heal," Peat recalled in a [[2011-01-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-inflammation-i|2011 discussion on inflammation]]. "But in the last 10 or 20 years, the change has been seeing it occurring in all of the degenerative diseases: Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, heart disease, arthritis, and so on."

This recognition that inflammation underlies virtually every chronic condition—from [[cancer]] to [[heart-disease]] to [[dementia]]—represents one of the most important shifts in modern medicine. Understanding what actually causes chronic inflammation, and how to prevent it, becomes the central question of health.

## The Prenatal Model: Life Without Inflammation

Peat draws a striking contrast between fetal and adult healing. In the womb, tissue heals perfectly—without swelling, without scarring, without the cascade of events we recognize as inflammation. "If you look at the injury of the fetus, it heals without inflammation and it doesn't produce a scar. Basically, it's perfect healing."

What changes at birth? Two things happen simultaneously. First, exposure to [[carbon-dioxide]] drops dramatically while oxygen availability increases. Carbon dioxide, Peat argues, functions as a natural anti-inflammatory—"sort of a universal hormone of metabolism, keeping everything in the optimal condition." Second, the protective filtering of the placenta disappears. The uterus and placenta act as barriers that let very little [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fat]] into the developing organism. "Practically all newborns are considered deficient in the so-called 'essential fatty acids,'" Peat notes—but this apparent "deficiency" is actually a protected state.

The accumulation of polyunsaturated fats in tissues throughout childhood, combined with reduced carbon dioxide, "accounts for basically all of the features of inflammation and degeneration that are distinct from the prenatal healing process."

## PUFA: The Inflammatory Substrate

At the molecular level, inflammation requires substrate—and [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]] provide exactly that. These unstable oils oxidize readily at body temperature, producing lipid peroxides that trigger cascading damage. As Peat explained in a [[2009-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-bowel-endotoxin|2009 episode on bowel health]], "Since polyunsaturated fats are the main material that causes an explosion of free radicals once the process starts with endotoxin and nitric oxide, simply minimizing those in the diet is a basic way to defend yourself."

The evidence from animal research is remarkable. "A 1927 study showed no spontaneous cancer in rats on a fat-free diet. Roughly 100 studies show the beneficial effects of a so-called deficiency of polyunsaturated fats." When rats are made "deficient" in PUFA—meaning they receive purified nutrients without these unstable oils—"those animals can stand tremendous physical trauma that would kill a normal rat. They can be given cobra venom and survive. Toxins that would produce diabetes in normal rats don't hurt the deficient rats."

Fish oils, despite their marketing as health supplements, are no exception. As Peat noted in a [[2008-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-and-polyunsaturated-fatty-acids|2008 interview on thyroid and fatty acids]], "The breakdown products of the spontaneously oxidizing fish oil include acrolein, which is a carcinogen, and ethane. Several of these toxic breakdown products are immunosuppressive. They have an anti-inflammatory effect that in the short run makes them seem beneficial." This immunosuppression may reduce symptoms temporarily while accelerating underlying degeneration.

For a complete understanding of how these fats disrupt metabolism, see [[PUFA]].

## The Endotoxin Connection

Another fundamental driver of inflammation is [[endotoxin]]—the lipopolysaccharide from intestinal bacteria. When gut barrier function fails, endotoxin enters the bloodstream and triggers inflammatory cascades throughout the body.

Peat describes the mechanism in detail: "It triggers a whole series of reactions. It seems to be a physical-chemical process that stirs up a cell to recognize a threat. It is the things that it sets into action that really cause the chronic, accumulating degenerative damage." The immediate effects include cellular calcium uptake, nitric oxide synthesis, and energy disruption. "A small amount of endotoxin can stimulate the intestine to contract more, but if the cells are getting overloaded with it... it can cause cells to produce so much nitric oxide that it becomes a major metabolic disruptor."

The liver normally neutralizes endotoxin before it reaches systemic circulation. "If your liver is well nourished and energized, it can cut out practically 100% of the endotoxin." But stress impairs this defense. "If the intestine is stressed in any way—which can be nervous stress increasing adrenaline—that cuts down the blood supply. The reduced blood supply releases histamine and serotonin, which make the intestine more permeable, letting more of the endotoxin get into the bloodstream."

This creates the phenomenon known as "leaky gut." As Peat explains, it is not merely gaps between cells but rather "leaky cell substance"—the actual cytoplasm becomes spongy and permeable when energy fails. "As the cytoplasm takes up water, instead of being fat-like and tending to exclude water, the introduction of this sugar connected to a fat acts like a soap. It makes the cell tend to admit not only more water but pretty much anything that is in its environment."

For more on gut health and its relationship to inflammation, see [[digestion]].

## Estrogen as Inflammatory Hormone

[[Estrogen]] functions as a primary inflammatory mediator in Peat's framework. The connection works through multiple mechanisms: estrogen increases [[serotonin]] and [[prolactin]], both of which promote inflammation. It also causes immediate changes in cell water handling—"the fastest change you can see in a cell within a minute or two of exposing it to estrogen is that it instantly starts taking up water."

As Peat explained in a [[2011-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-inflammation-ii|2011 episode on estrogen]], "Estrogen is related to asthma too. The effect of serotonin is to cause localized contraction of the tubes in your lungs as well as causing them to take up water and promote inflammation."

The estrogen-inflammation connection helps explain why inflammatory conditions often worsen with age, particularly in women. Contrary to the conventional view that estrogen declines with age, Peat argues that estrogen accumulates in tissues as [[progesterone]] production falls: "As a woman ages from 19 to 39, there is a steady average increase in the amount of estrogen in her bloodstream. In the 40s, progesterone production is no longer able to keep up with this steadily increasing estrogen."

For the complete picture of estrogen's metabolic effects, see [[estrogen]].

## The Nitric Oxide Problem

[[Nitric-oxide]] has been celebrated since the Viagra era as a beneficial molecule that relaxes blood vessels. Peat takes a sharply different view. Nitric oxide is produced in response to stress and inflammation, and while it may provide temporary symptom relief, it accelerates underlying damage.

"It will cause the intestine to lose the ability to contract, and it will poison the respiratory apparatus so that cells can't make energy to fight back. At that point, with increased nitric oxide and reduced energy, the cells also take up water."

The parallel to radiation damage is striking: "X-rays and gamma rays will produce essentially the same kind of change in brain cells or bowel cells that endotoxin does. If you're overexposed to X-rays, for example, you'll get constipated the same way that overexposure to endotoxin will cause constipation."

Drugs that increase nitric oxide, including Viagra-type medications, are "extremely toxic. It's like eating toxins, getting irradiated, and getting old ahead of time."

## Anti-Inflammatory Strategies

If inflammation is driven by PUFA accumulation, endotoxin absorption, estrogen excess, and impaired energy production, then the solution involves addressing each of these factors.

### Dietary Approaches

Minimizing polyunsaturated fat intake is foundational. This means avoiding seed oils (corn, soybean, canola, safflower) and being cautious with even "healthy" oils like fish oil. [[Saturated-fat|Saturated fats]] from sources like butter and [[coconut-oil]] are protective. "There are groups curing cirrhosis and hepatitis by giving more saturated fats and excluding the unsaturated entirely. They are anti-inflammatory and therapeutic."

Sugar, rather than being inflammatory as commonly believed, actually protects against inflammation when used appropriately. "The presence of sugar makes it possible to energize and repair cells without drawing down more of these unsaturated fats, which produce all of the features we know of as inflammation." This explains the traditional practice of packing wounds with honey.

Keeping the intestine moving quickly reduces endotoxin absorption. "Raw carrots or bamboo shoots happen to be antiseptic vegetable material that doesn't feed the bacteria and tends to suppress bacterial growth... They also bind the endotoxin and other chemicals that should be eliminated."

### Hormonal Support

Our natural anti-inflammatory hormones—[[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], and [[thyroid]]—all decline with age and stress. Supporting their production counteracts the inflammatory cascade.

"The fetus is exposed to extremely high concentrations of progesterone until it's born. Keeping our production of progesterone as high as possible can make a big difference."

Adequate [[thyroid]] function is essential because it enables the high-energy state that resists inflammation. "If you can run the system fast enough with plenty of thyroid, you'll make the carbon dioxide, inhibit lactic acid formation, and pretty well defend against producing nitric oxide. This gives you relative immunity to the lipopolysaccharide."

### Aspirin and Related Compounds

[[Aspirin]] works through multiple anti-inflammatory mechanisms. "Aspirin, surprisingly, can act as a laxative because it suppresses nitric oxide and defends against endotoxin." It also has anti-estrogen effects.

Many fruit chemicals share properties with aspirin. "A lot of fruit chemicals are similar to aspirin and have a protective effect." Naringenin in oranges is one example. Caffeine from [[coffee]] is another anti-inflammatory ally: "When we were talking about anti-inflammatory substances, I should have mentioned caffeine as one of them."

### Protective Nutrients

[[Vitamin-E]] "can tremendously strengthen the cell and help to hold it together so it doesn't leak its protein substance out into the environment." [[Vitamin-K]] and [[coenzyme-Q10]] "are extremely powerful at defending the respiratory apparatus from nitric oxide and endotoxin."

Cascara sagrada, a traditional laxative herb, has structural similarities to these protective compounds. "Vitamin K, Coenzyme Q10, and progesterone have structural similarities to Cascara. It reinforces our defense systems. It is very unusual among drugs because it reinforces all the good processes—it is anti-inflammatory, anti-carcinogenic, helps reduce anxiety, and increases energy production the way thyroid and progesterone do."

## Inflammation and Specific Conditions

Understanding inflammation as the common mechanism helps explain why the same approaches benefit such different-seeming conditions:

- **Skin conditions**: "Low thyroid is probably the most common thing associated with ordinary eczema. When your thyroid is low, the circulation in your intestine is poor, serotonin is high, and the combination of endotoxin and serotonin will cause the skin to have abnormal growth patterns." Both eczema and psoriasis respond to reducing gut inflammation and supporting thyroid.

- **Arthritis**: The inflammatory mediators serotonin and estrogen directly affect joint tissue. "These enzymes can also be found in inflamed joint tissue... Serotonin is involved in the pathology of those situations."

- **Irritable bowel**: "It is basically an inflamed bowel. The same chemicals that can cause the usual paralysis that develops with aging (constipation) can shift over to causing intensified peristalsis, leading to diarrhea. It's an inflammatory process triggered by irritants."

- **Autoimmune conditions**: Peat reframes autoimmunity not as the immune system attacking healthy tissue, but as cleanup after damage. "What that means is that antibodies are part of a cleanup process. Something is disturbed, and the antibodies prepare the situation for our phagocytes to remove the damaged material."

## The Metchnikoff vs. Ehrlich Paradigm

Peat often returns to the historical divergence between two Nobel laureates: Ilya Metchnikoff, who saw immunity as an organizing principle for maintaining body structure, and Paul Ehrlich, whose "magic bullet" approach framed immunity as warfare against invaders.

"The idea of immunity as killing 'bad organisms' took over throughout the United States and most of the world for most of the 20th century." This paradigm, useful for selling drugs, missed the deeper truth that inflammation is primarily about energy, organization, and the maintenance of structure. When we restore energy production, provide the right substrates, and remove inflammatory triggers, the body's inherent capacity for organization reasserts itself.

The prenatal model shows this is possible. Perfect healing, without inflammation or scarring, is our biological birthright. The challenge is recreating the protective conditions of the womb in an adult body living in a world full of inflammatory triggers.
