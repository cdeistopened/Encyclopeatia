# Kidney Disease

Mainstream nephrology reads chronic kidney disease as a slow drift of a filter toward failure. The glomerulus is the filter, GFR is the rate, creatinine and BUN are the markers that rise as it clogs, proteinuria is the early sign that it has begun to leak, and progression through CKD stages 1–5 ends at dialysis. The interventions follow the filter logic: lower glomerular pressure with an ACE inhibitor, restrict protein, restrict salt, add a statin, and manage the rising parathyroid hormone at the end. The pattern is defensible within its frame, has been studied for forty years, and has produced a public-health outcome that anyone who has watched a relative on dialysis recognizes: the trajectory bends but rarely reverses.

Peat's reading inverts much of this. The kidney is not, in his framework, a passive filter that wears out. It is a metabolically active tissue whose blood flow, tubular function, and structural integrity depend on the same upstream variables that govern every other organ system — thyroid hormone, [[pufa|PUFA]] accumulation, [[estrogen]] balance, [[mechanisms/inflammation|chronic inflammation]], cellular glucose oxidation, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone (RAAS) hormonal stack the body uses to defend its blood volume. CKD is the renal expression of the same systemic phenotype that produces the heart disease at [[conditions/heart-disease|heart-disease]], the diabetic nephropathy at [[diabetes-complications]], the bone loss at [[osteoporosis]], and the soft-tissue calcification Peat returned to again and again. The filtration number is a downstream signal. The upstream defects are what need treatment.

The iatrogenic amplification is the second half of the inversion. Severe low-protein dieting drives sarcopenia in patients already in catabolic states. Severe salt restriction activates the very RAAS cascade the kidney runs, producing more vasoconstriction, more sympathetic drive, more PAI-1. ACE inhibitor monotherapy blocks one limb of that cascade while leaving the upstream activation unaddressed. Statin co-administration depletes [[cholesterol]] — the substrate the kidney's distressed tissue needs for steroidogenesis and membrane integrity. Fish-oil recommendations imported from cardiology accelerate the lipid peroxidation that drives the renal lesion. The cumulative iatrogenic load on a CKD patient in the modern American system is, in Peat's reading, substantial.

This article frames CKD as that broader metabolic phenotype, separates it from the diabetic-nephropathy material that lives in [[diabetes-complications]] and the polycystic-kidney case that needs its own treatment, and lays out Peat's intervention stack alongside a strong safety constraint: nephrology coordination is essential, and existing medications should not be stopped without a clinician.

## What's actually happening

The Peat-frame picture of CKD has four interlocking lesions plus a hormonal-stress overlay.

**Glomerular capillary leakiness.** The glomerulus is structurally a high-pressure capillary bed wrapped in podocytes; like every capillary bed, its integrity depends on adequate cellular energy and intact membrane lipid composition. From [[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|*Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer*]]: "Kidney disease, diabetes, pregnancy toxemia and retinal degeneration are probably the best known problems involving vascular leakage." Microalbuminuria is the renal-sampling expression of generalized capillary leakiness — the same leakiness that produces edema and pulmonary congestion. It tracks closely with hypothyroidism. As Peat noted in [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]], "Hypothyroidism is known to include deposition of mucopolysaccharides in tissues, increased permeability of capillaries with leakage of albumin out of the blood." The intervention is the one that restores cellular oxidation: [[thyroid]], magnesium, reduced [[free-fatty-acids|FFA]] load.

**Tubular damage.** Renal tubules are some of the most metabolically active cells in the body — they reabsorb glucose, sodium, amino acids, and water against gradients, all of which costs ATP. Chronic FFA exposure (from PUFA-loaded adipose tissue mobilized under cortisol-and-adrenaline drive) blocks pyruvate dehydrogenase and forces the tubule toward inefficient fatty-acid oxidation. Cellular ATP drops; magnesium leaves the cell; calcium enters; reabsorption fails progressively. The mechanism mirrors what Peat described in cardiomyocytes (see [[diabetes-complications]], the Meerson parallel).

**Interstitial fibrosis.** From [[fibrosis]]: "Retroperitoneal fibrosis, which can seriously damage the kidneys and other abdominal organs, has been compared to uterine fibroid tumors, and it is commonly treated successfully with the antiestrogenic drug, tamoxifen." Estrogen drives fibrosis. The kidney is an estrogen-target tissue with aromatase expression; the chronic-estrogen overlay drives the renal architecture from "leaky and edematous" to "fibrotic and irreversibly scarred." Progesterone is the antagonist.

**Chronic inflammation** — elevated [[endotoxin]], elevated [[serotonin]], elevated [[prostaglandins]], and the [[nitric-oxide]] surge Peat described in [[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|*When Energy Fails*]]. Endothelin-1, a powerful renal vasoconstrictor, is induced by the same cytokines that produce the wider inflammatory cluster. Chronic renal vasoconstriction is what mainstream nephrology measures as falling effective renal blood flow. Intervention is upstream anti-inflammatory: aspirin, vitamin D, progesterone, reduced PUFA, gut-endotoxin reduction.

**The RAAS activation cascade** is the hormonal-stress overlay. From [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|*Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity*]]: "It has been known for many years that decreasing sodium intake causes the body to respond adaptively, increasing the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). The activation of this system is recognized as a factor in hypertension, kidney disease, heart failure, fibrosis of the heart, and other problems." Angiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole (raising glomerular pressure short-term but driving long-term sclerotic damage), stimulates aldosterone, and activates sympathetic outflow. The standard nephrology move — ACE inhibitor plus salt restriction — addresses one downstream branch while sustaining the upstream activation. The Peat-frame correction is to *de-activate* RAAS: eat salt to physiological needs, support [[progesterone]] (a natural aldosterone antagonist), restore [[thyroid]], and reduce the systemic inflammation keeping the cascade running.

The late-CKD "uremic toxin" picture has a hormonal reading as well. From the [[kidney|*Email Topic: Kidney*]] file: "Has his vitamin D been checked? When it's low, parathyroid hormone rises, and it's one of the 'uremic toxins.'" PTH is pro-inflammatory and pro-calcification; vitamin D deficiency drives PTH up; PTH drives the soft-tissue calcification (heart valves, blood vessels, kidney parenchyma) that defines late CKD lethality. From [[2021-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2021-kmud|the 2021 *September* episode]]: "With aging and kidney failure, calcification of the kidney tissue and blood vessels becomes the lethal problem... Just supplementing a moderate amount of vitamin D, like 5,000 units a day for an adult, can be enough." Vitamin D plus dietary calcium suppresses PTH and removes the most consequential single uremic toxin.

## Types of kidney disease

**CKD stages 1–5** (eGFR > 90 to < 15). The Peat-frame intervention has the most leverage in Stages 1–3, where tubular and capillary lesions are still largely reversible. Stage 4–5 patients carry substantial structural fibrosis; metabolic correction still improves cardiovascular and quality-of-life trajectory, but full GFR recovery becomes implausible.

**Diabetic nephropathy** is the leading cause of ESRD in the U.S. and is treated at length in [[diabetes-complications]]. The Peat-frame addition is that microalbuminuria is the first measurable sign of generalized hypothyroid-tissue capillary leakiness; the intervention that closes the leak is thyroid restoration plus PUFA reduction — not just ACE inhibitor blockade of the hemodynamic consequence.

**Polycystic kidney disease (PKD)** is genetically driven (PKD1, PKD2) but progression is heavily modulated by metabolic variables; treated separately below.

**IgA nephropathy** sits in the chronic-inflammation cluster: chronic endotoxin and gut-derived antigens drive the IgA elevation. Progesterone, thyroid, aspirin, and gut-endotoxin reduction apply.

**Hypertensive nephropathy** traces to the same upstream cluster — hypothyroidism, salt restriction activating RAAS, magnesium deficit, PUFA-driven endothelial damage. See [[salt]] and [[when-energy-fails-edema-heart-failure-hypertension-sarcopenia-etc|*When Energy Fails*]].

**Lupus nephritis** and other autoimmune nephritides sit in the chronic-inflammation cluster with strong estrogen overlay (the female lupus predominance is striking); progesterone, thyroid, and the [[estrogen-detox]] stack apply.

## Peat's causal cluster

**PUFA accumulation in renal tissue.** Like every other tissue, the kidney's membranes reflect dietary fatty-acid composition over decades. Renal tubule cells are aerobic and PUFA-rich tissues are exquisitely susceptible to peroxidative damage under stress. The intervention is the [[pufa-depletion|PUFA depletion]] protocol — multi-year, slow, but the only durable fix at the membrane-lipidomic level.

**Iron accumulation.** [[iron|Iron]] is a Fenton-reaction catalyst; the kidney accumulates iron with age, and iron-loaded tubular cells with PUFA-loaded membranes are the ferroptosis substrate. Adequate copper, [[milk]] (low-iron, high-calcium), and avoidance of iron-fortified foods are part of the protective stack.

**Estrogen excess driving fibrosis.** The retroperitoneal-fibrosis/uterine-fibroid parallel from [[fibrosis]] is the renal-relevant capstone. Estrogen drives renal fibrotic deposition as it does in other organs. Progesterone is antagonistic. The chronic-estrogen-excess state women acquire after menopause (when progesterone falls) and men acquire with rising aromatase activity is a major driver of the fibrotic trajectory.

**Hypothyroidism reducing renal blood flow.** The hypothyroid kidney has reduced renal blood flow, reduced GFR (from the perfusion deficit before any structural damage), reduced tubular reabsorption, and the mucopolysaccharide-and-capillary-leakiness pattern of hypothyroid tissue generally. The single intervention that improves the largest number of CKD-relevant variables in the shortest time is thyroid restoration. See [[hypothyroidism]] and [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

**Chronic inflammation.** The gut-kidney axis is real; many CKD patients have substantially elevated systemic endotoxin and trimethylamine-N-oxide. The [[endotoxin-reduction|endotoxin reduction]] protocol — carrot salad, well-cooked starches, antibiotic flagyl in extreme cases, [[activated-charcoal|activated charcoal]] — applies. Aspirin reduces prostaglandin contribution.

**Salt-restriction iatrogenesis raising RAAS.** The standard recommendation is sodium under 2 grams daily. The Dinkov-Peat literature consensus (see [[salt-research]]) is that anything below ~5 grams of elemental sodium chronically activates RAAS. Sodium restriction, in the very population mainstream nephrology applies it to, accelerates the cascade it intends to slow. The Peat-frame correction is "salt food to taste."

## Peat's intervention stack

**Thyroid optimization** anchors the protocol. From the [[kidney|*Email Topic: Kidney*]] file: "When there's existing kidney disease, supplementing thyroid and progesterone speeds recovery. Urea is safe, but progesterone, pregnenolone, and thyroid are often curative for chronic kidney disease." From the [[2014-10-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-longevity|2014 *Longevity* episode]]: "You want to correct all the hormones involved in regulating kidney physiology, especially thyroid, progesterone, estrogen, cortisol, and pituitary stress hormones." Clinical signature: body temperature reaching 98.6 °F midday, pulse 75–85 bpm at rest. T3-containing supplements (NDT, Cynoplus, compounded T3) are preferred over levothyroxine alone — see [[thyroid-dosing]]. Dose under clinician guidance, especially in CKD where renal hormone clearance is altered.

**PUFA depletion** is the long-arc structural intervention. Eliminate dietary PUFA — no seed oils, no liquid vegetable oils, no fish oil supplements, no nut-heavy diets. Substitute saturated fats: butter, [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], [[ghee]], [[tallow]]. Adipose PUFA half-life is 2–4 years. See [[pufa-depletion]].

**Aspirin** daily is the standard Peat-frame anti-inflammatory base. The aspirin-versus-other-NSAIDs distinction matters more here than almost anywhere else in medicine: aspirin reduces prostaglandin-driven inflammation, has anti-fibrotic effects, and at low doses has been associated with renoprotection. NSAIDs other than aspirin (ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin, COX-2 class) reduce renal prostaglandins essential for afferent arteriole tone and should be avoided in established CKD. Dose 325 mg with food, clinician-coordinated.

**Gelatin plus adequate protein.** Mainstream nephrology recommends protein restriction (0.6–0.8 g/kg/day in non-dialysis CKD); Peat's reading is that this is mechanically backward in most cases and accelerates the sarcopenia-mortality trajectory. From the 2014 *Longevity* episode: "Normal functioning kidneys aren't stressed by a high-protein diet. However, when something is impairing kidney function, such as hypothyroidism or other sickness, then with low kidney function and bacteria in your intestine breaking down amino acids, you can get accumulated ammonia." The ammonia issue is the legitimate concern that motivated protein restriction; Peat's response is to address it directly — [[gelatin]] (rich in glycine, lowest ammonia profile), potato juice (Peat: "a few people who had ammonia on their breath every time they ate protein found that just a couple of meals of potato juice allowed them to start assimilating proteins"), gut-endotoxin reduction, raw carrot — rather than starve the patient. Full critique below.

**Salt to physiological needs.** From the [[2015-06-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-continuing-research-on-urea|2015 *Urea* episode]]: "Salting your food to taste is the best way to tell... The blood volume gets oxygen and nutrients to the kidneys and keeps them working." Chronic baseline: sodium to appetite, which in a healthy hormonal milieu lands at 4–6 grams of elemental sodium daily. Exception: the patient with acute severe edema and frank volume overload may need short-term sodium restriction during decompensation.

**Coffee, 2–4 cups daily** in generic CKD. The observational kidney literature consistently shows lower CKD progression and lower CKD mortality in coffee drinkers. Mechanism is multiple: caffeine, niacinamide precursors, magnesium, polyphenols. Exception: advanced PKD (next section).

**Magnesium** at 200–400 mg elemental daily (glycinate or chloride). The hypothyroid CKD kidney is magnesium-wasting; the deficit is one of the most consistent findings in CKD lab panels. Stage 4–5 patients coordinate with nephrology given clearance issues.

**CoQ10** at 100–200 mg daily for mitochondrial support in tubular cells.

**Vitamin D** to maintain serum 25(OH)D at 50–80 ng/mL. 5,000 IU daily is the typical adequate dose; this is the PTH-suppression intervention that drives the calcification-prevention picture.

**Progesterone (women)** at 25–50 mg daily during the second half of the cycle premenopausally, or daily postmenopausally, in [[progest-e]] or compounded oil-based forms. Anti-fibrotic, anti-aldosterone, anti-inflammatory.

**Adequate sugar** from fruit, [[orange-juice|orange juice]], milk, and honey — the substrate that allows the liver to make T3 and prevents the cortisol-adrenaline drive that mobilizes FFA.

## The protein-restriction critique

The MDRD trial (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease, 1994) is the largest randomized trial of dietary protein restriction in CKD. It enrolled 840 patients with moderate-to-severe CKD and randomized them to usual-protein, low-protein, or very-low-protein diets with keto-acid supplementation. The result, much-quoted in support of restriction, was substantially weaker than the recommendation that followed: the low-protein arm did not show statistically significant slowing of GFR decline in the primary analysis. Subsequent meta-analyses (including the 2009 Cochrane review) found small benefits at best, balanced against substantial concerns about protein-energy malnutrition. Long-term follow-up of MDRD showed *increased mortality* in the very-low-protein arm — a finding the nephrology field has largely buried because it contradicted the protocol.

The sarcopenia-mortality trajectory is the strongest argument against routine restriction. CKD patients are already catabolic from chronic inflammation, often hypothyroid (compounding the catabolism), and frequently anorexic. Reducing protein below 0.8 g/kg/day in such a patient drives muscle wasting, reduces albumin synthesis (paradoxically worsening proteinuria), and accelerates frailty. The Peat-frame correction is to address the legitimate underlying concern (ammonia and acid load on a damaged kidney) directly: gelatin-rich protein generates less ammonia per gram than meat-derived protein; alkalinizing minerals (calcium, magnesium, potassium from fruit) reduce the pH-balancing protein-breakdown burden; gut-endotoxin reduction reduces the bacterial-ammonia load. The potato-juice intervention exploits the ketoacid content of potato — the same ketoacids that nephrology prescribes as expensive supplements (Ketosteril) in advanced CKD.

The exception is the advanced ESRD patient with frank uremia, where short-term protein moderation is part of standard medical management. The argument against routine restriction applies to the much larger Stage 2–4 population in whom mainstream practice routinely prescribes 0.6–0.8 g/kg/day.

## Polycystic kidney emphasis

PKD has features that differentiate it from generic CKD and require modifying the stack.

**Vasopressin is the central modifiable driver.** Cyst growth in PKD is driven by vasopressin acting through V2 receptors on cyst epithelium, raising intracellular cAMP and driving fluid secretion. The mainstream pharmacological correlate is tolvaptan (V2 antagonist). The Peat-frame approach is to reduce vasopressin physiologically through adequate hydration (dehydration stimulates vasopressin), avoidance of caffeine in advanced PKD, and adequate dietary salt.

**Caffeine moderation in advanced PKD only.** The generic-CKD coffee recommendation does *not* apply to PKD patients with substantial cyst burden — caffeine raises cyst-fluid cAMP and accelerates cyst growth in PKD models. Early-stage PKD with mild cyst burden may tolerate moderate coffee; advanced PKD should moderate or eliminate.

**Protein from meat reduced; gelatin retained.** Animal-protein-heavy diets accelerate cyst growth through protein-load-induced glomerular hyperfiltration. Gelatin-anchored protein from collagen, eggs, and dairy is protective.

**Progesterone in women** has particular relevance in PKD: estrogen accelerates cyst growth, and the female PKD population progresses faster than the male population in some series.

**Hydration adequate but not excessive.** Keep urine specific gravity around 1.010 — approximately 2.5–3 liters of total fluid daily for an average adult, distributed across the day rather than in single large boluses.

## Acute kidney injury versus chronic

AKI is an emergency-medicine entity — sepsis, contrast, NSAID toxicity, rhabdomyolysis, post-renal obstruction. The acute drop in GFR over hours to days is hospital-managed; the Peat-frame chronic-metabolic framework does not apply during the acute episode. The patient post-AKI who survives the acute event is at substantially elevated risk of progressive CKD, and the chronic framework applies fully in recovery — thyroid restoration, PUFA reduction, adequate protein and salt, vitamin D, progesterone, aspirin, coffee.

## What to avoid long-term

**NSAIDs other than aspirin.** Ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin, and COX-2 inhibitors reduce renal prostaglandins essential for afferent arteriole tone. Associated with both AKI and accelerated CKD progression.

**Long-term ACE inhibitor or ARB without RAAS-balancing intervention.** The drugs are not contraindicated; they have an evidence base in proteinuria reduction. The critique is that they are typically deployed alongside salt restriction and untreated upstream dysfunction. Add the upstream interventions (salt to taste, thyroid, progesterone, magnesium) so the underlying RAAS activation declines and the ACE inhibitor can eventually be reduced under clinician supervision. **Do not stop ACE inhibitors or ARBs without nephrology coordination — abrupt discontinuation in advanced CKD with proteinuria can produce rebound damage.**

**Statins when alternatives exist.** Cholesterol is the substrate the kidney's distressed tissue needs for membrane integrity and steroidogenesis. The CKD-specific statin trial data (SHARP, AURORA) is mixed; the renal-outcome data is weaker than the advertising suggests. Address the underlying hypothyroid-driven hypercholesterolemia (thyroid restores cholesterol-to-pregnenolone conversion) rather than chemically blocking synthesis. Discuss with nephrology and cardiology before any change.

**Severe low-protein restriction** below 0.8 g/kg/day in non-dialysis CKD. Covered above.

**Fish oil supplements.** Hammes 1996 on accelerated retinopathy with fish oil (cited in [[diabetes-complications]]) extends mechanically to the renal microvasculature. Fish oils are highly oxidizable; CKD is a high-oxidative-stress state; the combination accelerates the very lesion the supplement is given to prevent.

**Iron supplementation** without confirmed iron-deficiency anemia. CKD-associated anemia in late stages is treated with erythropoietin under nephrology supervision; routine iron supplementation is a net pro-oxidant intervention.

**Phosphate-loaded food and drink** — cola, processed meats, packaged foods with phosphate additives. Calcium intake adequate to suppress PTH (1,000–1,500 mg daily from dairy) is the framework. See [[substances/minerals/phosphate|phosphate]].

## Tracking signals

The mainstream renal panel — serum creatinine, eGFR, BUN, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, electrolytes, PTH, vitamin D, hemoglobin — is appropriate and should not be abandoned. The Peat-frame additions:

- **Body temperature** on waking (target 97.8–98.6 °F) and after breakfast (target 98.6 °F) — the basic indicator of recovering cellular oxidation.
- **Pulse** at rest (target 75–85 bpm) — the second basic metabolic indicator.
- **Energy and stamina** subjective tracking — the most useful integrative signal. Stamina improves before lab numbers shift.
- **Edema** in ankles and around eyes — the visible expression of the capillary-leakiness picture. Improves with thyroid restoration and FFA reduction.
- **Proteinuria** — improvement on the protocol typically appears at 3–6 months.
- **GFR/creatinine** — in Stage 2–3, plateau or improvement is realistic; in Stage 4–5, plateau is realistic, reversal less common.
- **Blood pressure** — declining BP on stable medication doses signals the upstream intervention is working.
- **Serum 25(OH)D** at 50–80 ng/mL.
- **Serum magnesium** at high-normal.

## Counter-arguments

**ACE inhibitor renoprotection** has the strongest evidence base in proteinuric CKD. RENAAL (losartan), IDNT (irbesartan), and the broader meta-analytic literature show significant slowing of progression to ESRD. The Peat-frame argument is not that the drug is wrong but that upstream conditions are typically not addressed in parallel; addressing them allows the drug to eventually be reduced. The drug should remain in place until upstream intervention has produced sustained metabolic improvement.

**The MDRD low-protein-diet defense** acknowledges the modest GFR-decline benefit while accepting the sarcopenia trade-off. The strongest mainstream defense applies to the very-late-stage patient approaching dialysis where ammonia and acid-load symptoms dominate. The Peat-frame disagreement is largest in Stage 2–3 where restriction is routinely applied.

**Statin renoprotection** has been claimed in some trials (SHARP) and not others (AURORA). The defensive nephrology position is that CKD patients have such high cardiovascular risk that statin benefit applies even where renal-specific benefit is uncertain. The Peat-frame response is that the underlying hypothyroidism, PUFA load, and inflammatory state are doing the cardiovascular damage; cholesterol elevation is a marker.

**KDIGO guidelines** represent international consensus and are the basis for the practice patterns this article diverges from. The Peat-frame critique is that the guidelines optimize for measurable surrogate endpoints (GFR, proteinuria, BP) within a narrow intervention window without adequately considering the upstream metabolic state.

**Dialysis as inevitable late-stage outcome.** Stage 5 ESRD patients face a structural reality the upstream framework cannot reverse — nephron mass has been lost, fibrotic architecture is set, renal replacement therapy is necessary for survival. The Peat-frame contribution at this stage is supportive: maintain thyroid status, manage cardiovascular risk, optimize quality of life. Dialysis modality, transplant eligibility, and end-of-life planning are nephrology-coordinated.

**Hospital nephrology has the urgent-care competence** the Peat-frame framework does not claim. AKI, electrolyte emergencies, dialysis access, transplant immunosuppression are mainstream-medicine domains.

## Data gaps

- **No dedicated CKD-stage outcome data** on the integrated Peat-frame protocol applied to defined stages with mainstream-comparable reporting. The Broda Barnes diabetic-complications counterfactual is the closest historical analogue but is anecdotal.
- **Limited polycystic kidney coverage** in the Peat corpus directly. The vasopressin-and-cyst-growth mechanism is well-documented in the mainstream literature; the intervention emphasis is a reasonable extrapolation.
- **Acute kidney injury recovery protocols** are minimally covered in Peat's own writing. The mainstream nephrology-coordination requirement is firm.
- **Drug-specific renal clearance and dosing** for supplements (T3, progesterone, aspirin, magnesium) interacts with CKD-altered clearance in ways that require individualized clinician coordination.
- **Patient phenotyping for response prediction** — not every CKD patient responds to the same intervention with the same speed, and prospective phenotyping is not currently part of either Peat-frame or mainstream practice.

## Strong safety constraint

Chronic kidney disease is a serious diagnosis. Medications a CKD patient is currently on — ACE inhibitors, ARBs, statins, diuretics, phosphate binders, vitamin D analogs, erythropoietin — should not be stopped without nephrology coordination. The Peat-frame upstream interventions (thyroid, PUFA reduction, aspirin, gelatin, salt to taste, magnesium, vitamin D, progesterone) are introduced *alongside* the existing medical management, not instead of it. Improvements in upstream metabolic state allow downstream medication reduction over months to years, under clinician supervision and with regular lab monitoring. Abrupt changes — stopping an ACE inhibitor with active proteinuria, stopping a statin in a patient with active cardiovascular disease, adding high-dose thyroid in a patient on multiple cardiac medications — risk rebound damage and should be avoided.
