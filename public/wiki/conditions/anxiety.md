# Anxiety

Anxiety is not a psychiatric disorder requiring psychiatric drugs in Ray Peat's framework—it is a metabolic symptom of elevated stress hormones, particularly [[adrenaline]]. When cellular energy production fails, the body compensates by increasing stress hormone output to maintain blood sugar and vital functions. The person experiences this compensation as anxiety, tremor, insomnia, and racing thoughts. Addressing the metabolic root—typically [[hypothyroidism]]—resolves the anxiety that drugs can only suppress.

As Peat explained in a [[2019-07-18-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-milk|2019 episode]], "Low thyroid people chronically have anxiety. When energy production goes down—which happens to everyone during the night—the body compensates with increased adrenaline and cortisol."

## The Adrenaline Mechanism

The relationship between thyroid function and anxiety operates through adrenaline compensation. In a [[2016-05-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-exploring-alternatives|2016 episode]], Peat described how hypothyroid patients often have chronically elevated adrenaline: "When your thyroid is chronically low, you run around in the daytime with elevated adrenaline and cortisol. I've seen people with several times the normal amount of adrenaline production."

This adrenaline excess creates the classic anxiety symptoms: rapid heartbeat, shallow breathing, racing thoughts, difficulty relaxing, sleep disruption. The person is not psychologically disturbed—their body is running on stress hormones because their normal energy production is inadequate.

Some people can tolerate elevated adrenaline better than others. As Peat noted, "Some people can function normally with four times as much adrenaline as normal, but that's why some low thyroid people are constantly anxious and others seem calm." The variation in anxiety symptoms among hypothyroid patients reflects differences in stress hormone tolerance, not differences in psychological constitution.

## The Magnesium Connection

[[Magnesium]] deficiency compounds thyroid-related anxiety through a specific mechanism. In a [[2014-11-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-nitric-oxide|2014 episode]], Peat explained: "Thyroid makes your cells able to use magnesium. A big organ like your skeletal muscles and bones can take up so much from your blood that your brain and heart have trouble getting the magnesium they need to respond to the thyroid."

The result: "Then you get an exaggerated stress and adrenaline reaction" even when thyroid supplementation begins. This explains why some patients feel more anxious initially on thyroid—their magnesium status doesn't support the increased metabolic activity.

The solution is adequate magnesium intake alongside thyroid support. Foods like [[milk]], coffee, and cocoa provide magnesium in metabolically appropriate contexts. The mineral allows cells to relax properly, tempering the excitation that thyroid hormone promotes.

## Blood Sugar and Anxiety

[[Hypoglycemia]] directly triggers anxiety through adrenaline release. When blood sugar falls, adrenaline surges to mobilize glucose from glycogen stores and free fatty acids from adipose tissue. The person feels the adrenaline—anxious, shaky, unable to concentrate—even though the blood sugar number may look "normal" by the time they check it.

This explains why anxious people often feel better after eating, particularly after eating [[sugar]] or [[fruit]]. The glucose stops the hypoglycemic signal, allowing adrenaline to fall. Frequent small meals with adequate carbohydrate prevent the blood sugar crashes that trigger anxiety episodes.

## Correcting the Root Cause

Peat's approach to anxiety addresses metabolic function rather than brain chemistry:

**Thyroid optimization** is primary. Adequate T3 restores cellular energy production, eliminating the need for compensatory adrenaline. Many chronically anxious people are undiagnosed hypothyroid.

**Adequate carbohydrate intake** prevents the hypoglycemia that triggers adrenaline release. [[Sugar]], [[fruit]], [[orange juice]], and [[milk]] provide readily available glucose.

**Magnesium sufficiency** allows cells to respond appropriately to thyroid hormone without excessive excitation.

**[[Progesterone]]** opposes the excitatory effects of [[estrogen]] and provides neurosteroid calming. Many women's anxiety correlates with their menstrual cycle due to progesterone fluctuation.

**[[Calcium]]** and **[[sodium]]** support metabolic stability and reduce stress hormone activation.
