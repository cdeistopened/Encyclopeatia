# Obesity

In Ray Peat's framework, obesity is not a disease of gluttony, not a defect of "willpower," not a regulated set-point that the body defends against caloric restriction, and not — strictly speaking — a single disease at all. It is the most visible surface expression of a metabolic-respiratory failure: a body whose mitochondria have been damaged by accumulated polyunsaturated fat, whose thyroid signal is suppressed, whose stress hormones chronically mobilize free fatty acids that block glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]], whose endotoxin load keeps cortisol and estrogen elevated, and which therefore deposits and retains fat as a downstream consequence of an upstream substrate-handling defect. The "calories in, calories out" doctrine is thermodynamically true and physiologically misleading — it describes the bookkeeping without describing the regulation, and the fifty-year orthodoxy that built treatment around restriction (the eat-less-move-more imperative, the bariatric procedures, the GLP-1 agonists, the appetite suppressants, the fasting protocols) is iatrogenic on its own terms: caloric restriction destroys lean tissue and lowers the metabolic rate, producing the conditions for rebound weight gain at a lower set-point of respiration. Peat's central inversion of the obesity-treatment paradigm is that *body fat is the output and metabolic rate is the input*: the way to lose excess fat is to raise the rate at which the body burns substrate, which means feeding it adequately rather than starving it, and that the population-scale obesity epidemic is best explained not by behavior or genetics but by the slow seventy-year reformulation of the food supply around the same antithyroid polyunsaturated oils that 1940s farmers used to fatten livestock. Three companion articles carry the specialized loads: [[leptin-resistance-and-set-point|leptin-resistance-and-set-point]] for the modern-endocrinology critique, [[iatrogenic-obesity|iatrogenic-obesity]] for the epidemic-as-drug-and-diet-policy thesis, and [[weight-loss-protocol|weight-loss-protocol]] for the actionable clinical stack. *This page is documentation of Peat's framework, not medical advice; nothing here should be read as instruction to stop insulin, antipsychotics, GLP-1 agonists, or any other prescribed medication without your clinician.*

## What obesity is in this framework

The category error in mainstream obesity research, on Peat's reading, is the treatment of "obesity" as a disease entity whose defining feature is the accumulation of adipose mass above a BMI threshold. The 2013 American Medical Association reclassification of obesity as a disease formalized this category — and, in the process, accelerated the pharmacologic and surgical interventions that target the visible surface (adipose mass) rather than the upstream metabolic state. Peat's framing, which he held with little variation across forty years of newsletters and correspondence, is that adipose accumulation is the *symptom-cluster signature* of a particular metabolic phenotype: low [[thyroid|thyroid]] function, chronic free-fatty-acid exposure from [[pufa|PUFA]]-loaded fat stores, elevated stress hormones (cortisol, adrenaline), tissue-water mishandling, and — in women — estrogen excess and progesterone insufficiency. The same metabolic state, in a different person at a different life stage, expresses as edema, fatigue, infertility, brain fog, or cardiovascular fragility rather than as obesity per se. Treating the visible surface without changing the metabolic state forecloses recovery and produces the predictable iatrogenic complications.

The clinical pivot that follows from this framing is the distinction between *body composition* and *weight on the scale*. Peat repeatedly observed that successful metabolic intervention produces a characteristic pattern: water rebalancing first (often a brief weight rise or no scale movement as edema clears and intracellular hydration is restored), then muscle accretion (a continued period of stable or slightly rising scale weight as protein synthesis recovers on adequate thyroid and substrate), and only then sustained fat loss. From the [[weight-loss|email exchange on weight loss]]: "My recommendation is to eat to increase the metabolic rate (usually temperature and heart rate), rather than any particular foods. Usually the increased metabolic rate, with adequate protein, causes some muscle increase, and when that happens the basic calorie requirement will increase. The increase of muscle mass should continue for several weeks, and during that time the weight might increase a little, but usually the loss of water and fat will compensate for the greater muscle mass." The scale is the worst single indicator of metabolic recovery; mid-thigh circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, finger and toe warmth, the morning-to-afternoon temperature curve, resting pulse, and the speed of Achilles-reflex relaxation all carry more signal. The full body-composition-tracking layer sits in [[weight-loss-protocol|weight-loss-protocol]].

The framework is also not a denial that energy balance exists. A body that eats and absorbs more calories than it oxidizes will deposit the surplus; a body that oxidizes more than it absorbs will draw down stores. The conservation law is intact. The framework's claim is more specific: the *rate* of substrate oxidation, the *quality* of the substrate, and the *hormonal context* in which substrate is presented to the cell together determine whether a given caloric intake produces heat, lean tissue, fat storage, or some mixture. The first principle of obesity intervention, in this framing, is to ask what is wrong with substrate oxidation — not what is wrong with the patient's discipline.

## The 1940s livestock-fattening story

The single most evidentially distinctive piece of obesity history in Peat's writing is also one of the least cited in mainstream nutrition: the chemical and dietary lineage that runs from the 1940s feedlot industry's search for a livestock-fattening agent through the antithyroid drug trials, through the seed-oil substitution discovery, to the post-1950 reformulation of the human food supply around the same compounds that produced docile, fat, food-conserving cattle. Peat told the story most directly in the [[coconut-oil|"Coconut Oil"]] article: "In the l940s, farmers attempted to use cheap coconut oil for fattening their animals, but they found that it made them lean, active and hungry. For a few years, an antithyroid drug was found to make the livestock get fat while eating less food, but then it was found to be a strong carcinogen, and it also probably produced hypothyroidism in the people who ate the meat. By the late l940s, it was found that the same antithyroid effect, causing animals to get fat without eating much food, could be achieved by using soy beans and corn as feed."

The structure of the discovery is what makes it diagnostic. Farmers wanted feed that produced maximum carcass weight per pound of food consumed. Coconut oil — a cheap tropical saturated fat that became available in commodity volume in the early twentieth century — was the first candidate. It failed for fattening because it activates the metabolic rate: animals fed coconut oil ate more, burned more, moved more, and stayed lean. The next candidate was thiouracil, an antithyroid drug whose mechanism is the suppression of thyroid hormone synthesis. Thiouracil worked — animals fed thiouracil ate less, moved less, and gained weight efficiently — but it was found to be a carcinogen, and the meat from thiouracil-treated livestock was suspected of inducing hypothyroidism in the people who ate it. The drug was pulled from the feedlot. Then came the third candidate: soybean and corn-based feed. The animal fattening was equivalent to thiouracil's. The mechanism, as later research demonstrated, was the same — soy isoflavones (notably genistein and daidzein) and the polyunsaturated fatty acids of corn oil and soy oil are antithyroid in their own right, suppressing thyroid hormone synthesis, transport, and tissue response. The feedlot industry had its fattener; it was a chemical not officially classified as a drug; and the same compounds were already entering the human food supply as the cheap industrial oils that displaced butter, coconut oil, beef tallow, and lard from the American kitchen across the 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s.

The implication Peat drew, and the one mainstream nutrition has been institutionally unable to confront, is that the postwar American diet was reformulated around the chemical signature of a livestock-fattening agent. The aggregate per-capita seed-oil consumption in the United States rose from roughly two grams per day in 1909 to over eighty grams per day by 2010. Over the same period, average body weight rose, the diabetes prevalence rose, the metabolic-syndrome prevalence rose, and the population thyroid-marker distribution shifted toward hypothyroidism. The mainstream behavioral and genetic explanations — Americans got lazier, the genome accumulated obesity risk alleles, "obesogenic environments" emerged — have struggled to explain the timing, the dose-response, or the cross-cultural pattern in which populations that retained traditional saturated-fat cultures (the Tokelau islanders, the Yucatan coconut-eaters, the highland Swiss, the Inuit until refined-food introduction) stayed lean. The seed-oil-as-livestock-fattener hypothesis explains the timing, the dose-response, and the cross-cultural pattern with one mechanism. The chemical-fingerprint thread — thiouracil → methimazole → propylthiouracil → soy-isoflavone diet → linoleic-acid-rich seed-oil diet, all of them suppressing thyroid through different proximate mechanisms but with the same downstream output — is the through-line. The full historical, regulatory, and political-economic argument lives in [[iatrogenic-obesity|iatrogenic-obesity]] and in [[pufa|pufa]]; the hub names the smoking gun.

## Types of obesity

Peat treated the obesity category as physiologically heterogeneous rather than as a single disease. The same surface presentation can arise from different upstream lesions, and the intervention layer differs accordingly. The taxonomy below is descriptive, not prescriptive — the underlying axis is always the metabolic-rate / FFA / stress-hormone / hormonal-milieu state.

**Childhood-onset obesity.** Peat traced this back to prenatal and lactational PUFA imprinting. The mother eating large quantities of seed oils during pregnancy and lactation delivers PUFA-loaded milk and PUFA-saturated fat stores to the developing child; the developing adipose tissue, neuroendocrine system, and thyroid axis are calibrated downward; the child enters adolescence with a lower metabolic ceiling than a child of the same caloric intake would have had on a saturated-fat-dominant maternal diet. The intervention layer for childhood obesity is family-wide PUFA depletion, thyroid evaluation, gut decontamination, and explicit refusal of caloric restriction — children are growing and need substrate. The full child-feeding pattern sits in [[child-feeding|child-feeding]].

**Adult-onset obesity.** The most common type in the population data, typically presenting in the third through fifth decades. The mechanism is the slow accumulation of stored linoleic acid over decades (half-life roughly two to three years in adult adipose), the progressive suppression of T4-to-T3 conversion, the progressive elevation of resting cortisol and estrogen, and the visible weight gain that follows. The intervention layer is the [[weight-loss-protocol|weight-loss-protocol]] with the four-year PUFA-clearance horizon as the realistic timescale.

**Female obesity** has its own physiologic signature. Estrogen elevates free fatty acids via growth hormone, suppresses oxidative metabolism, increases water retention, and drives the gynoid (hip-and-thigh) fat distribution characteristic of premenopausal women. The female cycle imposes a roughly four-pound water-weight oscillation in the late luteal and premenstrual phases that mainstream weight-loss tracking misreads as fat regain. The estrogen-progesterone story is documented in [[estrogen|estrogen]] and [[progesterone|progesterone]]; the cycle-phase pattern is in the protocol page.

**Male obesity** typically presents as the central (android) pattern — visceral fat, the apple-shape — with portal-venous free-fatty-acid delivery to the liver as the proximate driver of the [[diabetes|metabolic-syndrome]] cluster. The mechanism overlaps with female PCOS-driven obesity through the same axis: estrogen elevation (from aromatization of testosterone in adipose tissue), low thyroid, chronic stress. The visceral-obesity-as-portal-FFA-pipeline thesis lives in [[insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome|insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome]] and is not re-derived here.

**PCOS-driven obesity** is the female cluster in which insulin resistance, androgen excess, and weight gain co-present in the second through fourth decades. The Peat-frame inversion is that the PCOS phenotype is driven by hypothyroidism + estrogen excess + endotoxin + stress, not by primary androgen overproduction. The full clinical picture sits in [[pcos|pcos]].

**Thyroid-driven obesity** is the textbook hypothyroid phenotype: cold extremities, fatigue, constipation, dry skin, slow Achilles relaxation, low resting temperature, and weight gain that resists every dietary intervention. From the [[weight-gain|email exchange on weight gain]]: "That sounds like standard hypothyroid symptoms, the body simply adjusts to holding more water, while losing sodium quickly. An increased intake of calcium is the single most important nutritional thing for losing weight." Thyroid restoration is the central intervention; the protocol is in [[hypothyroid-recovery|hypothyroid-recovery]].

**PUFA-driven obesity** is the chronic-substrate-quality phenotype that overlaps with all the others. The 1940s livestock-fattening story is the population-scale evidence; the personal anchor is Peat's own observation that switching from a PUFA-rich diet to a saturated-fat-dominant diet produced metabolic surges, slight transient hyperthyroidism, and progressive weight loss without caloric restriction.

**Drug-induced obesity** is the iatrogenic category — antipsychotics (olanzapine, clozapine, risperidone), corticosteroids (prednisone, dexamethasone long-term), insulin in Type 2 diabetes, SSRIs, beta-blockers, tamoxifen, lithium, antihistamines, anticonvulsants, the antithyroid-drug-as-livestock-fattener historical case. The full ledger lives in [[iatrogenic-obesity|iatrogenic-obesity]]. *Patients on any of these drugs should never stop them abruptly or without clinician coordination — the protocol page documents how to work alongside conventional treatment.*

**Gestational and postpartum weight retention** is the female life-stage variant. Pregnancy is, by design, a state of insulin resistance, elevated free fatty acids, and substrate redirection toward the fetus; the postpartum failure-to-mobilize is typically a thyroid and prolactin pattern rather than a calorie-balance failure. The full picture is in [[pregnancy-nutrition|pregnancy-nutrition]] and the protocol page.

**Stress-cortisol-driven obesity** is the chronic-allostatic-load phenotype: visceral fat accumulation under sustained cortisol elevation, often presenting as the Cushing's-syndrome-like cluster that Peat described in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues* (2012)]] as the misnamed "metabolic syndrome." The intervention layer is anti-stress diet + thyroid + endotoxin reduction.

## Peat's causal cluster

The framework organizes the causation of obesity around six tightly linked upstream conditions, each of which has its own dedicated vault page. The point of this section is to show how they converge on the obesity state, not to re-derive any of them.

**1. Hypothyroidism.** The proximate metabolic lesion. The thyroid axis controls the rate at which mitochondria oxidize substrate, the conversion of cholesterol to active steroid hormones, the maintenance of body temperature, the speed of gut transit, and the responsiveness of the heart to substrate availability. A low-thyroid body burns less substrate at every level of intake; the surplus is deposited as fat. Peat returned to this point in nearly every newsletter that touched obesity. The thyroid-restoration layer is documented in [[thyroid|thyroid]] and [[hypothyroid-recovery|hypothyroid-recovery]].

**2. PUFA accumulation.** The structural carcinogens of the framework are also the structural obesogens. Stored linoleic acid and arachidonic acid in adipose tissue have a half-life of roughly two to three years; the slow tissue-clearance timeline (four years to substantially deplete a heavily PUFA-loaded adult) is the rate-limiting step in metabolic recovery. The mechanism — PUFA suppression of T4-to-T3 conversion, PUFA suppression of tissue response to thyroid hormone, PUFA substitution into mitochondrial cardiolipin weakening cytochrome oxidase, lipid-peroxidation products that drive inflammation — sits in [[pufa|pufa]] and [[pufa-depletion|pufa-depletion]].

**3. Chronic stress and cortisol.** Sustained cortisol elevation mobilizes free fatty acids from adipose tissue into the bloodstream, blocks glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]], drives visceral fat deposition, and degrades lean tissue. The cortisol-adrenaline cascade is documented at the systemic level in [[blood-sugar|blood-sugar]] (Selye's 300 mg% rule, the never-be-hungry grazing principle), and the visceral-obesity-as-Cushing's-cluster framing is in [[insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome|insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome]].

**4. Estrogen excess in women.** Estrogen elevates free fatty acids, suppresses oxidative metabolism, stimulates lysyl-oxidase-mediated collagen cross-linking, drives water retention and intracellular alkalinization, and — via growth hormone — accelerates fat mobilization without restoring respiration. The female cycle imposes a roughly four-pound late-luteal water-weight oscillation that is often misread as fat regain. Estrogen excess in postmenopausal women, driven by aromatase activity in adipose tissue (which itself is elevated by adipose mass — a self-reinforcing loop), is the dominant female obesity driver. See [[estrogen|estrogen]].

**5. Gut dysbiosis and endotoxin.** Lipopolysaccharide leaking from the colon (the leak accelerated by PUFA, slow transit, stress, and a high-starch diet) reaches the liver via the portal vein, where it provokes Kupffer-cell activation, serotonin release, cortisol elevation, and free-fatty-acid mobilization. The chronic systemic inflammation that mainstream literature blames on "inflammaging" is, in Peat's reading, largely an endotoxin signal in a stressed-respiration tissue field. The bowel-motility intervention layer (daily raw [[carrot-salad|carrot salad]], well-cooked mushrooms, judicious cascara, occasional antibiotics) is in [[endotoxin-reduction|endotoxin-reduction]]. From the [[weight-loss|email on weight loss]]: "Yes, I know people who have lost weight just by eating a raw carrot every day, reducing endotoxin stress."

**6. Low CO₂ and structured-water disturbance.** The slowest-burning thread in Peat's framework, and the least well documented in mainstream physiology, is the role of carbon dioxide in stabilizing cell water, supporting respiration, and preventing the structural-water mishandling that produces edema. Low metabolic rate produces low CO₂; low CO₂ produces unstable cell water and tissue swelling; tissue swelling presents on the scale as weight gain that has nothing to do with adipose mass. The bag-breathing and altitude effects on weight are real and underappreciated; the full mechanism is in [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative-metabolism]].

These six drivers converge. None of them act in isolation. The obesity state is the predictable steady-state output of a body running on PUFA-substituted mitochondria, with hypothyroidism, chronic cortisol, estrogen excess (in women), endotoxin load, and low CO₂ all reinforcing each other. The intervention layer addresses all six.

## The "calories in, calories out" critique

The Peat-frame position on the energy-balance equation is precise enough to warrant a careful statement, because the mainstream framing of his work as "anti-CICO" has produced both straw-man dismissals and overzealous embraces. The conservation law is intact: a body cannot deposit fat from caloric intake that did not occur, and cannot mobilize fat to replace caloric intake that exceeds expenditure. CICO is a thermodynamic identity. The critique is that it is *only* a thermodynamic identity — it describes the bookkeeping after the fact, but it does not describe the regulatory mechanism that determines, at any given caloric intake, how much of that intake becomes heat (oxidation to CO₂ and water), how much becomes lean tissue (protein synthesis), how much becomes fat storage, and how much is lost as undigested substrate, water, or other excretions.

The regulatory mechanism is the one this framework is built around: thyroid hormone, free-fatty-acid level, stress hormones, estrogen-progesterone balance, gut endotoxin, and substrate quality (saturated vs. unsaturated fat; sugar vs. starch; protein-amino-acid balance) together determine the disposition of any given caloric load. A 2,500-calorie diet of low-fat milk, eggs, orange juice, fruit, and a daily carrot, on a body with restored thyroid and depleted PUFA stores, will produce lean tissue accretion, high heat output, and fat loss. The same 2,500 calories from corn-oil-fried foods, soy-protein bars, and starchy snacks on a PUFA-loaded, hypothyroid, cortisol-elevated body will produce fat deposition, water retention, low heat output, and weight gain. Per Peat in the [[weight-loss|weight-loss email]]: "Per calorie, sugar is less fattening than starch, partly because it stimulates less insulin, and, when it's used with a good diet, because it increases the activity of thyroid hormone." The substrate matters. The hormonal context matters. The mitochondrial state matters. The thermodynamic identity is true but underdetermined.

The clinical corollary is that the CICO model, when applied as treatment, gets the causal arrow backward. The model predicts that lowering caloric intake will produce weight loss in a roughly linear fashion. The data show that caloric restriction produces a brief weight loss followed by metabolic adaptation: thyroid output falls, T4-to-T3 conversion drops, lean tissue is cannibalized to spare adipose stores (because the body reads sustained caloric deficit as a famine to be survived), resting metabolic rate falls by ten to twenty percent within months, and the body settles at a new steady state in which the lower caloric intake matches the lower output. When the patient resumes eating at any level above the adapted floor, fat is preferentially deposited because the adapted physiology is now a fat-storage physiology. The Biggest-Loser cohort, tracked six years post-restriction by Hall and Kerns, showed persistent resting-metabolic-rate suppression of roughly 500 calories per day. This is not a discipline failure on the part of the dieters. It is the predicted consequence of treating the symptom (high adipose mass) without addressing the upstream metabolic state. The full critique of caloric restriction sits in [[intermittent-fasting|intermittent-fasting]].

## Why low-fat dieting fails

The 1970s through 2000s low-fat orthodoxy — replace saturated fat with seed oils, eat complex carbohydrates, restrict total calories — was the most thoroughly tested obesity-prevention experiment in human history, and obesity prevalence in the United States roughly tripled across the same decades. The Peat-frame explanation is multifactorial. The replacement fats (corn, soy, safflower, canola) are precisely the antithyroid PUFAs the framework identifies as obesogens; substituting them for butter, lard, and tallow accelerated the metabolic suppression the dietary advice was meant to prevent. The carbohydrate channel — when filled with refined starches rather than ripe fruit, milk sugars, or sucrose — drove endotoxin production and the chronic-inflammation pattern Peat documented as obesogenic. The calorie-restriction layer produced the adapted-metabolism rebound. The cholesterol-reduction came at the cost of pregnenolone and progesterone synthesis — the same steroid pathway that maintains thyroid responsiveness and antagonizes estrogen. The low-fat orthodoxy converted half the substrate channel (fats) into an antithyroid input and the other half (carbohydrates) into a stress-and-endotoxin input. The predictable output is what happened.

## Why low-carb and keto dieting fail

The countermovement that emerged in the 2000s — the Atkins revival, the Lustig sugar-cult literature, the Taubes carbohydrate-insulin model, the ketogenic-diet boom — got one thing right (refined starches and seed oils are obesogenic) and inverted the framework on every other axis. Peat opposed carbohydrate restriction with the same vigor as fat restriction. Sugar — specifically the sucrose-fructose-lactose family of natural-food sugars — is the cleanest substrate the human mitochondrion can burn: it bypasses the Randle block, stimulates thyroid activity, supports liver glycogen replenishment, and produces the calcium-magnesium-sodium handling that prevents hypothyroid-pattern edema. Restricting sugar forces the body to run on free fatty acids — exactly the substrate state the framework identifies as the proximate driver of insulin resistance and chronic stress.

The ketogenic-diet weight loss is, in the Peat frame, water loss (glycogen binds roughly three grams of water per gram) followed by adapted-low-metabolism stabilization on chronically elevated cortisol and growth hormone. Sustained over months, the pattern produces the same adapted-low-metabolism state as caloric restriction, with the added cost of suppressed T3 and PUFA mobilization from adipose stores (worse in a PUFA-loaded body, because more linoleic-acid metabolites enter the bloodstream). The carbohydrate-insulin model's central claim — that hyperinsulinemia is the cause of obesity, and lowering insulin via carb restriction will reverse it — inverts the Peat-frame causal arrow: hyperinsulinemia is the body's secondary response to PUFA-driven blocked glucose oxidation; the upstream lesion is the PUFA, not the insulin. Fuller critique in [[insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome|insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome]] and [[leptin-resistance-and-set-point|leptin-resistance-and-set-point]].

## The Peat counterexamples

The framework's most distinctive evidentiary anchors are three observations that the calorie-balance and carbohydrate-insulin models cannot account for and that the metabolic-rate model predicts.

**The Yucatan +25% metabolic rate datum.** G. W. Crile and his wife, working in the 1930s, surveyed basal metabolic rates across populations and found that "the metabolic rate of people in Yucatan, where coconut is a staple food, averaged 25% higher than that of people in the United States" ([[coconut-oil|*Coconut Oil*]]). The adaptive prediction would have been the opposite — populations in hot climates typically run lower metabolic rates to reduce heat load. The Yucatan population ran hotter despite the climate. The dietary signature was coconut: a tropical saturated-fat-dominant diet, low in seed oils, with traditional sugar sources (fruit, agave, honey) and adequate calcium from lime-processed maize. The same population was, by Peat's reading of the field reports, notably lean and notably free of the menopausal symptoms common in the United States. The Yucatan datum is the population-level anchor for the entire framework: a body running on saturated tropical fat substrate, with active thyroid and adequate sugar, runs hot and stays lean almost regardless of the caloric arithmetic.

**The Schweitzer Africa observation.** Albert Schweitzer, operating his clinic in tropical Africa in the early twentieth century, reported that "it was many years before he saw any cases of cancer, and he believed that the appearance of cancer was caused by the change to the European type of diet" ([[coconut-oil|*Coconut Oil*]]). The same population showed extremely low rates of the obesity, diabetes, heart disease, and cancer cluster that Western nutrition came to call the "diseases of civilization." The dietary signature was traditional African — saturated palm and coconut fat, traditional grains, fresh fruit, traditional fermented beverages, low refined-seed-oil exposure. The transition to the European diet (the introduction of seed oils, refined flour, and processed foods) was accompanied by the emergence of the disease cluster. The cancer story is documented in [[cancer|cancer]]; the obesity dimension of the same observation is that the populations that ate traditionally did not become obese, and the populations that adopted the European diet did.

**The Peat self-experiment: 185 → 175 lbs on +200-250 daily calories of coconut oil.** The personal anchor for the entire framework's prediction is Peat's own published experiment with himself. From [[coconut-oil|*Coconut Oil*]]: "I had put a tablespoonful of coconut oil on some rice I had for supper, and half an hour later while I was reading, I noticed I was breathing more deeply than normal. I saw that my skin was pink, and I found that my pulse was faster than normal — about 98, I think. After an hour or two, my pulse and breathing returned to normal. Every day for a couple of weeks I noticed the same response while I was digesting a small amount of coconut oil, but gradually it didn't happen any more, and I increased my daily consumption of the oil to about an ounce. I kept eating the same foods as before (including a quart of ice cream every day), except that I added about 200 or 250 calories per day as coconut oil. Apparently the metabolic surges that happened at first were an indication that my body was compensating for an anti-thyroid substance by producing more thyroid hormone; when the coconut oil relieved the inhibition, I experienced a moment of slight hyperthyroidism, but after a time the inhibitor became less effective, and my body adjusted by producing slightly less thyroid hormone. But over the next few months, I saw that my weight was slowly and consistently decreasing. It had been steady at 185 pounds for 25 years, but over a period of six months it dropped to about 175 pounds. I found that eating more coconut oil lowered my weight another few pounds, and eating less caused it to increase."

The experiment is diagnostic in three ways. It violates the calorie-balance prediction (added calories, lost weight). It demonstrates the metabolic-rate mechanism (the transient hyperthyroid surges, the visible pulse and breathing response, the pinker skin — all signs of the thyroid response being released as the antithyroid PUFA inhibition was diluted). And it inverts the dose-response: more coconut oil produced more weight loss, not less. The framework's central prediction — that the way to lose excess fat is to raise the metabolic rate by feeding the body the substrate it needs, not by restricting calories — is in this single observation. From the [[weight-loss|email exchange]]: "Yes, it's best to lose it slowly. When I tried adding about a tablespoon of coconut oil once a day I lost about two pounds a week, for several weeks, without eating less." The full substance-level case for coconut oil sits in [[coconut-oil|coconut-oil]]; the protocol-level dose-titration sits in [[weight-loss-protocol|weight-loss-protocol]].

## Mainstream treatment critique

The Peat-frame critique of mainstream obesity treatment runs across roughly seven categories of intervention, each of which targets the surface (adipose mass) rather than the upstream metabolic state. The full case lives in [[iatrogenic-obesity|iatrogenic-obesity]]; the hub presents the survey. *None of the following should be read as instruction to discontinue any prescribed medication. Patients on any of these interventions should never stop them without clinician coordination.*

**Caloric restriction** produces brief weight loss followed by metabolic adaptation — adapted-low-thyroid steady state, lean-tissue loss, rebound weight regain at a lower metabolic ceiling. The Biggest-Loser data and the broader adaptive-thermogenesis literature (Rosenbaum and Leibel; Hall and Kerns) confirm the prediction.

**Fasting and intermittent fasting** are caloric restriction concentrated into windows; the metabolic-adaptation cost is the same, with the added cost that prolonged fasting destroys tissue in a specific order (water → glycogen → lean tissue → fat), often cannibalizing brain mass and skeletal muscle before fat stores are seriously touched. The full critique sits in [[intermittent-fasting|intermittent-fasting]].

**Bariatric surgery** produces dramatic short-term weight loss through forced caloric restriction (gastric bypass, sleeve gastrectomy, banding) but at substantial long-term cost: micronutrient deficiencies, accelerated osteoporosis, sarcopenia, the dumping syndrome, an eating-disorder pattern that emerges in roughly a quarter of post-surgical patients within five years, and weight regain in the majority by ten years. The intervention attacks the visible adipose mass while leaving the upstream metabolic state intact; the predictable iatrogenic costs follow.

**GLP-1 agonists** (semaglutide, tirzepatide; brand names Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro) produce dramatic weight loss via slowed gastric emptying, suppressed appetite, and increased glucose-dependent insulin secretion. The side-effect signal — gastroparesis, pancreatitis risk, substantial lean-mass loss (often a quarter to a third of the weight lost is lean tissue), the muscle-wasting pattern that mimics the cachexia of advanced cancer, and rebound weight gain on discontinuation — is the predictable consequence of inducing a low-intake state on a stress-mediated system. Peat died before the GLP-1 boom; the framework's prediction is straightforward: drugs that lower intake without addressing the upstream metabolic state will reproduce the caloric-restriction adaptation pattern at higher pharmacologic intensity. Fuller treatment sits in [[diabetes-protocol|diabetes-protocol]] and in [[iatrogenic-obesity|iatrogenic-obesity]].

**Appetite suppressants and stimulant-thermogenic stacks** (phentermine, lorcaserin in its brief market life, ephedrine-caffeine, the various 1990s fen-phen and 2000s sibutramine fiascos) produce short-term weight loss at the cost of chronic adrenergic activation, cardiovascular risk, and the predictable adapted-metabolism rebound on discontinuation. The mechanism — driving the body harder on stress hormones to mobilize fat — produces the same chronic-cortisol pattern that the framework identifies as obesogenic.

**HRT and growth hormone for cosmetic fat loss.** The 1990s and 2000s anti-aging clinic boom marketed HRT in postmenopausal women and recombinant growth hormone in men as fat-loss interventions. Both achieve short-term cosmetic effects; both produce the long-term costs the framework predicts. Estrogen replacement increases breast and uterine cancer risk and the Women's Health Initiative cluster (stroke, blood clots, dementia). Growth hormone — Peat treated this in *Growth Hormone: Hormone of Stress, Aging, and Death* — is a stress hormone that mobilizes adipose tissue at the cost of accelerated cancer risk and the long-term acromegalic-metabolic-syndrome cluster. The cosmetic-fat-loss-at-the-price-of-long-term-cancer pattern is the through-line.

**Low-salt diets and diuretics.** The most-overlooked iatrogenic obesity driver in mainstream practice. Peat documented in [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|*Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity*]] that "since [the diuretics] functioned by causing sodium to be excreted in the urine, their sale was accompanied by advising the patients to reduce their salt intake to make the diuretic more effective." The downstream effects — activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, increased serotonin and sympathetic activity, increased PAI-1, capillary leakage, hypothyroid-pattern water retention — produce the edematous weight gain that the diuretics were meant to treat. The diuretic-fluid-retention cycle is one of the iatrogenic obesity drivers most invisible to mainstream clinicians.

The pattern across all seven categories is consistent: the intervention targets the visible surface (adipose mass, edema, appetite) rather than the upstream metabolic state, and the predictable iatrogenic costs follow. The full case is in [[iatrogenic-obesity|iatrogenic-obesity]].

## The metabolic-syndrome cluster

The clinical cluster that mainstream endocrinology calls "metabolic syndrome" — visceral obesity, fatty liver, elevated triglycerides, low HDL, hypertension, glucose intolerance, hyperinsulinemia, the PAI-1 hypofibrinolysis pattern, microalbuminuria — is the chronic-phenotype face of the obesity problem. Peat treated the cluster as substantially misnamed: the underlying state is closer to a low-grade Cushing's syndrome (chronic cortisol elevation with secondary visceral obesity and insulin resistance) than to a primary insulin-resistance disorder. The full clinical-phenotype treatment sits in [[insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome|insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome]] — the Wave 12 companion article — and is not re-derived here. The hub-level point is that the metabolic-syndrome cluster and the obesity cluster are the same upstream metabolic state expressed at different points on the severity axis; treating one without the other is the predictable mainstream error.

## Peat's intervention overview

The actionable clinical stack — what to eat, what to dose, in what order, with what tracking signal, in coordination with conventional treatment — is the territory of [[weight-loss-protocol|weight-loss-protocol]]. The hub presents the architecture in six moves; the protocol page does the dose ranges, the cycle-phase subprotocols, the alongside-vs-instead-of decision tree, and the four-year timescale.

**1. Restore respiration.** Thyroid is the central intervention. Coconut oil supplies medium-chain saturated fats that bypass the Randle block. Niacinamide lowers free fatty acids. Aspirin protects against prostaglandin and lipid-peroxidation cascades. B-vitamins (thiamine, pantothenic acid, B6) support carbohydrate-oxidation enzymes.

**2. Reduce the PUFA load.** Multi-year work — the four-year tissue-clearance horizon is the realistic timescale. Remove dietary seed oils, fatty fish, supplemental fish oil; substitute saturated fats (butter, coconut oil, tallow); support detoxification with vitamin E and antioxidants. See [[pufa-depletion|pufa-depletion]].

**3. Supply adequate sugar and protein.** The baseline diet from the [[weight-loss|email on weight loss]] is "low fat milk, eggs, orange juice, and a daily carrot or two." Two quarts of milk daily for the calcium-to-phosphate ratio. Eighty grams of protein minimum. Sugar via fruit, orange juice, milk lactose, and added sucrose where needed. Salt to taste.

**4. Address endotoxin.** Daily raw [[carrot-salad|carrot salad]], well-cooked mushrooms, restriction of fermentable starches. The carrot-only intervention has produced weight loss in patients without any other dietary change. See [[endotoxin-reduction|endotoxin-reduction]].

**5. Restore liver function.** The fatty, glycogen-depleted liver of the obese patient cannot convert T4 to T3 efficiently. The first three moves all support liver recovery; the specific protocol is in [[liver-restoration|liver-restoration]].

**6. Address the cycle and life stage (women).** Progesterone in the luteal phase, attention to the premenstrual water-weight oscillation, perimenopausal and postmenopausal adjustments to progesterone dosing, postpartum lactation as a fat-mobilization opportunity. See the women's-cycle subsections of [[weight-loss-protocol|weight-loss-protocol]].

The framework's clinical signature, consistent across all the condition-protocol pairs in the vault, is "alongside, not instead of": never stop insulin without practitioner coordination, never discontinue antipsychotics or GLP-1 agonists abruptly, monitor temperature and pulse daily to gauge metabolic recovery, watch for the body-composition-vs-scale-weight tracking pattern, and treat the four-year PUFA-clearance horizon as the realistic timescale.

## Tracking signal

The mainstream obesity monitoring panel — body weight on a scale, BMI, waist circumference, body-fat percentage on a bioimpedance device — was treated by Peat as informative but secondary. The scale is the worst single indicator because it conflates water, lean tissue, and fat into one number, and the three respond to intervention on different timescales and in different directions. The Peat-preferred markers are:

- **Temperature and pulse** (daily, taken under standard conditions): the primary signal of metabolic state. Body temperature on waking (target 97.8–98.6°F), midday (target 98.6°F or slightly above), and afternoon (the peak should be in the afternoon, not the morning — a morning-high pattern is the cortisol-and-adrenaline signal masking a low underlying metabolic rate). Resting pulse target 75–85 bpm in a well-fed, warm adult. From the [[weight-loss|email]]: "There are several convenient indicators of the metabolic rate — the daily temperature cycle and pulse rate (the temperature should rise after breakfast), the amount of water lost by evaporation, and the speed of relaxation of muscles (Achilles reflex relaxation)."
- **Finger and toe warmth.** A direct read of peripheral perfusion and thyroid response. Cold extremities with warm core is the hypothyroid pattern; warm extremities throughout the day is the recovered pattern.
- **Mid-thigh circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, photos, strength changes.** The body-composition layer that distinguishes lean-tissue accretion from fat loss. The first three months of intervention often show stable or slightly rising scale weight while mid-thigh and waist measures fall — the predicted body-recomposition pattern.
- **Mood, energy, sleep quality, libido, gut motility.** The systemic signs of metabolic recovery. Patients restoring thyroid and reducing PUFA report these improvements weeks before the scale moves.
- **Achilles-reflex relaxation speed.** The classic Barnes-era hypothyroid test, still informative.
- **Morning-to-afternoon temperature curve.** The shape of the curve carries more signal than any single reading. A curve that rises after breakfast and peaks in the afternoon is the recovered pattern; a curve that is flat or descending through the day is the stress-and-hypothyroid pattern.

The scale belongs in this panel only as a fifth or sixth indicator, never as the primary one.

## Counter-arguments

The Peat framework on obesity inverts mainstream medical and cultural consensus in nearly every direction; the counter-arguments are real and deserve explicit statement.

**The CICO orthodoxy** holds that obesity follows from caloric intake exceeding expenditure and that any successful intervention must close the gap via diet, exercise, surgery, or pharmacology. The Peat-frame response is that CICO is true as a thermodynamic identity but underdetermined as a regulatory model: the framework does not deny conservation of energy, it specifies the substrate-quality and hormonal-context variables that determine how a given caloric load is partitioned into heat, lean tissue, and fat storage.

**The low-carb / keto camp** (Lustig, Taubes, Ludwig, Ebbeling) holds that hyperinsulinemia from carbohydrate intake is the proximate cause of obesity. The Peat-frame response is that apparent low-carb success is largely water loss followed by adapted-low-metabolism stabilization, and that the carb-insulin model has the causal arrow on insulin backward — hyperinsulinemia is the body's secondary response to PUFA-blocked glucose oxidation, not the primary driver.

**The paleo camp** argues from evolutionary mismatch; the framework overlaps on seed oils and refined grains but inverts paleo on dairy (milk as a near-perfect food) and on the fundamental framing (mitochondrial-state and substrate-quality, not ancestral dietary pattern).

**The intermittent-fasting / fasting-heals camp** (Fung, Longo) is critiqued in [[intermittent-fasting|intermittent-fasting]]: fasting destroys tissue in a specific order, cannibalizes lean mass before fat, produces chronic cortisol and growth-hormone elevation, and reproduces the caloric-restriction adapted-low-metabolism pattern.

**The GLP-1 blockbuster era** has produced the most dramatic pharmacologic weight loss in medical history. The Peat-frame response: trials are short (mostly one to two years), lean-mass loss is substantial and underreported (a quarter to a third of weight lost is lean tissue), rebound on discontinuation is severe, and the long-term sarcopenia, gastroparesis, and stress-system costs will clarify with longer follow-up. The prediction is testable.

**The set-point and lipostat theory** (Friedman, Leibel, Rosenbaum) is critiqued in full at [[leptin-resistance-and-set-point|leptin-resistance-and-set-point]]: the set-point reframe is "habituation to a certain environment and way of living is another way of saying it" ([[weight-set-point|email on weight set point]]); elevated leptin in obesity is the predictable consequence of adipose-mass secretion, not brain resistance.

**Genetic determinism.** The largest GWAS hits explain less than two percent of BMI variance individually and roughly five to ten percent collectively; heritability estimates conflate genetic and shared-environmental contributions; the "obesity is genetic" research program functions in part as political displacement — studying MC4R when the most plausible cause (dietary and pharmaceutical) implicates the program's own funders. The parallel to the cancer-genomics displacement critique in [[cancer|cancer]] is direct. The framework does not claim genes are irrelevant; it claims the genes-vs-environment axis is the wrong one. The right axis is mitochondrial-respiratory state, substrate quality, and hormonal context.

## Data gaps

The corpus is thin or absent on several adjacent topics that deserve naming as gaps rather than synthesized as positions.

- **Bariatric surgery long-term metabolic and skeletal outcomes.** Peat wrote sparingly on bariatric procedures; the long-term sarcopenia and accelerated-osteoporosis literature emerging since 2015 sits largely outside his writing.
- **Per-drug antipsychotic mechanism detail.** The class-level weight-gain ledger is documented in the framework, but per-drug metabolic mechanisms (olanzapine via histamine and 5-HT2C, clozapine via the same plus muscarinic, risperidone via prolactin) need their own substance pages.
- **CGM, DEXA, and wearable-device interpretation in Peat-frame management.** The technology postdates most of Peat's writing; integration is a forward-looking gap.
- **Childhood-obesity epidemiology synthesis.** The framework documents the prenatal-PUFA-imprinting mechanism but does not provide a full epidemiologic synthesis.
- **Fat-acceptance and body-image discourse.** The corpus is silent on this; the framework's clinical orientation does not address the cultural and psychological dimensions of body-image research.
- **Nutrigenomics (Zoe, DayTwo, personalized-glycemic-response services).** Too recent for the corpus; the framework's substrate-quality emphasis would predict more between-person variance than the personalized-glycemic literature has thus far identified, but this is speculation.
- **Exercise prescription beyond walking.** The framework recommends walking as the rebuilding exercise and permits resistance training for muscle preservation, but the broader exercise-physiology literature (HIIT, zone-2 cardio, the fasted-cardio camp) is treated minimally.
- **Metabolic-flexibility research.** The post-2015 metabolic-flexibility literature (San Millán, Inigo, Hawley) gestures at the same substrate-switching that the framework names as the Randle cycle, but the integration is not yet done.
