# Iatrogenic Obesity

> *This page documents Ray Peat's framework on medication- and medical-orthodoxy-induced obesity. It is not medical advice. Do not stop antipsychotics, SSRIs, corticosteroids, hormonal contraceptives, insulin, beta-blockers, anticonvulsants, or any other prescribed medication on the basis of anything written here. Abrupt discontinuation of several of these drug classes is dangerous; psychiatric medications in particular require careful clinician-supervised tapering, and several can produce withdrawal phenomena (rebound psychosis, discontinuation syndrome, adrenal crisis from steroid stoppage) that are more harmful than the weight gain they cause. The framework below is documentation of how the drugs and the dietary orthodoxy contribute to the obesity phenotype, and how the patient and clinician together can minimize the iatrogenic weight effect while the underlying treatment continues.*

## What "iatrogenic obesity" means

"Iatrogenic" means caused by the physician — *iatros*, healer; *genesis*, origin. Iatrogenic obesity is the surplus adipose that arrives as a side effect of medical management. It is the weight gain on the prescription label that the patient sees as a betrayal of treatment and the prescriber sees as an acceptable cost. In Ray Peat's framework it occupies a particular category in the [[obesity|obesity]] hub: it is not the childhood-PUFA-imprinting phenotype, not the adult-onset chronic-substrate-loading phenotype, not the thyroid-driven phenotype, not the estrogen-and-cycle phenotype. It is the phenotype where the upstream lesion is the prescription pad — and, in its broader form, the dietary orthodoxy that the prescription pad sits inside.

The clinical signature is recognizable: the patient was a stable weight before the medication was started; the weight rose ten, twenty, sixty pounds in the months and years that followed; conventional diet-and-exercise advice failed to reverse it; the patient is now obese, often diabetic, often hypertensive, and the prescribing physician is unable or unwilling to admit the causal connection because to admit it would be to invite stopping the drug. In Peat's writing the iatrogenic-obesity category is broader still. It includes the obesity that arrives as a side effect of the chronic low-fat diet of the 1980-2010 American Heart Association orthodoxy, the obesity that arrives as a side effect of the chronic low-carbohydrate countermovement, the obesity that arrives as a side effect of decades of caloric-restriction-and-binge cycles, and the population-scale obesity epidemic that emerged across the same decades that the "eat less, move more" doctrine was institutionalized as nutritional consensus. The drug iatrogenesis is the per-patient story; the dietary iatrogenesis is the population story. They are the same story at different scales.

A complementary observation, from the [[obesity|hub article]], anchors the category. Forty percent of American adults are medicated for depression or anxiety; the most common antidepressants and antipsychotics in that pool are known to cause weight gain; the depression-obesity comorbidity that mainstream literature treats as bidirectional ("depressed people gain weight; obese people get depressed") collapses under inspection into a substantial fraction that is unidirectional: the drug given to treat the depression caused the obesity. The reframe is not denial of the comorbidity but identification of its causal arrow. Iatrogenic obesity is the hidden driver hiding inside the depression-obesity correlation.

## Drug-induced obesity catalog

Each of the following drug classes carries a documented and reproducible weight-gain signal. The catalog presents the class, the proximate mechanism in the Peat frame, and the alternative-considerations note where one exists. None of the entries is an instruction to stop the medication. *Discuss any change with the prescribing clinician.*

**Antipsychotics.** The drug class with the largest and most reliable weight-gain signal in modern psychiatry. The atypical antipsychotics olanzapine (Zyprexa) and clozapine (Clozaril) are the worst: average weight gain of twenty to sixty pounds in the first year of treatment, often accelerating into the diabetic phenotype within two to five years. Quetiapine (Seroquel) and risperidone (Risperdal) sit in the middle of the class. Aripiprazole (Abilify) and ziprasidone (Geodon) carry the smallest signal. The proximate mechanism, in the Peat frame, is a confluence of three actions: 5-HT2C serotonin-receptor antagonism (which restores appetite by removing the anorexigenic serotonin tone — see [[cyproheptadine|cyproheptadine]] for the counter-example showing that 5-HT2C antagonism alone is benign), H1 histamine-receptor antagonism (which adds sedation and reduces movement), and dopamine D2 antagonism (which lowers the reward signal that normally terminates a meal, producing the "I keep eating because eating feels muted" pattern that patients describe). The first-generation antipsychotics (haloperidol, chlorpromazine) carry a smaller weight signal because they act more selectively on D2 without the broad histamine-and-serotonin coverage of the atypicals. The framework does not deny the necessity of antipsychotic treatment in psychosis. The framework specifies the metabolic cost. The intervention layer is below.

**SSRIs and SNRIs.** Selective serotonin-reuptake inhibitors (sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram) and the serotonin-norepinephrine class (venlafaxine, duloxetine) collectively account for most prescribed antidepressant volume in the United States. The weight-gain signal varies across the class. Paroxetine (Paxil) is the worst — an average of ten to twenty pounds in the first year, often more in the second year, the gain often persistent after discontinuation. Mirtazapine (Remeron, a tetracyclic with strong H1 and 5-HT2 antagonism) produces equivalent gains. Sertraline (Zoloft) and citalopram (Celexa) produce a smaller average gain. Fluoxetine (Prozac) is the most weight-neutral of the class — sometimes even associated with modest weight loss in the first six to twelve months — and Peat's framework predicts why: fluoxetine has the longest half-life in the class and the most pronounced anti-appetite effect via 5-HT2C agonism in the early treatment phase. The general mechanism in the Peat frame is consistent: chronically elevated synaptic serotonin is a stress signal, and the body's adaptive response to chronic stress is the cortisol-and-visceral-fat cluster. From the [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression|*Serotonin, Depression, and Aggression*]] article, Peat documented bromocriptine experiments showing that "bromocriptine treatment significantly reduced weight gain (14.9 vs. -2.9 g, p < .01) and the areas under the GTT glucose and insulin curves by 29% and 48%, respectively" — the dopamine restoration that SSRIs lack. The clinical translation: SSRIs systematically tilt the dopamine-serotonin balance toward serotonin, and the body responds with the cortisol-and-visceral-fat phenotype. The Duke University depression-and-exercise trial — exercise treats depression as effectively as sertraline at six months and more effectively at ten months — sits in the background of this section as the alternative non-pharmacologic intervention that the framework supports.

**Corticosteroids.** Prednisone, prednisolone, dexamethasone, methylprednisolone, hydrocortisone in chronic supraphysiologic doses, and the inhaled steroids used at high doses in severe asthma. The mechanism is the most straightforwardly metabolic of any drug class in the catalog: chronic supraphysiologic glucocorticoid exposure produces the iatrogenic Cushing's syndrome — visceral fat deposition, moon face, buffalo hump, muscle wasting, glucose intolerance, hypertension, the full clinical picture that Cushing originally described as a disease of pituitary tumor. Peat treated this case directly. From [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]: "High doses of cortisone (prednisone, etc.) typically cause elevated glucose levels. Cushing's syndrome usually involves hyperglycemia. Normally, this is just a functional response to an excess of [stress hormones]." From the same body of work, the mechanism is the same as the framework's central model of obesity: cortisol mobilizes free fatty acids, blocks glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]], drives visceral fat deposition, and degrades lean tissue. Steroid-induced weight gain is the framework's model of obesity reproduced in pharmacologic time-lapse. Steroids cannot generally be discontinued abruptly — adrenal suppression is the predictable cost — and many of their indications (autoimmune disease, severe asthma, transplant rejection, certain cancers) make their use clinically necessary. The intervention layer is to minimize the dose, taper as tolerated under clinician supervision, and address the metabolic state through the channels below.

**Hormonal contraceptives.** Combined oral contraceptives (estrogen plus progestin), progestin-only pills, the contraceptive injection (depot medroxyprogesterone acetate; Depo-Provera), the contraceptive implant, and the hormonal intrauterine system. The signal varies sharply with the specific synthetic progestin. Depot medroxyprogesterone is the worst — average gain of eleven to twenty pounds across the first two years of use, with substantial water retention in addition to the fat deposition. The synthetic progestins in oral contraceptives (norethindrone, levonorgestrel, drospirenone, desogestrel) carry a smaller but real signal, with drospirenone the most water-neutral and the older androgenic progestins the most fat-driving. The mechanism in the Peat frame is twofold. First, the synthetic progestins are not [[progesterone|progesterone]] — they are differently-shaped molecules that activate some progesterone receptors but also activate other receptors (androgen, glucocorticoid, mineralocorticoid) in ways true progesterone does not. They produce a phenotype Peat repeatedly described as functionally pseudo-Cushingoid. Second, the estrogen component of combined contraceptives drives the elevated free-fatty-acid, elevated growth-hormone, elevated water-retention pattern that the framework identifies as obesogenic. Peat documented, in the [[obesity-research|obesity research file]], the experimental confirmation: "Among the women, those taking oral contraceptives had 10 ± 4% (P = 0.08) higher DHA concentrations... Administration of oral ethinyl estradiol, but not transdermal 17beta-estradiol, increased DHA by 42 ± 8% (P < 0.0005)." The PUFA-mobilization-into-circulation signature of oral synthetic estrogen is the same signature the framework identifies as obesogenic, recapitulated in the contraceptive pill. Alternative considerations exist — non-hormonal contraception, copper IUDs, fertility-awareness methods — and are appropriately a discussion between patient and clinician.

**Insulin therapy (Type 2 diabetes).** The paradox of conventional Type 2 diabetes treatment is that the drug given to lower blood glucose causes weight gain that worsens the diabetes. Peat treated this directly in the [[insulin|insulin]] article. Insulin is a storage hormone; injecting more insulin into a body whose primary lesion is the [[randle-cycle|Randle-cycle]] block of glucose oxidation does not restore glucose oxidation, it drives the surplus glucose into fat storage. The patient gains weight, the insulin resistance worsens, the dose escalates, and the cycle accelerates. From [[insulin|insulin]]: "insulin injections for type 2 diabetics don't address the block; they drive fat synthesis, accelerate atherosclerosis, and produce the very complications attributed to the disease. Hyperinsulinemia, far from being a protective response, is itself a stress state whose systemic effects rhyme disturbingly with those of [[estrogen]]." The framework does not deny the necessity of insulin in Type 1 diabetes, where the beta-cell loss is absolute. The framework documents the iatrogenic cost in Type 2, where the lesion is substrate-handling and the intervention is substrate-redirection rather than substrate-flooding. Metformin (a biguanide that lowers hepatic glucose output and modestly improves substrate handling) and the GLP-1 agonist class (semaglutide, tirzepatide) are weight-neutral or weight-reducing in trials. *Discuss any change in diabetic medication with the clinician.*

**Beta-blockers.** Propranolol, metoprolol, atenolol, carvedilol — the antihypertensive and post-myocardial-infarction class. Average weight gain of three to ten pounds in the first year of treatment, with the older non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol, atenolol) carrying the larger signal and the newer vasodilating ones (carvedilol, nebivolol) the smaller. The mechanism in the Peat frame is metabolic-rate suppression: beta-adrenergic receptors mediate a substantial fraction of resting thermogenesis (the catecholamine-driven beta-3 receptor signal in brown and beige adipose tissue, and the beta-2 signal in skeletal muscle), and blocking those receptors lowers resting energy expenditure by an estimated fifty to one hundred kilocalories per day. The body deposits the surplus. Beta-blockers also typically reduce exercise tolerance (the patient cannot reach the same heart rate during exertion), which reinforces the inactivity component. Several alternatives exist — calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin-receptor blockers, diuretics — and the choice between them is properly a discussion with the cardiologist or primary-care clinician.

**Gabapentin and pregabalin.** Gabapentinoids prescribed for neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia, anxiety, and an expanding off-label list. Average weight gain of five to ten pounds in the first year on therapeutic doses, often higher on higher doses. The mechanism is poorly characterized in mainstream pharmacology, but in the Peat frame the signature is the same H1-histamine-blocking, sedating, appetite-restoring pattern as the antipsychotic class, expressed at lower receptor occupancy and with the addition of fluid retention. Alternatives for neuropathic pain exist; the framework's underlying preference is to address the upstream metabolic state (the [[diabetes|diabetic-neuropathy]] or [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid-neuropathy]]) rather than to chronically dampen the signal.

**Sulfonylureas.** Glipizide, glyburide, glimepiride — the insulin-secretagogue class for Type 2 diabetes. The mechanism is the same as exogenous insulin therapy: drive the pancreas to secrete more insulin, which forces glucose into fat storage in a substrate-oxidation-defective body, which produces weight gain that worsens the underlying lesion. Average gain of four to ten pounds in the first year. The class has been substantially displaced in current Type 2 diabetes practice by metformin and the GLP-1 agonists, in part because of the weight-gain and hypoglycemia signals.

**Lithium.** The mood-stabilizer for bipolar disorder. Average gain of ten to fifteen pounds in the first year, with substantial fluid retention contributing. The mechanism in the Peat frame is twofold: lithium directly suppresses thyroid function (producing iatrogenic hypothyroidism in roughly one in five patients on long-term lithium, with the full thyroid-driven obesity phenotype following), and it shifts water handling toward retention via direct renal effects. The clinical pattern is well-known to psychiatrists; the framework adds the thyroid mechanism as the central lever for intervention.

**Tricyclic antidepressants.** Amitriptyline, nortriptyline, doxepin, clomipramine. Largely displaced by SSRIs but still prescribed for chronic pain and sleep. Average gain of five to fifteen pounds in the first year, with amitriptyline the worst. The mechanism overlaps with both the antipsychotic class (H1-histamine antagonism, anticholinergic appetite restoration) and the SSRI class (serotonin reuptake inhibition).

**Anticonvulsants.** Valproic acid (Depakote) and carbamazepine carry the largest signal of the older anticonvulsant class, often ten to twenty pounds in the first year. The newer agents (lamotrigine, topiramate, levetiracetam) are largely weight-neutral, and topiramate is consistently weight-reducing — sometimes prescribed off-label for that effect.

## Mechanism: how psychotropics drive weight gain

The proximate question — why do psychiatric medications, as a class, drive obesity? — has a partial answer in conventional pharmacology and a more complete answer in the Peat frame.

The conventional answer points to three receptor-level actions: H1-histamine antagonism (sedation and appetite restoration), 5-HT2C serotonin-receptor antagonism (further appetite restoration), and D2 dopamine-receptor antagonism (reduced reward signal that fails to terminate a meal). The atypical antipsychotics combine all three with high potency; the SSRIs add the layer of chronically elevated synaptic serotonin (which the body reads as chronic stress); the older tricyclics overlap with the antipsychotic class on H1 and add muscarinic anticholinergic action.

The Peat-frame answer adds three further mechanisms that the conventional account does not foreground.

First, metabolic-rate suppression. Many of the psychotropics carry a documented signal of lowered resting energy expenditure, often via thyroid suppression (lithium most clearly; the SSRI class produces a smaller but real T3 reduction in long-term users) or via reduced sympathetic outflow (the H1-antagonist sedation also reduces beta-adrenergic thermogenesis). The lowered metabolic ceiling means the same caloric intake produces more fat storage. From the [[serotonin-depression-and-aggression|*Serotonin* article]], Peat documented the mechanism in the bromocriptine experiment: the dopamine restoration that bromocriptine produces also reduces weight gain and improves glucose tolerance, demonstrating that the dopamine-vs-serotonin balance is itself metabolically active in addition to its mood effects.

Second, the chronic-cortisol downstream. Chronically elevated synaptic serotonin (the SSRI signature) and chronically blunted dopamine (the antipsychotic signature) both function as stress signals to the broader endocrine system. The body responds with sustained cortisol elevation, the cortisol mobilizes free fatty acids from adipose, the FFAs block glucose oxidation through the Randle cycle, and the visceral-fat-deposition pattern of the framework's general obesity model follows. The drug produces the chemistry of chronic stress; the body responds with the body composition of chronic stress.

Third, hunger dysregulation that is more than appetite restoration. The patient on an atypical antipsychotic does not merely eat what a non-medicated person eats; the patient often describes a hunger that does not terminate after a normal meal, an inability to feel satiety, a craving for carbohydrate that arrives within an hour of a substantial meal. The mechanism is multifactorial — reduced leptin sensitivity, slowed gastric emptying, the muted reward signal that fails to terminate eating, the histamine block that reduces the histamine-mediated satiety signal — and it is qualitatively different from the appetite restoration that cyproheptadine produces in a previously-anorectic patient.

The [[cyproheptadine|cyproheptadine]] counter-example anchors the framework's claim that the receptor pattern, not the broad pharmacologic category, is what matters. Cyproheptadine is an antihistamine with strong 5-HT2 antagonism — the same broad action as the atypical antipsychotics on two of the three core receptors — and yet cyproheptadine is the drug Peat recommended most after [[thyroid|thyroid]] and [[aspirin|aspirin]]. It restores appetite in undereaters, helps with sleep, treats anxiety and migraine, and produces only modest weight gain in patients whose appetite was suppressed by chronic serotonin tone. The difference is that cyproheptadine does not block dopamine, does not produce chronic-stress cortisol elevation, and does not suppress thyroid. The receptor pattern matters. "Antihistamines cause weight gain" is the wrong abstraction; "the broad-spectrum H1-blocking, D2-blocking, 5-HT2C-blocking, anticholinergic, thyroid-suppressing combination of the atypical antipsychotics causes weight gain" is the right one.

## Diet-induced obesity via medical orthodoxy

The drug-induced category covers the per-patient story. The diet-induced category covers the population story — the obesity that arrives as a side effect of medical advice institutionalized as public-health doctrine across a half-century. The Peat frame is distinct from the carbohydrate-insulin-model critique on the right and the calorie-balance-model defense in the center: Peat opposed all three of the major dietary orthodoxies of the postwar era — the chronic low-fat doctrine, the chronic low-carbohydrate countermovement, and the chronic-caloric-restriction-with-permitted-binges cycle — and identified each as iatrogenically obesogenic.

**The American Heart Association low-fat era (roughly 1977-2010).** The dietary guidelines published by the federal government from 1977 onward, in coordination with the AHA's "prudent diet" campaign, instructed Americans to replace saturated fat (butter, lard, tallow, coconut oil) with polyunsaturated vegetable oils (corn, soy, safflower, canola), to lower total fat intake to thirty percent of calories or below, to reduce dietary cholesterol, and to fill the carbohydrate channel with complex carbohydrates (refined grains, breads, pasta, breakfast cereals). The doctrine ran for three decades as American obesity prevalence approximately tripled. Peat's analysis, present across forty years of his newsletters, identified the dietary substitution as the mechanism. The replacement fats are precisely the antithyroid PUFAs the framework identifies as the central obesogen — the same chemical category that 1940s farmers used to fatten livestock when thiouracil was banned. The cholesterol reduction came at the cost of pregnenolone and progesterone synthesis, the same steroid pathway that maintains thyroid responsiveness. The refined-carbohydrate substitution drove endotoxin production. The low-fat doctrine converted the substrate channel into an antithyroid input on the fat side and a stress-and-endotoxin input on the carbohydrate side. The predictable output is what occurred: the rise of obesity, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease across the decades the doctrine was in force. Mainstream nutrition has rotated through several explanations (Americans got lazier; the obesogenic environment emerged; processed foods proliferated) without confronting the central mechanism. The chemical-fingerprint thread — seed-oil consumption rising from two grams per day in 1909 to over eighty grams per day by 2010 — sits in [[pufa|pufa]] and is summarized in the [[obesity|obesity hub]].

**The chronic low-carbohydrate countermovement (Atkins through keto, roughly 1990-present).** The reaction against the low-fat era produced a counter-orthodoxy with its own iatrogenic signature. Peat opposed carbohydrate restriction with the same vigor as fat restriction. The mechanism: restricting sugar and starch forces the body to run on free fatty acids, which is precisely the substrate state the framework identifies as the proximate driver of insulin resistance, chronic stress, and weight regain. The initial weight loss on a ketogenic diet is largely water — glycogen binds roughly three grams of water per gram, so depleting muscle and liver glycogen releases a kilogram or more of water in the first week — followed by adapted-low-metabolism stabilization on chronically elevated cortisol and growth hormone. Sustained over months, the pattern produces the same adapted-low-metabolism state as caloric restriction, with the added cost of suppressed T3 (the body reads sustained low carbohydrate intake as starvation and downregulates thyroid conversion) and accelerated PUFA mobilization from adipose stores. In a PUFA-loaded body, the keto diet mobilizes more linoleic-acid metabolites into the bloodstream, worsening the lipid-peroxidation load that the framework treats as central to systemic inflammation. The carbohydrate-insulin model that grounds the keto movement intellectually — Lustig, Taubes, Ludwig, Ebbeling — inverts the Peat-frame causal arrow. Hyperinsulinemia, on Peat's reading, is the body's secondary response to PUFA-blocked glucose oxidation, not the primary cause of obesity. Restricting carbohydrate addresses the symptom (high insulin) while worsening the upstream lesion (impaired substrate oxidation).

**The chronic caloric-restriction-and-binge cycle.** The third pillar of the iatrogenic-diet category is the model that ties the first two together. Patients who cycle through chronic caloric restriction punctuated by binges — the Weight Watchers model, the "1,200 calorie diet" model, the most common pattern in the female obesity demographic — produce the worst metabolic-adaptation signature of any dietary intervention. Each restriction phase lowers the metabolic ceiling (thyroid downregulation, T3 conversion drops, lean tissue cannibalized); each binge phase deposits the surplus into adipose with the now-lower ceiling. The patient ends each cycle heavier, with a lower resting metabolic rate, and with greater PUFA exposure if the binge foods are seed-oil-rich (which most processed binge foods are). The Biggest-Loser cohort tracked by Hall and Kerns showed roughly 500 kilocalories per day of persistent resting-metabolic-rate suppression six years after a single twelve-month restriction phase. The cycling pattern compounds this signal across decades. The framework's name for the result is the adapted-low-metabolism phenotype; the mainstream name is "yo-yo dieting." They describe the same iatrogenic injury.

**Gestational-diabetes carbohydrate restriction.** A specific subset of dietary iatrogenesis worth naming. The conventional management of gestational diabetes — diagnosis at 24-28 weeks, dietary counseling toward carbohydrate restriction, often supplemental insulin if blood-glucose targets are not met — addresses the symptom (elevated postprandial glucose) without the upstream lesion (the pregnancy-induced insulin resistance that is, by design, the maternal physiology's mechanism of redirecting substrate to the fetus). Restricting carbohydrate in pregnancy forces the maternal body onto free-fatty-acid substrate at exactly the moment that elevated FFA exposure to the fetus is most teratogenic and most likely to imprint the offspring's adipose-tissue setpoint. The intervention prescribed to treat gestational diabetes — carbohydrate restriction — is in the Peat frame an obesogenic intervention for both mother and fetus. The intervention layer sits in [[pregnancy-nutrition|pregnancy-nutrition]].

## The "eat less, move more" 50-year failure

The single longest-running natural experiment in the iatrogenic-obesity literature is the fidelity of American (and global) public-health institutions to the "eat less, move more" doctrine across a half-century. From the 1970s dietary guidelines through the 1980s exercise-promotion campaigns through the 1990s low-fat fervor through the 2000s portion-control emphasis through the 2010s "calorie-counts on menus" regulatory layer, the message has been remarkably consistent: obesity is caused by excess caloric intake and insufficient caloric expenditure, and the intervention is to consume fewer calories and burn more. Over the same fifty years, American obesity prevalence rose from roughly fifteen percent of adults to over forty percent, with severe obesity (BMI ≥ 40) rising more sharply still. The cross-national pattern is similar: every country that adopted the American dietary guidelines and public-health framing experienced a parallel obesity rise. The countries that retained traditional saturated-fat-dominant diets and did not adopt the Anglo-American doctrine (the Tokelauans on coconut, the highland Swiss on butter, the Japanese on traditional fats and rice until the postwar shift) stayed lean until the doctrine arrived.

The doctrine cannot be falsified by the natural experiment because the doctrine is unfalsifiable in its current institutional form. When the population stays obese despite the advice, the response is not "the advice was wrong" but "the population did not comply" — a frame that locates the failure in the patients' discipline rather than in the framework's accuracy. The Peat-frame response is that the doctrine got the causal arrow backward at the start. Body fat is the output of the metabolic rate; metabolic rate is the input. Telling people to eat less lowers the metabolic rate (the adapted-thermogenesis mechanism), which sets the body to deposit any subsequent intake more efficiently as fat. Telling people to move more drives compensatory eating in roughly half the population and compensatory inactivity in the other half (the Sherwood-Williams compensation literature in exercise physiology documents this in detail). The doctrine's two pillars work against each other and against the patient's metabolic state. Fifty years of fidelity to a backward causal model is, in the framework, the largest iatrogenic intervention in the modern history of medicine.

The dawning recognition that "eat less, move more" has failed has produced, since approximately 2018, a wholesale rotation of pharma investment toward GLP-1 agonists as the new front-line obesity intervention. The framework treats the pharma rotation as the prediction made flesh: the orthodoxy admits the eat-less-move-more model has failed by switching, in practice though not in stated dietary guidelines, to a pharmacologic intervention that bypasses the doctrine. Whether the GLP-1 era produces its own iatrogenic ledger is the open question of the next decade, and the framework's prediction (lean-mass loss, gastroparesis, stress-system costs, severe rebound on discontinuation) sits in the [[obesity|hub]] for testing.

## What to do if you must stay on the medication

The framework's clinical signature, consistent across every condition-protocol pair, is "alongside, not instead of." For iatrogenic obesity, this is the central operating constraint: the patient remains on the antipsychotic, the SSRI, the steroid, the contraceptive, the insulin, the beta-blocker, the gabapentinoid, while the metabolic intervention proceeds. The interventions below minimize the iatrogenic weight effect without stopping the drug. *Discuss any change with the prescribing clinician.*

**Substrate restoration.** The diet that opposes the chronic-cortisol, free-fatty-acid-loaded, hypothyroid pattern is the framework's [[anti-stress-diet|anti-stress diet]] — milk, eggs, orange juice, ripe fruit, a daily carrot, gelatinous cuts of meat, butter and coconut oil rather than seed oils, salt to taste. Two quarts of milk daily for the calcium-to-phosphate ratio. Eighty grams of protein minimum. Sugar via fruit, milk lactose, and added sucrose. The substrate channel is opened, the Randle block is bypassed, the thyroid is supplied, and the body has the material to oxidize at a higher rate even with the drug in place.

**Thyroid optimization.** Many of the drugs in the catalog (lithium most directly, SSRIs and antipsychotics indirectly via stress-axis suppression of T4-to-T3 conversion) lower the patient's thyroid status. Restoring thyroid — through diet, through liver support, and where indicated through clinician-supervised thyroid replacement — addresses the most common upstream lesion that the drugs create. The protocol layer sits in [[hypothyroid-recovery|hypothyroid-recovery]] and [[thyroid-dosing|thyroid-dosing]].

**PUFA depletion alongside drug exposure.** The four-year PUFA-clearance horizon documented in [[pufa-depletion|pufa-depletion]] proceeds independently of the medication regimen. Removing dietary seed oils, fatty fish, supplemental fish oil; substituting saturated fats; supporting detoxification with vitamin E — these reduce the chronic-substrate-loading half of the obesity equation without any change in psychiatric or other prescriptions.

**Walking, not chronic cardio.** The exercise prescription that the framework supports is daily walking — long-duration, low-intensity, in sunlight where possible — rather than chronic-cardio sessions or restricted-feeding-window training. Walking restores tissue circulation without driving the cortisol-and-stress signature that high-intensity exercise produces in a metabolically compromised body. Resistance training in moderation, especially the kind that emphasizes concentric over eccentric contraction (gelatin-supported, brief sessions), is permitted and useful for lean-mass preservation.

**Sleep restoration.** The drugs in the iatrogenic-obesity catalog often disrupt sleep — antipsychotics produce excessive sedation that interferes with deep sleep, SSRIs reduce REM, steroids drive insomnia. The framework's [[sleep-restoration|sleep-restoration]] protocol addresses the upstream stress signal and supports the metabolic recovery that drug-induced sleep disruption suppresses.

**Endotoxin reduction.** The daily raw [[carrot-salad|carrot salad]], well-cooked mushrooms, restriction of fermentable starches, and judicious use of cascara support the gut-decontamination layer that is independently important and is particularly important in the drug-induced obesity phenotype where slowed gastric emptying (a side effect of many of these drugs) increases endotoxin load.

**Monitoring with metabolic markers, not the scale.** The framework's preferred tracking signals — daily temperature and pulse, finger-and-toe warmth, mid-thigh circumference, mood and energy, Achilles-reflex relaxation speed — are largely independent of drug-induced fluid retention and are more informative about metabolic recovery than the scale. The temperature-pulse tracking sits in [[temperature-tracking|temperature-tracking]].

## When to discuss alternatives with clinician

The catalog above identifies several drug classes where weight-neutral or weight-reducing alternatives exist within the same therapeutic category, and where it is reasonable for the patient to raise the question with the prescribing clinician. *The decision to switch medications is the prescriber's; the framework documents the metabolic ledger.*

- **Within the antipsychotic class:** aripiprazole and ziprasidone carry the smallest weight signal; olanzapine and clozapine the largest. If clinical control is equivalent, the prescriber may consider the lower-weight alternative.
- **Within the SSRI/SNRI class:** fluoxetine, sertraline, and bupropion (bupropion is weight-neutral or weight-reducing, an atypical antidepressant outside the SSRI class proper) carry smaller weight signals than paroxetine or mirtazapine.
- **Within the antihypertensive class:** calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, and angiotensin-receptor blockers carry no metabolic-rate-suppression signal comparable to the beta-blockers.
- **Within the Type 2 diabetes class:** metformin and GLP-1 agonists are weight-neutral or weight-reducing; insulin and sulfonylureas are weight-driving.
- **Within the anticonvulsant class:** lamotrigine, levetiracetam, and topiramate are largely weight-neutral or weight-reducing; valproic acid and carbamazepine carry the largest weight signal.
- **Within the steroid class:** the dose, frequency, and route (inhaled vs. oral) of corticosteroid matter substantially; many chronic-steroid patients can be tapered to a maintenance dose with appropriate clinical management.

The framework does not assert that the lower-weight alternative is always the right clinical choice. Many of these substitutions involve trade-offs in efficacy, side-effect profile, or interaction with other medications. The framework asserts only that the metabolic ledger is a legitimate input to the prescribing decision and that the patient is within their rights to raise the question.

## Tracking signal

The iatrogenic-obesity phenotype is most cleanly tracked by the same panel that the [[obesity|hub]] uses for general obesity monitoring, with one additional signal: the time-course alignment between medication initiation and weight gain. A weight curve that rose steeply in the first six months after starting a new psychiatric medication, and that resists conventional dietary intervention, is presumptively iatrogenic until proven otherwise. The framework supports the clinical conversation in which patient and prescriber together examine that time-course alignment.

- **Daily temperature and pulse**, taken under standard conditions. Target temperature on waking 97.8-98.6°F, midday and afternoon 98.6°F or slightly above. Target resting pulse 75-85 bpm in a well-fed warm adult. The drug-induced sedation pattern will often suppress pulse; the recovery curve is the relevant signal.
- **Finger and toe warmth** as the most direct read of peripheral perfusion and thyroid response. Drug-induced hypothyroidism shows here before the scale moves.
- **Mid-thigh circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, photos, strength changes** as the body-composition layer. The drug-induced visceral-fat-deposition pattern shows in waist-to-hip ratio.
- **Mood, energy, sleep quality, libido, gut motility** as the systemic recovery markers. Improvement in these often precedes the scale.
- **Time-course alignment of weight and medication.** The patient's records, where available, of weight before, during, and after each medication change.

## What to AVOID

- **Stopping psychiatric medications cold turkey.** Antipsychotic and SSRI discontinuation syndromes are real and sometimes severe. Rebound psychosis, agitation, suicidal ideation, akathisia, and seizures have all been documented in abrupt-stoppage cases. Any discontinuation should be supervised by the prescribing clinician with a graduated taper.
- **Stopping corticosteroids abruptly.** Adrenal suppression from chronic steroid use is the predictable cost; abrupt discontinuation can produce an adrenal crisis (hypotension, hypoglycemia, electrolyte derangement, shock) that is life-threatening. Steroid tapers must be supervised.
- **Stopping insulin in Type 1 diabetes.** The framework's critique of insulin in Type 2 diabetes does not extend to Type 1; in Type 1, the beta-cell loss is absolute, and insulin is necessary for survival.
- **"Natural" replacements without clinician coordination.** St. John's wort for SSRI replacement, herbal "adrenal support" for steroid replacement, "natural thyroid" without TSH and free-hormone monitoring — these substitutions have produced serious clinical events and are not a substitute for clinician-supervised medication management.
- **Caloric restriction as the primary intervention.** The framework treats caloric restriction as an iatrogenic intervention on its own; layering it on top of drug-induced obesity worsens the adapted-low-metabolism component without addressing the upstream lesion.
- **High-intensity-interval training and chronic-cardio sessions** as the primary exercise intervention. The framework supports walking and moderate resistance training; the cortisol-and-stress signature of HIIT in a metabolically compromised body is contraindicated.
- **Extended fasting as the weight-loss strategy.** See [[intermittent-fasting|intermittent-fasting]] for the full critique.

## Counter-arguments

The iatrogenic-obesity framework will be contested at several points by mainstream clinical and academic positions; the counter-arguments deserve explicit statement.

**"The drug is doing more good than harm."** Often true. An antipsychotic that prevents an acute psychotic break in a patient with schizophrenia is doing more good than the weight gain costs, and the framework does not dispute this. The framework's claim is that the metabolic cost is real and underacknowledged, not that the cost is never worth paying. The prescribing decision is the clinician's; the metabolic ledger is the patient's information to weigh.

**"Lifestyle modification" as the pharma-supported counterframe.** Pharmaceutical companies marketing drugs with known weight-gain signals typically include in their patient education materials the language of "lifestyle modification" as the intervention that responsible patients should pursue alongside the drug. The framing relocates the cause of weight gain from the drug (which is not adjustable by the patient) to the patient's compliance (which is). The framework's response is that "lifestyle modification" in the form of caloric restriction and exercise compounds the iatrogenic injury rather than mitigating it; the framework-aligned interventions (substrate restoration, thyroid optimization, PUFA depletion, walking) work alongside the drug without inducing the adapted-low-metabolism rebound.

**The GLP-1 reversal of the pharma trajectory.** The dominant pharma narrative since 2020 has been that the GLP-1 agonist class (semaglutide, tirzepatide) represents a transformative intervention that finally solves the obesity problem. The framework's prediction, expressed in the [[obesity|hub]] and recapitulated here, is that the trial duration so far (one to two years) is insufficient to surface the lean-mass-loss, sarcopenia, gastroparesis, and rebound costs that the mechanism predicts. The pharma rotation toward GLP-1s is itself the implicit admission that the eat-less-move-more doctrine has failed; whether the new doctrine produces a smaller iatrogenic ledger than the old one is testable, and the test will play out across the next decade.

**Mainstream "the depression-obesity comorbidity is bidirectional."** The conventional psychiatric literature treats the depression-obesity correlation as a mutual-causation phenomenon — depression causes weight gain via behavioral changes, and obesity causes depression via stigma and metabolic changes. The framework's response is that a substantial fraction of the correlation is unidirectional: the antidepressant given for the depression caused the obesity. The fraction is contestable; the existence of the unidirectional component is not.

**"Drug-induced weight gain is reversible on discontinuation."** Sometimes true (the contraceptive pill, in many patients); often partially true (the SSRI class, with persistent gain in a substantial fraction); rarely fully true for the atypical antipsychotic class. The framework's claim is that even where reversal is possible, the metabolic injury sustained during the years of exposure leaves lasting effects (PUFA loading, thyroid suppression, adapted metabolic ceiling) that the patient must address through the intervention layer.

## Data gaps

- **Per-drug mechanistic detail.** The catalog above identifies receptor-level mechanisms at the class level; the per-drug mechanisms (olanzapine via 5-HT2C and H1 and muscarinic; clozapine adds muscarinic and 5-HT2A; risperidone adds prolactin elevation; aripiprazole as partial D2 agonist) deserve their own substance pages.
- **Long-term GLP-1 outcomes.** Trial durations are still mostly one to two years; ten-year and twenty-year outcomes will clarify whether the lean-mass-loss and metabolic-adaptation costs of the class accumulate or stabilize.
- **Synthetic-progestin per-molecule signatures.** The catalog notes the class-level signal of the synthetic progestins but does not detail the per-molecule mechanism differences across norethindrone, levonorgestrel, drospirenone, desogestrel, dienogest, and medroxyprogesterone.
- **Antiepileptic-class mechanism detail.** Why valproic acid is so dramatically more weight-driving than lamotrigine is incompletely characterized in either mainstream pharmacology or the Peat frame.
- **Childhood and adolescent psychiatric medication exposure.** A growing fraction of children and adolescents are on long-term SSRIs, stimulants, atypical antipsychotics, and combinations. The iatrogenic-obesity signature in the developing brain and developing adipose tissue is not well-characterized in the framework and warrants its own treatment.
- **Pregnancy-exposure outcomes.** The metabolic consequences for offspring of maternal antidepressant, antipsychotic, or beta-blocker exposure during gestation are studied for psychiatric and cardiovascular outcomes but rarely for adipose-tissue setpoint or thyroid development.
- **Stimulant medications.** ADHD stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamine salts) typically suppress appetite and are weight-neutral or weight-reducing in the short term; long-term effects on metabolic rate, adapted thermogenesis, and post-discontinuation rebound are an underexplored gap.
- **Drug-induced thyroid suppression at the population level.** Lithium is well-characterized; the SSRI and antipsychotic classes produce a smaller signal whose epidemiological magnitude — across the substantial fraction of the adult population now on these drugs — is not fully tallied.
