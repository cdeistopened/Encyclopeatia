# Allergies and Asthma

The orthodox model treats allergy as an immune malfunction: the body misidentifies a harmless antigen — pollen, peanut, cat dander — and mounts an IgE-mediated attack against it. Asthma, in this view, is allergy's lung-shaped sequel, a bronchial overreaction to inhaled triggers that needs to be suppressed with inhaled steroids and rescued with a bronchodilator. Ray Peat treated this whole framework as a category error. The IgE antibody, in his reading, is "as much an effect of allergy as it is a cause." The triggers are real, but they only trigger because the underlying tissue has been set into a chronic, hyper-reactive metabolic state — high histamine, high serotonin, low CO2, hypothyroid, leaky-gut, PUFA-loaded, sugar-starved at the wrong moments. Take the same person out of that state and the pollen counts no longer matter. Leave them in it and removing one antigen at a time simply pushes the symptom from skin to gut to airway and back.

This is the most consistent thread across Peat's writing on allergic disease, from the 1986 [[asthma-and-metabisulfite|*Asthma and Metabisulfite*]] note through the 2011 [[2011-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-inflammation-ii|*Inflammation II*]] and 2011 [[2011-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-endotoxins-stress|*Serotonin, Endotoxins, Stress*]] episodes and into the 2016 [[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|*Ask the Herb Doctor: Allergy*]] show that is the single best entry point to his thinking on this subject. The phenotype is "atopic" or "asthmatic" only on the outside. On the inside it is the same chronic-inflammation field that produces winter sickness, migraines, IBS, eczema, and chronic urticaria in the same patients at different points in life. The right intervention works on all of them at once.

## What's actually happening

The IgE story is real but downstream. "Histamine is probably the most effective signal in telling the B cells to start making the IgE antibody," Peat told Andrew Murray on the [[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|March 2016 allergy show]]. "If you don't have inflammation from the stressed cells producing histamine, you won't be likely to have so much IgE." The B cells switch their antibody class in response to a histamine-rich environment; they do not create the environment. Treating the IgE without treating the histamine field is rearranging deck chairs.

What is upstream of the histamine field is a set of mediators that Peat repeatedly listed together: [[histamine]], [[serotonin]], prostaglandins, leukotrienes, nitric oxide, and [[endotoxin]] from gram-negative gut bacteria. Each one amplifies the others. Histamine triggers nitric oxide; nitric oxide signals more inflammation; serotonin and prostaglandins each push mast cells to release more histamine. "Histamine turns on the production of nitric oxide, and nitric oxide signals a bunch of other inflammatory processes," Peat noted in the 2016 show. "The worst thing is that it interferes with oxygen energy production." The cascade ends not in a discrete immune event but in an energy deficit that keeps the cells stuck in a reactive state.

The CO2 piece is the part most allergy specialists never see. [[Carbon-dioxide|Carbon dioxide]] stabilizes mast cells. Lose CO2 — through chronic hyperventilation, anxiety, altitude, fever, or the shallow chest-breathing patterns typical of stressed people — and the mast cell granules pop open at lower thresholds. Peat made the point bluntly in [[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|*Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer*]]: "Simply hyperventilating for a couple of minutes will increase leakage from the blood into the lungs; hyperventilation decreases carbon dioxide, and increases serotonin and histamine." Allergic and asthmatic people tend to under-breathe between attacks and over-breathe during them. Both states drop CO2. Both states load the gun for the next reaction.

Serotonin's role in asthma is more direct than histamine's. Peat described it on the [[2011-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-inflammation-ii|*Inflammation II*]] episode to a caller whose asthma resolved after she came off her SSRI: "The effect of serotonin is to cause localized contraction of the tubes in your lungs as well as causing them to take up water and promote inflammation. The contraction is the thing best studied in connection with asthma." A bronchospasm, in this reading, is not a reflex misfire. It is what serotonin does to bronchial smooth muscle when the local concentration rises. Anything that raises systemic serotonin — endotoxin, PUFA, estrogen, low blood sugar, SSRIs, 5-HTP, tryptophan loading — biases the lung toward bronchoconstriction.

## The atopic march

Pediatric allergists describe the "atopic march" — eczema in infancy, food allergies in toddlerhood, asthma in school age, seasonal allergic rhinitis in adolescence — as if it were a developmental sequence with distinct stops. Peat treated it as one continuous state expressing through whichever tissue happened to be most stressed at a given age. "The gut and the skin have some antigens and enzymes that are so closely connected that when one is inflamed, the other gets inflamed," he said in the 2016 show, when Andrew Murray noted that 95% of atopic dermatitis patients are colonized with *Staphylococcus aureus*. The skin is the gut's external membrane; the lungs are its internal one. Inflame the gut and both follow.

The clinical implication is that chasing the symptom-of-the-year through topical steroids, then elimination diets, then inhalers, then antihistamines, leaves the underlying field untouched while building up iatrogenic damage. The child whose eczema is treated with hydrocortisone develops food allergies because the same gut-skin axis is unaddressed. The food allergies are treated by elimination — narrowing the diet, often into greater dysbiosis. By the time asthma arrives, the system has been three years deeper into the very state that produced the eczema. Peat's framing is that the eczema, the food allergy, the asthma, and eventually the rhinitis are all the same disease — chronic mast-cell-and-endothelial-cell hyper-reactivity riding on a hypothyroid, PUFA-saturated, endotoxin-leaking metabolism — and that they yield together when the metabolism is corrected.

## Peat's causal cluster

Five interlocking inputs sustain the allergic-asthmatic state. None of them is the "cause" in isolation; their combination is what locks the field in place.

**Gut dysbiosis and endotoxin leak.** "Sometimes the inflammation in a particular area, like the nose, ears, throat, or eyes, is really something irritating your intestine," Peat said in the 2016 show. Mast cells are densest in the intestinal lining, and the bacteria producing endotoxin live a few cell-layers away from them. A leaky barrier and a fermentation-friendly diet keep the mast cells continuously primed, and the histamine, serotonin, and nitric oxide they release circulate to every membrane in the body. "Avoiding things that feed bacteria so that you don't produce so much endotoxin can help all types of allergy and inflammatory conditions." This is the same mechanism described at length in [[endotoxin]] and operationalized in [[endotoxin-reduction|the endotoxin reduction protocol]]; allergy and asthma are downstream readouts of that same gut state.

**PUFA, prostaglandins, and the inheritance of the field.** Polyunsaturated fatty acids are the substrate for the prostaglandins that activate mast cells, and prostaglandin E2 in particular signals histamine release. Peat tied the secular rise in childhood allergy directly to the dietary shift toward seed oils and "good fat" advice: "allergic women have babies born with higher polyunsaturated fatty acid content, and those babies are more likely to develop allergies at an early age." The mechanism runs through both substrate availability and oxidative damage. "Besides making the prostaglandins, [PUFA] cause changes in cells that disrupt their functions. Every function of the cell can be disrupted by too much of the polyunsaturated fats. One of the worst things they do is interrupt oxidative metabolism. The energy deficit is ultimately the thing that leads to really serious allergy problems." A child whose tissues were built from her mother's linoleic-acid-rich diet starts life with the substrate for chronic histamine release already in place.

**Hypothyroidism and cold mucosae.** [[Hypothyroidism]] sits underneath the whole picture. Low thyroid means low CO2 production, poor capillary perfusion of mucous membranes, cold extremities (the same cold extremities show up in the bronchial tree as poor mucosal circulation), and an excess of all the stress mediators. "In hypothyroidism, it is common for there to be an excess of adrenalin/noradrenalin, serotonin, histamine, and some of the pituitary hormones," Peat wrote in [[autonomic-systems|*Autonomic Systems*]]. T3 directly stabilizes mast cells so they "don't secrete serotonin, histamine, and other inflammatory substances." Untreated hypothyroidism keeps the bronchial smooth muscle and the nasal vasculature in a permanently irritable state that even careful trigger avoidance cannot resolve.

**The serotonin–lung axis.** Serotonin's bronchoconstrictor effect is direct, fast, and dose-responsive. The lung itself has machinery for destroying serotonin — "Peroxide formed in the lungs helps to destroy the serotonin. That seems to be how negatively ionized air helps to relieve asthma and improve mood — because it helps to destroy serotonin in the lungs," Peat said on [[2011-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-endotoxins-stress|the 2011 *Serotonin* episode]]. When the production overwhelms the lung's clearance, the result is bronchospasm, mucosal edema, and the longer-term remodeling that shows up as pulmonary artery hypertension and emphysema-like diffusion thickening. The same mechanism that drives carcinoid-syndrome heart valve damage in serotonin-secreting tumors drives garden-variety asthma at lower intensities.

**CO2 deficit.** Allergic and asthmatic people tend to hyperventilate. Hyperventilation drops CO2, raises arterial pH, destabilizes mast cells, and primes the very release the patient is hyperventilating in fear of. The Buteyko school built an entire asthma practice around this loop; Peat's framing is identical at the chemistry level, with the bag as the simplest tool. See [[bag-breathing]] for the operating manual.

These five inputs are also five exits. The treatment plan is whichever combination of them the patient can move first.

## Peat's protocol

The interventions below run in parallel, not in sequence. The point is to lower the field — histamine tone, serotonin tone, endotoxin load, mast-cell reactivity — from several angles at once, so that no single intervention has to carry the whole load.

**[[Cyproheptadine]] for both acute reactions and chronic mast-cell tone.** This is the single most useful pharmaceutical in Peat's allergy and asthma toolkit, because it blocks both histamine (H1) and serotonin (5-HT2) receptors in one molecule. Dosing is fractional — half a milligram to two milligrams, often at bedtime — and effects are durable. See [[cyproheptadine]] for the full dose ladder. For a child with eczema-plus-asthma, a fraction of a tablet at night often interrupts the wake-up sneeze, the morning peak-flow drop, and the night scratching simultaneously, in a way that no single-target drug can.

**Thyroid optimization.** Restoring T3 to a level where waking temperature reaches 98.5°F by mid-morning, resting pulse runs 75–85 bpm, and extremities stay warm is upstream of nearly all the other interventions. Peat treated this as non-negotiable in chronic allergy or asthma. Dosing is covered at [[thyroid-dosing]] and [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

**Gut decontamination and endotoxin reduction.** Daily raw [[carrot-salad]] is the starting move; [[bamboo-shoots]] and well-cooked white mushrooms rotate in. Brief, low-dose courses of an antibiotic — "Sometimes a third of the standard dose of penicillin, tetracycline, or erythromycin will work" for a flare, as Peat noted in the 2016 show — are appropriate in some cases under clinician oversight. The full operating manual is at [[endotoxin-reduction]].

**PUFA depletion.** No seed oils, no fish oil, no "healthy fats" from chicken fat or pork fat. Butter, coconut oil, beef tallow. The tissue half-life of stored PUFA is years, so the dietary change matters more than any single supplement. See [[pufa-depletion]].

**Magnesium for bronchodilation.** Intravenous magnesium has been an emergency-room asthma treatment for decades; oral magnesium glycinate or magnesium chloride at 300–600 mg/day approximates the chronic version. "Magnesium is essential and works with thyroid," Peat noted in the 2016 show. Magnesium relaxes bronchial smooth muscle and stabilizes mast cells; it is one of the few interventions in this list that conventional pulmonology and Peat agree on.

**[[Bag-breathing]] for CO2 elevation.** A small paper bag, one to five minutes at a time, two or three times a day, and during any anxiety or pre-attack flush. The bag is the cheapest intervention on the list and the one most people skip because it looks too simple to work. The mechanism is laid out at [[carbon-dioxide]] and the operating manual is at [[bag-breathing]].

**Vitamins K2 and D, calcium, and magnesium.** "Calcium and Vitamin D are very strongly anti-allergic. Magnesium is the most famous because a deficiency causes a range of inflammatory diseases. You could cure skin diseases, heart disease, nerve disease, and liver disease just by correcting a magnesium deficiency. Vitamin D and calcium work with magnesium. Some people cure their allergies just by supplementing Vitamin D or Vitamin K." Adequate dairy plus sunlight plus 20–80 µg/day vitamin K2 carries this layer for most people.

**Salt and sugar to lower the stress-trigger pattern.** Allergic flares often track low-blood-sugar events: the parasympathetic sleep dip, the post-protein insulin spike, the long-gap-between-meals adrenaline surge. Peat repeatedly observed that a small dose of sugar — orange juice, milk-and-sugar, even Coca-Cola — interrupts a flare in motion. The Selye-lab experiments he cited showed that animals with blood glucose held two to three times normal could not be killed by even the deadliest allergens, while the same animals dropped into hypoglycemia died of "the mildest allergen." Salt to appetite plus sugar from ripe fruit, juice, milk, and honey throughout the day is the metabolic floor on top of which the other interventions work.

## Acute asthma intervention

The Peat-aligned acute protocol differs from the conventional one in emphasis, not in safety boundaries. The boundary is unchanged: anaphylaxis is an epinephrine emergency, full stop, and severe acute asthma with falling oxygen saturation is a hospital problem.

Inside that boundary, the components are: a dose of [[cyproheptadine]] (one to two milligrams) at the onset of the flare; [[bag-breathing]] for one to two minutes, repeated; warmth at the extremities to pull the sympathetic surge back; a small dose of sugar with salt if the flare is hypoglycemia-triggered (the classic 2 a.m. wake-up pattern); and an aspirin (300–500 mg) if the flare is exertional or prostaglandin-driven. Peat's [[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|2016 description]] of his own sleep-onset asthma includes the older anticholinergic option — "Jimson weed or atropine — the belladonna type chemical — breaks the muscarinic part of the parasympathetic reaction" — but he flagged it as "an emergency treatment," because chronic anticholinergic burden causes dry-mouth-driven dental decay and, in older patients, raises dementia risk. The combination of cyproheptadine, the bag, sugar, and warmth covers the same ground without the side-effect tail.

The contrarian piece is the warning against routine albuterol overuse. Beta-2 agonist inhalers work by binding the same receptors the adrenal system uses for endogenous bronchodilation, and chronic use desensitizes those receptors. The result is escalating doses with diminishing returns and, in the published literature, increased mortality at high cumulative use. The conventional response has been to combine albuterol with an inhaled corticosteroid, which addresses the bronchospasm but adds its own long-term cost (see *What to avoid* below). Peat treated the rescue inhaler as a real tool for genuine emergencies and a trap when used as the daily floor.

One specific 1986 warning is worth preserving. In [[asthma-and-metabisulfite|*Asthma and Metabisulfite*]], Peat documented that Alupent (metaproterenol), an asthma inhaler, listed metabisulfite as an excipient — a substance "known to cause trouble in asthmatics." The "paradoxical bronchospasm" listed as a rare side effect of the drug was, he argued, not paradoxical at all if the patient was reacting to the metabisulfite. Patients with asthma should check excipient lists on any inhaler they are prescribed; sulfites are the worst offender, and they show up where they should not.

## Children with asthma

The pediatric piece is the most leveraged, and the easiest to get wrong. Peat tied the secular rise in childhood asthma directly to three dietary shifts: PUFA-loaded baby foods, early grain introduction, and especially soy infant formula. "I think it has seriously harmed lots of kids, partly through the estrogenic effect," he said on the 2016 iodine episode about soy formula. The estrogenic effect feeds the histamine-serotonin cascade; the PUFA content feeds the prostaglandin cascade; the early grain exposure feeds the gut dysbiosis. By age two, a child raised on this profile has a body chemistry that produces the atopic march on schedule.

The corrective frame is laid out at [[child-feeding]]: whole milk or properly-prepared homemade infant formula (never soy formula), fruit, gelatin-rich meats, salt, no PUFA, late grain introduction or none, and tolerance to mess. A child whose eczema clears in two weeks on this baseline rarely develops the food allergies and almost never develops the asthma. A child whose eczema is already there gets cyproheptadine in fractional doses, thyroid if indicated, dietary correction, and time. The pattern usually reverses within months rather than years.

## What to avoid

**Chronic inhaled corticosteroids as the long-term default.** Topical to the lung is not topical only — significant systemic absorption occurs, and the long-term effects on bone density, adrenal function, and pediatric growth are real. The drug class has its uses in severe disease that has not responded to underlying-physiology correction, but the routine prescription of inhaled steroids to any child with two episodes of wheeze is a mistake.

**Montelukast (Singulair).** A leukotriene receptor antagonist that addresses one downstream mediator while doing nothing about the upstream field. It also carries a black-box warning for neuropsychiatric effects — depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation — that aligns predictably with the serotonin and inflammatory cascades it leaves untreated.

**Antihistamine-only treatment without addressing the root.** A daily second-generation antihistamine (loratadine, fexofenadine) blocks H1 receptors but does nothing about the histamine release, the serotonin elevation, the endotoxin load, the PUFA, or the CO2 deficit. Symptoms migrate. A patient who needs daily antihistamine after six months on the program has not been treated; they have been masked.

**Restrictive elimination diets without addressing the gut.** The gold-standard food-allergy work-up — eliminate, then provoke, then label — produces a long list of "allergens" without ever asking why the gut is reactive. A patient ends up on a four-food diet with worse dysbiosis than they started with. The right frame is to correct the gut, then re-test tolerance.

**SSRIs in any patient with asthma.** This is downstream of the serotonin–lung axis above. The 2011 caller who came off her SSRI and lost her asthma is not an anecdote; she is the rule. Any asthma patient prescribed an SSRI should be re-evaluated, and the wean should be slow, gelatin-and-cyproheptadine-supported, and watched closely.

## Tracking signals

The right metrics are simple and cheap, taken daily.

- **Waking temperature and one-hour-post-breakfast temperature.** Target: waking ~97.8°F, mid-morning ~98.5°F. Below this is hypothyroid; above-with-cold-extremities is stress-driven.
- **Peak flow** if asthma is part of the picture. Daily morning reading, log it. Trend over weeks matters more than any single reading.
- **Symptom frequency and severity score.** Three numbers a day, on a phone note: skin, gut, airway. The trajectory is the signal.
- **Sleep continuity.** The pattern of waking at 2–4 a.m. with sneeze, itch, or wheeze tracks the histamine-serotonin-blood-sugar loop. Resolution of these night wakings is one of the earliest and most reliable signals that the protocol is working.
- **Gut comfort and bowel pattern.** One or two soft, formed stools a day, no urgency, no cramping. Constipation or chronic loose stools is the gut piece talking.
- **Skin signal.** Eczema patches, hives, "dark circles" under the eyes, chronically congested single nostril — all of these are visible readouts of the same field. They move together.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream consensus on allergy and asthma is built around three positions that Peat's framework contradicts.

**The IgE-and-biologics era.** The current allergy-immunology direction is monoclonal antibodies targeting IgE (omalizumab) or specific cytokines like IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 (dupilumab, mepolizumab). The drugs work, in the narrow sense that they suppress the targeted pathway and produce measurable symptom improvement in a subset of patients. Peat's response was not that these targets do not exist — they clearly do — but that they are downstream, expensive, and indefinite. A drug that requires biweekly injection for life at tens of thousands of dollars a year, addressing one mediator in a cascade of twenty, is what a system produces when it refuses to ask what is upstream. The biologics era is real evidence that the IgE-and-cytokine pathway is involved in allergy; it is not evidence that this pathway is the primary cause.

**Allergy as a primarily genetic disease.** The familial clustering is real, and twin studies show heritability. Peat's framework absorbs this without trouble: the heritable piece is the metabolic predisposition — PUFA composition of tissues, thyroid setpoint, gut barrier function, mast-cell density — not a fixed immune destiny. The same heritable substrate, raised on the 1950 American diet, produced 5% allergy prevalence. Raised on the 2020 American diet, it produces 30%. Genes that have not changed cannot explain a sixfold increase in two generations. The environmental piece is doing the work, and the environmental piece is exactly the metabolic-inflammatory field this article is about.

**Asthma as primarily a bronchial-constriction problem.** The conventional model treats asthma as airway smooth-muscle hyperreactivity, with inflammation as a contributing factor and everything else as background. Peat's model treats the smooth-muscle reactivity as the visible end of a chain that runs through serotonin, histamine, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, nitric oxide, mast cells, endotoxin, hypothyroidism, and CO2 deficit. The reductionism of the conventional model is what makes inhaled-steroid-plus-bronchodilator look like a complete protocol; once you see the chain, the protocol obviously addresses only the last two links. The biologics era is, ironically, the conventional model's own admission that more of the chain matters than the bronchial-spasm framing implies.

The Peat framework is not without its own weak spots. The most honest one is that allergy and asthma have not been formally studied in this configuration. There is no randomized trial of cyproheptadine-plus-thyroid-plus-bag-breathing-plus-PUFA-depletion versus standard care. The supporting evidence is mechanistic, anecdotal, and circumstantial. It is internally coherent and clinically useful in the hands of people who use it, but it is not yet evidence-based medicine in the formal sense.

## Data gaps

The most useful study that does not exist would be a long-term randomized trial of a Peat-aligned protocol against the GINA guidelines in pediatric asthma. The next most useful would be measurement of arterial CO2, serotonin, and endotoxin markers in stable versus exacerbated asthma, with the question of whether the chronic baseline differs from controls. The third would be a formal trial of low-dose cyproheptadine as a steroid-sparing agent in atopic dermatitis with comorbid asthma — a question the conventional literature has not seriously asked since the 1970s.

Specific mechanism questions remain open. The relative contributions of histamine versus serotonin to bronchospasm in different asthma phenotypes are not well-characterized; the literature has tended to assume histamine, and the serotonin contribution that Peat emphasized has been under-studied. The role of intestinal endotoxin in steady-state airway inflammation — versus its established role in acute lung injury and ARDS — is similarly under-characterized. The CO2 setpoint in chronic asthma, despite the long Buteyko tradition, has not been mapped to symptom severity in a way that would let a clinician calibrate bag-breathing dose.

The pediatric soy-formula question is unresolved in the formal literature even where Peat treated it as decided. Cohort studies exist; randomized trials, for obvious ethical reasons, do not. The mechanistic case against soy formula in infant feeding is strong but indirect.

## Safety

This article documents a framework, not medical advice. Allergic and asthmatic disease can be life-threatening. The interventions described here are best applied alongside, not in place of, ongoing allergy or pulmonology care, with the clinician informed. Specific safety boundaries:

- **Anaphylaxis is an epinephrine emergency.** A patient with a history of anaphylaxis carries an EpiPen and uses it; nothing in this article displaces that.
- **Acute severe asthma with falling oxygen saturation is a hospital problem.** Bag-breathing is contraindicated when pulse oximetry is below ~92% — the breathing pattern is doing real work and should not be suppressed.
- **Wean off inhaled corticosteroids slowly, under clinical supervision.** Abrupt withdrawal can precipitate adrenal insufficiency and rebound asthma.
- **Wean off SSRIs slowly, under clinical supervision.** The serotonin-syndrome and discontinuation-syndrome literature is real; gelatin, cyproheptadine, and time are the supports.
- **Cyproheptadine carries the anticholinergic-burden caveat in older patients.** Lowest effective dose, intermittent use. See [[cyproheptadine]] for full failure modes.
