# Multiple Sclerosis

Mainstream neurology describes multiple sclerosis as an autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the myelin sheaths of the central nervous system, producing the plaques, the demyelination, and the slow accumulation of neurological deficits that define the clinical picture. Disease-modifying therapies — interferons, glatiramer acetate, the monoclonal antibodies that suppress B-cells or T-cells — all proceed from the assumption that the immune attack is the lesion. Ray Peat read the evidence in the opposite direction. The immune cells around an MS plaque are doing the same thing they do at the site of any chronic injury: cleaning up debris, secreting their inflammatory mediators, attempting to wall off the damaged tissue. They arrive because something has gone wrong in the myelinating cells themselves. The autoimmune label is a description of the cleanup crew, not the cause of the fire.

The two pieces of Peat's writing on this disease are unusually direct. In [[multiple-sclerosis-and-other-hormone-related-brain-syndromes-1993|*Multiple Sclerosis and Other Hormone-Related Brain Syndromes* (1993)]], he laid out the framework: pregnenolone-producing oligodendrocytes fail under the combined load of hypothyroidism, estrogen excess, short winter days, and metabolic poisons; the myelin they normally maintain begins to break down; and the surrounding immune activity that pathologists see at autopsy is the downstream consequence. In [[multiple-sclerosis-protein-fats-and-progesterone|*Multiple Sclerosis, Protein, Fats, and Progesterone* (2009)]], he sharpened the position: "In MS, it is clear that the inflammatory process itself is destructive, and that estrogen is a major predisposing factor. Unsaturated fatty acids, and dietary imbalance of amino acids interact closely with hyperestrogenism and hypothyroidism to produce the autoimmune degenerative diseases." Reducing the mediators of inflammation is fundamentally different from suppressing a single arm of immunity with cortisol or interferon. The first restores the conditions under which the brain can stop being inflamed. The second numbs the cleanup crew and lets the underlying defect grind on. The engine — failure of brain energy production combined with hormonal imbalance — is the same engine Peat described for [[dementia|*dementia*]] and the rest of the brain-energy cluster.

## What's Actually Happening: Myelin, Oligodendrocytes, and the Cleanup Reframe

The myelin sheath that wraps central nervous system axons is not a passive electrical insulator. It is the elaborated cytoplasm of the oligodendrocyte, a steroid-producing glial cell that converts cholesterol into pregnenolone in its mitochondria and feeds that pregnenolone into the immediate neighborhood of the nerve fiber it supports. Peat made the point repeatedly: "The oligodendrocytes are steroid-producing cells, and steroidogenesis is dependent on thyroid hormone, and on thyroid-dependent respiratory enzymes and on the heme-enzyme P-450scc, which are all sensitive to poisoning by carbon monoxide and cyanide. The steroid produced by the oligodendrocytes is pregnenolone, which is known to have a profound anti-stress action, and which appears to be the main brain-protective steroid."

The myelin sheath is therefore the delivery system for the brain's largest source of protective neurosteroid. When an oligodendrocyte fails — because the [[thyroid]] hormone it needs for steroidogenesis is suppressed, because the [[pufa|polyunsaturated fats]] in its membranes are oxidizing in the presence of iron and nitric oxide, because the estrogen in its surroundings has poisoned its respiratory chain — the local supply of pregnenolone collapses. The myelin sheath, no longer being maintained by an actively respiring cell, breaks down. Small clots and vasospasms appear in the capillary that had been supplying it. Pathologists see all of this and call it an immune attack because immune cells have moved in to clean up.

The vascular pattern of MS lesions is the structural fingerprint of this sequence. In 1936 Tracy Putnam published the observation that MS plaques tend to form around individual small blood vessels in which clots can usually be found, and multiple independent groups have confirmed the pattern. From the 1993 newsletter: "Various researchers have demonstrated that the plaques of MS usually occur in the area served by a single blood vessel, and some have suggested that clotting is the cause. MS patients have been found to have an abnormal clotting time." In animal models, brain lesions resembling MS can be produced by carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning — both of which poison the same P-450scc enzyme that converts cholesterol to pregnenolone in the oligodendrocyte. The lesion, experimentally, is not an immune event but an energy-supply event that the immune system arrives to clean up.

The remyelination cycle complicates the immune-attack story further. MS is classically relapsing-remitting in its early stages because demyelinated axons can be reinvested with new myelin during periods when the upstream load decreases. The brain has not lost its capacity to produce myelin. It has lost the metabolic conditions under which the cells that produce it can do their job. Restore the conditions and the cycle resumes — until the next pulse of estrogen, PUFA mobilization, or cold-season stress overwhelms the system again.

## Peat's Causal Cluster

Peat identified six interlocking loads that converge on the oligodendrocyte. None of them is sufficient alone. Together they produce the chronic neuroinflammatory state that pathologists call MS.

### Estrogen Excess

[[estrogen|*Estrogen*]] is the load Peat treated as most diagnostic in MS. Women develop the disease at roughly twice the rate of men; onset clusters in the reproductive years when estrogen exposure is highest; symptoms often worsen premenstrually when estrogen peaks unopposed by progesterone; and pregnancy — the natural high-progesterone state — frequently produces remission. The 1999 Bansil study Peat cited in the 2009 article showed that women with the highest ratio of estrogen to progesterone had the most active brain lesions on MRI. The mechanism is not hormonal receptor signaling alone. Estrogen activates mast cells (which release histamine and serotonin into the brain), causes platelets to aggregate (releasing more serotonin and producing the local vasospasms that show up around MS plaques), increases [[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]] synthesis (which catalyzes lipid peroxidation in myelin), and shifts cellular metabolism away from oxidation toward fermentation. The full mechanism is on the [[estrogen]] hub. The relevant point for MS is that estrogen does, in the brain, exactly the things that produce the histological picture of the disease.

The orthodox medical position — that women get more MS because they are estrogen-deficient and need supplementation — is, in Peat's reading, the same inversion that has plagued the [[dementia|Alzheimer's]] literature: "the stove burns you because it isn't hot enough." The Seattle hysterectomy data cited in the dementia article cuts in the same direction for MS. Hysterectomy is, in Peat's framework, a downstream indicator of chronic estrogen excess, and the women who later develop neurodegenerative disease are dramatically over-represented among those who underwent the procedure in mid-life.

### Polyunsaturated Fat in the Myelin Membrane

Myelin is roughly 70% lipid by dry weight, and across the twentieth century the lipid composition of myelin in industrialized populations has shifted toward an ever-higher fraction of polyunsaturated fatty acids — primarily linoleic acid and its derivatives, secondarily the omega-3s. [[pufa|*Polyunsaturated fats*]] are inherently unstable; their double bonds are attacked by every free radical biology generates, and in the presence of iron and nitric oxide they undergo the chain reaction of [[lipid-peroxidation|lipid peroxidation]] that produces aldehyde toxins like acrolein and 4-hydroxynonenal. A myelin membrane built on PUFA is a membrane that degrades whenever oxidative stress rises. Peat made the point in the 2009 newsletter: "Lipid peroxidation is very high in multiple sclerosis. Nitric oxide (whose synthesis is promoted by estrogen in most parts of the brain) is a free radical that activates peroxidation."

The epidemiological clue Peat returned to repeatedly is the pork-and-horsemeat correlation. MS incidence correlates with the consumption of pork and horsemeat but not with the consumption of beef, lamb, or goat. The biochemical difference is in the fat: ruminants (cows, sheep, goats) contain bacteria in their rumens that saturate the polyunsaturated fats they eat, so their tissue fat is dominated by saturated and monounsaturated forms. Monogastric animals (pigs, horses) have tissue fat that closely mirrors what they ate, meaning that in industrial agriculture their tissue is loaded with linoleic acid from corn and soy. The MS-pork correlation, in Peat's reading, is a PUFA correlation. The full mechanism is on the [[pufa]] hub.

### Hypothyroidism

[[hypothyroidism|*Hypothyroidism*]] is the upstream defect that connects most of the others. Thyroid hormone is required for the oligodendrocyte to absorb and use T3, for the P-450scc enzyme to convert cholesterol to pregnenolone, for the liver to clear estrogen, and for the basal energy production of every cell. A hypothyroid brain is a cold brain, and a cold brain is one whose mitochondria are running below the velocity at which they efficiently consume electrons. Peat described the clinical picture from his own practice: "In the late 1970s I was seeing a lot of people who had puzzling health problems. In a period of two or three years, there were five people who had been diagnosed by neurologists as having multiple sclerosis. In talking to them, it seemed clear that they had multiple symptoms of hypothyroidism. They weren't severely disabled. Since they weren't fat or lethargic, their physicians hadn't thought they could be hypothyroid. When they tried taking a thyroid supplement, all of their symptoms disappeared, including those that had led to their MS diagnosis."

The basal temperature data supports the same reading. People with MS regulate their body temperature poorly, and their nerve conduction is paradoxically slower at normal body temperature than at lower temperatures — the opposite of what conduction in a healthy nerve does. Peat read this as evidence that the nerves in MS are operating in a "pre-fatigued" state: estrogen and cortisol have dissipated their internal structure, and additional thermal load only accelerates the failure. Restoring thyroid function — slowly, in combination with the other supports — re-establishes the structural integrity of the nerve fiber. The [[thyroid]] hub has the full dosing framework.

### Endotoxin and Serotonin

When the gut wall becomes excessively permeable — under hypothyroidism, stress, or PUFA-induced inflammation — lipopolysaccharide endotoxin enters the portal circulation, activates Kupffer cells, raises inflammatory cytokines, and increases [[serotonin|serotonin]] release from enterochromaffin cells and platelets. Brain mast cells (which Peat noted are more numerous in MS brains than in normal brains) release histamine and serotonin in response. The serotonin antagonists ondansetron and ketanserin have produced symptomatic improvement in MS trials, which Peat read as direct confirmation that the serotonin load is mechanistic rather than incidental.

The protein-deficiency angle ties this to nutrition. From the 2009 newsletter: "A simple protein deficiency has many surprising effects. It lowers body temperature, and suppresses the thyroid, but it increases inflammation and the tendency of blood to clot…cortisol must be produced continuously to mobilize amino acids from the expendable tissues, which are mainly the skeletal muscles. These muscles have a high concentration of tryptophan and cysteine, which suppress the thyroid. Cysteine is excitotoxic, and tryptophan is the precursor for serotonin." A vegan or low-protein diet is, in this framework, a serotonin-elevating, thyroid-suppressing, endotoxin-amplifying diet — exactly the loads that produce MS.

### Vitamin D Deficiency and Short-Day Brain Stress

The latitude correlation in MS — incidence rises with distance from the equator, drops in sunny dry climates and at high altitude — is the best-established epidemiological signal in the disease. Mainstream neurology translates this into a vitamin D hypothesis. Peat read the same correlation but emphasized a broader cluster. Short-day stress suppresses thyroid function. Light reverses carbon monoxide and cyanide poisoning of the cytochrome enzymes that produce pregnenolone. High altitude raises tissue CO2, stabilizing myelin and reducing serotonin's effects. From the 1993 newsletter: "Studies in Leningrad began revealing that mitochondria are injured during darkness, and repaired during daylight…Winter's darkness is stressful in a very fundamental way, and like any stress it tends to suppress thyroid function. In the hypothyroid state, any estrogen which is produced tends to accumulate in the body, because of liver sluggishness." [[vitamin-d|*Vitamin D*]] is part of the answer, not the whole answer.

## Why Women Get It More

The female:male ratio in MS is roughly 2:1, onset clusters between ages 20 and 40 (the reproductive years), and the menstrual-cycle modulation of symptoms is a classical clinical finding. Peat treated all of this as direct evidence for the estrogen mechanism. The premenstrual exacerbation of MS symptoms maps onto the period of progesterone withdrawal — exactly when estrogen's effects on mast cells, platelets, and nitric oxide are unopposed. The Bansil 1999 MRI data found that women with the highest estrogen:progesterone ratio had the most active lesions.

The 1976 case Peat described in the 1993 newsletter is the clinical anchor: a woman in her mid-thirties disabled by an MS-like condition (possibly Devic's disease, the optic-neuritis variant) recovered enough to climb stairs, drive her car, and deliver a lecture on progesterone therapy after starting progesterone injections. She had read Katharina Dalton's progesterone literature in the medical school library and convinced a physician to give her a trial. The recovery time scale — weeks, not months — matches what Peat saw repeatedly across the brain-energy cluster: a defect whose downstream sign reverses quickly under hormonal intervention is not a structural lesion in the irreversible sense.

## Peat's Protocol

The action layer crosses the [[brain-orthomolecular-protocol]] but emphasizes the loads specific to MS.

**Progesterone.** For women with MS, the most direct intervention. Doses calibrated to symptom response, often 100-200 mg/day topically (Progest-E or equivalent), higher during a relapse. The goal is to push the estrogen:progesterone ratio back toward what it would be in the third trimester of pregnancy. Men benefit at 50-100 mg/day for the general anti-estrogen and anti-cortisol effects.

**Thyroid optimization.** Restoring [[thyroid]] function — guided by basal temperature near 98°F and resting pulse near 85 — addresses the upstream defect that suppresses pregnenolone synthesis, slows estrogen clearance, and lowers mitochondrial energy production. Peat consistently emphasized natural desiccated thyroid (T3+T4) over T4 monotherapy, since pure thyroxine can suppress brain respiration and produce paradoxical neurological symptoms. Dose slowly — see [[thyroid-dosing]].

**Pregnenolone.** As the steroid the oligodendrocytes themselves produce, [[pregnenolone]] addresses the local brain steroid deficit directly. 30-100 mg/day with food; higher doses (200-400 mg) occasionally during acute flares.

**PUFA depletion.** The slowest variable to change — body fat half-life for linoleic acid is roughly two years. Eliminate seed oils, eliminate fish oil supplements, switch cooking fats to butter, coconut oil, and ghee. The stored PUFA will continue to mobilize during stress for years. Full protocol at [[pufa-depletion]].

**Aspirin.** [[aspirin|*Aspirin*]] is anti-inflammatory, antiestrogenic in some contexts, a powerful antioxidant, and a platelet aggregation inhibitor — addressing both inflammation and the small clots that show up around MS plaques. 325-650 mg one to three times daily, with vitamin K2 to offset bleeding risk.

**Vitamin K2 and Vitamin D.** [[vitamin-d]] addresses the cold-climate correlation; K2 supports calcium handling and offsets aspirin's bleeding risk. Vitamin D without K2 can drive soft-tissue calcification.

**Methylene blue (periodic).** [[methylene-blue|*Methylene blue*]] supports cytochrome oxidase activity at low doses (1-5 mg). Once or twice weekly in the morning.

**Sun and walking.** Daily sunlight reverses cytochrome poisoning and addresses the latitude correlation. Walking — concentric exercise — raises CO2 and builds mitochondrial density.

**Protein and gelatin.** Adequate protein from milk, cheese, eggs, beef, and gelatin (bone broth) supplies amino acids that prevent cortisol-driven catabolism. Gelatin specifically is rich in glycine and proline and low in the tryptophan and cysteine that feed serotonin and excitotoxic load. See [[proteins-ranked]].

## What to AVOID

**Interferons (the disease-modifying therapies).** Beta-interferons and the broader DMT class suppress immune activity and modulate the inflammatory cascade. They can reduce relapse frequency in some patients on short timescales. Peat's framework predicts — and the long-term outcome data from the DMT literature is consistent with — that suppressing the cleanup response without addressing the upstream metabolic defect leads to slower symptomatic relapses but no improvement in long-term disability progression. Worse, the interferons themselves produce a flu-like inflammatory response, raise cortisol, and add to the systemic load the framework is trying to reduce. Coordinate with a neurologist before discontinuing — the safety note below is non-negotiable on this point — but understand that the long-term mechanism cuts against the framework.

**Long-term immunosuppressants.** Mitoxantrone, cyclophosphamide, and the newer monoclonal antibodies (natalizumab, ocrelizumab, alemtuzumab) all produce serious side effect profiles, including PML (progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy) — a fatal demyelinating disease that the immunosuppressant itself induces by allowing JC virus reactivation. The risk-benefit calculation in the Peat framework is dramatically different from the standard neurology calculation because the framework predicts that the underlying defect can be addressed without immunosuppression.

**Vegan diet.** A vegan diet combines several of the loads MS is most sensitive to: B12 deficiency, saturated fat deficiency, high linoleic acid intake from seed oils and nuts, low gelatin intake (no animal collagen), low calcium intake, and a tryptophan-heavy amino acid profile that drives serotonin. The popularity of the Swank diet (low animal fat, high seed oil) in MS circles is, in Peat's reading, exactly backwards. Swank's protocol does reduce relapse frequency in some patients because it reduces overall animal fat intake, but it does so by replacing saturated fat with the linoleic acid that drives the underlying lesion.

**Fish oil supplements.** The omega-3 PUFAs marketed as anti-inflammatory are, in Peat's framework, the most peroxidation-prone fats in the human diet. Their breakdown products — particularly acrolein, which Peat singled out repeatedly in the dementia literature — are among the most neurotoxic small molecules biology generates. Fish oil for MS delivers the precursor of the most damaging aldehyde directly into a brain whose iron stores are already primed to catalyze its production.

**Chronic cold exposure.** Ice baths, cold plunges, and the broader cold-therapy fashion add to the short-day stress load that is already overrepresented in MS. The hormetic argument that brief cold exposure stimulates mitochondrial biogenesis is not load-bearing in a person whose mitochondrial respiration is already failing. Warmth — sun, sauna, warm clothing in cold seasons — is the protocol-consistent direction.

## Pregnancy and MS

The natural remission of MS during pregnancy is the cleanest piece of evidence in the literature for the progesterone mechanism. Progesterone rises tenfold or more across gestation, peaking near 250 ng/mL in the third trimester. Across multiple large prospective studies (PRIMS being the landmark), MS relapse rates drop by roughly 70% in the third trimester and rise sharply postpartum, typically peaking three months after delivery. The orthodox interpretation centers on pregnancy-induced shifts in T-helper cell populations from Th1 to Th2. Peat read the same data as direct confirmation that progesterone is the protective factor — the third-trimester level is at the high end of any dose he would clinically recommend, and the postpartum relapse spike coincides exactly with the postpartum progesterone collapse. From the 2009 newsletter: "Progesterone counteracts estrogen's effects on both mast cells and platelets."

The practical implication is unambiguous in the framework: dose progesterone to approximate the protective effect that pregnancy produces naturally. The protocol cannot reproduce pregnancy exactly (estriol, prolactin, and the full placental endocrine output are not replicable), but it can reproduce the third-trimester progesterone level, and that is where the largest single piece of evidence in the disease points.

## Tracking Signals

The metrics that matter in this framework, in approximate order of sensitivity:

- **Relapse frequency.** The orthodox outcome variable, but the easiest to track. Months-to-relapse is the simplest summary.
- **Basal body temperature** (oral, on waking). 97.0°F is hypothyroid; 98.0°F is approaching the protocol target.
- **Resting pulse rate.** 85 is the rough target; 65 with cold extremities suggests the metabolism is not yet restored.
- **MRI lesion stability.** Annual or semi-annual scans, with attention to the appearance of new T2 lesions and any gadolinium-enhancing (active) lesions.
- **Cognitive function.** Subjective alertness, word-finding, reading endurance. The brain-energy framework predicts this is the first variable to improve and the most sensitive measure of whether the protocol is working.
- **Gait and motor function.** Slower to respond but more clinically obvious.
- **Energy.** Patient-reported, but the most direct read of whether oxidative metabolism is being restored.

## Counter-Arguments

The autoimmune orthodoxy is not built on nothing. The case for it includes myelin-reactive T-cells in the cerebrospinal fluid of MS patients, the inflammatory infiltrate visible at autopsy in active plaques, genetic associations with HLA class II alleles, the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) animal model, and the relapse-reduction efficacy of the DMTs. Peat's framework reads every one of these findings as downstream of the metabolic lesion rather than as evidence of the lesion itself: the myelin-reactive T-cells are a normal consequence of any tissue injury that exposes self-antigens, the inflammatory infiltrate is the cleanup crew at a metabolically failing tissue, the HLA associations are markers of variable immune-response intensity, the EAE model does not reproduce the vascular pattern of human MS, and the DMTs reduce short-term relapse frequency without altering long-term disability progression because they suppress the cleanup without addressing the upstream defect.

The interferons reduce relapse rates by about 30% in their pivotal trials, and the monoclonal antibodies produce larger short-term effects, but long-term outcome data — disability progression, brain volume change over decades, conversion to secondary-progressive disease — show much smaller benefits. Peat's framework reads this gap as evidence that the relapse is not the disease: the underlying metabolic substrate continues to deteriorate regardless of whether the immune response to that deterioration is being suppressed.

The genetic-fatalism position holds that MS susceptibility is primarily heritable, with HLA-DRB1*15 conferring the largest single-gene risk. The framework grants the susceptibility but reads it through the same lens applied to APOE4 in [[dementia|*dementia*]]: a slightly less-resilient variant matters only under the stress conditions that estrogen, PUFA, hypothyroidism, and endotoxin produce. The same allele in equatorial populations eating ruminant fat does not produce the disease.

The vitamin-D-as-only-needed-intervention position has emerged in integrative neurology as the closest mainstream-friendly approximation to a metabolic reading. The framework treats it as partial confirmation but incomplete: vitamin D is one factor in the cluster, not the cluster itself.

## Data Gaps

- No randomized trial of progesterone monotherapy versus placebo in MS, despite the strength of the pregnancy-remission signal. The closest published work is POPARTMUS (postpartum progestin) which showed no benefit, but the synthetic progestin used falls outside the framework Peat predicted to work.
- No published data on the combined protocol (progesterone + thyroid + PUFA depletion + aspirin + pregnenolone). Anecdotal case reports exist in the bioenergetic community but no systematic collection.
- The endotoxin contribution is mechanistically plausible but undermeasured in MS-specific studies.
- Iron accumulation in MS plaques is well documented but deserves more framework attention than Peat gave it directly. The mechanism mirrors the iron-PUFA-peroxidation loop in dementia.
- Long-term safety data for high-dose progesterone in women with MS over decades is thin.
- The natural history of MS in populations that follow a Peat-consistent diet from childhood has never been studied.

## Safety Note

This article describes Ray Peat's framework for understanding multiple sclerosis. It is adjunct context for clinical decision-making, not a replacement for neurological care. Multiple sclerosis is a serious, progressive disease in many patients, and the disease-modifying therapies — whatever their long-term limitations — can reduce short-term relapse frequency and short-term disability accumulation in ways that matter for individual lives. **Do not discontinue interferons, monoclonal antibodies, or other DMTs without coordinating with the prescribing neurologist.** The Peat protocol layers on top of conventional care during the transition, and any decision to step down from DMTs should be made in consultation with a clinician who understands both frameworks. Progesterone, thyroid, and the other interventions discussed here have their own risk profiles (progesterone can affect liver enzymes, thyroid can precipitate cardiac symptoms in patients with subclinical coronary disease, aspirin carries bleeding risk) and warrant clinical supervision when used at the doses the framework recommends.
