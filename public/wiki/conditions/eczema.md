# Eczema

In the orthodox model, atopic dermatitis is a barrier defect on a primarily immune-driven disease: filaggrin mutation lets allergens through, a Th2-skewed immune system mounts an IgE response, and the itch-scratch-inflame cycle gets suppressed with topical steroids, calcineurin inhibitors, or — in the new era — biologics like dupilumab targeting IL-4 and IL-13. Ray Peat treated this whole picture as the visible last stop on a train that started in the gut. The eczema rash, in his reading, is the skin's readout of a systemic mast-cell-and-endothelial-cell hyper-reactivity that the same patient will express as food allergies at age two, asthma at age six, and seasonal allergic rhinitis at age twelve. It is the first symptom in the atopic march because the skin is the easiest target — the gut and the skin "have some antigens and enzymes that are so closely connected that when one is inflamed, the other gets inflamed," he told Andrew Murray on the [[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|2016 allergy show]] — and because infant skin lipid composition reflects, almost directly, the mother's polyunsaturated-fat intake during pregnancy and lactation.

This article is the eczema-specific extension of [[allergies-asthma|allergies and asthma]]. The underlying field is the same. What is specific to eczema is the skin layer: stratum-corneum lipid composition, the *Staphylococcus aureus* colonization that 95% of atopic-dermatitis patients carry, the role of sebum and sunlight, and the topical interventions that help versus the ones that perpetuate the cycle.

## The atopic march is one disease

The pediatric allergy literature recognizes the atopic march as a phenomenon: eczema in infancy predicts food allergies in toddlerhood, which predicts asthma in school-age, which predicts allergic rhinitis in adolescence. The orthodoxy treats it as a developmental sequence to be interrupted at each stop — emollients for the eczema, elimination diet for the food allergies, inhaler for the asthma, antihistamine for the rhinitis. Peat's framing is that it is not a sequence; it is one state expressing through whichever tissue happens to be most stressed at a given age. The skin presents first because it has the highest density of mast cells outside the gut and because infant skin is barely protected — the stratum corneum is half as thick as adult skin and its lipid composition reflects what the mother ate during the third trimester and what the infant is fed in the first year.

Treating each stop separately leaves the underlying field untouched while building iatrogenic damage. The child whose eczema is suppressed with hydrocortisone develops food allergies because the gut-skin axis was never addressed; the food allergies get treated by elimination, narrowing the diet into worse dysbiosis. By the time asthma arrives, the system has had three to five additional years deepening the state that produced the eczema. Eczema, food allergy, asthma, and rhinitis are the same disease — chronic mast-cell and endothelial hyper-reactivity riding on a hypothyroid, PUFA-saturated, endotoxin-leaking metabolism — and they yield together when the metabolism is corrected.

## Peat's causal cluster

Five inputs sustain the eczema field. None is the cause in isolation; their combination locks the rash in place.

**Gut dysbiosis and endotoxin leak.** "Celiac disease is one of the causes of both skin disease and arthritis," Peat said on the 2012 *Politics and Science* fundraiser when a caller asked about eczema and leaky gut. "Celiac disease will allow endotoxin into your circulation. Low thyroid is probably the most common thing associated with ordinary eczema. When your thyroid is low, the circulation in your intestine is poor, serotonin is high, and the combination of endotoxin and serotonin will cause the skin to have abnormal growth patterns." Endotoxin from gram-negative bacteria primes mast cells throughout the body; cutaneous mast cells release histamine and serotonin into the dermis, producing the wheal, the itch, the broken skin, and the secondary *Staph* colonization. This is the mechanism described at length in [[endotoxin]] and operationalized in [[endotoxin-reduction]].

**Histamine and serotonin in skin.** The cutaneous mast cell does what its intestinal cousin does, on a tissue with no comparable mucus or smooth-muscle defense — it just inflames. Histamine drives the wheal and the itch; serotonin drives the persistent edema and the "abnormal growth patterns" Peat referenced. Both rise together when blood sugar drops, endotoxin is absorbed, estrogen is high, or stress mediators elevate against a hypothyroid background. "In hypothyroidism, it is common for there to be an excess of adrenalin/noradrenalin, serotonin, histamine, and some of the pituitary hormones," Peat wrote in [[autonomic-systems|*Autonomic Systems*]].

**PUFA in skin lipids and the prostaglandin substrate.** The stratum corneum's lipid envelope is built from whatever fatty acids the body has on hand. A child raised by a linoleic-acid-rich mother and weaned onto soy-oil-containing baby food builds a barrier high in arachidonic-acid precursors and biased toward prostaglandin production. Prostaglandin E2 is one of the strongest mast-cell histamine-release signals. The dermatology literature calls this "essential fatty acid deficiency" and recommends more PUFA topically — which Peat treated as exactly backwards. The Burrs' 1929 experiment producing "dandruff, dermatitis, slowed growth, sterility, and fatal kidney degeneration" by stripping fat from rat diets was producing vitamin and trace mineral deficiencies the field had not yet discovered, not proving a need for linoleate (see [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|*Unsaturated Fatty Acids: Nutritionally Essential or Toxic?*]]). Tissue half-life of stored PUFA is years; dietary correction matters more than any single supplement.

**Hypothyroid mucosal barrier.** Low thyroid produces cold, poorly-perfused, slow-turnover skin. Sebum production drops. The barrier becomes drier and more permeable. Capillary perfusion of the dermis is poor, so inflammatory mediators linger. The same hypothyroid state that gives the patient cold hands and a 96.5°F morning temperature gives them a skin that fissures at the elbows and behind the knees because local circulation cannot clear the inflammatory load. T3 directly stabilizes mast cells; correcting thyroid status is upstream of nearly every other intervention.

**Estrogen excess in women.** Adult-onset or perimenstrual eczema is one of the most reliable estrogen signatures in dermatology, though it is rarely framed that way. "Estrogen turns on both the multiplication of mast cells and their tendency to release histamine and serotonin," Peat said in the 2016 allergy show. "Getting your thyroid to a good level will reduce estrogen and increase progesterone." Women whose eczema worsens premenstrually, on combined oral contraceptives, in the estrogen-dominant first trimester, or in perimenopause are reading their own hormone profile through their skin.

## Children's eczema and the formula question

The pediatric eczema epidemic of the last three generations is the cleanest natural experiment in this framework, and the formula question is the central variable. Peat tied the secular rise in childhood atopy directly to three dietary shifts: PUFA-loaded baby foods, early grain introduction, and soy infant formula. "I think it has seriously harmed lots of kids, partly through the estrogenic effect," he said about soy formula. The estrogenic effect feeds the histamine-serotonin cascade; the PUFA content feeds the prostaglandin cascade; the phytate burden disrupts mineral status; early grain exposure feeds the gut dysbiosis.

First signs typically appear at three to six months — cheek rash, scalp cradle cap, dry patches behind the knees — exactly when the infant transitions from a higher-saturated-fat milk to a polyunsaturated-fat-dominant formula or to early grain cereals. A breastfed infant whose mother is eating a low-PUFA, dairy-and-fruit-rich diet rarely develops the rash; the same breastfed infant whose mother has been advised to "eat healthy fats" — salmon, walnuts, flax, vegetable-oil-based "Mediterranean" cooking — often does.

The corrective frame is at [[child-feeding]]: whole-fat milk or properly-prepared homemade infant formula (never soy), fruit, gelatin-rich meats, salt to appetite, no PUFA, late or no grain introduction. A child whose eczema clears in two to four weeks on this baseline rarely develops the food allergies and almost never develops the asthma. A child whose eczema is already entrenched gets fractional cyproheptadine, thyroid support if indicated, the dietary correction, and time. The pattern usually reverses within months rather than years.

## Peat's protocol

The interventions run in parallel, not in sequence. The point is to lower the field — histamine tone, serotonin tone, endotoxin load, mast-cell reactivity, estrogen excess — from several angles at once, so that no single intervention has to carry the load alone.

**[[Cyproheptadine]] for both flares and chronic mast-cell tone.** The single most useful pharmaceutical here, because it blocks both histamine (H1) and serotonin (5-HT2) receptors in one molecule and the cutaneous mast cell uses both. Dosing is fractional — quarter to half a tablet (1–2 mg) at bedtime for adults, a small wedge for children — and effects are durable. See [[cyproheptadine]] for the full dose ladder. For a child with eczema-plus-asthma, low-dose evening cyproheptadine interrupts the wake-up scratch, the night-cough, and the morning peak-flow drop simultaneously.

**Thyroid optimization.** Restoring T3 to a level where waking temperature reaches 98.5°F by mid-morning, resting pulse runs 75–85 bpm, and extremities stay warm is upstream of nearly every other intervention. Peat treated this as non-negotiable. Pregnenolone is sometimes useful as an adjunct — on the 2012 *Politics and Science* call he noted that "pregnenolone, by balancing all of the others" can interrupt the stress-flare pattern even before thyroid is fully restored. Dosing is at [[thyroid-dosing]] and [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

**Gut decontamination.** Daily raw [[carrot-salad]] is the starting move; well-cooked white mushrooms and [[bamboo-shoots]] rotate in. Brief low-dose courses of penicillin, tetracycline, or erythromycin are appropriate for severe flares under clinician oversight — "sometimes a third of the standard dose" interrupts the gut piece long enough for the metabolic interventions to catch up. The full operating manual is at [[endotoxin-reduction]]. Constipation is the most reliable trigger; one or two soft formed stools a day is the floor.

**PUFA depletion.** Butter, coconut oil, beef tallow; no seed oils, no fish oil, no PUFA-rich baby food. Tissue half-life is years, so dietary change matters more than any single supplement. See [[pufa-depletion]]. This is the single intervention most parents balk at — current pediatric guidance pushes the opposite direction — and the one that determines whether the eczema returns when other interventions are tapered.

**Vitamin A, D, K2, E plus zinc, from food where possible.** From the [[eczema|eczema email]]: "Liver and thyroid would be better than trying to use separate vitamins — vitamin A deficiency is the most likely, but some B vitamins could be involved." Liver weekly covers A, Bs, copper, and zinc together. Vitamin D from sun is preferred; K2 at 20–80 µg/day works with D and calcium. "Vitamin E has an antihistamine effect; it prevents the formation of prostaglandins and consequently prevents the degranulation of mast cells," Peat noted in 2016.

**Sun exposure on affected skin, in non-burning doses.** Vitamin D synthesis, mast-cell stabilization, local circulation, sebum normalization, and an antimicrobial effect on *Staphylococcus aureus* run together. Summer eczema is often half what winter eczema is. Narrow-band UVB phototherapy is essentially controlled sunlight; ordinary sun is upstream.

**Minimize harsh soap and hot water.** Hot showers, daily soap on affected skin, and alcohol sanitizers strip the lipid layer the body is failing to rebuild fast enough. Peat's own practice was "I rarely use soap on my skin"; he used coconut soap or baking soda on hair. Patients showering twice a day with antibacterial soap often see significant improvement in two weeks of simply stopping.

## Topical management

Topicals do not address the field, but they buy time and reduce damage while the metabolic interventions take effect. Three components carry most of the value.

**Coconut oil with a small amount of vitamin E** as the daily moisturizer. The medium-chain saturated fat is the most stratum-corneum-compatible plant oil and has in-vitro antimicrobial effects against *Staphylococcus aureus*. A few drops of mixed-tocopherol oil per tablespoon of coconut extends antioxidant and anti-prostaglandin coverage into the skin. Two to three times a day on affected areas.

**Aspirin paste for acute flares.** Crushed aspirin (one to two 325 mg tablets) in warm water or coconut oil, applied to the flaring patch for 20–30 minutes and wiped off. The salicylate inhibits local prostaglandin synthesis; effect is often visible within an hour. As-needed. Systemic counterpart: oral aspirin 300–500 mg with vitamin K2 (1 mg per 325 mg aspirin).

**Salt-water baths in moderation.** A handful of unrefined salt or sodium bicarbonate in a warm bath, ten to fifteen minutes, two to three times a week. Avoid extended hot baths — heat strips the barrier faster than salt helps.

**Avoid PUFA-containing topical preparations.** The single most-violated rule in OTC eczema care. Most commercial moisturizers and "barrier repair" creams contain sunflower, safflower, evening primrose, borage, or other polyunsaturated oils. Short-term softening followed by oxidative damage to the very lipid layer it is supposed to repair. If the ingredient list includes any seed oil, use coconut, beef tallow, or shea instead.

## What to avoid

**Chronic topical steroids as the long-term default.** Topical corticosteroids suppress visible inflammation and have a place in acute severe flares. Chronic use thins the skin, atrophies the dermis, depigments the area, and produces "topical steroid withdrawal" rebound when tapered. Use acutely to break a flare, then taper while metabolic interventions take effect.

**Chronic systemic immunosuppressants when alternatives exist.** Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) carry a black-box cancer warning — chronic T-cell suppression in UV-exposed tissue is the mechanism for skin malignancy. Methotrexate, cyclosporine, and the biologics each address one downstream pathway while leaving the upstream field untouched. Appropriate for severe disease that has failed physiology correction; not first-line.

**Restrictive elimination diets without gut focus.** Eliminate dairy, eggs, wheat, soy, nuts, fish; reintroduce one at a time; build an allergen list — and end up on a four-food diet with worse dysbiosis. Correct the gut, restore the metabolism, then re-test tolerance. Most "food allergies" in eczema patients are histamine-release patterns that resolve when the histamine load drops.

**Cool, dry skin-care orthodoxy.** "Bathe less," "avoid sun," "moisturize three times a day with our PUFA-containing barrier cream" — partially right (less aggressive cleansing) and partially exactly wrong (the sun, the PUFA, the perpetual occlusion).

## Tracking signals

- **Waking and one-hour-post-breakfast temperature.** Target: waking ~97.8°F, mid-morning ~98.5°F. Below this is hypothyroid.
- **Itch severity at bedtime and on waking.** Night-itch (waking at 2–4 a.m. scratching) tracks the histamine-serotonin-blood-sugar loop; its resolution is the earliest reliable signal the protocol is working.
- **Weekly photo of the worst patch.** Same angle and lighting. Week-over-week trajectory matters more than any single day.
- **Bowel pattern.** One or two soft formed stools a day, no straining. Constipation is the most reliable flare trigger.
- **In women, cycle-day correlation.** Luteal-phase clustering is the estrogen signature; stress clustering is the cortisol-blood-sugar signature.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream consensus on atopic dermatitis is built around three positions that Peat's framework contradicts.

**Eczema as a primarily immune-mediated disease.** Current allergy-immunology treats atopic dermatitis as a Th2-skewed immune dysregulation with a filaggrin-mutation barrier component. The biologic era (dupilumab targeting IL-4 receptor alpha, tralokinumab targeting IL-13, JAK inhibitors targeting downstream cytokine signaling) is real evidence that those pathways are involved. The Peat response is that they are downstream, expensive, and indefinite. A drug requiring biweekly injection for life at tens of thousands of dollars a year, addressing one cytokine in a cascade of twenty, is what a system produces when it refuses to ask what is upstream.

**The filaggrin / barrier-defect framing.** Filaggrin loss-of-function mutations are real and do correlate with eczema. The Peat-compatible reading is that the mutation is a vulnerability factor, not a cause — the same genotype produces eczema in a 2020 American child eating soy formula and rarely produces it in 1950 dietary conditions. The heritable piece is metabolic predisposition expressed through the barrier; the environmental piece is doing the work.

**The topical-steroid orthodoxy.** Mainstream dermatology treats topical steroids as the safe, first-line, long-term-acceptable foundation of eczema care. The patient-community literature on topical steroid withdrawal, skin-thinning, dermal atrophy, and rebound after years of use has been slow to make it into formal guidelines. The Peat reading aligns with the patient community: steroids are useful for breaking acute flares, dangerous as chronic prescription, and incompatible with resolving the underlying state.

The Peat framework has its own weak spots. The most honest one is that pediatric atopic dermatitis under a low-PUFA, dairy-and-fruit, cyproheptadine-supported protocol has never been formally trialed against standard care. The supporting evidence is mechanistic, anecdotal, and circumstantial. Severe atopic dermatitis with documented IgE elevation, extensive *Staphylococcus aureus* colonization, and family disruption can warrant the biologic era as an intervention while the metabolic work catches up.

## Data gaps

The most useful study that does not exist would be a randomized trial of a Peat-aligned protocol (cyproheptadine, thyroid optimization, PUFA depletion, gut decontamination, low-soap topical regimen, sun exposure) against standard care in pediatric atopic dermatitis. A formal trial of low-dose oral cyproheptadine as a steroid-sparing agent in moderate eczema with comorbid asthma is a question the dermatology literature has not seriously asked since the 1970s.

The relative contributions of histamine versus serotonin to cutaneous mast-cell release in eczema have not been well characterized; the literature tends to assume histamine. The role of intestinal endotoxin in steady-state atopic dermatitis — versus its established role in acute sepsis — is similarly under-characterized. The infant-formula PUFA question is unresolved for obvious ethical reasons; cohort data exists, randomized trials do not.

## Safety

This article documents a framework, not medical advice. Severe atopic dermatitis can be genuinely debilitating and the interventions here are best applied alongside ongoing dermatology care, with the clinician informed.

- **Severe or infected eczema needs dermatology coordination.** Extensive *Staphylococcus aureus* infection, eczema herpeticum (disseminated HSV), or rapid worsening with systemic signs is not a self-management situation.
- **Wean topical steroids slowly, under clinical supervision.** Abrupt withdrawal after long use can precipitate severe rebound flares; topical steroid withdrawal syndrome is real and can take months to resolve.
- **Pediatric thyroid dosing is a clinician's job.** Adult self-dosing of T3 has a defined approach; pediatric dosing is not the same protocol.
- **Cyproheptadine in children has dose constraints.** Lowest effective dose, evening administration, monitored for sedation. See [[cyproheptadine]].
- **Eczema herpeticum is an emergency.** Sudden onset of clustered vesicles, fever, and rapid spread on eczematous skin warrants immediate medical attention.
