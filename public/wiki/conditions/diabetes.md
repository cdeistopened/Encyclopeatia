# Diabetes

Mainstream medicine tells a simple story about diabetes: too much sugar causes high blood sugar, which damages the body. Ray Peat inverts this entirely. In his framework, diabetes is not caused by sugar but by fat—specifically, the [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] that block glucose oxidation at the cellular level. "Diabetics typically have elevated lactate, which shows that glucose doesn't have a problem getting into their cells, just getting oxidized," Peat explained in his email correspondence. The glucose is there. The cells cannot burn it. And the reason they cannot burn it traces back to a mechanism identified in 1963 that mainstream medicine has largely ignored.

## The Randle Cycle: Fat Blocks Sugar

In 1963, researcher P.J. Randle demonstrated that fatty acids compete with glucose for oxidation in cells. When free fatty acids are elevated—whether from dietary fat, stress-induced lipolysis, or stored PUFA mobilization—cells preferentially burn fat and lose their ability to oxidize glucose. The glucose accumulates in the blood: hyperglycemia.

Peat described this mechanism repeatedly throughout his work. In a [[2008-12-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cholesterol|2008 episode]], he explained: "In 1963, a researcher named P.J. Randle observed that he could block the oxidation of glucose just by feeding an increased amount of fatty acids." In a [[2010-11-01-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-dangers-of-pufas|2010 discussion of PUFA]], he stated more directly: "The immediate effect of unsaturated fats is to block the oxidation of glucose. This is called the Randle Cycle. It immediately impairs the ability to oxidize glucose, leading to diabetes-like issues."

The hospital demonstration is particularly striking. As Peat recounted in a [[2010-09-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 episode on sugar]], "About 15 minutes after injecting emulsified soy oil, people would get hyperglycemic." Intravenous fat produces an instantaneous diabetic response—not over years of dietary indiscretion, but within minutes. This is the [[Randle cycle]] in real time.

The implications are profound. If diabetes is fundamentally a problem of fat blocking glucose oxidation rather than glucose overwhelming the system, then restricting sugar while consuming fat—the standard dietary prescription for diabetics—worsens the underlying problem. And promoting polyunsaturated "heart-healthy" oils to diabetic patients accelerates the very mechanism driving their disease.

## PUFA: The Specific Toxin

Not all fats are equally diabetogenic. [[Saturated fat]] does activate the Randle cycle to some degree, but polyunsaturated fats inflict additional damage that makes their effects cumulative and progressive.

In a [[2021-09-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2021-kmud|2021 episode]], Peat explained the dual mechanism: "The long-range thing that corrects chronic diabetes is to get your polyunsaturated fats lower. The polyunsaturated fats are specifically toxic to your insulin-producing tissues, create resistance in your tissues." PUFA does not merely block glucose oxidation temporarily—it destroys the pancreatic beta cells that produce insulin and creates a chronic state of insulin resistance in peripheral tissues.

The chronicity distinguishes PUFA from saturated fat. As Peat elaborated in a [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|2011 interview]], "The Randle Cycle is the instantaneous diabetogenic effect of fatty acids. Chronically, this poisons the beta cells in the pancreas." The acute Randle effect is reversible when fatty acid levels drop. The chronic damage to beta cells from PUFA oxidation products is not.

The oxidative breakdown of PUFA generates aldehydes, free radicals, and isoprostanes that directly damage insulin-producing cells. Since these fats are stored in tissue and released during every [[stress]] response, a person with high stored PUFA reinjures their beta cells with every missed meal, every sleepless night, every emotional disturbance. The "progressive" nature of Type 2 diabetes reflects this ongoing chemical assault rather than any inherent disease trajectory.

## Sugar: The Protective Factor

Peat's defense of sugar is perhaps his most counterintuitive position on diabetes—and the one most directly supported by the Randle mechanism. If elevated fatty acids block glucose oxidation, then maintaining adequate glucose availability prevents the stress response that mobilizes those fatty acids in the first place.

"Sugar is my current understanding of what is most protective," Peat explained in a [[generative-energy-55|Generative Energy episode on bioenergetic nutrition]]. "Stress tends to increase your circulating free fatty acids, activating the Randle effect, blocking the ability to use sugar for energy." The logic is circular but robust: sugar prevents the stress that releases the fats that block sugar oxidation.

Peat's newsletter "Sugar, Stress and the Degenerative Diseases" traced this understanding from Claude Bernard's 19th-century observations through modern research, arguing that hypoglycemia—not hyperglycemia—is the primary metabolic danger. Hypoglycemic shock damages the brain, triggers cortisol-driven tissue catabolism, and mobilizes the stored PUFA that perpetuates the diabetic state.

Excess sugar in the presence of adequate saturated fat actually serves a protective function. As Peat noted in his emails, when excess sugars are consumed alongside PUFA, "they convert to saturated fatty acids—which are anti-stress, anti-inflammatory." The body's conversion of excess carbohydrate to saturated fat through de novo lipogenesis is a detoxification mechanism, replacing harmful PUFA with metabolically stable saturated fats.

This does not mean unlimited sugar consumption cures diabetes. It means that the conventional restriction of sugar while permitting or encouraging polyunsaturated fats misunderstands the disease entirely.

## The Thyroid Connection

[[Thyroid]] function is central to glucose metabolism because thyroid hormone drives every step of oxidative respiration. Without adequate T3, cells cannot oxidize glucose efficiently regardless of how much insulin is present. The result looks like diabetes—elevated blood sugar, elevated insulin, elevated lactate—but the root cause is energetic rather than hormonal in the conventional sense.

As Peat noted in his correspondence, "Diabetes usually causes some interference with the formation of the active thyroid hormone, leading to increased cortisol relative to progesterone and DHEA." The relationship is bidirectional: PUFA suppresses thyroid function (creating the metabolic environment for diabetes), and the diabetic state further impairs thyroid conversion (worsening metabolic efficiency). Breaking the cycle requires addressing thyroid function directly.

Hypothyroid patients frequently show elevated blood sugar and insulin resistance that resolves with thyroid supplementation—not because thyroid "treats diabetes" but because adequate thyroid function restores the oxidative capacity that prevents it.

## Cortisol and the Catabolic Spiral

When blood sugar drops and the Randle cycle prevents efficient glucose oxidation, [[cortisol]] rises to maintain blood sugar through gluconeogenesis—breaking down tissue proteins into amino acids and converting them to glucose. As described in the [[cortisol]] article, this catabolic process consumes muscle, bone, thymus, and other tissues.

The cortisol response worsens diabetes through multiple channels. Cortisol raises blood sugar (appearing as worsening hyperglycemia on lab tests), increases insulin resistance in peripheral tissues, and mobilizes free fatty acids from adipose stores—which, if polyunsaturated, further block glucose oxidation through the Randle cycle. The cortisol-PUFA-Randle interaction creates a spiral: each blood sugar crisis produces the conditions for the next one.

Peat's newsletter on carbon monoxide and cancer described this spiral: "The body's response to decreasing glucose is to produce larger amounts of adrenal hormones, especially cortisol. Cortisol raises the blood sugar by mobilizing amino acids from tissues and converting them to glucose, but this is at the expense of muscle and other tissue structure." In diabetes, this emergency mechanism becomes the default metabolic state.

## Cancer and Diabetes: Shared Metabolism

The connection between diabetes and [[cancer]] runs deeper than the statistical correlation. As Peat's newsletter on carcinogenic metabolism observed, "Insulin resistance produces an inability to oxidize glucose, and is associated with chronic hyperglycemia, or 'type 2 diabetes.' Diabetes of that type is associated with a high risk of cancer."

Both conditions share the same fundamental defect: impaired oxidative [[metabolism]] that shifts cells from efficient respiration to fermentative glycolysis. In diabetes, this produces lactic acid and hyperglycemia. In cancer, it produces the aerobic glycolysis that [[Otto Warburg]] identified as the defining feature of malignant cells. The difference is one of degree and tissue response, not of kind.

The elevated cortisol that accompanies diabetes also supports cancer growth by suppressing immune function and providing glucose to feed cancer cells' glycolytic metabolism. Correcting the metabolic basis of diabetes—restoring oxidative capacity through thyroid support, PUFA elimination, and adequate glucose—simultaneously addresses the metabolic environment that permits cancer.

## Ketosis: Not the Answer

The ketogenic diet's popularity for diabetes management represents, in Peat's view, a fundamental misunderstanding. As Peat noted in a [[2015-03-01-politics-and-science-politics-science-biochemical-health|2015 episode]], ketosis only occurs "under stress, starvation, diabetes, or high levels of stress." Ketosis is not an alternative fuel system that the body uses happily—it is an emergency state that the body enters when glucose oxidation has failed.

A ketogenic diet may lower blood sugar (by removing the glucose that cannot be oxidized), but it does not correct the underlying problem. The Randle cycle remains activated by the high fat intake, PUFA continues to damage beta cells if present in the diet, and the stress hormones required to maintain blood sugar through gluconeogenesis continue their catabolic effects. The lab numbers improve while the metabolic dysfunction persists or worsens.

## Gestational Diabetes: A Manufactured Category

Peat extended his critique to gestational diabetes, which he viewed as a medicalized normal variation. In a [[2020-02-01-butter-living-podcast-butter-living-podcast-fertility-and-pregnancy|2020 interview on fertility and pregnancy]], he stated: "They created Type 2 diabetes to sell insulin, and then gestational diabetes." The diagnostic thresholds for gestational diabetes have been progressively lowered, capturing more pregnant women in a diagnostic category that leads to medical interventions—including insulin administration—that may not be warranted.

During pregnancy, the body naturally shifts toward insulin resistance to preferentially direct glucose to the fetus. Labeling this adaptation as disease and treating it with insulin reflects the same conceptual error that pervades diabetes medicine: treating the glucose measurement rather than the metabolic context.

## Correcting the Metabolic Basis

Peat's approach to diabetes addresses the Randle cycle directly:

**PUFA elimination** is primary. Removing polyunsaturated fats from the diet stops the ongoing damage to beta cells and gradually reduces tissue PUFA stores. As the body's fat composition shifts toward saturated fats over the four-year replacement period, the Randle effect during stress responses diminishes.

**Adequate sugar intake** prevents the stress-hormone cascade that mobilizes stored PUFA. Orange juice, ripe fruits, and honey provide glucose that the body can use immediately, preventing the cortisol-driven catabolism and fat mobilization that worsen insulin resistance.

**[[Thyroid]] support** restores oxidative capacity so cells can actually burn the glucose available to them. Many diabetics are functionally hypothyroid, and thyroid supplementation can improve glucose tolerance directly.

**[[Progesterone]]** and **[[pregnenolone]]** oppose the cortisol-estrogen stress response that perpetuates the diabetic state.

**Frequent meals** prevent the blood sugar drops that trigger cortisol and free fatty acid release. The goal is metabolic stability—maintaining glucose availability without relying on stress hormones.

**[[Aspirin]]** blocks the inflammatory prostaglandins produced from PUFA that contribute to insulin resistance and beta cell damage.

**[[Saturated fat]]** from coconut oil, butter, and ruminant animals provides fat that does not damage beta cells, does not suppress thyroid, and does not generate inflammatory breakdown products. The Randle effect from saturated fat is temporary and reversible, unlike the cumulative damage from PUFA.

The approach is not to manage blood sugar numbers but to restore the metabolic environment in which blood sugar regulates itself—the state of efficient oxidative metabolism that polyunsaturated fats and their metabolic consequences have disrupted.
