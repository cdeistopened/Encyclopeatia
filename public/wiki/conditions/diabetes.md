# Diabetes

In Ray Peat's framework, diabetes is not a sugar-overload disease; it is a metabolic-respiratory failure expressed as glucose dysregulation. The cell cannot oxidize glucose because free fatty acids — released from PUFA-loaded fat stores under the influence of stress hormones, estrogen, and hypothyroidism — have shut down the [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|pyruvate-dehydrogenase]] step that converts pyruvate to acetyl-CoA. The blood glucose rises because cells can't absorb and burn it, not because the patient ate too much sugar. The insulin gap is secondary, derivative of beta-cell injury by the same PUFA, estrogen, endotoxin, and cortisol cascade that produces the systemic energy deficit in the first place. The conventional sugar-causes-diabetes doctrine has the etiology, treatment, and prevention all inverted. In the 1850s, when the disease was understood as a wasting syndrome, the first scientifically documented recoveries came from doctors who *added* eight to twelve ounces of sugar to their patients' daily diet — and the medical profession suppressed the finding because, as Peat noted, "describing patients recovering from an incurable disease... has usually been enough to make the medical profession antagonistic." The introduction of insulin in 1922 was followed by a 65 percent rise in diabetes mortality between 1920 and 1944. The full iatrogenic story — the 1922–1944 mortality inversion, the non-diabetic apes that developed "diabetic complications" after being given insulin, the Hammes 1996 finding that fish oil accelerates diabetic retinopathy, Broda Barnes's observation that diabetic patients given thyroid never developed the expected complications — sits at the center of this hub. Three companion articles carry the specialized loads: [[diabetes/insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome|insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome]] for the chronic clinical phenotype, [[diabetes/diabetes-complications|diabetes-complications]] for the iatrogenesis-of-treatment case, and [[diabetes-protocol|diabetes-protocol]] for the actionable clinical stack.

## What diabetes is in this framework

The central claim Peat made about diabetes for fifty years is that the hyperglycemia mainstream endocrinology calls "the disease" is the body's compensatory response to a more fundamental respiratory failure. Cells need energy. When the polyunsaturated-fatty-acid-laden body releases free fatty acids into the blood — under stress, during fasting, with estrogen, during hypothyroidism — those fatty acids block the oxidation of glucose at the level of pyruvate dehydrogenase. The cell senses an energy deficit. The brain reads the deficit as a glucose shortage and triggers the cortisol-and-adrenaline cascade that pours more glucose into the blood from liver glycogen and from cannibalized protein. Blood sugar rises. But the rising blood sugar can't be used by the tissues that are blocked from oxidizing it. The result is the textbook diabetic pattern: high glucose in the blood, low glucose inside the cell, accumulating lactate (because what the cell can metabolize, it ferments rather than oxidizes), and progressive tissue damage from chronic energy starvation. The mechanism — the substrate-switching, the PDH block, the free-fatty-acid trap — is the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] and is documented in full there. The clinical inversion that follows from it is the territory of this hub.

The lactate signal is the diagnostic key. "Diabetics typically have elevated lactate," Peat wrote in the [[diabetes|email exchange on diabetes]], "which shows that glucose doesn't have a problem getting into their cells, just getting oxidized." The cell takes up the glucose; it cannot complete the oxidation; it ferments to lactic acid; the lactate cycles back to the liver and is reconverted to glucose; blood sugar rises further. Peat: "In hyperglycemia, what you have is the body's adjustment to the inability of cells to get enough energy because they can't oxidize glucose because the fats are blocking it... To a great extent, the rising blood sugar shouldn't be fought in itself. The cause that interferes with the use of glucose should be concentrated on" ([[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|*Ask the Herb Doctor: Diabetes I* (2014-02-21)]]). This frame inverts the entire mainstream architecture: instead of lowering blood sugar with drugs that increase tissue uptake of an unusable substrate, the Peat-frame intervention removes the fatty acids that blocked the oxidation in the first place. See [[blood-sugar]] for the broader glucose-regulation cascade — cortisol, adrenaline, the liver as buffer, Selye's 300 mg% rule, the "diabetes inversion" section that this hub builds on rather than re-derives. See [[insulin]] for the substance-level treatment of insulin as an estrogen-like stressor, the hyperinsulinemia-precedes-resistance argument, and the inversion of the sugar hypothesis.

What distinguishes Peat's framework from "diabetes is insulin resistance" is the priority of the lipid environment over the hormone signal. Insulin resistance, in this reading, is not a defect of insulin receptors or a primary failure of the beta cells; it is the tissue's protective response to a chronic state of free-fatty-acid-induced metabolic blockade. Hyperinsulinemia comes first, driven by PUFA accumulation and estrogen excess; the resistance follows as a homeostatic adaptation that prevents fat synthesis from runaway insulin signaling; the eventual beta-cell exhaustion is what happens when the protective adaptation can no longer hold. The "insulin resistance is the root cause" framing inverts cause and effect.

## The disease-naming history (Budd, Piorry, and the homeopathy-vs-allopathy split)

The story of how diabetes became "the sugar disease" is, in Peat's reading, the cleanest single illustration of how medical doctrine ossifies around what an early ideology required rather than what the evidence shows. The Greek word *diabetes* means "to pass through," referring to the excessive urination that defined the syndrome; *mellitus* — "honeyed" — was added centuries later, when a physician noted that the urine tasted sweet. Until 1848, when Claude Bernard discovered that the body could convert tissue protein into sugar, the assumption was simple and naive: sugar in the urine means too much sugar in the diet. "Patients were ordered to avoid sweet foods, and in hospitals they were sometimes locked up to keep them from finding sweets," Peat wrote in [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|*Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes* (2012)]]. "The practice was derived from ideology, not from any evidence that the treatment helped." Patients continued to lose sugar in their urine until they died, despite the elimination of sugar from their diet, and the policy was still defended.

The first scientifically documented inversion came in 1857. The Parisian physician M. Piorry and the Bristol Royal Infirmary's senior physician William Budd, working independently, observed that a diabetic patient losing a pound of sugar per day in ten liters of urine was, by simple arithmetic, going to die unless that sugar was replaced. Budd prescribed eight ounces of white sugar daily, along with a normal diet including beef and beef broth, to a woman with classic wasting diabetes. Over several weeks she recovered. The amount of glucose in her urine dropped as she ate more sugar — the opposite of what the doctrine predicted. The following year Budd published a second case, a young man too weak to work, who responded to a similar regimen — eight ounces of white sugar plus four ounces of honey daily — by ceasing to lose sugar in his urine, regaining weight, and returning to strength.

The medical profession's response was rapid and revealing. A London physician, Thomas Williams, ran a two-or-three-day trial of what he claimed was a similar diet, declared his patients were getting worse, and published a sarcastic rebuttal framing Budd's work as "metaphysical" and "deluded homeopathic ideas." Budd had not framed his experiment in metaphysical or homeopathic terms; he had offered a practical observation — the patients were losing more sugar in urine than they were eating, so replacing it would at minimum slow the wasting. The rebuttal was nevertheless treated as a scientific refutation, and Budd's documented recoveries were quietly written out of the literature. As Peat put it: "Drs. Budd and Piorry described patients recovering from an incurable disease, and that has usually been enough to make the medical profession antagonistic. Even when a physician has himself diagnosed diabetes and told a patient that it would be necessary to inject insulin for the rest of his life, if that patient recovers by changing his diet, the physician will typically say that the diagnosis was wrong, because diabetes is incurable" ([[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|*Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes*]]).

The deeper structural split the Budd–Williams episode exposes is what Peat called the homeopathy-vs-allopathy frame. Homeopathic medicine — in the 19th-century usage Peat invoked, not the modern infinitesimal-dilution caricature — treated the organism's capacity to heal itself as the central object of medical practice. Fever was part of recuperation; appetite for what the body was losing was treated as informative. Allopathic medicine treated the disease entity as the enemy and the patient's idiosyncrasies as noise. The diabetic's craving for sugar was, on the homeopathic reading, useful information; on the allopathic reading, a symptom to be suppressed. Medicine consolidated almost entirely on the allopathic side. The diabetes that emerged was diabetes-as-enemy, sugar-as-cause, restriction-as-treatment — a framework refuted in 1857 but with unstoppable institutional momentum.

The disease-naming history also continued in another direction. By the 1860s and 1870s, German researchers including E. Kulz had documented that diabetic patients could assimilate fructose better than glucose. In 1864 Piorry was still treating diabetes with added sucrose; in 1867 Budd published further cases. By 1874 Kulz's fructose-tolerance finding had been independently confirmed. Sucrose, which is half glucose and half fructose, was repeatedly shown to be metabolized by diabetic patients without the elevated urinary glucose that pure starch loads produced. The fructose-therapy lineage continued sporadically into the early twentieth century. Then, in 1922, insulin was introduced — and within a decade the sugar-and-fructose therapy literature was effectively erased. As Peat noted in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues* (2012)]]: "With the discovery of insulin in 1922, fructose therapy was practically forgotten, until the 1950s when new manufacturing techniques began to make it economical to use."

## The four diabetes types — Peat's reframes

The standard textbook taxonomy is Type 1 (autoimmune beta-cell destruction), Type 2 (insulin resistance), gestational diabetes, and MODY (maturity-onset diabetes of the young, a monogenic mitochondrial subtype). Peat accepted the descriptive categories but reframed each one substantively. Each subtype gets a fuller treatment in the companion articles; this section is the taxonomic survey.

**Type 1.** The orthodox account is autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells, often triggered by a viral infection in a genetically susceptible child, leaving the patient dependent on exogenous insulin for life. Peat accepted some elements — viral injury can precipitate beta-cell damage, stress accelerates it — but inverted the autoimmune causal arrow. "For many years," he wrote in [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|*Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes*]], "physicians have been taught that diabetes is either 'genetic' or possibly caused by a viral infection, that might trigger an 'autoimmune reaction,' but the study of cellular respiration and energy metabolism and endocrinology has provided more convincing explanations. The antibodies that are found in the 'autoimmune' conditions are evidence of tissue damage, but the damage may have been done by metabolic toxins, with the immune system's involvement being primarily the removal of defective cells." The implicated metabolic toxins are PUFA membrane fragility, estrogen receptor activity on beta cells, chronic endotoxin from the gut, and lipid peroxidation products from the same fats. The dairy-protein cross-reactivity literature (the antibodies to bovine serum albumin found in some Type 1 patients) is, on Peat's reading, antigen mimicry following the loss of antibody specificity that characterizes autoimmune disease generally, not the primary insult. The clinical implication is that beta cells can regenerate. "I have known people who believed they had insulin deficiency, who recovered completely," he wrote in the [[diabetes|email exchange on diabetes]]. "The pancreas beta cells can regenerate quickly, polyunsaturated fats are continually damaging them." His father's case — severe diabetes in the 1930s, recovered after a few weeks on brewer's yeast, never relapsed — was Peat's earliest evidence point. The 1995 rabbit DHEA studies and the 2008 Jetton glucose-infusion-doubles-beta-cell-mass paper provided the modern mechanistic confirmation. Type 1 is not, in this framework, an irreversible autoimmune death sentence; it is a state of acute beta-cell injury, often induced by stress plus viral injury plus PUFA, in which regeneration is possible if the toxic environment is removed.

**Type 2.** The standard account is insulin resistance leading to compensatory hyperinsulinemia, eventually progressing to beta-cell failure. Peat's reframe inverts the order: hyperinsulinemia comes first, driven by PUFA accumulation + estrogen + endotoxin + stress; insulin resistance follows as a protective adaptation that prevents runaway fat synthesis; eventual beta-cell exhaustion occurs when the protective adaptation can no longer hold. The chronic clinical phenotype — the metabolic-syndrome cluster, the visceral obesity, the diabetic dyslipidemia, the PAI-1 hypofibrinolysis pattern — is treated in [[diabetes/insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome|insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome]]. The mechanism — substrate-switching, the FFA block of glucose oxidation, the PDH inhibition — is documented in [[randle-cycle|the Randle cycle page]] and is not re-derived here. The key inversion is the priority of the lipid environment: insulin "resistance" is rational protection when the upstream insult is PUFA-driven hyperinsulinemia.

**Gestational.** The orthodox account is that maternal insulin resistance in pregnancy produces hyperglycemia, threatening the fetus, requiring restriction-of-carbohydrate intervention and sometimes exogenous insulin. Peat treated this category as substantially manufactured. "Normal pregnancy can be considered 'diabetic' by some definitions based on blood sugar," he wrote in [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones* (2006)]]. The estrogen surge of pregnancy elevates free fatty acids, blocks glucose oxidation by the maternal tissues, and raises blood glucose — but the rising blood glucose is doing its job: passing more glucose across the placenta to the rapidly growing fetus, whose brain is glucose-hungry. "I got interested in this when I talked to a healthy 'diabetic' woman who had a two year old child whose IQ must have been over 200, judging by his spontaneous precocious hobbies. Old gynecologists told me that it was common knowledge that 'diabetic' women had intellectually precocious children." Pregnancy stabilizes blood sugar at a higher level by design; the [[progesterone]] of pregnancy favors glucose oxidation over fat oxidation; the placenta is voracious for glucose. Restricting carbohydrate in pregnancy on the basis of a 130 mg/dL postprandial reading is, on Peat's reading, doing physiologic harm to the fetus to "treat" what is in fact an adaptive maternal response. The gestational-diabetes diagnostic threshold has been progressively lowered over decades; the population of women "diagnosed" has expanded accordingly; the clinical evidence base for the resulting interventions is thin. The full treatment of how to refuse the gestational-diabetes restriction paradigm sits in [[diabetes-protocol|diabetes-protocol]] and links to [[pregnancy-nutrition]].

**MODY.** The maturity-onset-diabetes-of-the-young category is a small (~1.5% of diabetics) genetic-mitochondrial subtype in which mtDNA mutations produce impaired insulin secretion. Peat treated MODY as the subtype that proves the framework rather than refutes it. The pathomechanism, as the Gerbitz 1995 review put it, is "probably a delayed insulin secretion due to an impaired mitochondrial ATP production in consequence of the mtDNA defect." Peat's comment in [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]: "It is interesting that it is suggested to operate by way of impaired ATP production, which can be the result of so many factors, such as excess unsaturated fats, low thyroid, low magnesium, low copper, etc." That is, MODY is the diabetic state arrived at through a genetic mitochondrial defect; the other types are the same diabetic state arrived at through environmentally induced mitochondrial damage. The unifying axis is mitochondrial respiration. MODY exists as a discrete clinical category because the underlying lesion is single-gene and inherited; the rest of the diabetic taxonomy is polygenic, environmental, or both, but the final common pathway is identical.

## The iatrogenesis story (the heretical-positive-side angle)

The conventional account of insulin therapy is that it transformed diabetes from a fatal wasting disease into a manageable chronic condition. The data Peat assembled to challenge this account are blunt enough to be quoted directly. From [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]: "Insulin was introduced into medicine in the 1920s. According to the *Britannica Book of the Year* for 1947, page 265, 'Mortality from diabetes in 1920 in the United States was 16.0 per 100,000, 14,062 deaths, but in 1944, it was 26.4 per 100,000, 34,948 deaths.'" A 65 percent rise in mortality across 22 years of the institutional adoption of a treatment that was framed as life-saving. The mortality figure includes the people who would have died of acute ketoacidosis before insulin became available, which would have *lowered* the rate after 1922 if insulin were doing what it was claimed to do. Instead, the rate rose. The explanation Peat offered, drawing on multiple lines of evidence, is that insulin therapy itself produces many of the complications attributed to "the disease" — and that the diabetic population diagnosed and treated expanded faster than the population genuinely needing emergency rescue.

The most striking single piece of evidence is the non-diabetic-apes-given-insulin study. From [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|the same newsletter]]: "When non-diabetic apes were given insulin treatments, they developed some of the same 'complications of diabetes' that are seen in humans, and antibodies to insulin were found in their retinas, suggesting that some 'complications of diabetes' were complications of insulin treatment. Patients were seldom well informed of the arguments against the use of insulin, but the justification for the new genetically engineered human insulin is precisely that it avoids immunological damage." The animals that did not have the disease developed the complications when given the treatment. Either insulin itself is producing the retinal damage attributed to hyperglycemia, or chronic free-fatty-acid mobilization secondary to insulin's effect on fat metabolism is, or both. The 2006 newsletter sketches the mechanism: insulin acutely lowers blood sugar, the resulting hypoglycemia triggers a counter-regulatory adrenaline-and-cortisol surge, the surge mobilizes free fatty acids from stores into the bloodstream, and the chronic exposure of capillary endothelium and retinal tissue to PUFA-laden free fatty acids drives the slow-grade peroxidative damage that produces the "diabetic" complications. Peat noted, gesturing at the same mechanism in different language in [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|*Ask the Herb Doctor: Diabetes II* (2014-03-21)]]: "Once the tissues are unable to get glucose just from that one episode of stress-induced lack of insulin, the cells are basically dying because they can't get the glucose they need. So they call on fatty acids from stores, and the fatty acids, in turn, kill the remaining or regenerating insulin-producing cells. So it starts a vicious circle."

The most ideologically distinctive thread Peat ran is the "diabetes might have a positive side" argument. The phrase comes from the 1995 Robinson and Johnston letter in *Nature* — "Advantage of Diabetes?" — which observed that diabetic patients had lower rates of certain inflammatory conditions. Peat extended the observation by collecting parallel evidence: the Vianna and Garcialeme 1995 paper showing that diabetes reduces symptoms of asthma, and that insulin treatment makes asthma worse; the old gynecologists' clinical lore about intellectually precocious children of "diabetic" mothers; the observation that the elevated blood sugar of pregnancy serves a fetal-glucose-supply function rather than constituting a pathology to be suppressed. The point is not that diabetes is "good" — Peat did not argue that — but that the hyperglycemic state is sometimes an adaptive response to an upstream stress, and treating the response as the disease forecloses the investigation of the upstream stress. The asthma case is particularly illuminating because the same insulin that "treats" the diabetes worsens the asthma; the apparent therapeutic action of the diabetic state on asthma was destroyed by the diabetic treatment. As Peat put it in the 2006 newsletter, when teaching at the naturopathic medical school in Portland: "I tried to make it clear that 'diabetes' (a term referring to excessive urination) is a function, and that a high level of glucose in the blood or urine is also a function, and that the use of insulin should require a greater diagnostic justification than the use of aspirin for a headache does, because insulin use itself constitutes a serious health problem."

The fuller iatrogenesis case — the Hammes 1996 fish-oil-accelerates-retinopathy study, the diabetic-cardiomyopathy that mirrors Meerson's stress-injured-heart description, the diabetic complications that Broda Barnes prevented with thyroid alone, the diabetic-foot pathway driven by PUFA peroxidation rather than by glucose itself — is the territory of [[diabetes/diabetes-complications|diabetes-complications]]. The point that anchors the hub is that the iatrogenic component of modern diabetes is large enough to deserve naming as a category, and that the inversion of the 1857 sugar-as-treatment lineage has been the most consequential single error.

## Mainstream treatment critique

The Peat-frame critique of mainstream diabetes treatment is reportorial: name the agents, name what they actually do at the cellular level, name what the long-term data show. The pattern mirrors the [[cancer/cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis|cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]] structure. The companion article [[diabetes/diabetes-complications|diabetes-complications]] carries the full case; this section sketches the four-class survey.

**Insulin** acutely lowers blood glucose; chronically it is profoundly lipogenic, the hypoglycemic episodes that punctuate intensive therapy are themselves stress events that mobilize free fatty acids, and the non-diabetic-apes study points at the mechanism by which the same therapy that rescues acute decompensation produces, over years, the retinopathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular damage characteristic of "advanced diabetes." Insulin is appropriate for acute decompensation, ketoacidosis, and frank Type 1; the goal in management is the smallest dose compatible with metabolic stability while addressing the upstream PUFA, estrogen, and endotoxin load.

**Metformin** lowers blood glucose by mitochondrial Complex I inhibition — partial suppression of respiration. The cell, with respiration partially suppressed, takes up glucose to ferment to lactate, blood glucose drops, the surrogate marker improves. The cost is respiratory suppression, B12 depletion, gut-microbiome disruption, and rare lactic acidosis. Metformin is the pharmacological mimic of the underlying disease state — it lowers blood glucose by lowering oxidative metabolism, which is what PUFA already does. Fuller treatment is deferred to a future [[metformin|metformin]] page.

**GLP-1 agonists (Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro).** Peat died before the GLP-1 weight-loss boom; direct writing is limited. The mechanism — slowing gastric emptying, suppressing appetite, increasing glucose-dependent insulin secretion — produces dramatic surrogate-marker improvement; the side-effect signal (gastroparesis, pancreatitis risk, substantial lean-mass loss) is the predictable consequence of inducing a low-intake state on a stress-mediated system. Fuller treatment deferred to a future [[glp1-agonists|glp1-agonists]] page.

**SGLT2 inhibitors** lower blood glucose by causing the kidney to dump glucose into the urine — literally treating diabetes by inducing the symptom that gave the disease its name. The cardiovascular benefit in EMPA-REG and similar trials is real; the side-effect signal (euglycemic ketoacidosis, recurrent UTIs, rare Fournier's gangrene) is the predictable consequence of dumping a metabolic substrate from an already-compromised system.

The pattern across all four classes is consistent: the treatment targets the surrogate marker (blood glucose, HbA1c) rather than the upstream environment that produced it. The treatments work on the surrogate while leaving the underlying respiratory failure intact.

## Peat's interventions (overview)

The actionable clinical stack — what to do in what order, with what dose ranges, in coordination with conventional treatment — is the territory of [[diabetes-protocol|diabetes-protocol]]. The hub presents the architecture in six moves.

**1. Restore respiration.** Thyroid is the central intervention; [[broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]]'s observation that his diabetic patients never developed the expected complications because he gave them thyroid is the anchor data point. Coconut oil supplies medium-chain saturated fats that bypass the Randle block; niacinamide lowers free fatty acids; aspirin protects against the prostaglandin and lipid-peroxidation cascade; B-vitamins (thiamine, pantothenic acid, B6) support carbohydrate-oxidation enzymes.

**2. Reduce the PUFA load.** Multi-year work — adipose turnover is slow, the half-life of stored linoleic acid is two to three years. Remove dietary PUFA (seed oils, fatty fish, supplemental fish oil), substitute saturated fats, support detoxification with vitamin E and antioxidants. See [[pufa-depletion|pufa-depletion]]. The rate-limiting nature of this work is the single biggest difference between a Peat-frame recovery (years) and pharmacological "control" (immediate but symptomatic).

**3. Supply adequate sugar.** The historical literature (Budd 1857, Piorry 1857, Kulz 1874 on fructose, the Argentine researchers in the 1940s) converges with the modern mechanism on the conclusion that adequate dietary sugar is therapeutic. Preferred sources: ripe fruit, [[orange-juice|orange juice]], milk (lactose), [[sucrose|sucrose]]. The 1993 Bantle Type-II diabetic study showing metabolic equivalence between sucrose and complex carbohydrates is one modern piece; the broader case is in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues*]].

**4. Reduce estrogen** (women in particular). The female diabetes-risk amplifier; the gestational pattern; the estrogen-PUFA convergence on protein-kinase-C; estrogen elevates free fatty acids via growth hormone. See [[estrogen|estrogen]] and [[progesterone|progesterone]].

**5. Address endotoxin.** Gut bacteria on a high-starch diet produce endotoxin and nitric oxide, both of which damage beta cells and contribute to insulin resistance. Daily raw [[carrot-salad|carrot salad]], well-cooked mushrooms, restriction of fermentable starches. See [[endotoxin-reduction|endotoxin-reduction]].

**6. Restore liver glycogen capacity.** The diabetic liver is depleted of glycogen, fatty, and inefficient at converting T4 to T3. The first three moves all support liver recovery; the specific protocol is in [[liver-restoration|liver-restoration]].

The framework's clinical signature is "alongside, not instead of": never stop insulin without practitioner coordination, never discontinue metformin abruptly, monitor temperature and pulse daily to gauge metabolic recovery, watch for hypoglycemia as B1 and thyroid begin to restore glucose oxidation.

## Complications survey

The full clinical inventory — neuropathy, retinopathy, nephropathy, diabetic foot, cardiovascular disease, microalbuminuria, diabetic osteopenia, calcification of soft tissues, the diabetic-cardiomyopathy that mirrors Meerson's stress-heart, the capillary-spacing-and-tissue-hypoxia anatomical pattern — is in [[diabetes/diabetes-complications|diabetes-complications]]. The hub presents only the framing: most "diabetic complications" are, in Peat's reading, complications of insulin treatment plus chronic free-fatty-acid exposure plus the chronic hypothyroidism that develops because the diabetic liver cannot convert T4 to T3 in the absence of adequate glycogen. The non-diabetic-apes data point makes this concrete; the Broda Barnes clinical counterfactual ("no diabetic of mine ever developed the complications because I gave them thyroid") makes it actionable; the 1996 Hammes finding that omega-3 fatty acids accelerate diabetic retinopathy makes the supplement-industry advice difficult to defend on the framework's terms. The neuropathy-and-nerve-restoration sub-protocol — progesterone for its neurosteroid action, thyroid, B-vitamins, magnesium, methylene blue — is anchored in [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|the 2014 Diabetes II episode]] and is detailed in the protocol article.

## Tracking signal

The mainstream diabetic monitoring panel — HbA1c, fasting glucose, two-hour postprandial — was treated by Peat as informative but secondary. The HbA1c value reflects average blood glucose over roughly three months, but the *damage* attributed to glycation, Peat argued, is largely PUFA-derived lipid-peroxidation damage that happens to attach to the same proteins. The HbA1c is a good population-level marker of glucose exposure and a poor individual-level marker of cellular metabolic state. The Peat-preferred markers, where they overlap with mainstream diabetic monitoring and where they diverge, are:

- **Temperature and pulse** (daily, taken under standard conditions): the primary signal of metabolic state. Body temperature on waking and after breakfast, resting pulse — if these are improving, the underlying respiratory capacity is improving regardless of what the HbA1c is doing month-to-month. The pulse should be 75–85 bpm at rest in a well-fed, warm adult; temperature should be 97.8–98.6°F on waking and 98.6°F midday. See [[temperature-pulse-tracking|temperature-pulse-tracking]] (queued).
- **Fasting glucose in context**: useful as a comparison to two-hour postprandial. A high fasting glucose with a stable two-hour postprandial pattern, especially in a person with a low body temperature on waking, is often the cortisol-and-adrenaline signal rather than a true substrate-handling defect. The morning glucose rise is driven by overnight cortisol mobilizing free fatty acids and stimulating gluconeogenesis; the intervention is bedtime snacking and adequate evening calories, not metformin.
- **Two-hour postprandial glucose**: a more useful single number than fasting glucose for assessing substrate handling. A two-hour postprandial that returns to baseline within the normal range after a substantial meal of mixed carbohydrate, protein, and saturated fat is the signal that respiration is functioning. A flat curve or a sustained elevation is the signal of Randle-block dominance.
- **Vitamin D and TSH** (occasional blood tests): the minimum-viable lab monitoring panel Peat suggested in the [[diabetes|email exchange]]: "I think it's valuable to have a blood test for vitamin D to regulate the dose; TSH can be useful, too." Vitamin D is metabolically protective at adequate levels; TSH responds to thyroid supplementation; cortisol may be added when the clinical picture warrants.
- **Qualitative symptoms**: warm hands and feet (not cold extremities), sleep continuity (no 3 AM cortisol-driven wakeup), absence of stress-driven cravings, stable mood, regular bowel function. These are the daily clinical signals that the system is moving in the right direction. Peat treated them as more reliable than monthly lab numbers.

## Counter-arguments

The framework above conflicts with several mainstream and alternative-medicine positions and would be incomplete without naming them.

**Modern endocrinology orthodoxy.** The standard view treats insulin resistance as the primary lesion in Type 2 diabetes, autoimmune beta-cell destruction as the primary lesion in Type 1, and carbohydrate restriction (or carbohydrate counting and insulin matching, in intensive Type 1 management) as the central therapeutic strategy. HbA1c targets have tightened across decades: 7.0%, then 6.5%, then in some guidelines 6.0%, with cardiovascular and microvascular outcomes used as the rationale. The framework above disputes the causal arrow, the role of dietary sugar, and the safety of intensive insulin titration. The orthodox view has the weight of the diabetes-control-and-complications trials behind it; the framework's response is that the trials measure the surrogate marker against the same surrogate marker, and the long-term mortality and cardiovascular outcomes have been more equivocal than the marketing implies.

**The Lustig–Yudkin "sugar-as-toxin" school.** A subset of the popular nutrition literature — the lineage Peat traced from John Yudkin in the 1950s through Robert Lustig more recently — argues that dietary sugar, particularly fructose, is the metabolic toxin responsible for the obesity-diabetes-cardiovascular cluster. This is the modern "sugar cult" Peat critiqued at length in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues*]]. The framework's response is that the experimental literature supporting "fructose causes fatty liver" almost always adds 10% or more PUFA-rich oil to the diet and attributes the resulting damage to the sugar rather than the PUFA. Strip the PUFA from the experiment and fructose is hepatoprotective. The cultural force of the sugar-cult argument is partly its alignment with the seed-oil industry's interest in displacing blame from PUFA onto sugar.

**Carbohydrate restriction and ketogenic therapy.** A serious and growing movement argues that low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diets reverse Type 2 diabetes by lowering insulin demand and restoring "metabolic flexibility." The short-term surrogate markers improve, sometimes dramatically. The framework treats this as the temporary calming of an over-stimulated insulin-spiking system, achieved at the cost of cortisol, lost muscle, and eventual hypothyroid features. The Virta Health and DiRECT trial data are the strongest single body of evidence on the carbohydrate-restriction side; the framework's response is that the short-term success disguises long-term costs and that the underlying respiratory damage is not being addressed. The disagreement here is empirically live.

**Diabetes-as-genetic-fatalism.** The position that diabetes is overwhelmingly heritable, that genetic risk scores predict it, and that therapeutic interventions are essentially palliative. The framework's response is the Shaltout data showing that non-European Jewish populations developed diabetes rapidly on moving to Israel, the rapid expansion of diabetes among populations adopting modern industrial diets (the Pima, Indigenous Australians, the Inuit), and the historical absence of the modern diabetic phenotype in pre-industrial populations consuming whole-food sugar. Genetic susceptibility is real; the genetic-determinist conclusion is not supported by population-history data.

**Bariatric surgery school.** Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy produce dramatic remissions of Type 2 diabetes, often before significant weight loss, suggesting a gut-hormone-mediated mechanism. The framework would not dispute the empirical finding — the surgery does what it does — but would frame the mechanism as a forced reduction in calorie throughput on a damaged metabolic system, plus a probable improvement in endotoxin handling via altered gut anatomy. The cost is the surgical morbidity, the nutritional deficiencies (B12, iron, calcium), and the eating-disorder-like patterns that follow chronic restriction. Bariatric surgery is, on the framework's terms, the most aggressive possible form of carbohydrate restriction, with proportional benefits and proportional costs.

**The GLP-1 era.** The cultural moment of Ozempic, Wegovy, and Mounjaro deserves separate treatment. The mainstream framing treats these as transformative metabolic medications. The framework's preliminary view is that the dramatic short-term effects are produced by inducing a low-intake state, and the long-term costs (lean-mass loss, gastroparesis, pancreatitis risk, the still-emerging cardiovascular and bone-density data) are the predictable consequences of chronic suppression of appetite on a stress-mediated system. The protocol article addresses coordination; the substance-level analysis is deferred to a future [[glp1-agonists|glp1-agonists]] page.

## Data gaps

The framework above is built primarily on the corpus of basic-science papers Peat assembled across decades, on his clinical observations, and on the historical literature from the 1850s through the 1990s. Several genuine gaps deserve naming.

**Modern randomized controlled trial validation.** The carbohydrate-restriction school has produced substantial RCT data (Virta Health, DiRECT, several Mediterranean-diet comparison trials). The Peat-frame approach — restore respiration, deplete PUFA over years, supply adequate sugar, restore thyroid — has not been the subject of a comparable RCT program. The reasons are partly structural (no pharmaceutical sponsor, the intervention is multi-year, the outcome metrics that matter to the framework differ from the surrogate markers conventional trials measure) but the absence is real. The framework rests on mechanistic reasoning and clinical case observation more than on RCT-grade efficacy data.

**Long-term mortality data.** The HbA1c-targeting paradigm has 30+ years of mortality data behind it (ACCORD, ADVANCE, UKPDS). The Peat-frame intervention has clinical case reports, the Houssay coconut-oil-and-diabetes data, the Barnes thyroid-and-diabetes-complications observation, but no population-cohort mortality comparison.

**Pediatric Type 1 specifically.** The framework's claim that beta cells can regenerate is strongest in adult-onset cases (Peat's father, the rabbit DHEA studies, the Jetton glucose-infusion mass-doubling study). Pediatric Type 1, where the autoimmune destruction is typically more aggressive and the loss more complete by the time of diagnosis, is a thinner part of the corpus. The protocol article treats pediatric Type 1 conservatively: support, never substitute for, pediatric endocrinology.

**The modern continuous-glucose-monitor era.** Peat's writing largely predates the wide availability of CGMs. The framework's tracking signal (temperature, pulse, qualitative symptoms, two-hour postprandial) is compatible with CGM data but does not depend on it. The CGM era has produced a new quantified-self literature on diabetes that is partly aligned with the framework (postprandial spike management, glycemic-variability minimization) and partly orthogonal. A future [[cgm-and-temperature-tracking|cgm-and-temperature-tracking]] page would address this.

**The microbiome literature.** The rapidly developing understanding of gut microbiome dynamics in metabolic disease overlaps with the framework's endotoxin story but extends it in ways Peat did not fully address. Specific microbiome interventions (precision-prebiotic strategies, low-FODMAP variants, fermented-food protocols) are areas where the framework offers general guidance but where the modern literature is moving rapidly.

## Safety framing

The Peat framework treats diabetes as a metabolic-respiratory disease whose long-term reversal involves substantial dietary and supplemental change. It is adjunctive, not a replacement, for diabetes management. The specific cautions:

- **Insulin-dependent diabetes (Type 1, late-stage Type 2):** Do not stop insulin. Coordination with an endocrinologist is non-negotiable. As thyroid and B-vitamin support begin to restore glucose oxidation, insulin requirements may decrease — sometimes substantially — and unmonitored dose adjustment risks hypoglycemia. The protocol article details the coordination workflow.
- **Pregnancy:** Reframing gestational diabetes does not mean ignoring genuinely high glucose readings in pregnancy. It means assessing whether the threshold-based "diagnosis" reflects a real pathology or an adaptive maternal response. Coordination with the obstetric team is required.
- **Pediatric diabetes:** Pediatric Type 1 should be managed primarily by pediatric endocrinology. Supportive measures (PUFA depletion over time, adequate sugar within total carbohydrate counting, vitamin D and thyroid evaluation) can be added but never substituted.
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis:** Acute decompensation is a medical emergency. The framework's interventions are preventive and chronic; they are not appropriate substitutes for emergency care.
- **Bariatric-surgery patients:** The framework's emphasis on adequate calorie and sugar intake conflicts with post-bariatric guidance. Specific coordination with the surgical team is required.
- **Severe diabetic complications:** Late-stage retinopathy, nephropathy approaching dialysis, advanced diabetic foot disease — these require their respective specialist teams. Framework interventions can be added but cannot reverse advanced organ damage on their own.

The framework's claim is that the upstream metabolic environment can be repaired, that the disease's trajectory can be bent, and that many of the complications attributed to "the disease itself" are tractable to addressing the PUFA, estrogen, endotoxin, and thyroid load that drives them. It is not a claim that insulin can be stopped, that endocrinologists can be bypassed, or that conventional emergency medicine can be ignored.
