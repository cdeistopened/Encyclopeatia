# Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD)

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is the most common chronic liver condition in the industrial world. The standard estimate puts prevalence at roughly 25% of adults globally and 30% to 40% in the United States, with rates among the obese and the diabetic running closer to 70%. The disease has tracked, decade for decade, the rise of seed-oil consumption, the introduction of the low-fat dietary guidelines, the spread of high-fructose-corn-syrup beverages, and the diagnostic broadening of "metabolic syndrome." Mainstream hepatology prescribes the dominant intervention package — lose weight, restrict calories, cut sugar, exercise, and increasingly adopt a ketogenic diet — and the package has not produced population-level reversal. Ray Peat spent four decades arguing that it never would, because it leaves the actual driver in the kitchen.

The Peat framework starts in a different place. Fatty liver is the predictable storage problem of a liver that has been handed, year after year, a fat mix it cannot oxidize cleanly (polyunsaturated fats from corn, soy, safflower, and canola oils), in a hormonal environment that suppresses oxidation (estrogen excess, hypothyroidism, chronic cortisol), with a gut that is constantly leaking endotoxin into the portal vein. The triglycerides accumulating in hepatocytes are a symptom, not the disease. The disease is the substrate switch — the cell running on free fatty acids instead of glucose, in the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] sense — and the lipid-storage finding is the visible end of a longer chain.

## What NAFLD Is — And What Comes Next

The clinical entity has a spectrum. Simple hepatic steatosis — triglycerides in more than 5% of hepatocytes — is the earliest form, usually asymptomatic and often discovered incidentally on ultrasound or elevated liver enzymes. Non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) adds inflammation, hepatocellular ballooning, and the first stages of fibrosis. About 20% to 30% of steatosis patients progress to NASH, and about 20% of NASH patients progress to advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis over fifteen to twenty years. NASH-cirrhosis is now the fastest-growing indication for liver transplant in the United States.

The progression is striking for two reasons. The first is that the histology of late NAFLD is indistinguishable from alcoholic steatohepatitis — fatty change, ballooning hepatocytes, Mallory-Denk bodies, pericellular fibrosis. Peat in [[liver-diseased|*Email Topic: Liver (Diseased)*]]: "Alcohol shifts metabolism away from oxidation, and that metabolic shift is responsible for aneurysms as well as fatty liver." Whatever alcohol does to the liver, the modern diet is doing to the non-drinking population. The second is that every stage tracks the same upstream substrate dysregulation. The Wanless and Lentz autopsy study Peat cited in [[food-junk-and-some-mystery-ailments-fatigue-alzheimer-s|*Food, Junk, and Some Mystery Ailments*]] put it directly: "this study supports the hypothesis that fatty acids have a role in the hepatocellular necrosis found in some obese individuals." Steatohepatitis after jejunoileal bypass surgery — which floods the portal vein with free fatty acids from rapidly mobilizing adipose — is histologically identical to alcoholic disease. The mechanism is the same: free fatty acid load on a liver that cannot oxidize them cleanly.

The grade matters clinically because early forms are reversible and late forms are not. A liver without bridging fibrosis can clear stored triglyceride within months on a corrected diet. A cirrhotic liver cannot. The Peat-intervention window is widest in steatosis, narrower in NASH, narrow in F2–F3 fibrosis, and effectively closed in F4 cirrhosis — at which point the protocol retains palliative value but cannot regrow hepatocytes. Early diagnosis is the most consequential one.

## Why Fructose Got Blamed

The mainstream literature has spent two decades building a case against fructose as the primary driver of NAFLD. Robert Lustig's *Fat Chance* and his 2009 "Sugar: The Bitter Truth" lecture made the public version of the argument: fructose is metabolized exclusively by the liver, bypasses leptin signaling, lipogenically converts to fat, and is "alcohol without the buzz." The argument has academic respectability. It also has an experimental record that, on Peat's reading, was constructed by feeding rats fructose alongside large amounts of polyunsaturated oil and attributing the resulting hepatic damage to the sugar.

Peat traced the substitution in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues* (2012)]]: "The 'high fructose' diets consistently add 10% or more corn oil or other highly unsaturated fat to the diet. These large quantities of PUFA aren't necessary to prevent a deficiency, but they are needed to obscure the beneficial effects of fructose." Only 0.6% of calories as PUFA prevents essential-fatty-acid deficiency; the experiments use seventeen times that amount. The rats develop fatty liver, the papers report that fructose caused it, the polyunsaturated oil in the same diet — the actual lipid being stored — receives no attention. The lipid composition of the steatotic liver in these animals is dominated by linoleic acid and its peroxidation products, not by de-novo-lipogenesis-derived saturated fats. The fat in the liver is dietary PUFA, repackaged.

Human evidence runs in the same direction. The studies that purport to show fructose causing NAFLD are almost universally done with sugar-sweetened beverages on a background diet that derives 7% to 10% of calories from seed oils. When researchers isolate fructose from PUFA, the harmful effects vanish. Peat cited a 300-calorie drink experiment comparing glucose, fructose, and orange juice: "Those receiving the glucose had a large increase in oxidative and inflammatory stress (reactive oxygen species, and NF-kappaB binding), and those changes were absent in those receiving the fructose or orange juice." On [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|the first sugar-myths episode]] Peat pushed the inversion: "For many years people have been showing that fructose detoxifies many things that enter the liver, including alcohol. It can almost double the rate of destruction of alcohol and prevent liver damage in the process." See [[fructose]] for the Randle-cycle bypass, the glycogen-replenishment role, and the redox-restoring properties.

The practical implication: the soda-and-packaged-food matrix matters more than the sugar molecule within it. A glass of fresh orange juice with breakfast is in a different category from a can of Coke drunk with a basket of vegetable-oil-fried fries. The fructose camp identified a real population effect — sugar-sweetened beverages correlate with NAFLD — but mis-located the causal agent.

## Peat's Causal Cluster

Peat's account of fatty liver is multifactorial in the same sense that his account of every degenerative disease is multifactorial: a primary lipid driver, an oxidative-metabolism failure, an endocrine cluster, a gut component, and a stress-axis component, all reinforcing each other. The cluster has six pillars.

**PUFA accumulation, the primary lipid driver.** The triglycerides accumulating in NAFLD livers are heavily enriched in linoleic acid (omega-6) and its peroxidation products. Adipose-tissue PUFA content has approximately tripled in the United States since 1960, tracking the displacement of dairy fat and tallow by liquid vegetable oils. The liver, drawing on this adipose pool when it mobilizes fat, receives a PUFA-rich substrate it cannot oxidize cleanly; the peroxidation products (4-HNE, MDA, F2-isoprostanes) are inflammatory and cytotoxic. See [[pufa]] for the mechanism. NAFLD is, in a literal compositional sense, a PUFA-storage disease.

**Hypothyroidism, the oxidative shutdown.** A hypothyroid liver cannot oxidize what it stores. Peat in [[liver-diseased|*Email Topic: Liver (Diseased)*]]: "Low thyroid function and vitamin D deficiency are important factors in the development of fatty liver." Thyroid hormone drives mitochondrial respiration, fatty-acid beta-oxidation, and the deiodinase enzymes that convert T4 to active T3 in the liver itself. Most NAFLD patients have subclinical hypothyroidism by Peat's diagnostic criteria — body temperature in the 97s, resting pulse below 70, afternoon energy crash — even when TSH is "normal." See [[hypothyroidism]] for the diagnostic disconnect.

**Estrogen excess.** Estrogen elevates growth hormone, which elevates free fatty acids, which feed hepatic substrate-switching. Estrogen also impairs bile flow, slows estrogen-glucuronidation, and recirculates conjugated estrogen back into the bloodstream via deconjugation by gut bacteria. The loop runs both ways: high estrogen burdens the liver, and a burdened liver fails to clear estrogen. NAFLD is markedly more common in postmenopausal women on conventional hormone replacement, and women with PCOS have NAFLD rates approaching 50%. See [[estrogen]] and [[estrogen-detox]].

**Gut endotoxin, the constant inflammatory load.** Lipopolysaccharide from gram-negative gut bacteria enters portal circulation continuously — more so in patients with constipation, dysbiosis, or compromised intestinal barriers. The liver processes it through Kupffer cells, which respond with TNF-α and IL-6; chronic endotoxin load is one of the best-documented amplifiers of progression from simple steatosis to NASH. See [[endotoxin]] for the mechanism and [[endotoxin-reduction]] for the levers (raw carrot salad, regular bowel movements, charcoal when indicated, well-cooked starches over raw fiber).

**Low protein quality, the gelatin deficit.** A liver short on glycine cannot keep up with Phase II conjugation (glycine conjugation, glutathione synthesis) that disposes of hormones, drugs, and bacterial metabolites. The standard American protein pattern — muscle meat without bone broth, chicken breast without skin or connective tissue — leaves the liver glycine-limited. See [[gelatin]].

**Chronic cortisol, the stress-axis driver.** Cortisol drives gluconeogenesis, suppresses thyroid, mobilizes adipose free fatty acids, and depletes liver glycogen. The chronic-stress phenotype Peat described in [[insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome|*Insulin Resistance and Metabolic Syndrome*]] is the underlying state that drives NAFLD in most patients: hypothyroid, estrogen-elevated, FFA-mobilized, glycogen-depleted, hyperinsulinemic. The interventions that lower chronic cortisol — adequate carbohydrate, sufficient salt, glycine, light, warmth, sleep — are the same interventions that lower the substrate load on the liver.

The six pillars are not independent risk factors. They reinforce each other in a loop that runs for years before clinical NAFLD presents and that continues running unless multiple pillars are addressed simultaneously.

## The Peat Protocol

The full sequenced rebuild lives in [[liver-restoration]] — five phases (reduce load, glycogen restoration, cofactor supplementation, active restoration, maintenance) on a multi-month timeline. The high-leverage moves specific to NAFLD:

**PUFA depletion is the single most important intervention, over years.** Stop new PUFA intake — every seed oil, every restaurant fried food, every salad dressing made with vegetable oil, every fish oil capsule. Cook in butter, coconut oil, beef tallow, or olive oil. Then wait. The existing PUFA in adipose tissue has a tissue half-life of approximately two years and full clearance of roughly four. NAFLD reversal is not a six-week program; it is a four-year rebuild of the body's lipid composition. Peat in [[liver-diseased|*Email Topic: Liver (Diseased)*]]: "Eliminating all PUFA would be the most important thing." This is the floor; without it, nothing else works.

**Thyroid restoration, adequate sugar, gelatin, coffee, aspirin.** Body temperature into the 98s; resting pulse 75 to 85; three meals plus three snacks daily pairing carbohydrate with protein; the fructose-and-glucose mix from ripe fruit, orange juice, milk, and honey as the preferred substrate; 10 to 30 grams of gelatin daily for Phase II glycine; two to four cups of coffee with cream and sugar; baby aspirin daily with food. See [[liver-restoration]] for the dose detail, the sequencing, and the contingencies; see [[thyroid-dosing]] for when dietary correction alone is insufficient.

The result Peat expected from this protocol, applied consistently for six to twelve months: liver enzymes trending toward the lower half of the reference range; body temperature in the 98s; afternoon energy stable; 3 AM wake-ups gone; weight rebalancing toward lean mass without conscious caloric restriction. Imaging (ultrasound, MRI-PDFF, fibroscan) lags the biochemistry by months and typically shows steatosis reduction at the six-to-twelve-month mark in adherent patients.

## Why Mainstream "Low-Fat" and "Low-Carb" Both Fail

The two dominant mainstream prescriptions for NAFLD are diametric opposites. Both fail in the same place.

**The low-fat / Mediterranean prescription.** The AASLD, EASL, and most national hepatology bodies recommend caloric restriction, weight loss, exercise, and a "Mediterranean" pattern emphasizing olive oil, fish, nuts, and whole grains. The pattern works modestly for patients who actually lose substantial weight — perhaps 30% see meaningful steatosis reduction at 12 months — but does nothing about the underlying PUFA composition of the diet. Nuts and seeds, fish oil, soybean oil (the dominant "vegetable oil" in the United States and increasingly substituted into Mediterranean cooking as olive oil has become expensive), and whole grains all deliver linoleic acid. The patient who switches from butter to soybean-oil margarine on physician advice is increasing rather than decreasing the PUFA load. Population-level NAFLD prevalence has continued to rise through the entire forty-year period these guidelines have been in force.

**The ketogenic / low-carbohydrate prescription.** Cut carbohydrate aggressively, induce ketosis, force the liver to oxidize fat instead of store it. The short-term results are striking — weight loss in the first three to six months, dropping liver enzymes, often steatosis improvement on imaging. The intervention works because it removes the carbohydrate-and-insulin axis from the substrate equation. The problem is that the fat being mobilized from adipose tissue, and the fat being consumed on a typical ketogenic diet (bacon, butter, cheese, eggs, plus the omnipresent nuts, seeds, avocado, and "keto-friendly" packaged products), is heavily contaminated with stored PUFA. The liver is being asked to process, under glycogen depletion and stress-hormone elevation, the same polyunsaturated fats that put it into trouble. The short-term steatosis improvement masks a longer-term oxidative cost; a meaningful fraction of long-term ketogenic patients develop elevated cholesterol, hair loss, menstrual disruption, and thyroid suppression — the chronic-stress phenotype the diet itself induced.

Both diets share a conceptual failure. They treat fatty liver as a problem of *energy balance* when Peat's framework treats it as a problem of *substrate quality and oxidative capacity*. The body in NAFLD is not eating "too much"; it is eating the wrong fat, in a hormonal environment that cannot oxidize it, with a gut feeding inflammatory triggers into the portal vein. Neither low-fat nor low-carb addresses any of those three.

## The Coffee Finding

The single piece of dietary evidence on which mainstream hepatology and Peat's framework cleanly agree is the protective effect of coffee. The meta-analytic record is unusually consistent: heavy coffee drinkers (three or more cups daily) have approximately 20% to 30% lower NAFLD prevalence, 40% lower NASH progression rates, 40% lower rates of cirrhosis, and roughly 50% lower rates of hepatocellular carcinoma compared to non-drinkers. The dose-response curve is monotonic up to four to five cups, and the effect holds across populations, decades, and study designs.

Mainstream hepatologists accept the finding but treat the mechanism as obscure. Peat's framework predicts it directly. Coffee raises metabolic rate (thyroid-mimetic effects of caffeine and polyphenols), induces Phase II detoxification enzymes, stimulates bile flow (bitter compounds activating bile receptors), reduces oxidative stress, and lowers iron absorption from the same meal. The 2-to-4-cup-daily target lines up with the epidemiological dose-response. Peat's instruction to drink it with cream and sugar — rather than black on an empty stomach — addresses the only meaningful side effect (the cortisol bump from black coffee in a low-glycogen state) while preserving the hepatoprotection. See [[coffee]] for the full treatment.

## Tracking Signals

The protocol works when several signals improve together. Tracking only one is misleading.

- **Liver enzymes.** ALT, AST, and GGT trending toward the lower half of the reference range over 8 to 16 weeks. GGT is the most sensitive single marker — a GGT in the high 20s or low 30s in a non-drinker is a yellow flag even within "normal" range.
- **Fibroscan or MRI-PDFF.** Imaging measure of hepatic fat fraction and stiffness. Steatosis lags biochemistry by three to six months. F1 and F2 fibrosis can regress with sustained intervention; F3 and F4 typically do not.
- **Body temperature.** Morning oral temperature drifting up 0.5°F to 1°F over the first eight to twelve weeks and settling in the high 98s in maintenance. Sustained temperatures below 97.8°F indicate persistent hypothyroidism downstream of an under-glycogenated liver.
- **Energy stability and sleep.** No afternoon crash, no caffeine dependency, no 3 AM wake-up. Each maps onto a specific liver function (mid-day glycogen reserves, hepatic gluconeogenesis at rest, overnight glycogen sufficiency).
- **Resting pulse.** Settling in the high 70s to mid 80s. Lower indicates persistent hypothyroidism; higher indicates ongoing adrenaline stress.

## What to AVOID

The list mirrors the failure modes in [[liver-restoration]] §"Failure Modes," compressed for the NAFLD-specific case.

**Statins, when an alternative is possible.** Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase — the same enzyme upstream of CoQ10, pregnenolone, progesterone, vitamin D, and bile acid synthesis. The liver in NAFLD is already struggling at these functions; suppressing them pharmaceutically rarely improves the underlying biology, and the long-term consequences (muscle wasting, statin myopathy, increased diabetes risk) are well-documented. The conditions statins are prescribed for in NAFLD patients are usually addressable with diet, aspirin, and thyroid restoration. See [[heart-disease]] for the broader statin critique.

**Fish oil supplements, absolutely.** Concentrated EPA/DHA capsules are the highest-density PUFA dose in the modern supplement industry. They oxidize in the capsule, in the gut, and in the bloodstream. The RCT literature on fish oil for NAFLD is mixed-to-negative at clinically relevant endpoints. Peat was uniformly skeptical; the omega-3-deficiency framing is, on his reading, downstream of the same PUFA-vs-PUFA confounding that produced the case against fructose.

**Prolonged fasting and intermittent fasting attempts.** Each missed meal depletes liver glycogen, triggers gluconeogenesis, mobilizes adipose free fatty acids (including the stored PUFA) into a liver that cannot keep up, and forces stress-hormone-driven substrate switching. Peat: "If you slow down the liver's activity — which happens in a fast — your brain and organs are exposed to chemicals diffusing out of fat and muscles. The best cleansing diet is one that increases your liver's action. Tasty food, sugar, and thyroid activate the liver." Three meals plus three snacks; mandatory bedtime snack.

**Alcohol on a regular basis.** During active reversal, none. In maintenance, occasional wine with food. The interaction with stored PUFA is especially destructive: "If a person eats polyunsaturated fats, alcohol is more likely to cause oxidative reactions between iron and the fats" ([[alcohol]]).

**"Liver cleanse" supplement stacks.** Milk thistle, NAC, glutathione precursors in isolation, dandelion root, detox teas. The mechanism Peat documented is upstream of the conjugation pathways these supplements target; without addressing the energy state and substrate quality, isolated cofactors are delivered into a system that cannot use them.

## Counter-arguments

**The Lustig / fructose-toxicity camp.** Lustig and the broader anti-sugar movement in pediatric hepatology argue that fructose drives NAFLD through de novo lipogenesis, uric acid elevation, and direct hepatic insulin resistance. The argument has real experimental support — fructose at high doses, in isolation, does drive lipogenesis. The Peat response, developed in [[fructose]], is that the experimental record systematically confounds fructose with PUFA and that the human population effect tracks the soda-and-packaged-food matrix, not the molecule. Partial agreement: HFCS beverages of the standard American diet are harmful. Sharp disagreement: the harm is in the matrix, not the sugar.

**The ketogenic-diet-for-NAFLD camp.** Westman, Phinney, and the Virta team argue that fatty liver is carbohydrate-driven and that ketosis is the direct intervention. The short-term biochemistry and imaging often improve dramatically. The Peat response: the improvement is real but comes at the cost of mobilizing stored PUFA into a glycogen-depleted liver, suppressing thyroid, and inducing the chronic-stress phenotype the body has been protecting itself from. Five-year outcome data does not yet exist at scale; the Peat prediction is that long-term adherence produces thyroid suppression and rebound steatosis on relaxation. Empirically testable on the decade timescale.

**The "statin defends the liver" position.** Some hepatologists argue statins are net protective in NAFLD because their lipid-lowering reduces substrate for hepatic re-esterification. A handful of trials show modest benefit. The Peat response: the same effect can be achieved with diet, aspirin, and thyroid restoration, without the steroidogenic-pathway suppression and CoQ10 depletion. Where coronary disease is established and statin therapy is medically indicated, the lower dose suffices; where the alternative is the Peat protocol, statins are unnecessary.

**The exercise-and-weight-loss consensus.** The strongest single mainstream finding in NAFLD: patients who lose 7% to 10% of body weight reliably improve steatosis. The Peat framework accommodates this — weight loss in the right hormonal environment (saturated fat replacing PUFA, sufficient sugar, restored thyroid) preferentially mobilizes visceral fat. The disagreement is about method. Mainstream guidance recommends caloric restriction; Peat would say the weight loss that matters is the kind that occurs spontaneously when the metabolism is restored, not the kind forced by chronic underfeeding that itself drives the cortisol-FFA cycle.

## Data Gaps

**The PUFA-depletion timeline in humans.** The two-year half-life and four-year full-clearance figures come from adipose-tissue biopsies in the 1960s through 1980s; modern data are sparser. Individual variation (lean mass, basal metabolic rate, ongoing exposure) likely produces a wider range than the canonical numbers suggest.

**Hepatocyte regeneration at intermediate fibrosis stages.** Whether F2 and early F3 fibrosis reliably reverses on the Peat protocol has not been studied in formal clinical trials. Case-report-level evidence in the Peat-aligned community suggests it does; the absence of randomized data is a genuine gap.

**Pediatric NAFLD.** The rapidly growing incidence of NAFLD in children is one of the most alarming trends in modern hepatology. The Peat framework predicts the pediatric form is the early end of the same disease, but the protocol's application in growing children — particularly thyroid dosing — has not been worked out in detail.

**The endotoxin-quantitative-threshold question.** How much portal endotoxin is "too much"? The literature has shifted to dose-response markers (lipopolysaccharide-binding protein, soluble CD14, anti-LPS antibodies), but the Peat-aligned interventions have not been quantitatively benchmarked against these markers in NAFLD specifically.

**Long-term statin-and-fatty-liver interaction.** Whether long-term statin therapy in NAFLD patients accelerates or retards fibrosis progression remains genuinely contested; the trial evidence is too short-term to settle it.
