# Stress

Stress, in mainstream medicine, is treated as a psychological experience—something managed with deep breathing and meditation. Ray Peat's framework redefines it as something far more concrete: a measurable metabolic event in which the organism's energy production fails to meet the demands placed on it. "Stress is when the environment puts demands on you that you don't have the internal resources to meet smoothly," Peat explained in a [[2019-04-27-jodellefit-jodellefit-stress-and-your-health|2019 interview]]. This definition strips away the vagueness. A skipped meal is stress. A cold room is stress. An argument, an infection, a night of poor sleep—each produces the same cascade of hormonal and metabolic events, differing only in degree.

## Selye's Framework: Shock, Adaptation, Exhaustion

Hans Selye's research in the 1930s and 40s gave stress its modern scientific meaning. His General Adaptation Syndrome described three phases: the initial shock response, a period of adaptation, and finally exhaustion—the point at which the organism's defenses collapse. As Peat recounted in a [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|2011 interview]], "In Selye's studies, he never talked about adrenal fatigue; 'exhaustion' was his word. When you stress an animal terribly, it enters the shock phase, then the adaptation phase, and finally the exhaustion phase where the animal would die from stress."

Peat built on Selye's observations while correcting what he saw as a critical error. Selye had proposed that organisms are born with a finite supply of "adaptive energy" that stress depletes—a kind of biological bank account drawn down over a lifetime. Peat rejected this deterministic framing. In a [[2011-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-endotoxins-stress|2011 episode on serotonin and stress]], he argued: "The only mistake Hans Selye made was talking about a limited ability to adapt because we are born with a certain amount of 'adaptive energy.' I don't think there is such a thing." What Selye interpreted as depleted energy, Peat reframed as accumulated damage—primarily from [[PUFA]], [[estrogen]], and the inflammatory mediators produced during each stress response. The body does not run out of energy; it becomes poisoned by the byproducts of inadequate energy production.

This distinction matters practically. If adaptive energy is fixed, decline is inevitable. If the problem is accumulated toxins and metabolic suppression, the process can be reversed by removing the causes and restoring efficient [[metabolism]].

## The Metabolic Definition of Stress

At the cellular level, stress is always the same event: a shift from oxidative to fermentative metabolism. A cell that cannot produce enough ATP through mitochondrial respiration switches to emergency glycolysis, producing [[lactic acid]] instead of carbon dioxide. This is the metabolic signature of stress, whether triggered by low blood sugar, oxygen deprivation, cold exposure, emotional distress, or physical injury.

The shift is not neutral. Lactic acid production acidifies tissues, promotes edema, and when chronic, damages blood vessels—particularly in the brain. [[Cortisol]], [[adrenaline]], and [[serotonin]] all rise to mobilize emergency energy, but the mobilization comes at the cost of tissue integrity. Cortisol breaks down muscle to provide amino acids for gluconeogenesis. Adrenaline liberates stored fatty acids, which—if they are polyunsaturated—further suppress [[thyroid]] function and block glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]].

As Peat described in a newsletter on adaptogens, saturated fats protect against "the damage that normally results from stress-induced fatty acid oxidation," while polyunsaturated fats cause cardiac necrosis and tissue destruction when mobilized during stress. The fat you have stored determines how much damage each stress response inflicts.

## Estrogen as Stress

One of Selye's lesser-known contributions was recognizing that [[estrogen]] itself mimics the stress response. As Peat noted, "Selye was the first to propose that estrogen imitates a shock reaction." This observation connects stress physiology to the broader pattern of degenerative [[aging]].

Estrogen creates cellular stress by the same mechanisms as oxygen deprivation: it impairs cytochrome oxidase, shifts cells toward glycolysis, increases water uptake, and activates excitatory neurotransmitters while blocking inhibitory ones. A tissue exposed to excess estrogen is a tissue under chronic stress—regardless of the organism's psychological state.

This explains why women experience more stress-related conditions during periods of estrogen dominance (premenstrual syndrome, postpartum depression, perimenopausal anxiety) and why men develop stress-related diseases as their testosterone-to-estrogen ratio declines with age. The hormonal environment determines the threshold at which ordinary demands become pathological stress.

## The Progesterone Shield

Perhaps the most striking evidence for Peat's metabolic view of stress comes from Selye's own experiments with pregnant rats. As Peat's newsletter "Money, Progesterone, and Life" recounted, Selye discovered that pregnant rats whose adrenal glands had been removed survived stresses that killed non-pregnant adrenalectomized animals. The placenta's production of [[progesterone]] was sufficient to replace adrenal function entirely.

"Suspecting that it was progesterone produced by the placenta that made the difference," Peat wrote, "he found that the adrenalectomized rats could live a normal, stress-resistant life span, if they received a regular progesterone supplement." This experiment demonstrated that progesterone is not merely a reproductive hormone but a fundamental anti-stress substance—one that supports oxidative metabolism, stabilizes cell membranes, and prevents the excitatory damage that defines the stress response.

The practical implication is that a person's vulnerability to stress is not fixed by personality or willpower but by hormonal status. Adequate progesterone, thyroid hormone, and pregnenolone raise the threshold at which demands become stress. As Peat explained in a [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 episode on stress and trauma]], "If you're really healthy, you can meet challenges without experiencing something that Hans Selye would have called stress."

## The Stress Hormones: A Cascade, Not a Switch

The stress response involves a sequence of hormonal changes, each with distinct consequences:

**[[Adrenaline]]** responds first, mobilizing glucose from glycogen stores and liberating free fatty acids from adipose tissue. This acute response is appropriate and brief in a healthy organism with adequate glycogen reserves. But when glycogen is depleted—from fasting, low-carbohydrate diets, or chronic stress—adrenaline draws increasingly on stored fat, flooding the bloodstream with [[free fatty acids]] that suppress thyroid function and block glucose oxidation.

**[[Cortisol]]** follows when the acute response proves insufficient. It breaks down tissue proteins—primarily muscle—to provide amino acids for glucose synthesis. As Peat noted in his email correspondence, "Food proteins stimulate insulin secretion, and to prevent hypoglycemia cortisol is increased. The food proteins, along with tissue proteins, can be used for energy under the influence of cortisol." Chronic cortisol elevation is catabolic in the strictest sense: the body consumes itself to maintain blood sugar.

**[[Serotonin]]** and **[[prolactin]]** rise with sustained stress, promoting [[inflammation]], vascular permeability, and the fibrotic tissue changes that characterize chronic stress damage. Far from the "feel-good" neurotransmitter of popular imagination, serotonin is an integral part of the stress-inflammatory cascade.

**[[Estrogen]]** increases through stress-activated aromatase, which converts androgens to estrogen in every stressed tissue. This local estrogen production amplifies the metabolic failure that caused the stress, creating a self-reinforcing cycle: stress produces estrogen, estrogen impairs respiration, impaired respiration produces more stress.

## Why Skipping Meals Is Serious

A seemingly minor daily stress—going without food for extended periods—illustrates how the cascade operates. When blood sugar falls, the body must mobilize its reserves. In a person with adequate glycogen and low stored PUFA, this mobilization is orderly and brief. In a person with depleted glycogen and high tissue PUFA—which describes most adults on modern diets—the response involves cortisol-driven tissue breakdown and the liberation of thyroid-suppressing, inflammation-promoting polyunsaturated fats.

As Peat described, "the complete changeover takes about four years," referring to the time needed to replace stored PUFA with saturated fats. Until that replacement occurs, every period of fasting or intense exercise releases stored poisons into circulation. This explains why intermittent fasting and intense exercise—promoted as health interventions—can worsen the condition of metabolically compromised individuals. The stress they impose is real, measurable, and cumulative.

The protective strategy is straightforward: frequent meals containing sugar and protein prevent the stress-hormone cascade from activating. "If you eat frequently and thoroughly avoid stress-causing foods—don't let yourself get hungry enough to call on stress hormones to liberate fat—you can very quickly shift over to efficient metabolism," Peat explained.

## Altitude, Oxygen, and the Stress Threshold

Peat's discussion of altitude reveals how environmental factors modulate stress independently of psychological perception. In a [[2019-09-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-education-and-reeducation|2019 episode]], he explained that high oxygen pressure at sea level can itself create oxidative stress, while moderate altitude allows the retention of carbon dioxide that supports efficient oxidative processes. Populations living at moderate altitude consistently show lower rates of cancer, heart disease, and other stress-related degenerative conditions.

This connects to the broader principle: stress is not about what you experience but about whether your energy systems can handle what you experience. Carbon dioxide, produced by efficient oxidative metabolism, is itself protective—it improves oxygen delivery to tissues, stabilizes cells against excitation, and opposes many of the inflammatory effects of the stress cascade.

## Reversing the Stress Pattern

If stress is metabolic failure, the solution is metabolic restoration:

**[[Thyroid]] support** is foundational because thyroid hormone drives every aspect of oxidative metabolism. Without adequate thyroid function, the body cannot produce enough energy to meet even modest demands, making ordinary life stressful. Peat noted in his emails that "thyroid is needed for the adrenals to function well, and adequate cholesterol, as raw material."

**[[Progesterone]]** and **[[pregnenolone]]** raise the stress threshold directly. As Peat explained, "thyroid hormone, vitamin A, and cholesterol support the formation of pregnenolone, and the well nourished body is able to make large adjustments in these, to minimize the need for cortisol." The popular concept of "pregnenolone steal"—where the body supposedly diverts pregnenolone to cortisol under stress—reflects a failure of production, not a zero-sum competition.

**PUFA elimination** reduces the damage inflicted by each stress response. When stored fat is predominantly saturated, the fatty acids mobilized during stress do not suppress thyroid or generate inflammatory breakdown products.

**Frequent feeding with sugar and protein** prevents the cortisol-driven catabolic cascade from initiating. The goal is to maintain stable blood sugar without relying on stress hormones.

**[[Aspirin]]** blocks the inflammatory prostaglandins generated from PUFA during stress, providing acute protection while the longer-term process of tissue PUFA elimination continues.

**Light and warmth** support metabolic rate directly. Cold and darkness are stressors that increase demand on the thyroid-adrenal system.

The aim is not to avoid all challenges—Peat never advocated a stress-free existence—but to build a metabolic foundation robust enough that ordinary demands can be met through efficient energy production rather than emergency hormone mobilization. When oxidative metabolism is adequate, challenges become stimulation rather than stress.
