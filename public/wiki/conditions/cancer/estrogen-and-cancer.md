# Estrogen and Cancer

*Mainstream oncology treats hormonal cancers (breast, ovarian, uterine, endometrial) with surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, and anti-estrogen drugs (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors, GnRH agonists), and frames synthetic progestins as carcinogenic alongside bioidentical progesterone. This article reconstructs Peat's inversion — that unopposed estrogen is the proximate driver, that bioidentical progesterone is anti-cancer at the mitochondrial level, and that the progesterone-as-carcinogen classification reflects regulatory capture rather than the underlying science. This page is a synthesis of Peat's framework, not medical advice; women with active or suspected hormonal cancer should not modify or discontinue conventional treatment based on what is written here, and any hormonal intervention belongs in coordination with a clinician.*

The hormonal axis of the [[cancer]] story, in Ray Peat's reading, is one phenotype expressed in four tissues. Breast, ovarian, uterine, and endometrial cancer are not separate diseases that happen to share a hormonal risk factor; they are the local manifestations in differently estrogen-sensitive tissues of a single underlying defect — the loss of [[respiration|oxidative respiration]] capacity, the metabolic shift to the [[warburg-effect|Warburg state]], and the failure of [[progesterone]] synthesis that accompanies that shift. Estrogen is the proximate driver because estrogen is uniquely good at activating the [[mitochondria|mitochondrial]] transhydrogenases that pull a cell into glycolysis under stress, and progesterone is its natural antagonist because progesterone is what healthy mitochondria make when they have spare respiratory capacity. The political history that surrounds this physiology — Wyeth's *Premarin* franchise, Robert Wilson's *Feminine Forever*, the 2002 Women's Health Initiative reversal, the IARC and California Proposition 65 misclassification of progesterone as a carcinogen, the RU486 paradox of an anti-progesterone drug marketed simultaneously as an abortifacient and a cancer treatment — is not separable from the science. It is the reason most physicians do not understand what they are looking at when they read a hormonal cancer chart. This article assembles the science and the political history together. The substance-level cases live at [[estrogen]] and [[progesterone]]; the broader cancer frame lives at [[cancer]]; the regulatory-capture template lives at [[regulatory-capture]]. What lives here is the unified hormonal-cancer thesis and its history.

## The hormonal cancer thesis

Peat's claim, repeated across fifty years of writing, is that prolonged unopposed estrogen exposure is the single best-documented carcinogenic state in the human female. The evidence is layered. Animal studies from the 1930s through the 1970s demonstrated repeatedly that continuous estrogen exposure produces cancer in every species tested — rodents, dogs, primates — and that progesterone given continuously alongside the estrogen prevents or reverses the tumorigenesis. Epidemiology mapped the same pattern onto human populations: women with naturally high estrogen and low progesterone (long anovulatory follicular phases, polycystic ovaries, obesity-driven aromatase activity, delayed first pregnancy) had higher cancer incidence in the breast, ovary, uterus, and endometrium. The 2002 Women's Health Initiative trial — abruptly terminated when the harm signal exceeded the stopping rules — confirmed in a randomized prospective cohort what the animal and epidemiological data had been showing for sixty years: women given estrogen plus a synthetic progestin had higher incidence of breast cancer, stroke, blood clots, heart attacks, and dementia than women on placebo. In the three years after the WHI report, U.S. hormone-replacement use fell, and breast-cancer incidence fell 7% in parallel ([[estrogen-progesterone-and-cancer-conflicts-of-interest-in-regulation-and-product-promotion|*Estrogen, Progesterone, and Cancer* (2007)]]).

The mainstream account treats estrogen-receptor-positive (ER+) cancer as a special subset and tries to reconcile estrogen-as-mitogen with the broader genomic story by treating estrogen as one of many growth signals a cancer cell may or may not depend on. The Peat-frame inversion is that *all* of the major female cancers are estrogen-driven, that the receptor doctrine itself was an industry-funded artifact built on a single Elwood Jensen experiment that has since been refuted, and that the female cancer triad needs to be read as a single hormonal phenotype playing out in different tissues with different latencies. Peat made the prediction explicit in a 2015 transcript: "With increased, uninterrupted exposure to estrogen, the sequence tends to go: first cancer of the uterus, then cancer of the breast, then of the lungs, kidneys, intestine, liver, and brain."

The unifying observation that connects the four hormonal cancers, beyond shared epidemiology, is that progesterone protects against all four. The animal literature from the 1940s and 1950s — Lipschütz on guinea pigs, the National Cancer Institute's 1951 trial of injected progesterone in cervical-cancer patients (eleven of seventeen women showed visible tumor regression after thirty-one to sixty-five days of treatment) — established this at clinical scale, and was then buried by a synthetic-progestin marketing campaign that rebranded progesterone as obsolete ([[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone* (2007)]]). The animal and clinical evidence has accumulated continuously since. The political reasons it has not been integrated into mainstream oncology are the subject of the second half of this article.

## Why progesterone is anti-cancer at the mitochondrial level

The standard pharmacological account of progesterone's anti-cancer action runs through receptor-mediated transcription: progesterone binds the progesterone receptor, the receptor binds DNA, and gene expression shifts toward differentiation and away from proliferation. That account is correct but incomplete. Peat's framework adds a deeper layer: progesterone is what a healthy [[mitochondria|mitochondrion]] makes when it is respiring well, and the molecular cascade by which it is synthesized depends on intact oxidative capacity at every step. Progesterone deficiency is not, in this reading, a primary endocrine disorder. It is a *symptom of mitochondrial-respiratory failure* — the same defect that produces the [[warburg-effect|Warburg shift]] toward glycolysis, that drives the lactate-prostaglandin-fibrosis cascade described at [[lactic-acid]], and that defines the cancer state.

The biosynthetic chain runs as follows. Mitochondrial cholesterol is converted to [[pregnenolone]] by the side-chain-cleavage enzyme (CYP11A1, P450scc), an oxidative enzyme that requires electrons from NADPH, oxygen, and the iron-sulfur cluster ferredoxin to function. The reaction takes place on the inner mitochondrial membrane. Pregnenolone is then converted to [[progesterone]] by 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, another oxidative enzyme. Every step of this chain — cholesterol synthesis from acetyl-CoA, transport of cholesterol into the mitochondrion, hydroxylation, dehydrogenation — costs reducing power, costs electrons, costs ATP, costs oxygen, and depends on the integrity of the same inner mitochondrial membrane whose cardiolipin is being substituted with [[pufa|polyunsaturated]] fatty acids in the aging cell. When the mitochondrion is failing — when cardiolipin is peroxidizing, when cytochrome oxidase is being inhibited by [[iron]] excess, when [[serotonin]] and [[cortisol]] are blocking complex IV — the cell cannot complete the pregnenolone-to-progesterone cascade. The mitochondrion shifts to lactate production for ATP. Progesterone synthesis collapses. Estrogen, which is synthesized by a different enzyme (aromatase) that thrives in the inflamed and PUFA-rich tissue field, rises. The progesterone-to-estrogen ratio collapses. The cell loses the antagonist that normally terminates the proliferation signal.

This is why Peat wrote that "in the ovary, the enzymes that synthesize cholesterol, along with the production of progesterone, are activated by the pituitary hormone, FSH, but also by estrogen. In the liver and uterus and vascular endothelium, which aren't specialized for the production of progesterone, stimulation by estrogen activates the enzymes to increase the formation of cholesterol. When cells are injured or seriously stressed, instead of being able to directly recover their normal quiescence, they may instead mobilize their systems for growing and replicating, to replace damaged or destroyed cells" ([[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone*]]). The progesterone in a healthy ovary is the marker of intact respiration. The lack of progesterone in an aging or stressed ovary is the marker that respiration has failed. In tissues that do not normally produce progesterone — breast epithelium, uterine endometrium, brain — local mitochondrial collapse manifests differently (cell-cycle dysregulation, fibrosis, dedifferentiation) but the underlying lesion is the same.

The clinical corollary is that progesterone supplementation is *not* a hormone replacement in the conventional sense. It is a substrate-side intervention that bypasses a failed biosynthetic step. This also explains Peat's preference for [[pregnenolone]] supplementation in older patients — a tissue that can still complete the pregnenolone-to-progesterone step (most can, even when CYP11A1 activity has fallen) will produce its own progesterone from supplemented pregnenolone in the right tissue-specific amounts. The conventional endocrinology of "progesterone replacement" almost always delivers a synthetic *progestin* (medroxyprogesterone acetate, norethindrone, levonorgestrel) that opposes most of progesterone's effects. The deeper thesis follows directly: the respiratory defect that produces cancer is the same defect that produces progesterone deficiency. Restoring respiration — [[thyroid]], [[pufa|PUFA]] depletion, iron control, CO₂ elevation, sugar, light — restores progesterone synthesis as a downstream consequence. This is why the [[cancer-protocol]] is essentially the same as the [[aging]] protocol.

## The transhydrogenase mechanism

The specific biochemistry by which estrogen produces the cancer phenotype is the transhydrogenase mechanism, which Peat returned to repeatedly because it is the molecular event that ties estrogen directly to the Warburg shift. The transhydrogenase enzymes — particularly the nicotinamide-nucleotide transhydrogenase on the inner mitochondrial membrane and the pyridine-nucleotide transhydrogenases in the cytosol — catalyze hydrogen transfer between NAD/NADH and NADP/NADPH pools. Their function, in healthy tissue, is to allow cells to shift metabolic energy between the glycolytic and the oxidative compartments depending on substrate availability and oxygen tension. Their biological role is most pronounced in hypoxia and in rapidly growing tissue: they let a cell continue growth and repair processes when oxygen is short.

Estrogen is uniquely good at activating these enzymes. The 1957 paper by Reynafarje and Potter compared transhydrogenase activity in rat liver and the Novikoff hepatoma and found that the hepatoma had dramatically elevated activity ([[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]]). Hershey's 1958 paper specifically identified an estradiol-sensitive transhydrogenase in normal and malignant breast tissue. Karavolas, Orr, and Engel's 1969 work on placental 17β-estradiol dehydrogenase showed that estradiol itself participates directly in hydrogen transfer reactions — that the steroid molecule is not a passive ligand on a "receptor" but an active participant in the redox chemistry of the cell. Peat summarized the mechanism: "The transhydrogenases, or the transhydrogenase function of the steroid dehydrogenases, which shift metabolic energy between glycolytic and oxidative systems, have been shown to explain these effects of estrogen, but the transhydrogenases can be activated by many stressors. The biological function of the transhydrogenases seems to be to allow cells to continue growth and repair processes in a hypoxic environment. Estrogen can start the process by creating new pathways for electrons, and will promote processes that are started by something else, and progesterone is estrogen's natural antagonist, terminating the process" ([[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]]).

This explains why estrogen is uniquely cancer-promoting compared to other growth signals. Other mitogens drive proliferation but do not directly reshape the cell's energy metabolism. Estrogen does both. Within minutes of exposure, an estrogen-treated tissue takes up water, alkalinizes, begins to synthesize fat, and starts producing lactic acid; the swelling activates the stress-response cascades injured cells activate; the cell finds itself in the Warburg state without ever having been formally "transformed." Estrogen, in this reading, is a carcinogen not because it causes mutations but because it pulls a cell into the metabolic configuration of cancer.

The Elwood Jensen "estrogen receptor" story exists in opposition to this picture. Jensen's 1961 isotope-labeling experiments at the Atomic Energy Commission's facilities — too expensive for anyone outside that small group to replicate — claimed that estradiol did not undergo chemical change during hormonal action and therefore could only act through static binding to a receptor protein. The claim was specifically intended to refute the transhydrogenase literature. As Peat noted, Jensen's "decisive and historically crucial experiment was false" — many subsequent experiments showed that practically every tissue interconverts estradiol and estrone in the normal course of hormonal action — but by the 1970s the marginalization of the transhydrogenase researchers was complete, and the receptor-doctrine textbooks that resulted have not been corrected ([[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone*]]). Drugs designed to block the estrogen receptor (tamoxifen, raloxifene, aromatase inhibitors) operate downstream of the metabolic event that drives the cancer; their partial efficacy in ER+ disease and failure in triple-negative disease is consistent with the Peat-frame reading.

## The female cancer triad

The unified frame is that breast, ovarian, uterine, and endometrial cancer are one disease expressed in four tissues. Each tissue has its own distinctive expression because its baseline hormonal environment, its progenitor-cell population, and its mechanical-and-stromal milieu differ, but the underlying lesion — chronic unopposed estrogen against a background of mitochondrial-respiratory decline and progesterone deficiency — is the same.

**Breast cancer.** The most-documented case. The evidence in [[breast-cancer|*Breast Cancer* (2006)]]: 1930s–40s animal work showing continuous estrogen produces breast tumors; the post-1940 incidence rise tracking estrogen prescribing, polyunsaturated cooking oils, and atmospheric radiation; the breast-density-as-collagen story; prenatal imprinting; and the strongest single signal in the modern literature, that mastectomy outcome is dramatically worse when surgery is performed in the follicular (estrogen-high, progesterone-low) phase than in the luteal phase (Lemon and Rodriguez-Sierra 1996, Senie and Kinne 1994, Gnant et al., Rageth et al.). The 2002 WHI reversal and the 7% post-WHI decline is the cleanest population-level confirmation. The progesterone-deficiency case has its own epidemiological signal: Cowan et al. (1981) found that women with documented endogenous progesterone deficiency had 5.4 times the risk of premenopausal breast cancer and a 10-fold increase in all-cause cancer mortality compared to non-hormonally-infertile controls.

**Ovarian cancer.** [[a-physiological-approach-to-ovarian-cancer|*A Physiological Approach to Ovarian Cancer* (2010)]] frames ovarian tumors as driven by chronic over-stimulation by pituitary gonadotrophins (FSH, LH), which rise at menopause because the hypothalamic neurons that normally restrain them have been killed or desensitized by past estrogen exposure. The experimental evidence: ovaries transplanted into the spleen (where secretions are liver-inactivated, leaving the pituitary unrestrained) produce tumors; irradiated ovaries produce tumors only when the contralateral ovary is also removed (raising gonadotrophins); women with many pregnancies or on ovulation-suppressing contraceptives have lower incidence; nuns have elevated incidence in all three female cancers. Progesterone reduces ovarian-tumor proliferation in animal models. The prevention strategy is to suppress the menopausal gonadotrophin rise through PUFA depletion, thyroid restoration, and progesterone supplementation.

**Uterine and endometrial cancer.** The cleanest case for the progesterone-as-protective story. Endometrium is where the estrogen-progesterone ratio governs cell-cycle behavior most visibly: estrogen drives follicular proliferation, progesterone induces luteal secretory transformation, and absence of the progesterone signal (anovulatory cycles, PCOS, perimenopause) leaves the endometrium continuously proliferative — hyperplasia, then adenocarcinoma. The NCI's 1951 cervical-cancer study (250 mg/day intramuscular progesterone, eleven of seventeen women showing regression in 31–65 days, [[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone*]]) and the Leonetti-Wilson-Anasti 2003 endometrial-antiproliferation study are the cleanest clinical evidence. The mainstream prescription for endometrial hyperplasia — give a progestin to drive shedding — is a partial acknowledgment of the same physiology.

The triad as a single phenotype is visible in three ways. The cancers cluster within individual women beyond what independent risk would predict. The risk factors converge (nulliparity, delayed first pregnancy, early menarche, late menopause, obesity-driven aromatase, exogenous estrogen, progesterone deficiency all elevate risk for all four). The protective factors converge (pregnancy, lactation, oophorectomy, progesterone supplementation all reduce risk for all four). Conventional epidemiology has to re-derive the same risk-factor list for each cancer; the Peat-frame reading just states the underlying physiology once.

## The HRT political history

The political history of hormone-replacement therapy is the spine of the [[estrogen-progesterone-and-cancer-conflicts-of-interest-in-regulation-and-product-promotion|*Estrogen, Progesterone, and Cancer* (2007)]] newsletter. Carla Rothenberg's 2005 Harvard Law paper traced the history in detail; Peat used Rothenberg as the spine of his own account.

The story begins in the 1930s, when synthetic estrogens (DES, conjugated equine estrogens) became commercially available. Animal studies through that decade and the 1940s demonstrated that estrogen caused tumors in every species tested. By 1941, the estrogen industry — led by what would become Wyeth-Ayerst — had launched a concerted public-relations and journal-placement campaign to shift the discourse from toxicity toward the framing of estrogen as a deficiency state requiring replacement. Rothenberg documented a 1940s conspiracy among industry executives, regulators, and medical-journal editors to control the framing of FDA approval. Estrogen was approved, the toxicity evidence was buried, and the foundation of the "hormone replacement" framework was laid.

The mid-century inflection point was Robert A. Wilson's 1966 book *Feminine Forever*, written with industry funding, which framed menopause as a "deficiency disease." Wilson promised that women on lifelong estrogen would not age, would not lose their sexuality, would not develop osteoporosis, would not develop dementia. By the late 1970s, Premarin was one of the most-prescribed drugs in the United States. The 1975 NEJM papers demonstrating elevated endometrial-cancer risk in unopposed-estrogen users forced the industry to add a synthetic progestin (medroxyprogesterone acetate, MPA — patentable, and pharmacologically not progesterone) to the regimen, creating combined estrogen-plus-progestin therapy (Prempro). The framing was that MPA "protected" against the endometrial cancer that estrogen caused — which obscures the larger truth that estrogen causes the cancer because the body's natural protective hormone is progesterone, not MPA.

The Women's Health Initiative is the second inflection point. Launched in 1991, the WHI's estrogen-plus-progestin arm was halted in 2002 ahead of schedule because the harm signal exceeded the stopping rule: women on Prempro had significantly elevated incidence of breast cancer, stroke, blood clots, heart attacks, and dementia compared to placebo. The estrogen-alone arm (in hysterectomized women) was continued and showed a weaker but qualitatively similar harm profile. Within three years of the report, U.S. hormone-replacement prescriptions had fallen by roughly half, and breast-cancer incidence had fallen 7%.

The industry response was three sequential maneuvers. First, blame the progestin: attribute Prempro's harm to "the progesterone" rather than the estrogen, exploiting the fact that the synthetic MPA shares a name-class with natural progesterone despite opposite pharmacology. Second, rehabilitate "estrogen alone" as safe in hysterectomized women, citing the weaker harm signal in that WHI arm as if it were a positive result. Third, distinguish "bad" Premarin (conjugated equine estrogens) from "good" bioidentical estradiol or estriol and promise that low-dose bioidentical estrogen does not cause cancer. The "good estrogen versus bad estrogen" distinction is not supported by the cancer biology — estrogen is estrogen at the transhydrogenase level — but it has revived the HRT market substantially since 2010. The pattern is that the industry has, at every inflection point, recovered roughly half the market it lost to the previous decade's harm revelations. The broader template is at [[regulatory-capture]].

## The "estrogen industry blames progesterone" maneuver

The 2007 newsletter's spine is one specific instance of this pattern: the systematic effort, beginning in the late 1990s and accelerating after WHI 2002, to reassign the cancer-causing effects of estrogen to progesterone. Peat described the maneuver with characteristic directness: "While Donald Rumsfeld answered the question, 'where are the weapons of mass destruction?' by saying 'We know where they are. They're in the area around Tikrit and Baghdad and east, west, south and north somewhat,' the estrogen industry responds to the evidence that estrogen causes breast cancer, strokes, heart attacks, blood clots and Alzheimer's disease by saying 'it's progesterone that is responsible'" ([[estrogen-progesterone-and-cancer-conflicts-of-interest-in-regulation-and-product-promotion|*Estrogen, Progesterone, and Cancer*]]).

The maneuver has three components. The first is rhetorical: collapse the distinction between natural progesterone and synthetic progestins (MPA, norethindrone, levonorgestrel) by using "progesterone/progestin/progestogen" interchangeably. This works because the synthetics were branded as "progestins" to imply they *are* progesterone, and because the WHI used MPA the harm signal can be rhetorically transferred to natural progesterone by anyone not attending to molecular details. The synthetics have largely opposite pharmacology — many are mildly estrogenic, androgenic, or glucocorticoid; a few (mifepristone/RU486) are directly anti-progesterone. The IARC 1987 monograph carefully distinguished MPA from progesterone in its sub-sections but collapsed the distinction in its final "Progestins are possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B)" line, which has been cited ever since as evidence that progesterone is carcinogenic.

The second component is the cherry-picked-study tactic. The IARC and NTP cite a small set of studies — Burrows and Hoch-Ligeti 1946, Jones and Bern 1977, Rebout and Pageaut 1973, Capel-Edwards 1973, Glucksmann and Cherry 1968, the 1979 Upjohn beagle study — each of which on examination is methodologically broken. Burrows-Hoch-Ligeti used intermittent injection in peanut oil (already known to be ineffective at preventing tumors). Jones-Bern injected newborn mice with 0.02 mL of sesame oil daily for five days (equivalent on a body-weight basis to injecting an adult human with more than a quart of sesame oil daily); the effects observed were the foreign-body-and-vegetable-oil response. The 1979 Upjohn beagle study used an undisclosed "aqueous vehicle" (cyclodextrins, themselves carcinogenic); 28 of 140 dogs died of unreported causes during the four-year run, none of cancer, and the eight authors all left the field. Rebout-Pageaut applied methylcholanthrene directly to the cervix in mice given a progesterone pellet of undisclosed composition with no vehicle control. Each study demonstrates either nothing or the carcinogenicity of the *vehicle*.

The third component is the suppression of contrary evidence. Hundreds of studies have demonstrated progesterone's anticancer effects. OEHHA considered fewer than 1% of the progesterone-and-cancer literature for its listing decision, while it considered roughly 50% of the saccharin-and-cancer literature for its 2002 saccharin-delisting decision. The asymmetry is trivial to explain on political-economic grounds — the saccharin manufacturers wanted delisting, the estrogen industry wanted progesterone listed — and the regulatory apparatus complied in both cases. Peat's diagnosis: "If the evidence showing that progesterone prevents or cures cancer could be weighed against the evidence purporting to show that it is carcinogenic, I think it would be clear that something like a cultural-commercial misogyny has been at work" ([[estrogen-progesterone-and-cancer-conflicts-of-interest-in-regulation-and-product-promotion|*Estrogen, Progesterone, and Cancer*]]).

## California Prop 65 + IARC + NTP misclassification

California's Proposition 65, formally the Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986, requires warning labels on products containing substances that the State of California "knows" to cause cancer. The substance list is maintained by the Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA) and its Carcinogen Identification Committee. Progesterone appears on the Prop 65 list. So do many products containing progesterone — natural progesterone creams, cosmetics, supplements. The listing is sourced to two outside authorities, the U.S. National Toxicology Program (NTP) and the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). The NTP cites IARC as its primary source. IARC cites a small group of methodologically deficient studies (described in the section above) and folds progesterone into its "progestins are possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B)" category without distinguishing the natural hormone from the synthetic analogs.

Peat described the OEHHA listing process in detail in the 2007 newsletter. The relevant committee meeting included testimony from one outside expert (Richard Edgren) who argued progesterone was not carcinogenic; he was given seven minutes during which six or seven other substances were also being considered. The chairman appeared confused about whether the carcinogenicity evidence applied to progesterone or to the synthetic medroxyprogesterone acetate, and the committee's decision appears to have turned on a single mention of "metastasis" in connection with beagle dogs that had been given synthetic progestins, not progesterone. The only biologist on the committee, Dr. Spangler, expressed open dissatisfaction with the evidence: "In one place, IARC says there is limited evidence; in another place, it says there is sufficient evidence. And NTP says there is sufficient evidence. And they cite as their sufficient evidence a variety of very convoluted experimental procedures, in which mouse mammary tumor virus positive mice were used in a study; and, in addition to that, some other carcinogen, some other potent carcinogen, was applied at the same time or after… It was just very confusing." The committee voted to list progesterone anyway.

The composition of the OEHHA Carcinogen Identification Committee at the time of the listing is the political-economic punchline. Three of the committee members were USC Keck School of Medicine professors who had co-authored papers with Malcolm Pike, the South African statistician who had spent the prior two decades arguing — against the prior fifty years of progesterone-protection literature — that progesterone was the primary cause of breast cancer. Pike controlled Balance Pharmaceuticals, Inc., a company developing a contraceptive based on progesterone suppression. Another committee member, Juliet Singh, was the chief executive of Trans Pharma Corporation, a company developing transdermal drug-delivery systems for hormones; under California's law, chemicals on the carcinogen list may be sold as drugs without a warning, so listing progesterone created a competitive moat for hormones sold through drug channels (which Trans Pharma serviced) against hormones sold through cosmetics and supplement channels (which compounding pharmacies serviced). The committee, Peat noted in the 2007 newsletter, "would look like a tainted jury" in a courtroom setting.

The downstream consequences have been substantial. California's Prop 65 warnings have caused many over-the-counter progesterone cream products to be pulled from the market or relabeled with carcinogen warnings; the warnings have driven women back to pharmaceutical prescriptions (almost universally for synthetic progestins, not natural progesterone) issued by physicians who have been trained by industry CME material to view progesterone with suspicion. The IARC and NTP classifications are cited globally; the same misclassification echoes through European, Australian, and other national regulatory frameworks. The 2007 Peat newsletter, the 2007 *Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone* newsletter, and Carla Rothenberg's 2005 Harvard Law paper remain the most thoroughly documented public refutations of this misclassification; they have had limited effect on the regulatory consensus because the political-economic asymmetry has not changed.

## RU486 and the anti-progesterone paradox

RU486 (mifepristone) is the diagnostic case for understanding what the industry actually believes about progesterone. RU486 is an antiprogesterone — its medical action is to block the progesterone receptor, mimicking progesterone deficiency. It is marketed for two indications: as an abortifacient (first-trimester induced abortion depends on disrupting the progesterone signal that maintains pregnancy) and as an experimental breast-cancer treatment ([[ru486-cancer-estrogen-and-progesterone|*RU486, Cancer, Estrogen, and Progesterone*]]).

The paradox is immediate. If progesterone causes cancer (the industry's post-WHI position), blocking progesterone should treat cancer. If progesterone prevents cancer (the prior fifty years of research), blocking progesterone should *cause* cancer. RU486 is being marketed simultaneously as an antiprogesterone and a cancer treatment, and the industry literature does not notice the contradiction. Peat's diagnosis: the industry has not concluded that progesterone causes cancer; it has concluded that progesterone is a competitor and must be discredited by whichever mechanism the regulatory regime will approve.

The experimental rationale for RU486 in breast cancer came from genetically engineered mice (BRCA1-knockout, p53-knockout) with elevated breast-tumor incidence that the authors attributed to progesterone signaling. Peat's reading is that these mice represent an extreme estrogen-excess-plus-progesterone-deficiency phenotype — BRCA1 deletion increases estrogen signaling, p53 deletion eliminates a key proliferation check — and the resulting tumors are exactly what fifty years of estrogen-excess literature would predict. To call them "progesterone-driven" because the progesterone receptor remains intact in the residual epithelium is to misread the physiology. The episode also bridges to the abortion-politics layer: natural progesterone supplementation has prevented miscarriage in progesterone-deficient women since the 1930s; RU486 terminates pregnancy by blocking the same signal. The same molecule can be marketed simultaneously to terminate pregnancy and to prevent breast cancer because the underlying business position is that progesterone suppression is profitable, and the medical rationale is whatever the regulatory market will buy.

## Prenatal exposure and adult cancer

One of Peat's distinctive epidemiological claims is that adult cancer is substantially programmed by prenatal hormonal and nutritional exposures — the responsiveness of adult tissues to adult-life triggers (estrogen, PUFA, radiation) is set during fetal development by the mother's hormonal and nutritional environment.

The DES (diethylstilbestrol) story is the cleanest experimental confirmation. DES was prescribed to pregnant women from 1940 to 1971 to prevent miscarriage (a claim never supported by evidence but supported by marketing). Daughters of DES-exposed pregnancies developed clear-cell adenocarcinoma of the vagina — a previously vanishingly rare cancer — at rates that forced the drug off the market. Adult ovarian, breast, and uterine cancer rates in DES daughters are elevated decades after exposure. DES sons show elevated testicular cancer and feminization of male reproductive-tract development ([[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]]). The mechanism: prenatal estrogen imprints the developing tissues with a permanently elevated estrogen-sensitive baseline, and cancers manifest decades later when adult-life triggers push the imprinted tissue past threshold.

The soy-isoflavone analog is the same mechanism with longer latency. Genistein and daidzein cross the placenta; infants fed soy formula in the formula-feeding decades received body-weight-equivalent phytoestrogen doses comparable to birth-control pills. Maternal PUFA load (high seed-oil consumption during pregnancy and lactation) elevates offspring cancer risk through cardiolipin substitution in the fetal mitochondrial chain — the offspring inherits a respiratory chain pre-disposed to fail under stress. Peat cited the 1927 Bernstein-Elias work showing that rats on fat-free diets had almost no spontaneous cancer; the modern version tracks twentieth-century seed-oil consumption against rising cancer incidence.

The deeper point is leverage. The cancer-prevention intervention with the largest expected effect size — larger than any pharmaceutical regimen — is the modification of maternal diet and hormonal environment during pregnancy and lactation. The same intervention layer (PUFA depletion, thyroid restoration, adequate progesterone, avoidance of exogenous estrogens, sugar over starch, saturated fat) is the [[cancer-protocol|cancer-prevention protocol]] for adults; the difference is the timescale on which it acts. The [[aging-ovaries-not-the-eggs|*Aging Ovaries, Not the Eggs*]] article extends the same framework to fertility decline: the same prenatal-imprinting axis that drives cancer drives reproductive aging.

## Counter-arguments

**The receptor-doctrine orthodoxy.** Modern endocrinology textbooks describe estrogen action exclusively through receptor-mediated genomic and non-genomic signaling; the transhydrogenase literature is absent from medical-school curricula. The mainstream defense is that the receptor doctrine has been productive — it yielded tamoxifen, the aromatase inhibitors, the SERM class, and the ER+/ER- clinical distinction. The Peat-frame response is that the doctrine has been *partially* productive in a narrow range of ER+ disease while broader cancer biology has stagnated (population-level cancer mortality has barely moved in fifty years), and that the doctrine cannot accommodate the transhydrogenase, redox-shift, and metabolic-respiratory data. The two frameworks make different predictions; the population-level data favors the Peat-frame predictions.

**The synthetic-versus-bioidentical distinction.** The current gynecology position is that WHI harm was specific to conjugated equine estrogens plus MPA, and that "bioidentical" estradiol patches plus bioidentical progesterone are not subject to the same harm. The Peat-frame counter is that estrogen is estrogen — the transhydrogenase mechanism does not distinguish bioidentical estradiol from conjugated equine estrogens. Where the framing has a kernel is the substitution of natural progesterone for synthetic progestins, which is genuinely safer; the kernel then gets generalized to estrogen and the protective effect is lost.

**The ER+ targeted-therapy successes.** Tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors, and the SERMs/SERDs have improved recurrence-free survival in ER+ disease. The Peat-frame reading acknowledges these as partial wins consistent with the framework — they all work by reducing estrogen signaling, which is what the framework predicts should help. They are downstream of the mitochondrial defect and do not restore respiration or raise progesterone, but they reduce the cancer-promoting signal at one step. The successes are not a refutation; they are a partial validation in a narrow target tissue.

**Pregnancy-after-breast-cancer protocols.** Mainstream oncology has historically counseled breast-cancer survivors against pregnancy on the assumption that any hormonal stimulation could reawaken dormant cells. The prospective cohort data has consistently failed to support this — survivors who become pregnant do not show elevated recurrence, and several studies suggest *reduced* recurrence. The Peat-frame reading is that pregnancy is the longest sustained high-progesterone period in a woman's life, and the breast tissue is bathed in protective progesterone for nine months; the data fits the progesterone-as-protective thesis cleanly.

**Raloxifene and the SERMs.** Raloxifene has both estrogenic (bone, lipid) and antiestrogenic (breast) effects and reduces breast-cancer incidence in postmenopausal women. The Peat-frame reading is that raloxifene behaves as a partial estrogen antagonist in breast tissue, which supports rather than refutes the estrogen-as-carcinogen thesis. Whether SERMs can substitute for the dual protective effects of natural progesterone (bone, brain, breast, vascular) is a separate question Peat answered no on, but the cancer-reduction effect itself is consistent with the broader frame.

## Data gaps

Several specific gaps remain. Peat's corpus does not incorporate the post-2015 non-genomic-estrogen-signaling literature, which has re-opened the receptor-versus-transhydrogenase debate under different terminology. Clinical-dose data for natural progesterone in active cancer treatment is thin — the 1951 NCI cervical-cancer study (250 mg/day intramuscular, eleven of seventeen women regressing) remains the most-cited human evidence, and no randomized trial of natural progesterone in adjuvant breast-cancer treatment has been conducted. The prenatal soy-isoflavone mechanism is less well-characterized than the DES mechanism, and the mainstream literature is split between treating soy as protective (Asian low-incidence cohorts) and carcinogenic (prenatal-imprinting framework). The status of "estrogen alone" bioidentical estradiol in cancer-prone populations is genuinely uncertain; the post-2010 observational cohorts are not yet large enough to settle it, and the political-economic obstacles to a definitive trial are substantial.
