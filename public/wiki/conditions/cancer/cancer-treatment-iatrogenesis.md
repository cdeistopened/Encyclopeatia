Ray Peat's case against standard oncology rests on a single observation: the three pillars of conventional treatment — surgery, radiation, and cytotoxic chemotherapy — all *injure tissue*, and injured tissue emits the very signals (serotonin, nitric oxide, prostaglandins, estrogen, histamine, lactate, free fatty acids) that make a cancer field more, not less, hospitable to malignant growth. The doctrine of "the cancer cell" — a discrete enemy to be excised, irradiated, or poisoned — survives because of its institutional convenience, not because the survival curves it produces are impressive. This page works through Peat's specific mechanistic critiques of each treatment modality, the historical accidents (Gompertz, Laird, Taxol) that locked oncology into its current shape, and the contrarian metabolic therapies (aspirin, DCA, methylene blue, niacinamide) that mainstream medicine has refused to test seriously. It is *not* a recommendation to refuse oncology care; Peat himself acknowledged conventional treatment as the right call in defined settings. The page is critique of an industry, not advice to a patient. For the broader Peat cancer framework, see [[cancer]]; for the metabolic substrate, [[warburg-effect]] and [[lactic-acid]]; for cancer prevention rather than treatment, [[cancer-prevention]].

## The "primum non nocere" frame for oncology

Peat returned to one line throughout the 2013 *Cancer: Disorder and Energy in its Origin and Treatment* newsletter: "Preventing injury should be a basic consideration, but the medical slogan, 'first do no harm,' just doesn't apply to the cancer treatment industry, and this results from the doctrine of 'the cancer cell,' which is something to be destroyed or kept from multiplying" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). The framing matters because every other treatment-side claim follows from it. If cancer is a property of the field — disordered respiration, accumulated polyunsaturated fats, excessive estrogen, chronic lactate production, a leaky and stress-activated matrix (see [[warburg-effect]], [[lipid-peroxidation]], [[estrogen]], [[lactic-acid]]) — then any therapy that *adds* to that disorder by inflaming tissues, lowering oxidation, fragmenting collagen, releasing free fatty acids, or stimulating stress hormones is iatrogenic by definition, regardless of whether the visible tumor temporarily shrinks.

Peat's diagnostic example is the workup itself. By the time a patient reaches "treatment," they have typically been through multiple x-ray examinations, sometimes positron-emitting radioisotope scans, and toxic MRI contrast agents — every one of which adds to inflammatory burden ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). Pain control then routinely uses opiates that release histamine, which itself promotes tumor growth — a fact "decisions about pain control usually disregard." The patient is being metabolically primed for recurrence before the first dose of cytotoxic agent.

Voice note: the rest of this page reports Peat's specific arguments. Peat was a careful reader, not a polemicist, and he repeatedly distinguished defensible uses of conventional therapy (acute leukemias responsive to glucocorticoids, surgical debulking of an obstructing mass, ablation of a localized lesion in an otherwise healthy field) from the indiscriminate application of all three modalities to nearly every diagnosis.

## The radiation-bystander effect (unique-signal section)

For seventy years after the 1927 Muller fruit-fly experiments, oncology assumed radiation caused cancer through a direct hit to the genome — a mutation in a single cell that then clonally expanded ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). The bystander effect undid this. In the 1990s, experimenters found that when a culture dish of irradiated cells received fresh, unirradiated cells, the *newcomers* developed mutations and died. They had received no radiation. They had received signals.

Peat's reading is direct: "The radiation causes cells to emit excitatory, inflammatory substances such as serotonin and nitric oxide, which injure the cells that are later put near them" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). This is the experimental smoking gun for systemic-cancer-field theory. The somatic mutation hypothesis — radiation cancer as a discrete, local genetic accident — cannot accommodate the result. Injured cells communicate. The injury propagates.

What changes in vivo is that the propagation is no longer pathological at the level of individual cells; it is *the wound-healing response*. The radiologist's beam doesn't only damage cells it hits. The whole organism mounts an endocrine and inflammatory response: "The injured tissue takes on new functions, for example by locally synthesizing estrogen, cortisol, and other hormones, as well as stimulating the normal endocrine glands to secrete them" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). Peat then asks the question that exposes the disciplinary blind spot: "Has anyone ever heard of a radiologist or surgeon who measured estrogen or the various mediators of inflammation before, during, and after their treatments?"

A practical consequence: the timing of breast cancer surgery within the menstrual cycle predicts long-range survival ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). Operating during the high-estrogen luteal phase produces worse outcomes than operating during the follicular phase — a fact that has been replicated for decades and is essentially ignored in standard surgical scheduling. The bystander effect generalizes: any tissue injury, in an estrogen-dominant field, recruits surrounding cells into the dysplastic program rather than the regenerative program.

The radiation-bystander literature also undoes the linear-no-threshold safety models. A dental x-ray is enough to activate the same excitatory-inflammatory cascade as a therapeutic dose, only smaller; "a 'therapeutic' x-ray to any part of the body excites similar, but much greater, processes throughout the body" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). The bystander mechanism is dose-responsive, not threshold-gated.

For the broader Peat framework on radiation-induced metabolic injury, see [[radiation-injury]] (if present in the vault) and the [[free-fatty-acids]] and [[serotonin]] pages on the relevant mediators.

## Surgery stimulates tumor growth (mechanism + literature)

The Peat-Cancer-Matrix passage is unusually direct: "In 36% of women who had had a breast removed, from 7 to 22 years previously, identifiable (by the same tests used to diagnose breast cancer) cancer cells could be found circulating in their blood stream" ([the-cancer-matrix](../../sources/newsletters/the-cancer-matrix.md)). The surgery did not eradicate the disease — it locally cleared a mass while seeding the circulation, and the dormant micrometastases persisted for two decades. The surgical wound itself supplies the stimulus to re-awaken them.

A 1969 Harry Rubin autopsy study found that *everyone* over the age of fifty had at least one diagnosable cancer somewhere; "occult microscopic cancers are exceedingly common in the general population and are held in a dormant state by a balance between cell proliferation and cell death and also an intact host immune surveillance" ([the-cancer-matrix](../../sources/newsletters/the-cancer-matrix.md)). The same authors observed, plainly: "the stress of surgery stimulates tumor growth, by various mechanisms, and that surgery increases the risk of developing cancer in apparently cancer-free patients."

The mechanisms are not speculative. Surgical trauma produces:

- A surge of cortisol and adrenaline, which release free fatty acids ([[free-fatty-acids]]) and suppress respiration (the Randle cycle, [[randle-cycle]])
- Local estrogen synthesis by injured tissue (aromatase induction in the wound bed; see [[aromatase]] and [[estrogen]])
- Acute inflammation with prostaglandin, serotonin, histamine, and nitric oxide release (each individually pro-malignant in Peat's framework)
- Fibrin deposition and matrix remodeling that "interferes with the differentiated functioning of cells" and releases "peptides that increase cell growth, invasiveness, inflammation, and the production of new blood vessels, which in turn become leaky" ([bleeding-clotting-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/bleeding-clotting-cancer.md))
- Suppression of immune surveillance during the perioperative window

A surgical wound is, mechanistically, an estrogen-prostaglandin-fibrin field — and that field is exactly what micrometastatic cells are waiting for. Peat's framing: "Radiation, chemotherapy, and surgery all activate these processes of cell replacement, and unless something has changed to improve the organism's recuperative ability, it isn't clear why the cells which replace the missing part should be more able to satisfactorily complete the recovery process than the original cells were" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)).

Surgical resection of a localized lesion in an otherwise high-respiration field can still be the right call. Surgery into an inflammatory, estrogen-dominant, PUFA-loaded field, without any peri-operative attempt to shift the metabolic context, is what Peat critiqued.

## The Gompertz-growth-law critique

This is one of Peat's sharpest pieces of medical history. Benjamin Gompertz was an actuary; in 1825 he described the mathematical relation between age and probability of death. The curve is asymmetric — flat through middle life, then sharply rising. It is a *mortality* curve for human populations.

In 1964, Anna Laird published a paper titled *Dynamics of Tumor Growth* in the *British Journal of Cancer* applying that actuarial formula to tumor measurements ([cancer-disorder-and-energy ref 30](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md); Laird AK, *Br J Cancer* 18(3):490-502, 1964). Peat's gloss: "Laird's evidence consisted of 19 tumor samples, taken from 10 mice, 8 rats, and a rabbit" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). Nineteen tumors. From nineteen animals across three species. That dataset became the empirical foundation of "tumor doubling time" oncology — the mathematical justification for staging, the framework for chemotherapy dose-fractionation schedules, the basis on which "stable disease" and "progressive disease" are radiographically assessed.

Peat's verdict is reportorial: "Gompertz' reasoning that the probability of a person's dying increases with age has nothing to do with cancer cells, and there is very little evidence that his law of growth is useful for describing tumors. ... It seems to be the profession's great need for justification that has made a Law of Tumor Growth so important to them" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). Laird herself proposed the deceleration might represent a natural growth-regulating process — a hint that the tumor field was modulating its own behavior — but that interpretation was discarded. What survived was the actuarial curve as a property of cancer cells, divorced from organism context.

The downstream consequence is not abstract. The Gompertz-Laird framework treats a tumor as a population of cells with an intrinsic growth rate, which justifies destroying as many of them as possible as fast as possible. The Peat framework treats a tumor as a state of the surrounding tissue — a fact that would justify changing the surrounding tissue instead.

## The starving-the-tumor paradox (the Seyfried-ketogenic rebuttal)

This section is the article's strongest single rebuttal to a popular contemporary cancer theory, namely the Thomas-Seyfried-led argument that cancer is metabolic, that glucose feeds it, and that ketogenic diets, calorie restriction, or 2-deoxyglucose pharmacotherapy can "starve" tumors into regression.

Peat agreed that cancer is metabolic. He agreed glucose oxidation is impaired (the Warburg effect; see [[warburg-effect]]). He disagreed sharply about the therapeutic conclusion. The 1987 work by Sauer and Dauchy demonstrated that energy restriction *accelerates* tumor growth and metastasis in tumor-bearing animals — exactly the opposite of the predicted effect ([cancer-matrix](../../sources/newsletters/the-cancer-matrix.md) and related Peat correspondence on metabolic restriction). The 1991 Schlappack, Zimmermann and Hill paper made the mechanism explicit: glucose starvation and acidosis raised the metastatic potential of murine tumor cells (*Br J Cancer* 64(4):663-670, 1991).

Peat's mechanistic explanation runs through matrix metalloproteinases:

> "Deprivation of glucose and oxygen, which can be the local result of a cellular environment of condensed, stiffened collagen and the cellular tension and activation produced in response, combined with systemic stress that causes free fatty acids to interfere with the oxidation of sugar, activates enzymes that can dissolve collagen (MMP-2 and MMP-9). These enzymes are involved in metastasis, allowing cells to escape from the condensed collagen, but although they are normally thought of as enzymes that act outside of cells, they can also enter the cell's nucleus, where they degrade the DNA, causing the mutations and chromosomal abnormalities that are so characteristic of cancer." ([the-cancer-matrix](../../sources/newsletters/the-cancer-matrix.md))

And, explicitly: "Like glucose deprivation, exposure to 2-deoxyglucose, often used in tumor imaging, promotes metastasis" ([the-cancer-matrix ref 28](../../sources/newsletters/the-cancer-matrix.md)).

The implications cascade:

1. Glucose deprivation does not weaken cancer; it activates the collagenase escape program. The cancer cell trapped inside fibrotic stroma, denied substrate, dissolves the matrix around it and disseminates.
2. The matrix-degradation enzymes that allow escape also translocate to the nucleus and degrade DNA — so glucose deprivation isn't just promoting metastasis, it's *causing* the chromosomal abnormalities that genomic-oncology then interprets as the cancer's driving mutations.
3. 2-deoxyglucose, the pharmacological glucose-deprivation agent that PET imaging exploits and that has been seriously proposed as anti-cancer chemotherapy, promotes metastasis directly.
4. Calorie restriction, fasting, and ketogenic diets — by raising free fatty acids and suppressing glucose oxidation (see [[free-fatty-acids]] and [[randle-cycle]]) — produce the same systemic state that activates the MMP cascade.

For a Peat reader, this is the answer to "should I do a ketogenic diet for cancer?" The framework predicts the opposite of what's commonly assumed. The cancer cell already cannot use glucose properly (Warburg); starving it of glucose forces it into the metastatic-escape program, and supplies its free-fatty-acid fuel.

The therapy implied by this analysis runs the other direction: protect glucose oxidation, suppress free fatty acid release (the aspirin mechanism, below), and keep the matrix soft. See [[cancer]] hub for the broader pro-metabolic protocol.

## Chemotherapy and the HIF/resistance feedback

Cytotoxic chemotherapy operates by killing rapidly dividing cells. The cells it cannot kill are precisely the ones that survive — which is the basic mechanism of acquired resistance. The Peat-relevant question is *what kind of survivors emerge?*

Cytotoxic stress, like surgery and radiation, generates local hypoxia. Hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), which transcriptionally activates glycolytic enzymes, lactate dehydrogenase, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF, the angiogenesis driver), and the matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1, MMP-13 — see the emodin passage in [cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). The chemotherapeutic round selects for cells with constitutively high HIF activity — that is, cells that are *more* Warburg-shifted, *more* glycolytic, *more* invasive, *more* angiogenic than the parent tumor. The "resistant recurrence" is metabolically more cancerous than the diagnosis-time tumor.

This is the mechanism by which a 40% initial response rate can degrade into a 5-year survival that barely improves on natural history: the residual cells are biologically worse than the pre-treatment cells. They've been selected on exactly the trait that defines malignancy in Peat's framework — a shift away from oxidative phosphorylation toward lactate-generating glycolysis (see [[warburg-effect]] and [[lactic-acid]]).

Chemotherapeutic agents also generate the same wound-healing field that surgery and radiation generate. Adrenaline, cortisol, free fatty acid release, prostaglandin elevation, fibrin deposition, immune suppression. Peat's specific example was Taxol (paclitaxel), discussed below, but the pattern generalizes across cytotoxic classes.

A Peat-aligned defense of chemotherapy: the leukemias and lymphomas, where the cancer cells are themselves uniquely fragile lymphoid lineage cells, are a real exception. Peat: "The use of glucocorticoid hormones to destroy thymus-derived cancers takes advantage of that natural sensitivity and fragility" ([taxol-yew-and-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/taxol-yew-and-cancer.md)). For solid tumors with low growth fractions, the response rates are much smaller and the iatrogenic cost is much higher.

### The Taxol case as institutional history

Peat's 1992 *Taxol, Yew, and Cancer* is a useful microcosm because it shows the gap between mechanism and adoption. Taxol's cytotoxicity is via microtubule stabilization — the same target as colchicine. Microtubules are essential for cell division, but also for axonal transport in nerves and for the amoeboid motility of neutrophils. So Taxol is predictably neurotoxic ("very toxic to nerve cells") and immunosuppressive ("very toxic to the neutrophils, effective tumor cell killers"). It also blocks the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone and progesterone — the two endogenous anti-cancer steroids in Peat's framework ([taxol-yew-and-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/taxol-yew-and-cancer.md); see [[progesterone]] and [[pregnenolone]]).

Peat's verdict, from 1992: "At best, taxol is an unproven cancer remedy, but when a drug monopoly is promoting a new product, facts hardly matter" ([taxol-yew-and-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/taxol-yew-and-cancer.md)). The contrast he drew was with cyanide-thiosulfate detoxification, which targets a *real* cancer-cell-specific vulnerability (cancer cells lack the sulfur-conjugation enzymes for cyanide detox) and is too cheap to be profitable to develop. The mechanism for adoption isn't mechanistic suitability; it's the patentability and exclusivity of the molecule.

The Taxol story matters as exemplar. Most cytotoxic chemotherapy agents enter clinical use the same way: a molecule that shrinks something in a culture dish, a monopoly-protected development pathway, and an institutional pressure to ship.

## The clotting-and-metastasis axis + anti-coagulant therapy

The fibrin-cancer connection is the through-line that links Peat's surgery critique to his aspirin advocacy. Both threads route through the clotting system.

Stephen Paget's 1889 "seed and soil" formulation framed cancer metastasis as cells finding congenial sites. Peat's update: the congenial soil is a stress-modified extracellular matrix, and circulating tumor cells survive transit through the bloodstream by recruiting fibrin coats and platelet aggregates. "Clots are formed when soluble fibrinogen polymerizes, condenses, and becomes insoluble. Even before the particles of fibrin become insoluble, a clot-dissolving system is continuously breaking it down into small peptides. These peptides tend to cause capillaries to leak" ([bleeding-clotting-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/bleeding-clotting-cancer.md)).

Circulating tumor cells aren't bare cells in plasma. They travel inside microclots. The fibrin shield protects them from immune clearance and from the shear stress of capillary transit. When they arrest in a downstream capillary, the resulting local leakage of fibrin degradation products causes capillary permeability changes that "increase cell growth, invasiveness, inflammation, and the production of new blood vessels, which in turn become leaky" ([bleeding-clotting-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/bleeding-clotting-cancer.md)). The metastatic niche is, literally, a clotting-and-leakage event.

This explains a set of clinical observations:

- The aspirin-cancer-incidence association in large epidemiological cohorts (lower incidence and lower metastatic rates in chronic aspirin users)
- The heparin-cancer literature, in which anticoagulation reduces metastatic establishment in animal models
- The clinical practice of using low-dose aspirin perioperatively for cancer surgery as anti-metastatic prophylaxis (a practice that has occasionally surfaced and as often retreated)

Peat's framing is that anticoagulation is *literally* anti-metastatic because it strips the fibrin armor off the circulating cell. The cell that arrives in distant tissue undefended is the cell that immune surveillance can clear. See [[bleeding-clotting]] hub for the broader clotting framework and [[leakiness]] for vascular permeability.

## Aspirin as cancer therapy (mechanism detail)

General aspirin lives in [[aspirin]]; this section concentrates on the specifically anti-cancer mechanisms that make aspirin, in Peat's reading, "very nearly an anti-cancer drug masquerading as an analgesic" ([aspirin-brain-and-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/aspirin-brain-and-cancer.md)).

The mechanisms are layered:

**1. Cyclooxygenase inhibition / prostaglandin suppression.** The orthodox mechanism. Aspirin acetylates COX-1 and COX-2, blocking prostaglandin synthesis from arachidonic acid. The prostaglandins are themselves pro-tumorigenic — they activate aromatase (driving local estrogen synthesis in the wound bed; see [[aromatase]]) and they stimulate the wound-healing inflammatory cascade. Suppressing them suppresses one major arm of tumor-promoting signal ([aspirin-brain-and-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/aspirin-brain-and-cancer.md)).

**2. Free fatty acid release blockade.** "The shift away from fat oxidation under the influence of aspirin doesn't lead to an accumulation of free fatty acids in the circulation, since aspirin inhibits the release of fatty acids from both phospholipids and triglycerides" ([aspirin-brain-and-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/aspirin-brain-and-cancer.md)). This is the mechanism most often missed in summaries. By blocking lipolysis, aspirin prevents the free-fatty-acid arm of the Randle cycle from suppressing glucose oxidation — protecting mitochondrial respiration in the tumor field. The contrast with estrogen, which "activates lipolysis," is stark.

**3. Lactate-fuel switch (fatty acid synthase inhibition).** A more recent finding incorporated into Peat's framework: aspirin inhibits fatty acid synthase (FAS), the enzyme responsible for de novo lipogenesis from acetyl-CoA. Cancer cells, in their lactate-and-glucose-fermenting state, depend heavily on FAS to convert their glycolytic carbon flux into membrane lipids and signaling lipids. Inhibiting FAS deprives them of that biosynthetic outlet and forces accumulated glycolytic intermediates into either oxidation or apoptosis.

**4. Carbonic anhydrase inhibition.** Aspirin acts as a mild carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, which lets CO₂ accumulate intracellularly. Higher intracellular CO₂ "lowers the intracellular pH, as well as inhibiting lactic acid formation, and restoring the oxidation of glucose increases CO₂" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)). Tumor cells with high carbonic anhydrase activity rapidly export protons, maintaining an alkaline intracellular pH that supports glycolysis. Aspirin tilts the equilibrium back toward acidic, respiration-supporting intracellular chemistry. See [[carbon-dioxide]].

**5. Collagen-deposition reduction (anti-fibrotic).** Aspirin reduces the fibrotic remodeling response to injury. Because the Peat framework sees fibrotic stroma as the launching pad for the MMP-escape program (above), reducing fibrosis around a tumor removes the substrate for the escape program. See [[fibrosis]].

**6. Anti-clotting.** Aspirin's platelet-aggregation effect strips circulating tumor cells of their platelet-fibrin shield, exposing them to immune clearance and shear destruction in transit. The mechanism overlaps with the bleeding-clotting axis above.

Aspirin is, in this reading, simultaneously an anti-inflammatory, an anti-lipolytic, a metabolic-shift agent, a CO₂-protective agent, an anti-fibrotic, and an anti-thrombotic. The cancer-relevant dose is higher than the cardioprotective baby-aspirin dose — Peat typically discussed 325 mg or higher, multiple times daily, with vitamin K co-supplementation to manage bleeding risk. Standard cautions (gastric irritation, bleeding risk, Reye's in children, drug interactions) apply.

## DCA, methylene blue, niacinamide as mainstream-refused options

A short tour of metabolic agents that mainstream oncology has declined to develop, ordered by Peat's frequency of discussion.

**Methylene blue.** A historical dye, used since the 1890s for methemoglobinemia, malaria, and as a urinary antiseptic. Methylene blue is an electron-cycling agent — it accepts electrons in the cytosol and donates them at complex IV of the mitochondrial chain, bypassing damaged complex I and rescuing respiration in cells with impaired electron transport. Peat cited it in the context of restoring oxidative metabolism in injured nervous tissue and in cancer cells. It's cheap, off-patent, has a 130-year safety record, and has never received serious oncology trial funding. See [[methylene-blue]] for the broader page.

**Dichloroacetate (DCA).** A pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) inhibitor. PDK normally inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase, which means PDK *suppresses* the entry of pyruvate into the mitochondria for oxidation. Cancer cells overexpress PDK as part of the Warburg shift — they actively block pyruvate from oxidation, forcing it to lactate instead. DCA, by inhibiting PDK, restores pyruvate's entry into the mitochondria and re-engages oxidative metabolism. The 2007 Michelakis lab paper (University of Alberta) demonstrated tumor regression in animal models. DCA is a small, off-patent molecule. It is in the same situation as methylene blue: mechanistically aligned with the Warburg-correction framework, never seriously trialed. See [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]].

**Niacinamide (nicotinamide).** Provides the NAD+ precursor that the respiratory chain requires. Peat treated niacinamide as one of the central metabolic protectives — it lowers free fatty acids, supports the citric acid cycle, suppresses excess lipolysis, and reduces the lipolytic-FFA arm of the cancer-stress cycle. Cheap, vitamin-status, off-patent. See [[niacinamide]].

**Caffeine.** A phosphodiesterase inhibitor that raises cyclic AMP, supports adenosine antagonism, and improves mitochondrial respiration. Peat treated coffee/caffeine as broadly protective. See [[caffeine]] if present.

The pattern Peat repeatedly highlighted: the agents that the metabolic theory predicts should be useful are uniformly cheap and off-patent. The agents that have been developed and patented (taxanes, anti-VEGF antibodies, kinase inhibitors, the modern immunotherapies) operate on the somatic-mutation or oncogene-addiction frame and earn back development cost through monopoly pricing. The development economics select for the wrong mechanism.

## The opiate/histamine/serotonin promotional effects on tumors

Pain control in cancer routinely uses opiates. Opiates release histamine from mast cells. Histamine is a tumor-growth-promoter and capillary-leakage agent. Peat's exact note: "Decisions about pain control usually disregard the effects of the drugs on tumor growth and general vitality — for example, the opiates stimulate histamine release, which increases inflammation and tumor growth" ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md)).

Serotonin, released in response to tissue injury and inflammation, is another tumor-promotional signal. Peat repeatedly listed serotonin alongside nitric oxide and the prostaglandins as the excitatory-injury substances emitted by the radiation-bystander cells, with autocrine effects on tumor growth and matrix degradation. See [[serotonin]].

Histamine itself plays an underappreciated role. Beyond the opiate-induced release, histamine is elevated in many cancer states, activates carbonic anhydrase (raising tumor intracellular alkalinity and supporting glycolysis), and increases capillary permeability — facilitating the leakiness that allows metastatic seeding ([cancer-disorder-and-energy](../../sources/newsletters/cancer-disorder-and-energy.md), [leakiness-aging-and-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/leakiness-aging-and-cancer.md)). Antihistamines (cyproheptadine in particular, see [[cyproheptadine]]) are, in Peat's framework, plausibly anti-cancer adjuncts.

The clinical implication is uncomfortable: standard supportive care in oncology — opiates for pain, SSRIs for "depression" (which elevate available serotonin), anti-emetics that themselves modulate serotonin in complex ways — is supplying a steady stream of the exact signals the cancer field thrives on. The patient is being managed for symptoms in ways that worsen the underlying disorder.

## When conventional treatment is the right call

This section exists because Peat himself would have insisted on it, and because the page is critique-of-an-industry, not advice-to-a-patient.

Peat acknowledged several settings in which conventional oncology is defensible or correct:

**Acute lymphoid and myeloid leukemias** where the cancer cells are uniquely fragile lymphoid-lineage cells, and where glucocorticoids and specific cytotoxics produce durable remissions ([taxol-yew-and-cancer](../../sources/newsletters/taxol-yew-and-cancer.md)). The exception proves the rule: the lineage's intrinsic fragility lets a poison kill the cancer without killing the patient. This is *not* the situation for most solid tumors.

**Surgical resection of a localized, well-defined solid tumor** in an otherwise high-respiration field — a small early-stage colon cancer in a metabolically sound patient, for example. The mechanical relief of an obstructing or compressive mass is genuinely useful; the post-operative period is also a window for metabolic-protective intervention (pregnenolone, progesterone, aspirin, niacinamide, T3, low-dose naltrexone in some framings) to mitigate the wound-healing-as-recurrence-stimulus problem.

**Debulking of a symptomatic mass** for quality-of-life reasons, where the goal is symptom palliation rather than cure.

**Radioiodine for differentiated thyroid cancer**, where the radioactive isotope is concentrated specifically by the tumor tissue, and the off-target dose is genuinely small. The lineage-specificity of the uptake mechanism makes this a defensible exception.

**Cyanide-thiosulfate** (an example Peat raised but which has not entered modern practice), where the cancer-cell vulnerability is real and exploitable.

Peat did not write as someone counseling refusal of care. He wrote as someone arguing that the metabolic *context* into which the conventional therapy is delivered determines whether it produces durable benefit or accelerated recurrence. The pro-metabolic protocol — protect respiration, suppress free fatty acids, lower estrogen, raise CO₂, soften the matrix — is what makes the conventional intervention not iatrogenic.

The most defensible reading of Peat's position is this: do not refuse oncology care; do refuse to be a passive recipient of it. Ask about peri-operative timing, about peri-operative metabolic support, about whether the proposed cytotoxic agent has any rationale better than "this is the protocol." Take the opiate-histamine-serotonin axis seriously in symptom management. Treat the wound, not just the cell.

## Counter-arguments

A fair page records the strongest objections to Peat's framing.

**The "Peat's framework lacks survival-curve data" critique.** Peat assembled his case from mechanism papers, animal studies, epidemiology, and case-series literature. He did not run randomized controlled trials. Conventional oncology, whatever its conceptual debts to Gompertz and Laird, has produced large RCT datasets demonstrating real survival improvements in specific settings — adjuvant chemotherapy in node-positive breast cancer, ABVD in Hodgkin lymphoma, FOLFIRINOX in pancreatic cancer for highly selected patients, the various tyrosine kinase inhibitors in chronic myeloid leukemia. A reader weighing the Peat critique against these data has to acknowledge the asymmetry: the critique is mechanistic and historical; the defense is empirical and trial-grounded.

The Peat response, when challenged on this, was that the trial improvements are *small in absolute terms* (single-digit five-year survival improvements in most adjuvant settings), that the trials measure response and recurrence-free survival rather than long-term overall survival, and that the metabolic-protective context of the trial population is uncontrolled. The critique stands at the mechanism level. The defense stands at the trial level. They do not, strictly, contradict.

**The immunotherapy era.** The checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4) produced durable remissions in melanoma, lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and several other settings beginning around 2014. These are not cytotoxic agents in the classical sense; they release suppressive brakes on T cells and let the immune system clear the tumor. Peat, who died in 2022, lived through the rise of the checkpoint era but did not significantly update his treatment-critique framework. The Peat-aligned reading of immunotherapy is that it works precisely because it does not destroy tissue and does not generate the wound-healing-as-recurrence-stimulus signal; the immune system's discrimination is finer than radiation's or cytotoxics'. The complications of checkpoint inhibitors (autoimmune colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, thyroiditis, hypophysitis) are themselves consistent with Peat's view that the existing immune surveillance is restrained — release the brake and you get both anti-tumor effect and autoimmunity.

**Targeted therapy successes.** Imatinib in chronic myeloid leukemia, trastuzumab in HER2+ breast cancer, the ALK and EGFR inhibitors in lung cancer subsets, the PARP inhibitors in BRCA-mutated ovarian and breast cancer. These cases are highly mechanism-specific and produce real responses. The Peat-aligned reading is that these are exceptions that prove the rule about lineage-specific vulnerabilities (a la the lymphoid-fragility argument) — they work precisely when there is a real cancer-cell-specific biochemical handle, not a population-statistical one. They do not generalize to most solid tumors.

**The "you'd let people die" critique.** This is the standard response to anti-conventional cancer framings, and it deserves a direct answer. Peat's argument is not "refuse treatment." Peat's argument is "the field that produces the cancer is the field that recurrences emerge from, and a treatment that destroys cells without changing the field is not a cure." A patient who pursues conventional therapy *and* aggressive metabolic protection is doing Peat's actual recommendation. A patient who refuses conventional therapy in favor of vitamin protocols alone is doing something Peat would have warned against.

**The Seyfried-ketogenic literature.** Seyfried's case is built on the Warburg substrate and on selected animal studies showing tumor regression on ketogenic diets and calorie restriction. The Peat counter, above, rests on the Sauer-Dauchy (1987) and Schlappack (1991) metastasis-acceleration findings and on the MMP-escape mechanism. The two frameworks both agree cancer is metabolic; they disagree on whether glucose restriction is therapeutic or accelerant. The mechanistic asymmetry is that Seyfried's framework predicts tumor shrinkage (a measurable but possibly transient effect), while Peat's framework predicts increased metastasis (a less easily measured but lethal effect). The published data — which Peat repeatedly cited — supports the Peat reading on metastatic acceleration.

## Data gaps

Specific places where Peat's framework is under-tested or where the literature is genuinely thin:

- A randomized trial of peri-operative aspirin plus pregnenolone plus niacinamide in breast cancer surgery, with metastatic recurrence as endpoint. The mechanistic case is strong; the trial has not been done.
- A direct head-to-head of low-dose DCA vs. placebo in any solid-tumor setting with adequate power.
- Peri-operative menstrual-cycle timing studies in breast cancer: the existing data favoring follicular-phase surgery is decades old, replicated, and still not standard practice.
- The radiation-bystander effect at therapeutic radiation doses in tumor-bearing organisms (most experimental data is in cell culture).
- Direct measurement of estrogen, prostaglandin, and serotonin levels before, during, and after radiation therapy in cancer patients — the measurements Peat called for, that have largely not been done.
- The 2-deoxyglucose-as-therapy literature: the metastasis-acceleration finding is established, but the dose-response in clinical settings is incompletely characterized.
- Methylene blue and niacinamide as adjuncts to conventional chemotherapy: phase II trials would clarify whether the metabolic protection produces durable survival benefit beyond response rate.
