# Cancer

In the mainstream view, cancer begins with a genetic mutation—a random error in DNA that transforms a normal cell into a malignant one. Ray Peat rejects this framework entirely. Drawing on the work of [[Otto Warburg]], Albert Szent-Gyorgyi, and his own research on [[estrogen]] and cellular respiration, Peat views cancer as fundamentally a metabolic disease: the consequence of impaired oxidative [[metabolism]] that forces cells to revert to a primitive, fermentative mode of energy production. "Cancer is the result of ordinary physiological processes which become autonomous because of regulatory weaknesses in the organism," he wrote in a newsletter on the subject. The cell does not break—it regresses.

## The Warburg Principle: Respiration Versus Fermentation

Otto Warburg's 1923 discovery remains the cornerstone of Peat's cancer framework. Warburg demonstrated that cancer cells consume large amounts of glucose and produce [[lactic acid]] even in the presence of oxygen—a phenomenon called "aerobic glycolysis." Normal cells oxidize glucose completely using mitochondrial respiration, producing carbon dioxide and water. Cancer cells cannot.

As Peat explained in a newsletter on carbon monoxide and cancer, "In Warburg's view, cancer has a unique requirement for glucose, because of its respiratory defect. The ability of cancer to consume large amounts of glucose means that the body interprets cancer as stress, since the waste of glucose is the key feature of stress." The body responds to this glucose drain by producing more [[cortisol]], which raises blood sugar—feeding the cancer further while suppressing the immune system.

Warburg and Szent-Gyorgyi both recognized that growth is the primordial function of all cells. Differentiation—the process by which cells specialize into liver cells, nerve cells, muscle cells—requires restraining this primitive growth impulse. As Peat noted in his articles, these researchers understood that "the differentiated functions of complex organisms involve restraints of that primitive function, imposed by a highly organized respiratory system." When respiration fails, those restraints dissolve, and the cell returns to its default program: divide.

This framing inverts the conventional understanding. The genetic mutations found in tumors are not the cause of cancer but its consequence—the downstream effects of metabolic chaos in a cell that has lost its respiratory capacity. For the detailed biochemistry of how this metabolic shift operates, see [[Warburg effect]].

## The Cancer Field: Environment Over Genetics

One of Peat's most important insights is that cancer is not merely a cellular disease but a tissue-level phenomenon. In a [[2022-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-how-irradiated-cells-affect-other-living-cells-in-human-body|2022 episode on irradiated cells]], he discussed the "cancer field" as a biological reality:

> "We know about the bystander effect: if a cell is irradiated outside the body and introduced back into the body, it can have a damaging effect. This isn't because it's releasing high-energy electrons, but because the stress signals the cell gives out affect other cells."

This means that a single damaged cell can create a metabolic environment that degrades its neighbors. The tissue as a whole shifts toward the fermentative, growth-promoting state. This explains why removing a tumor often fails—the "field" of metabolic dysfunction extends beyond the visible mass.

The concept also explains spontaneous regression, which conventional oncology struggles to account for. If the tissue environment is restored—through improved respiration, reduced estrogen, adequate thyroid function—the field that supports cancer dissolves, and cells can redifferentiate. As Peat described in *Generative Energy*, "the centers of degeneration can become centers of regeneration" with the right metabolic support.

## Estrogen: The Primary Carcinogen

The relationship between [[estrogen]] and cancer is perhaps the most thoroughly suppressed finding in modern medicine. Researchers in the 1930s and 40s demonstrated clearly that estrogen causes cancer in virtually every tissue with sufficient exposure. As Peat described in a [[2015-03-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-breast-cancer|2015 episode on breast cancer]]:

> "The animal studies in the 30s and 40s were very clear that it isn't just the normally responsive tissues like the uterus and the breast that will cancerize under the influence of estrogen—those are just the most sensitive. With increased, uninterrupted exposure to estrogen, the sequence tends to go: first cancer of the uterus, then cancer of the breast, then of the lungs, kidneys, intestine, liver, and brain."

The mechanism connects directly to Warburg's principle. Estrogen impairs cytochrome oxidase—the terminal enzyme in mitochondrial respiration—creating the exact respiratory defect that defines cancer cells. As Peat explained in a [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|2016 episode on cancer metabolism]], "when something overstimulates or irritates an area—and the irritating effect of things like estrogen was already known—that excitation exhausts the tissue oxygen. This was at the center of the idea of what cancer is."

Estrogen also promotes cancer through its excitatory effects on cells. By increasing glutamate signaling and water uptake, estrogen forces cells into an energetically depleted state—precisely the condition that triggers reversion to fermentative metabolism. The carcinogenic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons found in soot were recognized as dangerous specifically because they mimic estrogen's effects on cellular respiration.

The pharmaceutical industry's promotion of estrogen as beneficial required burying this research. Twelve major drug companies formed consortiums to fund studies positioning estrogen as protective, creating what Peat called "the medical cancer cult." For the full story of estrogen's metabolic effects, see [[estrogen]]. For breast cancer specifically, see [[breast cancer]].

## The Stress-Cancer Axis

Cancer does not emerge in isolation from the body's stress response—it is embedded in it. [[Cortisol]], [[serotonin]], and [[prolactin]] all promote the conditions that allow cancer to thrive.

Cortisol breaks down tissues to maintain blood sugar during stress, but this tissue breakdown releases [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] into the bloodstream. These fats suppress [[thyroid]] function and block glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]], creating the metabolic environment cancer requires. As Peat noted, cortisol "raises blood sugar by mobilizing amino acids from tissues and converting them to glucose, but this is at the expense of muscle and other tissue structure."

Prolactin works synergistically with estrogen. In his articles, Peat cited research showing that "prolactin and estrogen have synergistic effects in causing tissue degeneration, including cancerization, and that their effects tend to operate with fewer protective restraining influences in old age." This explains why cancer risk increases with aging—as protective hormones decline, the prolactin-estrogen axis goes unopposed.

Serotonin, far from the "feel-good" neurotransmitter of popular imagination, promotes cancer through its inflammatory and growth-promoting effects. It increases vascular permeability, promotes edema, and activates the stress cascade. For more on serotonin's harmful effects, see [[serotonin]].

## PUFA and Cancer Promotion

[[PUFA|Polyunsaturated fatty acids]] promote cancer through multiple mechanisms. Their spontaneous oxidation in warm-blooded animals generates aldehydes, free radicals, and isoprostanes that directly damage DNA. But more importantly, they suppress the oxidative metabolism that keeps cells differentiated.

Peat identified a practical threshold in his work. In a [[2017-09-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-proposition-65|2017 interview]], he stated: "The inflection point where you see cancer correlating closely to the amount of PUFA in the diet is around 4 grams per day." Above this threshold, the risk rises proportionally.

The immunosuppressive effects of PUFA further compound the problem. Research in the 1960s found that polyunsaturated fats prolong graft survival by suppressing the immune system—the same immune system responsible for eliminating nascent cancer cells. As Peat observed, "the polyunsaturated fats work by exactly the same mechanism that X-rays do: creating an immune deficiency and an inability to produce inflammation." The fats do not resolve cancer-promoting inflammation—they paralyze the body's ability to respond.

## Anti-Cancer Substances in Peat's Framework

If cancer results from impaired respiration and metabolic dysfunction, treatment should restore respiratory function rather than poisoning the cell further. Peat advocated several protective substances:

**[[Progesterone]]** opposes estrogen at every level and supports mitochondrial respiration. Alexander Lipschutz demonstrated that continuous estrogen exposure causes cancer, but "if you interrupt that with progesterone periodically, you don't get cancer." Lipschutz called pregnenolone and progesterone the "anti-fibromatogenic steroids"—substances that inhibit abnormal growth and protect against tumors. For progesterone's full anti-cancer mechanisms, see [[progesterone]].

**[[Thyroid]]** hormone is essential because it drives oxidative metabolism—the exact process that keeps cells differentiated and prevents the fermentative shift that defines cancer. Low thyroid function is consistently associated with increased cancer risk, and the relationship is causal, not coincidental.

**[[Aspirin]]** blocks the conversion of PUFA into inflammatory prostaglandins and inhibits the aromatase enzyme that produces estrogen locally in tissues. Its consistent association with reduced cancer risk in large population studies reflects these anti-metabolic-stress effects.

**[[Vitamin E]]** protects against the lipid peroxidation that PUFA undergoes in tissues, reducing the formation of carcinogenic aldehydes and free radicals.

**[[Coffee]]** provides caffeine, which Peat described as protective through its inhibition of phosphodiesterase and its support of oxidative metabolism. Caffeine has been shown to stimulate DNA repair mechanisms.

**Dietary sugar** may seem counterintuitive given cancer's glucose hunger, but Peat argued that adequate glucose prevents the stress response that mobilizes stored PUFA and elevates cortisol. The cancer-feeding effect of glucose is secondary to the cancer-promoting effect of the stress hormones that rise when glucose is restricted.

## The Inflammation Connection

Peat's view of cancer is inseparable from his understanding of [[inflammation]]. Every tissue injury activates aromatase, producing estrogen locally. This estrogen impairs respiration, promoting the fermentative shift. The inflammatory mediators—prostaglandins from PUFA oxidation, histamine from mast cells activated by estrogen, serotonin—all push tissue toward the dedifferentiated state.

The naked mole rat provides a striking illustration. As Peat noted, "A mole rat has never been known to develop cancer. Their serum C-reactive protein is extremely low, indicating that they are resistant to inflammation." The animal's cancer immunity is not genetic in the conventional sense—it reflects a metabolic and inflammatory environment that never permits the respiratory failure cancer requires.

This reframes cancer prevention. Rather than screening for mutations or targeting individual molecular pathways, the goal should be maintaining the metabolic environment that prevents cancer from arising: adequate thyroid function, low PUFA intake, controlled estrogen exposure, sufficient protective steroids, and minimal chronic inflammation.
