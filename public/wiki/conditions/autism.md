# Autism (Autism Spectrum Disorder)

The orthodox 2026 account treats autism as a polygenic neurodevelopmental disorder with heritability estimates in the 0.7-0.9 range, an etiology that is "largely genetic with some environmental contribution," and a primary intervention frame built around behavioral therapy (applied behavior analysis), psychiatric medications for irritability and aggression, and the increasingly accepted neurodiversity position that autism is an identity rather than a disease. Diagnostic prevalence has risen from roughly 1 in 2,500 in the 1970s to 1 in 36 in the 2023 CDC ADDM data — a sixty-fold increase across two generations the field explains as expanded criteria, increased awareness, and better case ascertainment.

Ray Peat treated this framework with the same skepticism he brought to the dementia, cancer, and diabetes literatures. The autism increase is too large and too fast to be a gene-frequency phenomenon, and it tracks too cleanly with the same dietary, environmental, and medical shifts that drive the rest of the chronic-disease landscape. In his framework, autism — like [[adhd|ADHD]], like depression in younger and younger children — belongs to a single family of developmental energy failures he called the [[mental-health-cluster|brain stress syndrome]]. The contributors are not single but stacked: maternal nutrition during gestation, maternal hypothyroidism and environmental estrogen exposure, in-utero stress, intestinal dysbiosis with chronically elevated serotonin in the infant, polyunsaturated fat accumulation in fetal and infant brain, mitochondrial dysfunction, and — the most contested item on his list — the cumulative inflammatory load of early-childhood medical interventions including the timing and adjuvant burden of the modern vaccine schedule.

This is the most sensitive topic in the Peat corpus. Families who have raised an autistic child, on either side of the conventional-versus-alternative divide, have invested years in understanding what happened and what to do. Nothing here dismisses that work or the experiences underneath it. The framework documented below is Peat's; the medical relationships families maintain with their pediatrician and developmental specialist are not displaced by what is written here. Pediatric specialist coordination is essential — for diagnosis, for safety on any intervention, and for the genuine emergencies where the right call is a clinic or a hospital. This is documentation, not medical advice.

## What's actually happening

Five findings in the developmental-neurology literature anchor any serious account of autism.

**Disordered neuronal pruning and connectivity.** The autistic brain in early childhood is, on average, slightly larger than the neurotypical brain, with the difference concentrated in the cortex. The normal pruning that eliminates roughly half of cortical synapses across childhood is reduced, producing local hyperconnectivity in nearby cortical regions combined with long-range hypoconnectivity across distant regions. This is the structural correlate of the cognitive signature families describe: intense local focus, difficulty integrating across modalities, characteristic strengths in domains that depend on local processing.

**Elevated whole-blood serotonin.** Roughly a third of autistic individuals have hyperserotonemia — the single most replicated biochemical finding in autism research, dating to Schain and Freedman's 1961 observation. Because 95% of body serotonin lives in the gut and not the brain (the [[serotonin|serotonin]] article develops the anatomy), the hyperserotonemia reflects gut and platelet biology, but the elevated tone is what the rest of the body is exposed to. Peat's autism email entry, the shortest thing he wrote on the topic, picks this up directly: "Since autism typically involves high serotonin, things like thyroid, lisuride, tianeptine, and gelatin could be helpful."

**Low dopamine in subgroups.** A subset show reduced dopamine signaling in prefrontal and striatal regions — the same energy-deficit profile the [[adhd|ADHD]] article describes, and the same profile Peat used to explain why stimulants "paradoxically" calm hyperactive children. Where serotonin-high / dopamine-low is present, the pattern pushes away from executive integration and toward repetitive lower-brain output.

**Mitochondrial dysfunction in a sizable subgroup.** A 2010 JAMA meta-analysis estimated 5% of autistic individuals meet criteria for definite mitochondrial disease and another ~30% show biochemical markers of mitochondrial dysfunction (elevated lactate, low carnitine, abnormal acylcarnitine, low ATP production in immune cells). This places autism inside the metabolic-psychiatry conversation and inside Peat's [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative-metabolism]] frame: where cells cannot oxidize glucose to CO2, the tissue cannot function, and in the brain that tissue is the one whose function defines the cognitive phenotype.

**Gut dysbiosis and intestinal inflammation.** Autistic children carry distinctive gut microbiome signatures — reduced diversity, increased Clostridia, biomarkers consistent with increased intestinal permeability and chronic mild inflammation. Sophie Lamb's anecdote on the 2019 [[2019-07-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-herbalist-sophie-lamb|*Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] — "One friend's son would go into 'stimming' — repetitive physical movements. A pediatrician prescribed a strong antifungal because it was suspected he had a fungal overgrowth producing alcohol in his system. The antifungals hugely modified his behavior" — is a single case, but it points at a mechanism the gastroenterology literature is now seriously investigating: yeast and bacterial metabolites crossing a compromised intestinal barrier and producing behavioral effects through the same pathways the [[endotoxin|endotoxin]] article describes for sicker adults.

None of these is "the cause" in isolation. Their combination is what the framework treats as the disorder.

## Peat's causal cluster

Peat's writing on autism is sparse — a brief email entry, the 2019 Sophie Lamb exchange, and the broader brain-stress-syndrome frame from *Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities*. The picture the corpus assembles is consistent with his accounts of [[adhd|ADHD]], [[allergies-asthma|allergies and asthma]], and the broader developmental cluster, with heavier emphasis on the gestational window. Six contributors stack.

**Maternal PUFA load.** The fetal brain is built from what the mother provides. A diet heavy in seed oils, fish-oil supplements, and "essential fatty acid" advice gives the developing brain a membrane lipid composition saturated with linoleic and arachidonic acid — the same substrate Peat tied to the secular rise in childhood allergy ("allergic women have babies born with higher polyunsaturated fatty acid content, and those babies are more likely to develop allergies at an early age"). The [[insomnia-and-hyperactivity|*Insomnia & Hyperactivity*]] newsletter (1994) connected this to behavior directly: "The increased use of unsaturated vegetable oils (proven to cause brain damage in rats) has certainly played a role. The more unsaturated an oil is, the more strongly it interferes with thyroid secretion, the transport of thyroid hormone in the blood, and the response of the tissue thyroid receptors. Hypothyroidism, both prenatally and during childhood, severely limits development of the brain." The mechanism runs through both substrate availability for prostaglandins and the oxidative damage PUFA does to membrane and mitochondrial function.

**Maternal hypothyroidism and environmental estrogen.** "Environmental estrogens, things that cause hypothyroidism, and obesity are known causes," Peat said on the 2019 Sophie Lamb episode when asked directly. Maternal hypothyroidism during early second trimester is the best-documented endocrine risk factor for neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring, with a small but consistent effect even in mainstream cohorts. Xenoestrogens from plastics, residue from medications and personal-care products, and the elevated endogenous estrogen of an obese mother compound the same axis: estrogen suppresses respiration, raises histamine and serotonin tone, and disrupts the cytoplasmic-water and electrical-gradient substrate the [[developmental-gradient|developmental-gradient]] article documents as the physical state the fetus inherits.

**In-utero stress and prenatal medicalization.** The autism email leads with the in-utero-stress hypothesis: "I suspect that it's an adaptive reaction to prenatal exposure to stress. The imbalances of endorphins, serotonin, catecholamines, and other nerve-regulators that have been seen in autism sometimes can be produced in adults by combined fatigue and poor nutrition, and when the liver's glycogen is depleted, it can be hard to restore the balance. Prenatal influences of different types could damage connectivity, while permitting cells to survive. Normally, a large proportion of brain cells die before birth, because of limited availability of glucose." The framing is unusually subtle. Peat is not describing autism as a destruction of brain cells. He is describing it as a *modification of the developmental pruning program* — cells that would normally have been culled in utero are instead retained, the cortical-hyperconnectivity finding derived from a cytoplasmic-energy mechanism rather than from a genetic specification. On the 2019 episode he extended the point to clinical practice: "A major source of adversity during pregnancy is medical radiation or medicalization in general. This includes too many X-ray exams, bad thyroid or endocrine therapy, and the use of toxic drugs. In Texas, a study showed Latino kids had a much lower rate of autism than the medicalized white residents."

**Vitamin K and nutrient adequacy at birth.** Peat's framework treated [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]] as a quietly essential cofactor in newborn coagulation, calcium handling, and broader carboxylation chemistry. The [[child-feeding|child-feeding]] article notes that "Standard intramuscular K at delivery is consistent with Peat's framework — protective against newborn hemorrhagic disease, supports later calcium handling." This matters because the decades-long alternative-medicine campaign against the K shot is not a Peat position. Hemorrhagic disease of the newborn — including the late-onset intracranial-bleed form that can present at 2-12 weeks — is a real and preventable cause of neurological injury, and refusing K is not a coherent move within the framework. Where Peat-aligned and conventional positions diverge is on adjacent practices (cord-clamping timing, the post-natal feeding sequence, soy-formula avoidance) rather than on the K shot itself.

**Gut dysbiosis and the serotonin-behavior link in infancy.** Infant gut colonization is shaped by mode of delivery, feeding choice, early antibiotic exposure, and the maternal microbiome that seeded the system. A dysbiotic gut produces elevated endotoxin, elevated serotonin, and the chronic low-grade inflammatory tone the [[endotoxin|endotoxin]] article documents. In a developing infant this is not "irritable bowel and a mood symptom"; it is a steady state the developing nervous system is taking instructions from. The Sophie Lamb anecdote describes the mechanism operating in a child already at the autistic presentation; the broader framework claim is that the same mechanism contributes to producing the presentation in the first place.

**Mitochondrial dysfunction.** The ~30% of autistic individuals with biochemical markers of mitochondrial dysfunction are, in Peat's frame, a subgroup whose primary problem is the oxidative-metabolism block he traced to PUFA accumulation, hypothyroidism, and chronic endotoxin tone. Interventions the mainstream metabolic-psychiatry literature is testing — carnitine, coenzyme Q10, biotin, B-vitamin packages, ketogenic-style approaches — are partially aligned and partially in tension. Ketogenic strategies, which help in some seizure-prone autistic subgroups, sit awkwardly against Peat's preference for adequate glucose to keep cortisol and free-fatty-acid mobilization down. The framework prediction is that low-PUFA, adequate-thyroid, adequate-glucose, low-endotoxin support produces similar mitochondrial improvement without the cortisol cost of chronic ketosis.

## The vaccine question

This is the part of the framework that has cost the most signal-to-noise across three decades, and it deserves careful representation.

The mainstream position is that the vaccine-autism hypothesis is closed. Andrew Wakefield's 1998 *Lancet* paper claiming an MMR-autism connection was retracted in 2010 after the journal concluded the findings were dishonestly presented and Wakefield had undisclosed financial conflicts; he was struck off the UK medical register. Subsequent large epidemiological studies — Danish registries covering more than half a million children, Madsen et al. (2002), Hviid et al. (2019) — have found no association between MMR vaccination and autism diagnosis. Thimerosal, the mercury-containing preservative once present in most US pediatric vaccines, was phased out of the routine childhood schedule between 1999 and 2001; autism diagnosis rates continued to rise. On those specific questions — does MMR cause autism, does thimerosal in pediatric vaccines cause autism — the answer the medical community has reached is no.

Peat's position was distinct from Wakefield's and is not refuted by the epidemiology that closed the Wakefield question. He raised three related but separable concerns across the 2014 [[2014-06-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vaccination-i|Vaccination I]] and [[2014-07-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vaccination-ii|Vaccination II]] episodes.

The first is about adjuvants. The aluminum salts used in many pediatric vaccines work, in the current immunological understanding, by activating the innate inflammatory system — the "danger theory" Polly Matzinger and Jamie Cunliffe developed in the 1990s. Peat read this as a real mechanism with potentially long-tail consequences: "There is a growing awareness that the adjuvant is there for the purpose of creating a generalized inflammation. This is where the long-range view of 'doing no harm' is being neglected. The embryological approach sees that what happens early in development will have permanent, lifelong effects. If you introduce an inflammatory state early in life, you have to wait 20, 30, or 40 years to see the effects. That hasn't been done." The claim is not that aluminum-adjuvant vaccines cause autism in the Wakefield sense; it is that the long-tail health effects of inflammation introduced during a developmental window have not been adequately studied.

The second is about thimerosal — not as the mercury-mass-as-toxin question the medical community closed, but as a presentation-of-toxin question Peat thought the closure missed. From the 2014 Vaccination I episode: "Some people focus on the amount of mercury as a toxin, but it is really the way the tiny amount of toxin or adjuvant is presented that causes the problem. For example, kids whose mothers ate lots of seafood often have high IQs along with a high body load of mercury. In a healthy body, mercury isn't necessarily toxic. But when presented in a way that activates these adaptive defensive reactions, it can lead to autoimmunity." The framing inverts the standard dose-toxicity model: the question is not how much mercury was in the dose, but what inflammatory state it was injected into and what developmental window the infant was in — a position the mercury-mass epidemiology was not designed to test.

The third is about timing and cumulative immune challenge. The current US schedule administers more than two dozen vaccine antigens in the first six months of life and 70+ by school age. The framework prediction is not that any single vaccine causes any single autism case, but that the cumulative inflammatory load — administered when brain substrate is being laid down, gut microbiome is being established, and the innate immune system is being trained — interacts with the maternal-PUFA / maternal-hypothyroid / gut-dysbiosis stack above to produce, in a subset of vulnerable infants, the disrupted developmental trajectory the field calls autism. The formal comparison — a randomized trial of the current US schedule against a delayed or attenuated schedule — has not been done and almost certainly will not be done. What exists is population epidemiology Peat regarded as too coarse to detect a vulnerable subgroup, plus ecological comparisons whose confounders make them difficult to interpret either way.

The honest summary: Peat's vaccine concern is empirically more nuanced than the Wakefield position the medical community closed; the specific safety questions he raised (cumulative aluminum, timing in vulnerable infants, developmental-window inflammation) have not been adequately answered by the studies that closed the Wakefield question; and the framework remains a minority position that a thoughtful clinician should treat as a hypothesis worth considering rather than a settled matter. A family making vaccine decisions is doing so under genuine uncertainty: the diseases the schedule is designed to prevent are real and historically have killed children, the immediate adverse-event profile is well-characterized, the long-tail developmental questions Peat raised remain partially open, and the right call depends on the specific child, the family situation, and the clinician relationship. This article does not tell families what to decide. It documents a framework that is more careful than its reputation and less certain than either side of the public debate has wanted to admit.

## Peat's intervention frame

Peat's autism intervention recommendations are explicit in the email entry: "Since autism typically involves high serotonin, things like thyroid, lisuride, tianeptine, and gelatin could be helpful." The fuller frame, assembled from adjacent protocols, runs in parallel across several axes.

**Serotonin reduction.** [[Cyproheptadine|Cyproheptadine]] is the single most useful pharmaceutical in Peat's serotonin-and-histamine toolkit — fractional doses (half a milligram to two milligrams, typically at bedtime) provide H1 and 5-HT2 blockade without the side-effect tail of stronger antipsychotics or SSRIs. The autism email names cyproheptadine's cousins lisuride and tianeptine; both are harder to access in the US and carry their own profiles. Cyproheptadine is the entry point, dosed and monitored by a pediatrician or developmental-pediatrics specialist familiar with the molecule.

**Thyroid evaluation.** Subclinical and clinical hypothyroidism are common in children with autism, ADHD-like presentations, and the broader brain-stress family, and the standard pediatric thyroid screen often misses what waking-temperature-and-pulse tracking would catch. The intervention is not "give every autistic child thyroid" but "screen thoroughly with morning temperature, mid-morning temperature, resting pulse, and a full thyroid panel including reverse-T3, and treat when the picture is hypothyroid." Pediatric endocrinology coordination is non-optional.

**Gut decontamination.** The [[endotoxin-reduction|endotoxin-reduction]] protocol applied at child scale. Daily raw [[carrot-salad|carrot salad]] is the starting move; well-cooked white mushrooms and a small amount of bamboo shoot rotate in if tolerated. The Sophie Lamb anecdote about the antifungal that "hugely modified his behavior" points at the next layer: a clinician-supervised antifungal or low-dose-antibiotic course can produce dramatic short-term improvements in a child whose gut has been colonized in a way that drives the symptom load. The long-term move is dietary — fermentable-fiber load down, PUFA load down, gelatin and broth load up.

**Vitamin A, B vitamins, magnesium.** Adequate vitamin A (from liver, egg yolk, dairy fat, not from high-dose retinyl-palmitate supplements) supports the gut barrier and thyroid axis. B1 (thiamine), B3 (niacinamide), and B2 (riboflavin) support oxidative metabolism in the mitochondrial-dysfunction subgroup. Magnesium (300-400 mg/day as glycinate or chloride, scaled to body weight) addresses the deficiency that shows up consistently in autistic-cohort studies.

**Sun, light, warmth.** A child whose body temperature runs low, who is chronically over-bundled in screens and indoor air-conditioning, and who does not get morning sunlight is a child whose oxidative metabolism is under-supported. Daily outdoor time, sunlight on skin when seasonally available, warm baths in the evening, and a warm bedroom for sleep are the lowest-cost, highest-yield environmental interventions.

**Low-PUFA child diet.** The [[child-feeding|child-feeding]] protocol applies in full and is the load-bearing dietary intervention. Whole milk (not soy formula), egg yolks, ripe fruit, gelatin and bone broth, small amounts of liver, butter and coconut oil instead of seed oils, no fish-oil supplementation, late and minimal grain introduction. This is not a "GFCF diet" in the standard alternative-autism sense; the framework treats dairy as foundational unless a child has documented cow's-milk-protein allergy, and treats restrictive elimination diets without addressing gut dysbiosis as a mistake.

**Methylene blue microdose if mitochondrial signal is present.** In the subgroup with biochemical markers of mitochondrial dysfunction, low-dose [[methylene-blue|methylene blue]] is one of the interventions Peat's broader corpus supports as an electron-transport-chain bypass. Pediatric dosing is not well-established in the literature; this is a specialist-coordination decision, not a parent-driven supplement choice.

**Low-dose aspirin in specific contexts.** Pediatric aspirin is constrained by the Reye's-syndrome concern in viral illness, and routine prescription to children is appropriately conservative. In clinician-supervised situations with no viral illness and no contraindications, low-dose [[aspirin|aspirin]] addresses the prostaglandin and serotonin tone the framework treats as central. This belongs with a pediatrician.

## Maternal preconception preparation

The highest-leverage intervention in the framework happens before conception. If the contributors above stack to produce the developmental trajectory, then preparing the maternal substrate before the fetus is present is the prevention strategy with the most physical leverage. The full protocol lives at [[pregnancy-nutrition|pregnancy-nutrition]] and at [[fertility|fertility]]; the autism-relevant summary is short.

A woman planning pregnancy in the next 6-12 months establishes the Peat-diet baseline: three quarts of milk daily or equivalent dairy, four to eight ounces of orange juice, eggs, salt to taste, gelatin or bone broth daily, liver weekly, ripe fruit at every meal, no PUFA in the form of seed oils or fish-oil supplements. Thyroid is evaluated thoroughly and treated to range if the picture is hypothyroid. Environmental estrogen exposure is minimized — non-plastic food containers, avoidance of fragranced personal-care products and household chemicals, avoidance of hormonal contraceptives in the months immediately preceding conception. Cumulative stress is addressed — sleep quality, blood-sugar stability, life-context that determines cortisol baseline. Progesterone supplementation is considered for women whose tracking shows a low luteal phase. Dental and medical workup happens before pregnancy, not during, so that X-rays and treatments Peat flagged as "medical adversity during pregnancy" are out of the way before the developmental window opens.

The framework prediction is that the cumulative effect of 6-12 months of this baseline is larger than any single intervention introduced after the child is born. The strongest version of the claim is the [[developmental-gradient|developmental-gradient]] argument: what the mother loads into the oocyte and the early uterine environment is the cytoplasmic substrate the fetal brain organizes itself against, and the maternal-preconception window is when the substrate is built.

## What to avoid

**Long-term antipsychotics in children when alternatives exist.** Risperidone and aripiprazole are FDA-approved for autism-associated irritability and aggression. They work, in the narrow sense that they suppress targeted behaviors. They also carry significant metabolic side effects (weight gain, type 2 diabetes, lipid abnormalities, hyperprolactinemia, extrapyramidal symptoms, tardive dyskinesia) that compound across years of pediatric use. There are clinical situations — severe self-injury, family-safety crises — where the calculus favors them. The frame's argument is to exhaust the cyproheptadine-thyroid-gut interventions first, and to treat antipsychotic prescription as a serious step rather than a routine one.

**SSRIs in children.** The serotonin-elevation mechanism above is the central reason. The pediatric SSRI evidence base is weaker than the adult evidence base, the suicidal-ideation black-box warning in adolescents reflects a real signal, and the framework prediction is that adding to an already-elevated serotonin tone will worsen the underlying disorder. Specific comorbid presentations may shift the calculus, but routine SSRI prescription to children on the autism spectrum is the intervention the framework most consistently flags.

**Restrictive elimination diets without addressing the gut.** The "autism diet" literature has produced GFCF, SCD, GAPS, Feingold, and dozens of variants. Where children improve, the improvement almost always reflects underlying gut-and-inflammatory mechanisms rather than the specific allergen the diet is named after. Removing dairy from a child who responds well to it — the central Peat-diet calorie source for a developing nervous system — to chase a casein theory that may or may not apply is a mistake. Get the gut workup, identify the actual mechanism, and adjust the diet to address that mechanism rather than to satisfy a category.

**Chronic chelation.** Chelation therapy (EDTA, DMSA, DMPS) for autism is associated with documented pediatric deaths and a long tail of adverse events. The framework does not endorse it. Heavy metal accumulation, where present, is addressed through gut barrier restoration, adequate copper and zinc, adequate selenium, and the metabolic-floor interventions above — not by aggressive mobilization of stored metals in a child whose excretory capacity may not handle the load.

**Experimental stem-cell therapies.** A handful of clinics offer stem-cell infusions for autism, often at high cost in jurisdictions with limited regulatory oversight. The framework does not endorse them. Families considering these interventions should do so only inside formal clinical trials with appropriate oversight.

## Tracking signals

Useful markers for tracking change, daily to weekly:

- **Behavioral.** Stimming frequency and intensity, transitions handled smoothly versus crisis, sensory tolerance, eye contact and joint attention, language frequency and complexity. Trajectory across weeks and months matters more than any single day.
- **GI symptoms.** Bowel pattern, gas, bloating, appetite. The earliest and most reliable indicator that gut decontamination is working.
- **Sleep.** Time to fall asleep, night wakings, restlessness, dream-disturbed versus deep sleep, morning state. The pattern Peat described in [[insomnia-and-hyperactivity|*Insomnia & Hyperactivity*]] — the hypothyroid child who cannot relax into sleep — is one of the most diagnostic signals.
- **Sensory tolerance.** Reactions to sounds, lights, fabrics, food textures. Often expands before other measures shift.
- **Speech and communication.** Vocabulary growth, receptive comprehension, intentional communication. The slowest of the trackable measures, and the most family-meaningful.
- **Waking and mid-morning temperature.** The hypothyroidism signal. Target waking ~97.4-97.8°F, mid-morning ~98.5°F when age-appropriate.

Tracking signals, not diagnostic criteria. The pediatrician and developmental specialist remain responsible for formal diagnosis and re-assessment.

## Counter-arguments

**Genetic-determinism orthodoxy and 80%+ heritability claims.** Twin studies put autism heritability in the 0.7-0.9 range, GWAS has identified over 100 risk loci, and rare de novo mutations account for a measurable share of cases. The mainstream conclusion: autism is fundamentally genetic. The Peat-framework counter is the same as the [[developmental-gradient|developmental-gradient]] counter to genetic determinism generally — heritability estimates describe within-population variance under stable environmental conditions, not the share of etiology attributable to genes when the environment shifts. The sixty-fold rise in diagnostic prevalence across two generations cannot be explained by changes in gene frequency, and the dietary, environmental, and medical shifts of the same period are the obvious candidate explanations. Two decades of "the autism gene" pursuit have produced risk loci, none of which has translated into a useful intervention. The framework prediction — that interventions targeting metabolism and inflammation will produce more clinical benefit than interventions targeting genes — is consistent with what the metabolic-psychiatry and microbiome literatures are finding.

**ABA as the only evidence-based intervention.** Applied behavior analysis has the strongest formal evidence base in the developmental-pediatrics literature. The framework critique is not that ABA does not work — there is genuine evidence that intensive early behavioral intervention improves outcomes — but that ABA addresses the observable behavioral output of a metabolic-and-inflammatory underlying state, and that addressing the underlying state in parallel produces gains ABA alone cannot. A growing autistic-adult community has articulated the further critique that older "Lovaas-style" ABA protocols produced documented psychological harm. The framework's position is that genuine behavioral intervention layered on top of metabolic correction is the integrated approach; either alone leaves leverage on the table.

**Psychiatric-medication-first approach.** The mainstream practice for autism-associated irritability, aggression, anxiety, and ADHD-like presentations is to medicate. The framework critique — that these molecules address downstream symptoms while compounding the underlying metabolic and inflammatory state — is the central tension. There are clinical situations where the calculus favors medication, but the framework argues for exhausting cyproheptadine-thyroid-gut-diet interventions first.

**The neurodiversity position: autism as identity, not disease.** The autistic-adult community has articulated a sophisticated case that autism is a way of being rather than a disorder to be cured — that the cognitive and sensory differences produce both costs and genuine gifts, that the "cure" framing pathologizes existence, and that interventions should support autistic functioning rather than aim at normalization. This position has real weight, particularly for the higher-functioning portion of the spectrum where adult outcomes can be excellent. The framework engages with it respectfully: where an individual is doing well, the framework does not argue for intervention; where an individual is suffering — severe self-injury, non-verbal frustration, GI pain, sleep disruption, family crisis — the framework treats the suffering as something to address rather than something to accept as identity. The frame is symptom-driven, not category-driven. A child whose autism comes with severe GI inflammation has a treatable medical problem alongside the developmental difference, and treating the medical problem is not the same as trying to cure the difference. The two framings can coexist.

**The vaccine-autism epidemiology question.** Covered above. The summary: the specific Wakefield MMR-autism hypothesis is closed; the broader timing-and-adjuvant questions Peat raised are not closed by the studies that closed the Wakefield question; the framework treats the open questions as worth taking seriously without endorsing the stronger anti-vaccine positions that have proliferated in the alternative-medicine space.

## Data gaps

The honest summary of the framework's evidence base is that it is thin.

**Etiology heterogeneity.** Autism is almost certainly a heterogeneous condition with multiple distinct etiological pathways — a mitochondrial subgroup, a gut-dysbiosis subgroup, a maternal-immune-activation subgroup, a syndromic genetic subgroup (Fragile X, Rett, tuberous sclerosis), a broader-autism-phenotype subgroup where the developmental and metabolic contributors are subtle. The framework treats these as overlapping rather than mutually exclusive, but the formal sub-typing that would let a clinician say "this is the mitochondrial subgroup, treat accordingly" is not mature. The biomarker panels exist in research settings but are not standardly available.

**Intervention RCTs are absent.** There are no randomized controlled trials of the Peat-aligned framework against standard of care in autism. The closest existing evidence comes from the metabolic-psychiatry literature, the microbiome-fecal-transplant pilot studies (which have shown durable behavioral improvements in some cohorts), the cyproheptadine literature in adjacent serotonin-elevation conditions, and the pediatric thyroid literature. None of this is autism-specific in a way that would convince a skeptical evidence-based-medicine clinician.

**Peat-corpus autism data is light.** The framework is constructed largely by extrapolation from adjacent conditions, with the autism-specific anchors being thin — an email entry, a brief 2019 exchange, scattered references in the broader brain-stress-syndrome literature. A reader treating Peat's writing as the primary evidence base should know that the autism-specific writing is thinner than the writing on dementia, on heart disease, or on most of the other conditions the wiki documents.

**The autism-cohort metabolic biomarker work has not been done at necessary scale.** Existing work (Frye, James, the Arkansas group) has produced consistent signals but at sample sizes the broader field has not taken as definitive. A properly-powered study comparing standard pediatric care plus comprehensive metabolic-and-microbiome workup with treatment, against standard care alone, would settle a great deal of what the framework currently asserts on lower-confidence grounds.

**The vaccine timing question has not been adequately studied.** The formal randomized comparison of the current US schedule against an attenuated or delayed schedule has not been done and almost certainly will not be done. Peat's open empirical question — whether the cumulative early-life inflammatory load contributes to a subset of autism cases — is not resolved by the studies that closed the MMR-autism question, and the evidence that would resolve it is not on a research agenda.

## Safety

Documentation, not medical advice. Autism is a diagnosable medical condition that affects life trajectory in significant ways, and the interventions a family chooses on a child's behalf carry weight. Specific boundaries:

- **Pediatric specialist coordination is essential.** A developmental pediatrician or pediatric neurologist familiar with the formal diagnostic process is the right person to confirm or refine the diagnosis. A pediatrician willing to work the metabolic and gastrointestinal side is the right person to coordinate the Peat-framework interventions. Without that coordination, parents are alone with high-stakes decisions in a domain where the evidence base is mixed.
- **The vaccine discussion is the family's decision to make in coordination with their pediatrician.** This article documents Peat's framework, which raises specific concerns about timing and adjuvants without endorsing categorical anti-vaccine positions. The diseases the schedule is designed to prevent are real. Measles, pertussis, Hib meningitis, diphtheria, and tetanus have killed children in living memory and continue to kill children where vaccination rates fall. Families weighing this decision should do so with a clinician they trust.
- **Acute regression, seizures, severe self-injury, feeding refusal, or sudden behavioral crisis is a medical emergency.** The pediatrician or hospital is the right call, not the wiki.
- **Cyproheptadine, thyroid, methylene blue, and aspirin in children are clinician-supervised interventions.** The framework rationale is documented; dosing belongs with a pediatrician.
- **Restrictive dietary changes in a growing child carry real risk.** Caloric and protein adequacy, micronutrient sufficiency, and the foundational role of dairy in the Peat-diet pediatric template are not optional. Restrictive diets without pediatric and nutritional supervision can do more harm than the underlying condition.
- **Parent autonomy and family wisdom are real.** Parents living with an autistic child accumulate knowledge of their child's patterns that no clinician will match. The framework respects that knowledge. It does not displace medical care, and it does not dismiss parental observation.
