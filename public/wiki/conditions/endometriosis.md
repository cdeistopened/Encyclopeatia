# Endometriosis

The orthodox view of endometriosis treats it as a mystery — uterine-lining tissue inexplicably growing on the ovaries, the peritoneum, the bowel, the bladder, occasionally the lung and the brain. It is described as autoimmune, as inflammatory, as "incurable." It is staged surgically. It is treated by chemical menopause (GnRH agonists like Lupron), by surgical excision, by hysterectomy with bilateral oophorectomy in the limit case. The 1920s theory of John Sampson — that menstrual blood refluxes through the fallopian tubes and seeds the peritoneal cavity with endometrial cells — remains the textbook explanation despite decades of falsifying observations: that retrograde menstruation occurs in ~90% of menstruating women but endometriosis in only ~10%; that endometriosis appears in fetuses, in newborns, in women born without a uterus, and (rarely) in men on estrogen therapy; that the disease has been documented in tissues no menstrual blood could ever reach.

Ray Peat's framework absorbs all of these anomalies into a single mechanism. The endometriotic lesion is not displaced uterine tissue. It is locally-formed tissue in a state of chronic estrogen stimulation, expressing the [[aromatase]] enzyme that synthesizes estrogen out of circulating androgens, expressing the cyclooxygenase enzyme that converts [[pufa|polyunsaturated fatty acids]] into [[prostaglandins]], and locked into a feed-forward inflammatory loop that mainstream gynecology lacks the conceptual vocabulary to describe. The disease is, in his words, "caused by a thyroid deficiency with estrogen excess" — and the clinical track record he reported is consistent enough that he repeatedly used phrases like "very high success rate" and "very first day" in describing what happens when the metabolic cause is corrected.

This article concerns endometriosis as a metabolic condition, not as a surgical one. For the underlying estrogen physiology see [[estrogen]]; for the protective hormone see [[progesterone]]; for the inflammatory amplifier see [[inflammation]]; for the environmental contribution see [[xenoestrogens]]; for the self-synthesis enzyme see [[aromatase]].

## Mechanism: the aromatase-prostaglandin loop

Endometriosis is one expression of what Peat called *tissue-bound estrogen* — estrogen made and used locally, never appearing at high concentrations in the blood, often missed entirely by standard hormone panels. The endometriotic lesion is a fragment of tissue that has lost its ability to oscillate.

Peat described the normal endometrial cycle as a brief, tightly-bounded estrogen pulse followed by a much larger progesterone surge. From the [Eastwest Healing interview, July 2013](app/public/transcripts/eastwest-healing/polished/2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism.md): "In endometrial tissue, it should be exposed to estrogen for only about 12 hours each month. Then progesterone should surge up to 50 or 100 times higher concentration and cause the cells to give up their production of estrogen. But instead, the aromatase keeps churning out estrogen. The estrogen excites the tissue and turns on the cyclooxygenase enzyme, which turns polyunsaturated fatty acids into prostaglandins, creating inflammation that causes the pain and contractions. The inflammation from the prostaglandin in turn creates more aromatase. So it gets a vicious cycle going."

That single passage compresses the entire pathophysiology. Trace the loop:

1. **Estrogen** stimulates cell division and motility in the endometrial cells. It also activates cyclooxygenase (COX-2).
2. **Cyclooxygenase** converts circulating and stored [[pufa|polyunsaturated fatty acids]] into prostaglandins — primarily PGE2 and PGF2α.
3. **Prostaglandins** drive the pain (uterine contractions, cramping, the pelvic ache that defines clinical endometriosis), the inflammation, the angiogenesis (new blood vessel formation feeding the lesion), and — crucially — they upregulate aromatase expression.
4. **Aromatase** synthesizes more estrogen locally from circulating androgens (androstenedione, testosterone). It does this in fat cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, breast and uterine tissue — and, when the loop is established, in the endometriotic lesion itself.
5. **The lesion produces its own estrogen**, which stimulates more cyclooxygenase, more prostaglandins, more aromatase. The system no longer needs ovarian estrogen to maintain itself.

From the 1996 newsletter [*Tissue-Bound Estrogen in Aging*](sources/newsletters/tissue-bound-estrogen-in-aging.md): "Aromatase can produce estrogen in fat cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, breast and uterine tissue, pancreas, liver, brain, bone, skin, etc. Its action in breast cancer, endometriosis, uterine cancer, lupus, gynecomastia, and many other diseases is especially important." This is the master conceptual move — estrogen as a *tissue* hormone, not just a *blood* hormone. Standard serum estradiol panels can read normal or low while a peritoneal lesion is bathing itself in concentrations 50 or 100 times systemic.

Peat cited the work of Zeitoun and Bulun specifically. The endometriotic lesion is not just an aromatase-expressing tissue — it has also *lost* the enzyme that would normally inactivate estradiol. Quoting from the appendix to the [*Tissue-Bound Estrogen*](sources/articles/tissue-bound-estrogen-in-aging.md) article (Zeitoun et al. 1998): "Aberrant aromatase expression in stromal cells of endometriosis gives rise to conversion of circulating androstenedione to estrone in this tissue, whereas aromatase expression is absent in the eutopic endometrium... It was previously demonstrated that progesterone stimulates the inactivation of 17β-estradiol through conversion to estrone in eutopic endometrial epithelial cells. In conclusion, inactivation of 17β-estradiol is impaired in endometriotic tissues due to deficient expression of 17βHSD-2, which is normally expressed in eutopic endometrium in response to progesterone."

The phrase to hold is "in response to progesterone." In the normal cycle, [[progesterone]] does two protective things at once: it inhibits aromatase (so less estrogen is made), and it activates 17β-HSD type 2 (so any estradiol that *is* made gets converted back to weakly-estrogenic estrone). The endometriotic lesion has escaped both arms of the brake. It is making estradiol it cannot inactivate.

## Why the lesion appears outside the uterus

Sampson's retrograde-menstruation theory locates the disease in misplaced cells. Peat's framework locates it in chronic estrogen and the [[aromatase]] enzyme's ubiquity. If aromatase is present "in fat cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, breast and uterine tissue, pancreas, liver, brain, bone, skin, etc.," then any tissue under sufficient estrogen stress can take on endometriotic character. The cells do not need to migrate. They need to dedifferentiate.

This view is broadly consistent with the older *coelomic metaplasia* hypothesis (associated with Robert Meyer and later Iwanoff): the peritoneal lining, the ovarian surface epithelium, and the endometrium all derive from the same embryonic coelomic epithelium, and any of them can undergo metaplastic differentiation back toward endometrium-like tissue under estrogenic stimulus. Coelomic metaplasia is the standard textbook explanation for the rare cases that Sampson's theory cannot accommodate — endometriosis in girls before menarche, endometriosis in fetuses, endometriosis in men receiving estrogen. Peat's contribution is to point out that the mechanism it requires (local estrogen activation of normally-dormant developmental programs) is exactly the mechanism that explains the common cases as well.

The 1985 newsletter [*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*](sources/articles/immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones.md) folds in the environmental dimension: "Endometriosis is stimulated by dioxins. Environmental estrogens affect the immune system." [[xenoestrogens]] — dioxin, DDT, PCBs, bisphenol A, the plasticizers and pesticides that have steadily increased in the environment since the 1940s — accumulate in fat, persist for decades, and produce a chronic background of estrogen-receptor activation that no woman in the modern industrial world entirely escapes. The Endometriosis Association's collaboration with rhesus-monkey studies at Wisconsin established that dioxin exposure in colony animals produced endometriosis at rates proportional to dose. This is not a small finding; it is the experimental confirmation of an environmental driver that Peat had been describing since the 1980s.

## Mainstream framing and its inversions

Standard gynecology teaches endometriosis as:

- **Cause:** Sampson's retrograde menstruation, with autoimmune or genetic contributions; ultimate cause unknown.
- **Diagnosis:** definitive only via laparoscopy with biopsy; surgical staging I–IV.
- **Treatment, first-line:** combined oral contraceptives (suppress ovulation), NSAIDs (suppress pain).
- **Treatment, second-line:** progestins (synthetic, not progesterone — typically norethindrone or medroxyprogesterone), the levonorgestrel IUD.
- **Treatment, third-line:** GnRH agonists ([[lupron|Lupron]], leuprolide, nafarelin) — induce a reversible chemical menopause by downregulating the pituitary gonadotropin axis. Newer GnRH antagonists (elagolix) target the same pathway more rapidly.
- **Treatment, surgical:** laparoscopic excision or ablation of visible lesions; hysterectomy with or without oophorectomy in the limit case.
- **Prognosis:** "incurable," "chronic," with recurrence rates of 20-50% within five years of surgery.

Each of these inverts cleanly under Peat's framework, and each inversion is worth tracing.

**On Sampson's theory.** Sampson observed retrograde menstruation in monkeys in 1927 and inferred causation from association. The observation has held; the inference has not. Roughly 90% of menstruating women show retrograde flow; roughly 10% develop endometriosis. The cells that reflux are also present in women without the disease. The theory cannot account for prepubertal cases, fetal cases, neonatal cases, the cases in agenesis-of-the-uterus patients (Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser), or the rare male cases. Peat's framework absorbs all of these: what matters is not whether endometrial cells are present, but whether any locally-competent tissue is exposed to chronic unopposed estrogen long enough to take on lesion character.

**On chemical menopause.** Lupron and the other GnRH agonists work by saturating the GnRH receptor at the pituitary, causing eventual downregulation of LH and FSH secretion, ovarian quiescence, and a drop in circulating estradiol. The intervention does shrink lesions and reduce pain — for some patients, for the duration of treatment. The drug's six-month courses are usually presented as time-limited because of the rapid bone density loss they cause; the loss compounds the underlying mechanism Peat described in [*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*](sources/articles/bone-density-first-do-no-harm.md), where suppression of the protective hormones (progesterone, thyroid, androgens) is itself osteopenic.

Peat's deeper objection is that Lupron treats blood estrogen and ignores tissue estrogen. The endometriotic lesion expresses its own aromatase. Suppressing ovarian estradiol does not stop the lesion from synthesizing its own. The dramatic post-treatment recurrence rates are predictable from this. The 1994 newsletter [*Estriol, DES, DDT, etc.*](sources/newsletters/estriol-des-ddt-etc.md) notes that DES — a synthetic estrogen — was historically prescribed for endometriosis, "amenorrhea, oligomenorrhea, dysmenorrhea," and a dozen other conditions, often at doses that would be considered abusive today. The intellectual move that allowed both DES-for-endometriosis in 1950 and Lupron-for-endometriosis in 2000 is the same move: treat the symptom by manipulating one estrogen variable while leaving the underlying physiology alone.

**On hysterectomy.** The mainstream framing treats hysterectomy as a definitive intervention for endometriosis — extreme but curative. The data don't support that. Endometriotic lesions outside the uterus continue to function after hysterectomy. Oophorectomy reduces but does not eliminate them (because of aromatase activity in fat, adrenal androgens, and tissue-bound estrogen). The 1989 newsletter [*Abnormal Cells in the Uterine Cervix*](sources/newsletters/abnormal-cells-in-the-uterine-cervix.md) contains a passage that bears directly on the broader hysterectomy critique: "I personally had contact with 35 women who were advised to have a hysterectomy because of abnormal cells found in Pap smears or biopsies, but who wanted to try this approach for a month or two. All of those women had normal tests when they went back to the doctor, usually in two months." The same pattern — surgery proposed on tissue evidence that was reversible under metabolic correction — recurs throughout Peat's clinical observations.

The 2022 [*Ask the Herb Doctor*](app/public/transcripts/ask-the-herb-doctor/polished/2022-05-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-may-2022-kmud.md) episode contains a striking confirmation. The caller's wife had been advised toward hysteroscopy and possible hysterectomy after prolonged bleeding. Peat responded: "I tracked about 36 women who were told they needed a biopsy or partial hysterectomy due to abnormal cells. Within a month of using supplements, including thyroid, every one of those 36 women went back and the doctors could find no evidence of abnormal cells." A second caller in the same episode reported personal vindication: "I had a similar situation five years ago. I went to the hospital, and they wanted to do a hysterectomy. I used Progest-E (the kind Ray recommends), Cynoplus, and Cytomel. As soon as I got my T3 up, the bleeding stopped... They wanted to do radiation and drugs that caused blood clots, but I refused."

These are anecdotes, not trials. They are also a consistent pattern across decades of Peat's clinical correspondence — a pattern that the surgical-excision research community (Cook, Sinervo, the late David Redwine and his students) has independently noted: that aggressive metabolic management can put many endometriosis cases into something indistinguishable from remission. Where the surgical proponents differ from Peat is in their belief that the lesion, once formed, must be physically removed before metabolic management can succeed. Peat's position is that the lesion is dynamically sustained by the metabolic state, and that withdrawing the maintenance signal allows the tissue to remodel.

**On the "incurable" framing.** Calling a disease incurable is a clinical posture, not a biological fact. Peat's typical formulation, from the [Eastwest Healing](app/public/transcripts/eastwest-healing/polished/2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism.md) interview: "I've seen people who had been suffering painful endometriosis for years who, in two or three days, completely stopped the symptoms permanently just with quick-acting thyroid, a good diet, and aspirin. Aspirin or other blockers of prostaglandin production all by themselves can greatly reduce the formation of estrogen and break the cycle." The 2020 [*Butter Living Podcast*](app/public/transcripts/butter-living-podcast/polished/2020-02-19-butter-living-podcast-butter-living-podcast-fertility-pregnancy-and-development.md) condensed the position: "The basic problem is low thyroid. When I've seen women correct hypothyroidism, endometriosis symptoms turn off immediately." The 2019 [*One Radio Network*](app/public/transcripts/one-radio-network/polished/2019-10-15-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-a-plethora-of-wide-randing-questions.md): "All of the women I've known who had endometriosis stopped having symptoms when they corrected their thyroid problem. The thyroid should bring the estrogen down under control."

The 2017 [*Ask the Herb Doctor*](app/public/transcripts/ask-the-herb-doctor/polished/2017-09-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-california-proposition-65.md) episode also flagged a contributor mainstream gynecology rarely names: the IUD itself. "It's well established that endometriosis comes from prolonged, excessive exposure to estrogen without interruption from progesterone. The mechanism of a plastic or copper IUD is to send a signal to the ovary that the uterus isn't ready for pregnancy because there is a foreign object in it. The uterus perceives this injury, and the signal travels up the fallopian tube telling the ovary *not* to make progesterone. It is a specific interruption of your hormone ratio, similar to the birth control pill." A device promoted as contraceptive may be removing the protective hormone whose cyclic surge is the body's only built-in defense against endometriosis.

## Protocols: breaking the loop

The five-part intervention follows directly from the mechanism. Each element targets one node of the aromatase-prostaglandin loop.

**1. Thyroid.** Thyroid (T3 or balanced T4/T3 preparations like Armour or Cynoplus) does several things at once. It increases hepatic estrogen clearance, increases progesterone production from the corpus luteum, inhibits aromatase directly, and raises metabolic rate, body temperature, and the overall mitochondrial competence that distinguishes "healthy estrogen-handling tissue" from tissue that goes into lesion. Peat's emphatic tone on this — "the very first day," "every one of those 36 women" — suggests he saw thyroid as more often the rate-limiting intervention than progesterone alone. See [[thyroid]] and [[hypothyroidism]] for the broader frame.

Cynoplus contains T4 and T3; Cytomel is T3 alone. Peat warned in the 2022 [*Ask the Herb Doctor*](app/public/transcripts/ask-the-herb-doctor/polished/2022-05-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-may-2022-kmud.md) episode that T4 has a two-week half-life: "if you try to get a quick effect with a T4-containing product like Armour Thyroid or Cynoplus, you might have a good effect immediately, but you will overdose after four or five days." His usual recommendation was to start small (a quarter or half tablet), to track pulse and temperature daily, and to add T3 (Cytomel) in small increments for symptomatic acute relief while letting T4 build steady state.

**2. Progesterone.** Natural progesterone — not progestins. Peat formulated [[progest-e|Progest-E]] (10% progesterone in vitamin E) precisely because oral progesterone in oil reaches systemic circulation more effectively than transdermal creams or micronized capsules. Progesterone simultaneously inhibits aromatase (cutting estrogen synthesis), induces 17β-HSD2 (catabolizing the estrogen that does form), suppresses cyclooxygenase (cutting prostaglandin formation), opposes estrogen at the receptor, and exerts independent anti-inflammatory effects. The 1995 [*Comparison of Progesterone and Estrogen*](sources/newsletters/comparison-of-progesterone-and-estrogen.md) summarizes the action profile: "Anti-tumor, anti-aging hormone. Shrinks fibroids of breast & uterus, cystic ovaries... Mimics estrogen if needed or inhibits excess estrogen."

Typical doses for active endometriosis are higher than for cycle support — 100-300 mg/day during the luteal phase, sometimes continuously through severe pain phases. Application directly to the affected mucosa (sublingual, vaginal, or vulvar) is part of Peat's standard protocol for uterine and cervical conditions. See [[progesterone]] for the broader framework.

**3. Aspirin.** Aspirin inhibits cyclooxygenase, which cuts the prostaglandin arm of the loop and, indirectly, the aromatase arm. Peat described this as one of the fastest acute interventions. From the [*Eastwest Healing*](app/public/transcripts/eastwest-healing/polished/2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism.md) episode: "Aspirin or other blockers of prostaglandin production all by themselves can greatly reduce the formation of estrogen and break the cycle." Typical Peat-recommended doses for inflammation are higher than the cardiovascular doses most readers know — 325-650 mg multiple times daily, with adequate vitamin K and B vitamins to balance the anti-clotting effect. See [[aspirin]].

**4. Low PUFA, adequate sugar.** The prostaglandins that drive the pain and feed the aromatase loop are synthesized exclusively from polyunsaturated fatty acids — primarily arachidonic acid, secondarily other omega-6 and omega-3 PUFAs. Without the substrate, cyclooxygenase has nothing to convert. Removing dietary PUFA (soybean, corn, safflower, sunflower, canola, fish oils) reduces new prostaglandin formation immediately, but the slow part of the intervention is the depletion of PUFA already stored in adipose tissue, which can take years. From the [*Eastwest Healing*](app/public/transcripts/eastwest-healing/polished/2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism.md) episode: "Sugar is one of the things that can reduce the flow of polyunsaturated fats out of your tissues into your bloodstream. Niacinamide is another thing that limits the release of these fatty acids. So both sugar and niacinamide back up the function of aspirin." See [[pufa]] and the [[pufa-depletion]] protocol.

**5. Xenoestrogen avoidance.** [[xenoestrogens]] — dioxin, DDT residues, PCBs, plasticizers (bisphenol A, phthalates), endocrine-disrupting pesticides — contribute a chronic background load of estrogen-receptor activation that no internal hormone regulation can fully compensate for. Practical avoidance includes filtered water, glass and stainless steel rather than plastic for hot foods and liquids, organic produce where the cost-benefit is favorable (the EWG Dirty Dozen captures the high-residue items), and minimizing personal-care products with parabens, phthalates, and synthetic estrogens. Reducing body fat (where lipid-soluble xenoestrogens are stored) is also part of the long arc, though Peat's framing of weight loss centers on metabolic competence rather than caloric restriction.

**Adjuncts.** Vitamin K (cofactor for liver detoxification of estrogen, also for the carboxylation of bone matrix proteins compromised by estrogen). Vitamin E (antiestrogenic at adequate doses; Peat used 100-400 IU/day as a baseline). Vitamin A (modulates estrogen receptor expression; the carrot in his daily protocol partly serves this function). Magnesium (depleted by stress, needed for the energy metabolism that thyroid is trying to up-regulate). Caffeine (lowers estrogen, raises metabolic rate, increases aromatase clearance — but only if the underlying hypoglycemia/hyperadrenergic state has been stabilized).

For the integrated dietary frame see [[anti-stress-diet]] and [[estrogen-detox]].

## What's distinctive in Peat's account

Three claims set Peat's framework apart from any other extant model of endometriosis. *First*, the lesion makes its own estrogen. Mainstream endocrinology has accepted the aromatase finding (Bulun and colleagues at Northwestern, late 1990s) but has not absorbed its implications for treatment — the standard interventions still target ovarian estrogen. Peat treated tissue-bound estrogen as the operative variable three decades before the mainstream literature acknowledged it.

*Second*, the disease is fundamentally hypothyroid. No mainstream framework treats endometriosis as a thyroid disease. The pivotal observation is the speed of response — "the very first day," "two or three days, completely stopped the symptoms permanently" — which is incompatible with any pure anti-inflammatory or progesterone-receptor-modulator framework, and is consistent with the rapid effect of T3 on hepatic estrogen clearance and aromatase suppression. The underlying hypothyroidism may be subclinical by TSH criteria (TSH is a poor read on tissue thyroid status; see [[thyroid]] and [[hypothyroidism]]); it may be unmasked only by basal temperature, pulse, and the response to a therapeutic trial.

*Third*, "incurable" is a description of the dominant treatment paradigm, not of the disease. A condition whose recurrence rate after surgery is 30-50% within five years, and whose chemical-menopause treatments produce time-limited remission with major side effects, is not incurable. It is undertreated. The undertreatment is structural: gynecology training is surgical, endocrinology training centers on diabetes and pituitary disease, and the metabolic primary-care framework that would integrate thyroid status, progesterone production, prostaglandin chemistry, and xenoestrogen exposure into a unified treatment does not exist as a specialty.

[[adenomyosis|Adenomyosis]] — endometrial-character tissue growing into the uterine wall rather than peritoneal sites — is treated by the same framework. The boundary between adenomyosis, endometriosis, [[fibroids|uterine fibroids]], and endometrial hyperplasia is, on Peat's view, more a question of which tissue compartment took on the lesion character than four distinct diseases. All four are estrogen-driven, all four involve aromatase upregulation, all four respond to the same protocol.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream literature pushes back at multiple points; the strongest cases deserve to be heard.

**On Sampson's theory.** The retrograde-menstruation observation is empirically robust — endometrial cells reflux into the peritoneum at high rates. Sampson's defenders argue that the disease occurs only when cells are deposited *and* the recipient peritoneum is permissive, which is consistent with the modern findings on local immune dysfunction, NK cell defects, and cytokine abnormalities in endometriosis patients. The strongest version of the modern Sampson view is that retrograde menstruation provides the cellular substrate and the lesion only forms in immunologically permissive hosts. That formulation is not fully incompatible with Peat's view — the "immunologically permissive host" is, in his terms, the estrogen-loaded, hypothyroid host — but it claims that the cellular substrate matters in a way Peat would dispute, and the existence of endometriosis in patients without a uterus is the experimentum crucis against it.

**On GnRH agonist efficacy.** Lupron and the other GnRH agonists do reduce lesion size and pain in placebo-controlled trials. Meta-analyses show real benefit for the duration of treatment. The clinical objection Peat would raise — that the underlying metabolic state is untouched — is consistent with the 50%+ recurrence rate after discontinuation but does not refute the immediate efficacy. For patients with severe pain who cannot wait for a metabolic protocol to take hold, GnRH agonists may be defensible as a bridge. The current standard of care often layers low-dose estrogen-progestin "add-back" therapy with GnRH agonists to mitigate bone density loss; this is, in Peat's framework, a tacit admission that ovarian suppression alone is incomplete and dangerous.

**On surgical excision.** The wide-excision-of-disease school (David Redwine, Andrew Cook, the Center for Endometriosis Care lineage) argues that endometriotic implants are histologically distinct, that complete removal produces durable remission rates well above conservative surgery, and that the recurrence statistics quoted above reflect inadequate surgical technique rather than the impossibility of cure. The peer-reviewed data on wide excision do support significantly lower recurrence than ablation. Peat's framework does not exclude excision — for fibrotic, deeply infiltrating disease that has not responded to metabolic intervention, surgical removal may be necessary — but it would predict that excision-without-metabolic-correction will continue to recur, and that the patients who do well long-term after excision are those whose underlying physiology was either spontaneously favorable or was corrected post-operatively.

**On the autoimmune framing.** Mainstream endometriosis research increasingly treats the disease as involving autoimmune components — altered cytokine profiles, defective macrophage function, abnormal NK cell activity. Peat would argue that all of this is downstream of the estrogen-prostaglandin-cortisol stress axis, not a primary cause. The empirical question is testable: do anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory interventions (corticosteroids, biologics) work as primary therapy? They generally do not. They suppress some symptoms while leaving the lesion intact. That observation is more consistent with Peat's framework than with a primary-autoimmune one.

**On the genetic contribution.** Twin and family studies show heritability estimates around 50%, and current GWAS literature has identified loci in WNT signaling, estrogen receptor regulation, and cell adhesion. Peat would treat these as constraints on the metabolic-environmental interaction, not as deterministic causes; see [[genetic-determinism]]. The dramatic geographic and historical variation in prevalence (low in pre-industrial populations, rising with industrialization and PUFA dietary penetration) is inconsistent with a primarily genetic disease.

**On the speed of metabolic response.** Peat's reports of two-or-three-day reversals are extraordinary claims that mainstream gynecology would demand controlled-trial evidence for. They have not received it. Trials of progesterone for endometriosis use synthetic progestins and small doses; thyroid trials have not been done in euthyroid endometriosis patients; high-dose aspirin trials do not exist. The protocol has not been falsified — but it has not been formally validated either. Peat's case series is suggestive, not definitive.

## Data gaps

Several questions are open even within Peat's framework.

The progesterone-receptor isoform problem is real and not fully addressed. Endometriotic lesions overexpress PR-A (which is anti-estrogenic on some targets) but underexpress PR-B (the broadly anti-proliferative isoform). High-dose progesterone may compensate for this in vivo, but the *in vitro* signal that endometriotic tissue is "progesterone-resistant" deserves more attention than Peat gave it. If the lesion has truly lost PR-B function, then progesterone alone may not be sufficient; the loop may need to be broken at the aromatase or prostaglandin step before progesterone can re-establish control.

The role of estrogen receptor isoforms is also incompletely worked out. ERα and ERβ have different distributions and different (sometimes opposing) functions. Endometriotic lesions have an altered ERα/ERβ ratio, with ERβ dramatically upregulated. Peat treated estrogen as a unitary signal; the receptor-isoform literature suggests it is not. Whether this affects the clinical response to his protocol is unknown.

The contribution of bowel endotoxin and gut estrogen recycling (the enterohepatic loop) is mentioned by Peat in passing but not developed for endometriosis specifically. The carrot-salad and gut-protection elements of his general protocol would predict benefit; no controlled data exist.

The deep-infiltrating endometriosis cases — where lesions involve bowel wall, ureter, sciatic nerve — represent the hardest clinical territory and the cases where Peat's framework was least obviously tested in his published correspondence. The metabolic interventions he described may shrink superficial peritoneal disease and may slow progression of deep disease, but whether they can dissolve deeply fibrotic, vascularized lesions in the limit case is unproven. Surgical removal followed by metabolic stabilization may be the realistic path for stage IV disease.

The relationship to fertility is partly clarified — endometriosis-associated infertility responds to the same protocol, often resolving along with pelvic pain — but the mechanism by which endometriosis impairs implantation (independent of mechanical distortion) is contested in the mainstream literature. Peritoneal-fluid alterations, ovotoxic free radicals, altered uterine receptivity — these all fit the inflammatory framework Peat described, but the specific molecular events have not been mapped onto his treatment protocol with the granularity needed for predictive use.

The cancer-risk linkage is real but underemphasized in his endometriosis discussions. Endometriotic ovarian cysts are associated with increased risk of clear-cell and endometrioid ovarian cancers. This is a small absolute risk but a real one. Peat's discussions of [progesterone in cancer prevention](sources/newsletters/preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone.md) imply that the same protocol that treats endometriosis should also reduce this risk, but the case has not been made explicitly.
