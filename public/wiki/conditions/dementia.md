# Dementia

Mainstream neurology has spent the last forty years searching for dementia inside the protein deposits that pathologists see at autopsy — the amyloid plaques between the brain's neurons and the neurofibrillary tangles inside them. Drugs aimed at clearing those deposits have failed in trial after trial, sometimes lowering plaque burden in PET scans while patients continue to decline. Ray Peat treated this failure as predictable. In his framework the tangles and plaques are not the disease. They are the late-stage debris of a brain that has been losing the ability to oxidize glucose for decades, and the search for a "cure" inside the debris is a search for the rust rather than the corroded pipe. "I am going to argue that Alzheimer's disease is analogous to the situation confronted by the developing chicken embryo or the rat or human fetus, when the environment is unable to meet the needs of the highly energetic, demanding and sensitive brain cells, and the brain cells begin to die, instead of developing into a more complex state," Peat wrote in his 2006 newsletter [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease as a Clue to Immortality*]]. The dementing brain is, on this account, a brain whose energy supply has been progressively suffocated.

The inversion is total. Where orthodoxy reads the amyloid plaque as the cause and brain-cell death as the consequence, Peat read the [[oxidative-metabolism|respiratory defect]] as the cause and the plaque as a chemically predictable artifact that follows once cells can no longer maintain their machinery. The therapeutic implications run in the opposite direction from anti-amyloid antibodies: restore the conditions that let neurons oxidize glucose — adequate [[thyroid]], abundant glucose, suppressed PUFA mobilization, lowered estrogen, drained iron, occasional methylene blue, [[red-light|red light]] on the scalp, [[progesterone]] and pregnenolone where available — and a great deal of the lost function returns, sometimes within days. The cases Peat documented across fifty years of writing make exactly this claim, and they make it in a tone that mainstream medicine has never found comfortable.

## The Energy Frame: Alzheimer's as Brain Diabetes

Brain tissue has the highest oxidative demand of any organ in the body. A neuron at rest consumes oxygen at roughly ten times the rate of an average cell, and the brain as a whole accounts for about twenty percent of the body's resting energy use despite making up only two percent of its mass. That energetic exposure means the brain is the organ most immediately damaged when [[oxidative-metabolism|complete oxidation of glucose to carbon dioxide]] starts to fail. Every chronic disease in Peat's framework is a partial reversion away from oxidative metabolism toward glycolytic fermentation; dementia is what that reversion looks like when the tissue under siege is the cortex.

The PET scan data that Alzheimer's researchers report as "regional hypometabolism" is, in Peat's reading, the diagnostic signature of the disease, not its complication. "In Alzheimer's disease, brain respiratory metabolism is inhibited, creating a carbon dioxide deficiency with an excess of lactic acid and ammonia," he wrote in [[homeostasis-and-aging|*Homeostasis and Aging* (1999)]]. The dementing brain produces less CO2, accumulates lactate, and runs an ammonia surplus that suppresses respiration further. The metabolic profile of an Alzheimer's cortex and the metabolic profile of a tumor — both ferment glucose to lactate even when oxygen is available — are the same [[warburg-effect|Warburg state]] applied to different tissues. "Aging is an energy problem, and in the brain, which has extremely high energy requirements, interference with the energy supply quickly causes cells to die," Peat wrote in [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women-depression-energy-stress|*Estrogen and Brain Aging in Men and Women* (2009)]].

A recent line of mainstream research has begun calling Alzheimer's "type 3 diabetes" on the basis of impaired cerebral insulin signaling and the inability of the dementing brain to use [[glucose]]. Peat had been making essentially the same claim for forty years, with a different mechanistic emphasis. From [[bse-mad-cow-scrapie-etc-stimulated-amyloid-degeneration-and-the-toxic-fats|*BSE, Mad Cow, Scrapie, etc.* (2007)]]: "In the dementias, the brain has a greatly reduced ability to use glucose." The cause is not insulin resistance per se but the [[randle-cycle|Randle-cycle]] block that polyunsaturated free fatty acids impose on every cell exposed to them — a block that suppresses pyruvate dehydrogenase, dumps glucose into lactate, and starves the mitochondria of the substrate they need. "Until the ability to use sugar is impaired, cells produce enough carbon dioxide to protect proteins against random glycation," Peat continued, "but with each exposure to free polyunsaturated fatty acids, the ability to use glucose is damaged." The protein damage attributed to "advanced glycation end products" of dietary sugar is, in Peat's reading, overwhelmingly the work of lipid-peroxidation aldehydes — acrolein, malondialdehyde, hydroxynonenal — generated by the [[pufa|PUFA]] that brain tissue has accumulated across the lifespan.

The same brain that cannot oxidize glucose cannot make its own protective neurosteroids either. Mitochondria do not just produce ATP; they perform the side-chain cleavage of cholesterol into pregnenolone, which is the parent of progesterone, DHEA, and the rest of the brain's anti-stress steroid system. A respiratory defect means a neurosteroid defect. In the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|*Ask the Herb Doctor* episode of December 2012]] Peat described the consequence directly: "The brain is one of our biggest steroid-forming glands in the body. Its concentration of steroids — progesterone, DHEA, and pregnenolone — is about 10 times higher than in the bloodstream. The production of these neurosteroids is one of the major uses of oxygen. When the brain is injured, besides not making these hormones as effectively as it should because of the damage to mitochondria, the aromatase enzyme begins turning testosterone into estrogen." The brain that can no longer respire is also the brain that can no longer protect itself.

## The Iron Accumulation: A Catalyst That Builds Up

The brain is the highest-iron organ in the body, and unlike most tissues it has no efficient mechanism for unloading it. From birth onward iron creeps upward in the substantia nigra, the hippocampus, and the cortex, and by the eighth or ninth decade the iron concentration in some brain regions has tripled. "Iron is now believed to have a role in skin aging, atherosclerosis, and cataracts of the lenses of the eyes, largely through its formation of the 'age pigment,'" Peat wrote in [[iron-cumulative-danger|*Iron: Cumulative Danger* (1997)]]. "Many recent studies show that iron is involved in degenerative brain diseases, such as Parkinson's, ALS (Lou Gehrig's disease), Huntington's chorea, and Alzheimer's disease." A later line in the same newsletter notes that "Aluminum and iron react similarly in cells and are suspected causes of Alzheimer's disease."

Iron's mischief in the brain is the same mischief it does everywhere: it catalyzes Fenton-type reactions that turn ordinary hydrogen peroxide into the hydroxyl radical, the most destructive oxidant biology has to deal with. In the presence of [[pufa|polyunsaturated fats]] — which the aging brain has been accumulating its whole life — that hydroxyl radical chews through the double bonds of membrane lipids, generating a chain reaction of [[lipid-peroxidation]] whose products are aldehyde toxins like acrolein and 4-hydroxynonenal. "The factors that are known to reduce the brain size at birth are also factors that are involved in the degenerating brain in old age or Alzheimer's disease," Peat wrote in [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease as a Clue to Immortality*]]: "lack of oxygen, excess unsaturated fats or deficiency of saturated fats, estrogen excess, progesterone deficiency, and lack of glucose. A lack of carbon dioxide is probably harmful in both."

The acrolein point bears its own emphasis because mainstream neurology has been slow to register it. Acrolein is generated preferentially from omega-3 fatty acids — the fats marketed for cognitive protection. In the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] Peat made the point bluntly: "Acrolein is a very tiny fragment that comes mainly from the Omega-3 fatty acids. I think because it's so small and chemically active, it has a greater effect by many times than the longer chain aldehydes. The fact that it comes from the oils that are being proposed as cures and treatments for Alzheimer's disease — fish oil supplements and other Omega-3 fats — are the main sources of acrolein." Fish-oil consumption in the elderly is, on this account, not a neuroprotective practice but a mechanism for delivering the precursor of the most potent neurotoxic aldehyde directly into a brain whose iron stores are already primed to catalyze its production. Full mechanism on the [[pufa]] hub.

What ties iron to estrogen is one of Peat's most distinctive insights. Iron absorption from the gut and iron release from ferritin storage are both upregulated by estrogen and by [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroidism]]. "Iron: Regulator of mRNA stability, heme synthesis; reacts with reductants and unsaturated oils, to produce free radicals and lipid peroxides; its absorption is increased by estrogen, hypothyroidism, anemia or lack of oxygen," Peat wrote in [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]. Estrogen, in addition to its primary respiratory-suppressing effect, drives iron loading. PUFA accumulation amplifies what the iron then does. The dementing brain is the intersection point of three pathologies that mainstream medicine treats as unrelated.

## Estrogen as the Brain Excitotoxin

Mainstream medicine treats estrogen as a brain-protective hormone whose decline at menopause causes cognitive decline, and for two decades the pharmaceutical industry ran a campaign suggesting that supplemental estrogen would prevent Alzheimer's disease. Peat held the opposite position with unusual force. Alzheimer's disease is two to three times as common in women as in men, and Peat read that female excess as estrogen's signature rather than its absence. The full architecture of estrogen's respiratory interference is at [[estrogen]]; for the dementing brain three of its actions are load-bearing.

The first is direct excitotoxicity. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "The specific approaches of this orientation — to energize but quiet the brain — are diametrically opposed to some of the 'therapies' for Alzheimer's disease that have been promoted recently by the drug industries: Things to increase stimulation, especially to increase cholinergic excitation; even the excitotoxic amino acids themselves and their analogs; and estrogen, which is a multiple brain excitant, proconvulsant, excitotoxic promoter, and anti-memory agent." Estrogen activates the glutamic acid pathway, interferes with the inhibitory adenosine system, increases [[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]] and intracellular calcium, and pushes neurons toward the calcium-overload death that follows any sustained excitatory stimulus. "A single injection of estrogen can kill a large portion of brain cells," Peat noted — a fact, as he repeatedly observed, that the estrogen-receptor doctrine cannot accommodate but the respiratory-interference framework explains directly.

The second is the case-control evidence buried under the pharmaceutical publicity machine. Peat cited the Seattle data summarized in [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "the demented had 155% as much former estrogen use as the normal group… progestogen use (normal patients had 50% more progestogen use than demented)… hysterectomy (demented had 44.1% vs. 17% in the normals, i.e., 259% as many)… the incidence of hysterectomies after the age of 55, which is a strong indication of a natural excess of estrogen, in the demented was 374% of the incidence in the non-demented." The point about hysterectomy is the diagnostic one: a hysterectomy in mid-life is, in Peat's reading, a downstream indicator of chronic estrogen excess (fibroids, dysfunctional bleeding, endometriosis), and women who had hysterectomies were dramatically over-represented among Alzheimer's patients decades later. "These observations, which tend to indicate that exposure to estrogen in middle-age increases the risk of Alzheimer's disease in old age, and that even medical progestogens offer some protection against it." Peat added the gloss that defined his attitude toward the medical literature: "Although this study might have been bigger and better, it is far better than the junk-studies that have been promoted by the pharmaceutical publicity machine. I have seen or heard roughly 100 mentions of the pro-estrogen anti-scientific 'studies,' and none mentioning this one."

The third is local aromatization. Injured tissue activates [[aromatase|aromatase]], the enzyme that turns androgens into estrogen, anywhere in the body. In the brain that means injured neurons begin generating their own estrogen, exporting it to their neighbors, and amplifying the very stimulus that injured them. In the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] Peat described the loop: "any injury to any tissue, anywhere in the body, happens to activate the enzyme which creates estrogen… in stress or any injury, aromatase appears and begins making estrogen right inside the tissue. And that happens in the Alzheimer's brain." The cumulative consequence is a positive feedback in which respiratory damage triggers estrogen production, the estrogen further suppresses respiration, the further suppression generates more injury, and the cycle continues until the cortex visibly atrophies.

The estrogen-PUFA-glucose convergence is the unifying claim. From [[bse-mad-cow-scrapie-etc-stimulated-amyloid-degeneration-and-the-toxic-fats|*BSE, Mad Cow, Scrapie, etc.*]]: "One of estrogen's central effects is to shift metabolism away from the oxidation of glucose, decreasing carbon dioxide production. There is a much higher incidence of Alzheimer's disease in women, and estrogen exposure exacerbates all of the changes that lead to it, such as shifts in nerve transmitters, increased vascular leakiness, and the increased production of the acute phase proteins."

## Hypothyroidism: The Cold Brain Syndrome

A hypothyroid brain is a cold brain, and a cold brain is a brain whose mitochondria are running below the velocity at which the respiratory chain efficiently consumes electrons. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "When the body temperature is very much below normal, mental functioning is seriously limited. I think the first question that should be asked about a demented person is 'is this the cold brain syndrome, or is something else involved?'" The clinical observation that hypothyroid patients are frequently misdiagnosed as demented, and that thyroid supplementation restores cognition in such patients, is a fixture of pre-1970s medical literature and a near-taboo in current practice. The full diagnostic apparatus that gave rise to the 40%-of-Americans hypothyroid estimate is at [[hypothyroidism]].

The thyroid–brain connection runs through every load-bearing variable in Peat's framework. T3 is the activator of [[oxidative-metabolism|complete oxidation]]; it accelerates the cytochrome oxidase enzyme that delivers electrons to oxygen; it stimulates the liver's clearance of estrogen (raising the cost of estrogen build-up over time); and it supports the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone in mitochondria, restoring the brain's neurosteroid pool. In the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] Peat made the cytochrome point explicit: "Thyroid increases the cytochrome oxidase enzyme, which is the one that is very sensitive to stress, estrogen, carbon monoxide, and basically all of the toxins. Thyroid causes the genes to be expressed and causes the copper to be absorbed that the enzyme uses as a cofactor." The same enzyme that PUFA peroxidation products poison and that estrogen displaces, thyroid maintains.

Peat's most-quoted clinical anecdote on this point is also the cleanest. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "A few years later, a man in his 80s showed the classical signs of senile dementia, with childishness, confusion, self-centeredness, and unstable emotions. A few days after getting a mixture of thyroid, pregnenolone, and progesterone, his mind was again clear, and he was able to work on a research project he had set aside years before." The recovery time scale — days, not weeks — is what gives the case its evidential force. A degenerative process whose downstream sign reverses within days of hormonal intervention is not a degenerative process in the structural sense; it is a functional failure whose substrate is still present and responsive.

## Mainstream Framework: The Amyloid Hypothesis Being Inverted

For perspective on what Peat was inverting: orthodox Alzheimer's research holds that the disease begins with accumulation of beta-amyloid (the Aβ peptide), a fragment cleaved from the larger amyloid precursor protein by beta- and gamma-secretases. Plaques of aggregated Aβ between neurons trigger an immune-microglial response, inflammation, hyperphosphorylation of tau protein into neurofibrillary tangles inside neurons, synaptic loss, and eventually neuron death. The genetic evidence — early-onset familial Alzheimer's linked to mutations in APP, presenilin-1, and presenilin-2, all of which affect amyloid processing — and the protective effect of APOE2 versus the risk-conferring effect of [[apoe4|APOE4]] are read as confirming amyloid's causal primacy. The therapeutic strategy that follows is anti-amyloid: monoclonal antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab, the long-disappointing aducanumab line) that clear plaques from the brain.

Peat's framework reads the same pathological inventory in the opposite direction. In [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]] he laid out the Alzheimer brain's findings — death of neurons, amyloid plaques associated with [[apoe4|APOE4]], fibrillary tangles, amyloid in blood vessels, and the underlying "mitochondrial energy problem, cytochrome oxidase and its regulation; body temperature/pulse-rate cycle disturbance; lipid peroxidation; respiratory defect" — and then traced the chain causally backward: "Vegetable oil suppresses the thyroid, increasing estrogen. Estrogen and calcium depolymerize microtubules. Microtubule transport for Apo E, transthyretin, thyroid, and cholesterol for pregnenolone synthesis is disrupted. Transthyretin and Apo E accumulate unused, and deposit in blood vessels, around nerves, and in cytoplasm. Pregnenolone and progesterone deficiency (aggravating thyroid deficiency) causes memory loss, destabilization of nerve cells, failure of myelin formation, and excess cortisol synthesis. Free radicals and calcium cause multiple cell injuries including nerve-death."

In this reading, amyloid is what builds up when microtubular transport fails, and microtubular transport fails because estrogen and calcium depolymerize microtubules, and estrogen rises because PUFA suppresses thyroid. The plaque is the dropped cargo of a stalled axonal-transport system. Clearing the plaque without restoring the transport system clears the symptom of the lesion without touching the lesion.

Peat reinforced the same skepticism on the diagnostic side. Real-world Alzheimer's pathology is heterogeneous; classification is often arbitrary. From [[bse-mad-cow-scrapie-etc-stimulated-amyloid-degeneration-and-the-toxic-fats|*BSE, Mad Cow, Scrapie, etc.*]]: "According to a 1989 study, 13% of the people who had died from 'Alzheimer's disease' actually had CJD. Between 1979 and 2000, the number of people dying annually from Alzheimer's disease increased 50-fold. Very competent neuropathologists differ radically in their descriptions of the dementia epidemic." A category that swells fifty-fold across a generation and absorbs cases that turn out to be prionic disease is not a category whose boundaries are tightly drawn.

The lipid-membrane data also push against the orthodox plaque-first reading. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "The platelets of Alzheimer's patients are less viscous, and lipids extracted from the brain are more fluid, and contain 30% less cholesterol than normal (on a molar basis, in relation to phospholipids). In general, lipid peroxidation causes cellular viscosity to increase, apparently by causing cross-linking of proteins, but I think the significance of the decreased cholesterol relates to its significance as precursor to pregnenolone and progesterone, and to the known association with Alzheimer's disease of a variant form of the cholesterol transporter protein, ApoE, which I suppose is a slightly less stable molecular form that is more susceptible to malfunction in stress." The APOE4 risk allele, on this reading, is not a primary driver but a slightly less stable cholesterol carrier whose pathology only matters under the stress conditions that PUFA, estrogen, hypothyroidism, and iron accumulation produce. The variant matters because the system is failing; the system fails for other reasons.

## Vascular and Frontotemporal Dementias: Same Engine, Different Mile Marker

Although Alzheimer's has dominated the public imagination of dementia, two other syndromes — vascular dementia and frontotemporal dementia — produce overlapping clinical pictures, and Peat treated them as variations on the same energy-failure theme. Vascular dementia is the textbook outcome of small-vessel disease in the brain: chronic ischemia, micro-infarction, white-matter loss. The orthodox explanation centers on hypertension, diabetes, and small-vessel atherosclerosis as separate cardiovascular phenomena. In Peat's framework these are all expressions of the same metabolic substrate that drives Alzheimer's. Estrogen, PUFA, and hypothyroidism each impair endothelial function, increase vascular permeability, raise platelet aggregation, and lower the CO2 production that would otherwise relax cerebral vessels. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "Hypothyroidism, estrogen excess, free unsaturated fats cause increased vascular permeability and brain edema, protein leakage, and alteration of the matrix." The same factors that produce intraneuronal amyloid in one region of cortex produce small-vessel leakage and ischemia in another.

Frontotemporal dementia involves preferential degeneration of the frontal and temporal lobes, often with disinhibition, behavioral change, and language impairment, and is now associated with various tauopathies and TDP-43 proteinopathies. Peat did not write about FTD by name, but the framework predicts that any region with high metabolic demand and high lifelong PUFA exposure will be vulnerable to the same lipid-peroxidation-driven injury, and the cortex's most synaptically active regions are precisely those most exposed. The pathological proteinopathies — tau in some FTDs, alpha-synuclein in Lewy-body dementia, TDP-43 — are, on Peat's reading, expressions of the same misfolded-protein endpoint that any cell drives toward when its energy supply fails and its CO2 buffer collapses. The protein that misfolds depends on the tissue; the underlying respiratory defect does not.

## Excitotoxicity and the Final Common Path

Most cell death, regardless of the upstream trigger, ends in an intracellular calcium overload that activates degradative enzymes and tears the cell apart. Peat quoted Fujita on this point in [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "All cell death is characterized by an increase of intracellular calcium…' 'Increase of cytoplasmic free calcium may therefore be called the final common path of cell disease and cell death. Aging as a background of diseases is also characterized by an increase of intracellular calcium. Diseases typically associated with aging include hypertension, arteriosclerosis, diabetes mellitus and dementia.'" The proximate cause of neuron death in dementia is the same as in stroke and excitotoxic injury: calcium floods in, mitochondria swell, the energy required to pump calcium back out collapses, and the cell dies.

Estrogen, PUFA, and lactic acid all push the cell toward this state. Unsaturated fats increase the membrane permeability that allows calcium entry. Estrogen activates the glutamic acid receptors that open calcium channels. Lactic acid lowers intracellular pH, which alters the calcium-binding capacity of cytoplasmic proteins and further dysregulates ion handling. The protective opposites — magnesium, [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]], [[progesterone]], and adenosine — all act at the same final common path, inhibiting calcium entry, supporting calcium extrusion, and quieting the excitatory drive that pushes cells toward the overload.

Seizures provide the clearest natural experiment. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "Seizures are known to be promoted by estrogen, by unsaturated fats, and by lipid peroxidation, and to cause an increase in the size of the free fatty acid pool in the brain. Prolonged seizures cause nerve damage in certain areas, especially the hippocampus, thalamus, and neocortex. Dementia is known to be produced by prolonged seizures." Epilepsy and Alzheimer's, on Peat's reading, share a substrate. The hippocampal sclerosis that follows prolonged seizure activity in temporal-lobe epilepsy is mechanically the same lesion that defines the early Alzheimer's brain. A treatment that quiets excitatory drive — progesterone, an adequate adenosine system, GABA-supporting supplementation, magnesium, naloxone — is, on Peat's account, a treatment that addresses both.

## Protocols: Energizing the Brain, Quietly

Peat's interventions for dementia fall into two stacked categories: things that reduce the ongoing injury (lowering estrogen production, draining stored PUFA, suppressing iron-catalyzed peroxidation, calming excitatory drive) and things that restore oxidative capacity (thyroid, glucose, light, neurosteroids, mitochondrial uncouplers). They are layered rather than alternative.

**Thyroid hormone.** The first move in nearly every Peat protocol for cognitive decline is to ensure adequate active thyroid hormone (T3 in particular, since T4 conversion in the liver is often the failing step). The diagnostic apparatus — pulse, temperature, Achilles reflex relaxation time — is described in [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroidism]]. From [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women-depression-energy-stress|*Estrogen and Brain Aging in Men and Women*]]: "As I have discussed previously, progesterone alone has brought people out of post-epileptic dementia and senile dementia, but it is reasonable to use a combined physiological approach, including thyroid." Thyroid is the metabolic switch; without it the other interventions are working uphill.

**Progesterone.** For women, the case is almost categorical. Progesterone destroys the estrogen receptor at the protein level (see [[progesterone]] and [[estrogen]] for the mechanism), restores neurosteroid concentration in the brain, supports CO2 production, opposes the seizure-promoting and excitotoxic actions of estrogen, and has been shown to recover brain function in clinical cases of dementia repeatedly in Peat's own practice. The 52-year-old woman whose recovery first pulled Peat into recommending progesterone directly was diagnosed with "epileptic dementia," and the recovery happened in days. From the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] Peat made the application to traumatic brain injury and, by extension, to chronic neurodegeneration: progesterone "stops the damage and promotes remyelinization of the fibers." For men, the case is weaker but still significant — progesterone opposes the local aromatase activity that turns testosterone into estrogen in the injured brain. The full mechanism is at [[progesterone]].

**Glucose, not ketones.** A standard mainstream recommendation for early Alzheimer's is a ketogenic diet, on the reasoning that ketones bypass the impaired glucose metabolism of the dementing brain. Peat opposed this approach on two grounds: it requires the cell to oxidize fat, which produces less CO2 per unit oxygen than glucose oxidation and pushes the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] further toward the fermentation that defines the disease; and it relies on lipolysis, which mobilizes stored PUFA into the bloodstream where it is delivered to the brain. The frequent, glucose-stable, sucrose-and-protein-and-milk diet Peat recommended (see [[anti-stress-diet]] and [[glucose]]) is designed to keep the liver from running out of glycogen overnight — which is when free fatty acids rise, adrenaline peaks, and the brain takes its nightly hit of PUFA. From the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 transcript]]: "as your liver gets less efficient, many people can only go an hour or even less without suffering the symptoms of starvation. Some people wake up every one or two hours during the night with nightmares or heart pounding, which happens when the liver runs out of its sugar supply."

**[[Aspirin]].** Aspirin is one of the few interventions for which population-level epidemiology agrees with Peat. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "Aspirin protects against iron toxicity, clot formation, and reduces lipid peroxidation while blocking prostaglandin formation. Aspirin and other antiinflammatory drugs, taken for arthritis, have been clearly associated with a reduced incidence of Alzheimer's disease." Its mechanism is multiple: it inhibits aromatase (lowering brain estrogen production), blocks the prostaglandin cascade that PUFA peroxidation drives, supports mitochondrial uncoupling and CO2 production, and chelates iron. The full mechanism is at [[aspirin]]. Doses for cognitive protection sit at the low-to-moderate end (typically 81–325 mg daily with adequate vitamin K).

**Iron-lowering.** The simplest intervention is also one of the most consistently underused. Donating blood every few months is the most effective long-term mechanism for lowering tissue iron stores; reducing dietary iron intake (avoiding iron-fortified flour, avoiding cooking in iron pans) is the second; drinking coffee or tea with meals (both contain polyphenols that chelate iron) is the third. A daily raw [[carrot]] is supportive because it both lowers estrogen via the gut route and shifts intestinal absorption of iron downward.

**Methylene blue.** Of all the substances Peat treated as interesting for the dementing brain, methylene blue is the most distinctive. Methylene blue is a redox-active dye that, at low doses, acts as an electron carrier within the mitochondrial respiratory chain — bypassing damaged Complex IV (cytochrome oxidase) and delivering electrons directly to oxygen. In a brain whose cytochrome oxidase has been poisoned by PUFA peroxidation products, methylene blue effectively short-circuits the failing step in the chain. The substance has a long history in human medicine (it was the first synthetic drug, used as an antimalarial in the 1890s, and is still on the WHO essential medicines list as a treatment for methemoglobinemia), and a smaller but consistent literature suggests it improves memory in aging rats, slows tau hyperphosphorylation in cell models, and produces measurable cognitive improvements in early-stage Alzheimer's patients. Peat referenced methylene blue most often in his discussions of brain energy and cancer; the same logic that makes it useful as an oxidative-respiration restorer in tumor tissue applies to the dementing cortex. Doses Peat mentioned were small (1–4 mg taken sublingually); the substance turns urine and tongue blue, is contraindicated in patients on SSRIs (serotonin syndrome risk), and should be considered an experimental rather than a routine intervention.

**Red light.** The cytochrome oxidase enzyme is photosensitive at red and near-infrared wavelengths in a way that produces an immediate increase in mitochondrial respiration. From the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]]: "Light is another major factor. With the stress of metabolism, the copper can get detached from the enzyme. Exposure to ordinary visible light — the red, long waves of light that will pass right into your brain — happens to lift the copper atom just enough to restore its position in the cytochrome enzyme. The copper is reactivated by the red light. Just in the last year, a group in the US did memory studies and showed that just shining some red light on the head improves memory." Peat recommended ordinary 250-watt incandescent heat lamps, sometimes two or three running simultaneously in the evening hours, for general use; the brain-specific application uses smaller red-LED arrays applied to the scalp. More at [[red-light]].

**Endotoxin and the gut.** Endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide from gut bacteria) suppresses mitochondrial respiration directly and elevates estrogen indirectly. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "Endotoxin: Antimitochondrial action, causes elevation of estrogen. It synergizes with unsaturated fats, and naloxone opposes some of its toxic effects." Reducing endotoxin exposure — daily [[carrot|raw carrot]] for the bound-fiber effect, occasional antibiotics in some cases, avoiding poorly digested grains and legumes — is part of the dementia protocol. The full route is at [[endotoxin]].

**Naloxone.** A standalone substance Peat returned to repeatedly. Naloxone is the opiate antagonist used to reverse heroin overdoses; in chronic low doses it opposes the endorphin-driven downregulation of metabolic activity that Peat associated with senility. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "I have proposed that the endorphins can cause or sustain some of the symptoms of aging. Naloxone appears to be a useful treatment for senility." He documented one personal observation: "I have seen it quickly, and dramatically, improve the mental clarity of a 60 year old woman who had used estrogen." [[low-dose-naltrexone|Low-dose naltrexone]] (LDN) is the orally available cousin used in current practice.

**Magnesium, vitamin E, B-vitamins.** Magnesium opposes calcium overload, vitamin E partially protects PUFA against peroxidation, and B-vitamins (B1, B3 as niacinamide, B6, B12) support the enzymes of oxidative metabolism. The thiamine point is particularly Peatian: he claimed that a 100-mg dose of B1 combined with adequate thyroid produces dramatic short-term cognitive improvement in young adults, and the same combination is plausibly useful in early-stage dementia. The mechanism is the cofactor requirement of pyruvate dehydrogenase, the gate enzyme between glycolysis and the Krebs cycle.

**What to avoid.** The prohibition list is the inversion of the protocol list. Avoid polyunsaturated oils (seed oils, fish oils, nuts as oils — see [[seed-oils]] and [[fish-oil]]). Avoid prolonged fasting (which mobilizes stored PUFA into the bloodstream where it is delivered to the brain). Avoid excessive iron from supplements and iron-fortified flour. Avoid supplemental estrogen. Avoid stimulant drugs that increase excitatory drive at the cost of energy reserves (cholinesterase inhibitors, the standard mainstream prescription, fall into this category in Peat's reading — they push a damaged system harder rather than reducing the damage). Avoid the night-fasting pattern that lets the liver run out of glycogen; eat a small carbohydrate-protein snack before bed.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream consensus against Peat's reading of dementia is genuine and load-bearing, and the framework requires honest engagement with what it contradicts.

**The amyloid hypothesis still has structural support.** The genetic evidence for amyloid's role in early-onset familial Alzheimer's — APP mutations, presenilin mutations, Down syndrome trisomy of chromosome 21 that includes the APP gene — is consistent with amyloid being upstream of the disease in at least the inherited cases. The development of lecanemab and donanemab as plaque-clearing antibodies that have demonstrated some slowing of cognitive decline in randomized trials (though with substantial side effects, including ARIA — amyloid-related imaging abnormalities reflecting brain edema and microhemorrhage) is the first pharmacological evidence consistent with the orthodox model. Peat's framework would predict these effects as modest at best — clearing the plaque without restoring the underlying respiratory capacity — and recent debate around the magnitude and clinical meaningfulness of the slowing has not settled the question.

**The Women's Health Initiative is mixed on estrogen.** Peat treated the WHI as confirmation that supplemental estrogen causes dementia. The WHI Memory Study (WHIMS) did show increased dementia risk in women starting hormone therapy after age 65, but other subgroup analyses (the "critical window" hypothesis) suggest that estrogen started in early menopause may be protective. Mainstream endocrinology has not embraced Peat's wholesale rejection of estrogen replacement. The evidence base is genuinely mixed; Peat read the unambiguously bad subgroup as the cleaner signal.

**The "diabetes type 3" framing is mainstream but not Peatian.** A substantial recent literature treats Alzheimer's as a brain-insulin-resistance phenomenon, which converges with Peat's energy-failure reading. But the mainstream interpretation tends to recommend low-carbohydrate / ketogenic interventions on the reasoning that the dementing brain cannot use glucose and ketones bypass the problem. Peat's framework reads ketosis as a stress-metabolism state that mobilizes PUFA and produces less CO2 per oxygen, and recommends the opposite intervention — frequent glucose intake stabilizing the liver and lowering free fatty acids. The empirical evidence for ketogenic diets in early Alzheimer's is limited but not negligible. The two frameworks predict different outcomes from the same intervention; the long-term randomized evidence has not been generated.

**The "Aducanumab and friends" story is unsettled.** The orthodox model has been damaged but not destroyed by twenty years of failed amyloid-targeting drug trials. Defenders argue that the failures reflect intervention too late in the disease course, not the wrongness of the hypothesis. Peat's framework predicts that anti-amyloid antibodies will continue producing modest, expensive, side-effect-laden benefits that do not bend the long-term trajectory — and so far that has been broadly correct.

**The lactate-as-brain-fuel literature.** Recent work (the astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle) has rehabilitated lactate as a normal inter-cellular fuel in the brain rather than a pure waste product. Peat's "lactate is poison" framing was developed before this literature matured. The Peatian reply would be that intercellular lactate shuttling reflects local metabolic stress rather than a healthy physiological program, but this position is not directly engaged in the corpus.

**The fish-oil-for-Alzheimer's literature.** Mainstream nutritional epidemiology has often (though not universally) reported associations between marine omega-3 intake and lower dementia risk. Peat treated the same fats as the primary source of brain acrolein. The randomized trial evidence is mixed and tilting toward null for primary prevention; observational associations are confounded by overall diet quality and socioeconomic gradient. The disagreement is unsettled even within mainstream nutrition.

**Methylene blue's evidence base is small.** The pharmacology is real, the rodent literature is consistent, the mechanism is plausible — but the human clinical trial evidence is small, mostly conducted by a single research group (TauRx), and confounded by methodological issues. Recommending methylene blue for dementia in 2026 is recommending an experimental intervention with modest published evidence; the framework that motivates it is more confident than the data.

## Data gaps

**The threshold problem.** Peat's framework predicts that dementia incidence should track tissue PUFA load, lifetime estrogen exposure, and iron accumulation. None of these are routinely measured. There is no validated threshold below which a person is protected and above which they are at high risk. The framework is qualitative throughout.

**The pediatric-adult transition.** Children's brains accumulate PUFA continuously through the second decade. Peat addressed this only sporadically. The optimal lifetime PUFA exposure pattern, particularly during the years of brain growth, is not specified.

**Sex-specific protocols.** Peat's progesterone recommendation for dementia is most directly applicable to postmenopausal women. The exact application in men, where progesterone use is more controversial and where local aromatase activity may be more variable, is underdeveloped.

**Methylene blue dosing.** The doses Peat referenced and the doses used in the TauRx trials differ substantially. Optimal dosing, frequency, timing, and the interaction with other serotonergic substances are not laid out in the corpus.

**Iron-lowering kinetics.** The half-life of brain iron stores under blood donation, low-iron diet, or coffee/tea consumption is not well characterized in primary literature. How quickly the dementing brain can be expected to lose its iron burden, and whether that translates into cognitive improvement, is unknown.

**The dementia–CJD overlap.** Peat noted that 13% of "Alzheimer's" autopsies in one 1989 study turned out to be CJD. The implications for current diagnostic categories — and for whether some fraction of mainstream "Alzheimer's disease" is actually a prionic disease driven by similar lipid-peroxidation-amplified protein misfolding — has not been worked out. The protein-folding chemistry that drives prionic disease and the lipid-peroxidation chemistry that drives Alzheimer's converge in this framework but are not unified.

**The mild-cognitive-impairment to dementia transition.** The conditions under which a person with measurable mild cognitive impairment progresses to clinical dementia versus stabilizes or recovers are not addressed by Peat directly. The framework predicts that adequate intervention at the MCI stage should reverse a substantial fraction of cases, but the evidence is anecdotal.

**The role of sleep.** Adenosine accumulates during waking and is cleared during sleep, and recent literature on the glymphatic system suggests that sleep is when the brain clears amyloid through cerebrospinal fluid exchange. Peat addressed sleep occasionally but did not develop the relationship between sleep architecture and the dementia trajectory in detail.

**Frontotemporal and Lewy-body dementias.** Peat's writings concentrate on Alzheimer's. The application of the same framework to FTD, Lewy-body dementia, and the parkinsonian dementias is plausible but not worked out at the level of specific clinical recommendations.
