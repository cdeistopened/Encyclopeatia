# Lyme Disease

Lyme disease arrived in Ray Peat's call-in shows the way it arrives in clinics — through patients, not through doctors. A caller from Humboldt opening a Lyme support group. A second caller from Salmon Creek pressing Peat on whether the spirochete really clears in three weeks. An Australian Lyme question routed through the Field Biology episode in 2014. A listener asking whether the tick bite triggers an anti-thyroid response. Peat's answers were unusually direct for a topic as contested as this one. He took the spirochete seriously — "that class of bacteria, the spirochetes in general, can be very harsh; some of them are very toxic and harmful and long-lasting" ([[6bbd09|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, September 2014]]). He took patients' chronic symptoms seriously. And he was openly skeptical of the chronic-Lyme treatment industry, which he saw as making "a simple problem complex" the same way the cortisone-for-arthritis school had a generation earlier.

The framework that emerges across his transcripts is consistent. Lyme is a chronic-spirochete-plus-metabolic-immune-failure complex. The bacterial component is real and treatable. The chronic persistence — when it occurs — sits primarily downstream of the metabolic state that lets a low-grade infection grind on. The treatment combines a short course of the right antibiotic (doxycycline or minocycline, with the tetracycline class his clear favorite) with the full Peat metabolic restoration: thyroid, anti-stress diet, gut decontamination, cyproheptadine for neuro-inflammation, vitamin K2 + D + A, sun and warmth. Without the metabolic substrate, antibiotics alone will under-deliver. Without antibiotics, the metabolic work alone may not clear the spirochete. This is a politically charged topic — the medical mainstream and the chronic-Lyme-clinic world disagree about almost every clinical question — and Peat's position aligns with neither orthodoxy. Strong clinician coordination, ideally with a physician who has Lyme experience, is essential. The article that follows is documentation, not medical advice.

## What Lyme is

Lyme disease is an infection by the spirochete *Borrelia burgdorferi* (in North America; *B. afzelii* and *B. garinii* in Europe), transmitted by *Ixodes* ticks — the deer tick in the eastern United States, the western black-legged tick on the Pacific coast. The spirochete shares its corkscrew morphology and tissue-penetrating motility with *Treponema pallidum*, the agent of syphilis. Peat returned to that parallel repeatedly. "One of the things about Lyme disease that hasn't been addressed is that it's from a spirochete, which means it's similar to syphilis," a caller noted on the [[6bbd09|2014 Field Biology episode]]. Peat agreed: "I think it's probably analogous. The right antibiotic is needed to treat tertiary syphilis. Something that gets into the brain and nervous system will do it."

The ticks that carry *Borrelia* commonly carry other organisms. *Bartonella henselae* and *B. quintana* (intracellular bacteria), *Babesia microti* and *B. duncani* (malaria-class protozoa), *Anaplasma phagocytophilum*, *Ehrlichia chaffeensis*, and *Rickettsia* species can all be transmitted from a single tick bite. The clinical syndrome that presents as "Lyme" in a chronic patient is therefore frequently not Lyme alone. This is a point where Peat-frame thinking, mainstream infectious disease, and Lyme-literate clinicians converge: the patient who is not responding to a clean *Borrelia* course often has at least one untreated co-infection. Each co-infection has a distinct biology and a distinct antibiotic profile.

The acute presentation, when it occurs in textbook form, is the erythema migrans rash — the expanding bull's-eye lesion at the tick bite site — appearing 3 to 30 days after exposure, often accompanied by flu-like symptoms (fever, headache, fatigue, muscle and joint aches). Many infections do not produce a visible rash. Weeks to months later, untreated infection can disseminate to joints (Lyme arthritis, often the knee), heart (carditis with conduction block), and nervous system (cranial neuropathy, radiculopathy, encephalopathy). Months to years later, the chronic presentation — persistent fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, joint pain, neuropathic symptoms — becomes the contested territory.

## Acute vs. chronic Lyme: the controversy

The acute case is settled medicine. A tick bite, a bull's-eye rash, early constitutional symptoms — the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) recommends doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 10 to 21 days, or amoxicillin or cefuroxime for the doxycycline-intolerant, and the cure rate at this stage is high. Peat's position on the acute case aligned with this: "A course of antibiotics for a week or two is a way to eliminate it. The Lyme disease can be eradicated, just as syphilis would be" ([[b0db9b|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, July 2011]]).

The chronic case is where medicine fractures. The IDSA position is that "chronic Lyme disease" — meaning a persistent active *Borrelia* infection in a patient previously treated — is not a real entity, that the syndrome of post-treatment fatigue, pain, and cognitive complaints (formally "Post-Treatment Lyme Disease Syndrome," PTLDS) affects perhaps 10-20% of treated patients but represents post-infectious immune dysregulation rather than ongoing infection, and that extended antibiotic courses do not benefit and may harm. The International Lyme and Associated Diseases Society (ILADS) and Lyme-literate clinicians hold that *Borrelia* can persist in tissues, biofilms, and immune-privileged sites despite standard treatment, that co-infections frequently go undiagnosed, and that combination antibiotic regimens lasting months to years can be appropriate.

Both positions have evidence and both have failure modes. Mainstream short-course orthodoxy explains roughly nothing about the patient who is still incapacitated two years after a clean 21-day doxycycline course. Chronic-Lyme-clinic protocols have helped some patients enormously and produced iatrogenic harm in others, sometimes alongside opportunistic billing. Peat's position sat uncomfortably across the divide. He doubted that years-long antibiotic protocols were the answer for most chronic-Lyme patients. He also doubted that those patients were faking, malingering, or somatizing. The reconciliation in his framework runs through metabolism.

## Peat's framework

The central claim is that chronic persistence of a low-grade spirochetal infection is primarily a host-state problem, not a bug-virulence problem. "Probably low thyroid people have a bigger inflammatory response and are more likely to fail to throw off the infection," Peat told John Barkhausen on the [[532e51|2012 fundraiser]]. The hypothyroid host generates the wrong inflammatory profile — too much serotonin, prolactin, cortisol, estrogen; too little progesterone, CO2, and oxidative metabolism — and the immune system can't mount a clearance response that finishes the job. The polyunsaturated-fat-laden tissue is full of substrate for [[prostaglandins|inflammatory eicosanoids]] that suppress thyroid further. Chronic stress keeps cortisol elevated, which suppresses thyroid and immune function in tandem. The gut, with its [[endotoxin]] leak and its bacterial deconjugation of bile-excreted estrogen, keeps the systemic inflammatory load high. Into that physiology, a *Borrelia* infection lands and grinds. The bacterial load is not impressive; the host's failure to clear it is.

This reframes the chronic case. The question is not "how do we kill the spirochete that survived doxycycline" so much as "how do we restore the metabolism that allows the host to clear what's left of the spirochete and to stop generating the chronic-inflammation symptom complex that, with or without active bacteria, dominates the clinical picture." The two layers — bacterial and metabolic — are interlocking. Hypothyroid host can't clear infection; infection drives the inflammatory cascade that keeps host hypothyroid. Break either layer and the other becomes tractable.

Peat repeated a particular skepticism about Lyme diagnosis specifically. "Hypothyroidism is often misdiagnosed as Lyme disease," he told Gene on the [[532e51|fundraiser episode]]. "Candida is the other thing. If you can't find the cause, then Lyme disease and Candida are the first suspects. But usually, it's just hypothyroidism." Asked what to do about a positive test, he answered: "Lots of people do [test positive]. We all tend to be exposed to spirochetes and there's overlap with other spirochetes. A course of antibiotics for a week or two is a way to eliminate it." Asked whether he had luck treating people for Lyme, he answered with the most telling line he ever gave on the subject: "No, but they get well and forget about the Lyme diagnosis."

That phrasing is precise. He is not denying that some of those patients had Lyme. He is reporting that addressing the hypothyroid metabolism made them stop being sick, regardless of what the antibodies showed. The framework predicts both — Lyme can be real and the host-metabolic state can still be the rate-limiting variable for resolution.

Andrew Murray, the herbalist who co-hosted Peat on KMUD for years, made the same point in different words on the [[6bbd09|2014 Field Biology episode]]: "More often than not when people test positive on an ELISA test for *Borrelia* antibodies, they have a lot of other metabolic defects. That is what causes people to be so sick. Especially with the protocols that are used — fairly heavy drugs — these can really play into someone's downfall. It's more of a failure of the organism under the barrage of drugs and chemicals to treat it."

## Peat's protocol

The protocol has two layers running in parallel: antibiotic clearance of the bacterial load, and metabolic restoration of the host state that allowed persistence.

**Antibiotic core.** Doxycycline is the tetracycline-class drug Peat returned to most often — see [[doxycycline]] for the long version. For acute Lyme, 100 mg twice daily for 14 to 28 days follows the standard infectious-disease protocol; Peat's view was that two or three weeks of "the right combination of antibiotics" cures most acute cases. For the chronic state where the patient is also doing metabolic restoration, lower sub-microbial dosing (20 to 40 mg daily, the Periostat range) carries the anti-endotoxin, MMP-inhibiting, iNOS-suppressing, and quinone-like effects that fit Peat's broader anti-inflammatory framework without the gut-destructive consequences of full-dose long-course antibiotics. Minocycline is the alternative when CNS penetration matters more (Lyme neuroborreliosis, persistent cognitive symptoms), though its side-effect profile is worse and dental staining and lupus-like reactions are documented risks. Azithromycin is sometimes added for co-infection coverage in Lyme-literate protocols. Combination regimens require a clinician.

**Thyroid optimization.** The non-negotiable layer. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]]. Temperature and pulse rate (foot warmth in the morning, pulse rising from waking through the day) are the practical signals. Most chronic-Lyme patients Peat encountered tested or behaved hypothyroid; correcting that often resolved the majority of symptoms attributed to chronic Lyme. T4 and T3 in combination, with T3 dosed across the day, was his standard approach when supplementation was warranted.

**Anti-stress diet.** Sugar, salt, milk, cooked tropical fruit, gelatin-rich meats, well-cooked or peeled vegetables, eggs. Saturated fat (coconut oil, butter) replacing polyunsaturated cooking oils. Adequate calcium (the Maasai 5,000 mg/day reference Peat liked to cite). Carbohydrate not restricted — chronic infection is metabolically expensive, and glycogen depletion drives the cortisol-prolactin cascade that keeps thyroid suppressed.

**Gut decontamination.** See [[endotoxin-reduction]]. The raw carrot salad, the activated charcoal trial, the well-cooked white mushroom, occasional low-dose tetracycline to suppress gram-negative flora. The principle is to lower the constant background LPS load that keeps the systemic inflammatory tone high.

**[[cyproheptadine]] for neuro-inflammation.** The antihistamine-anti-serotonin drug Peat recommended most often for stress states. Useful for the neurological and sleep symptoms (brain fog, anxiety, insomnia, mast-cell-feeling reactions) that dominate chronic-Lyme presentations. Low evening dose (2-4 mg) is usually starting territory.

**Methylene blue periodically.** Low-dose [[methylene-blue]] supports mitochondrial electron transport against the inflammatory lactate backdrop. Pulse dosing rather than chronic use.

**Vitamin K2, D, A, E.** [[vitamin-k|K2]] for calcification control and immune modulation. Vitamin D for receptor function in immunity (sun preferred; supplement only with clear deficiency). Vitamin A from liver and eggs for thyroid conversion and immune-cell function. Vitamin E in food form.

**Sun and warmth.** The two interventions Peat returned to most consistently for any chronic inflammatory state. Cold inputs and shade make hypothyroid metabolism worse. The patient who improves the most quickly is usually the one who has stopped sitting in air conditioning and started getting morning sun.

The Ménière's protocol Peat outlined in correspondence is structurally close to his Lyme thinking and worth reading as parallel evidence: "Salt, magnesium, calcium, aspirin, antihistamine, progesterone, tetracycline (or minocycline, doxycycline) and thyroid can correct the fluid imbalance" ([[37bbbe|Ménière's email]]). The shared stack — tetracycline-class antibiotic, antihistamine (i.e., cyproheptadine class), thyroid, the supportive minerals and metabolic substrates — is the same architecture deployed against a different presentation.

## The co-infection question

Many chronic-Lyme patients are not chronic-Lyme patients in the single-organism sense. The tick that transmitted *Borrelia* often also transmitted *Bartonella*, *Babesia*, *Anaplasma*, *Ehrlichia*, or some combination. Each has a distinct treatment requirement that Peat himself did not detail in his transcripts but that any clinician treating the syndrome needs to address.

*Bartonella* species are intracellular bacteria classically treated with doxycycline plus rifampin or azithromycin plus rifampin in combination — single-agent therapy frequently fails. The symptom signature includes characteristic stretch-mark-like striae, severe neurological symptoms, agitation and rage states, and pain in the soles of the feet on first standing in the morning. *Babesia* species are malaria-class protozoa — not bacteria, not responsive to antibacterial antibiotics, treated with anti-malarial protocols (atovaquone plus azithromycin, or quinine plus clindamycin). Babesiosis presents with air hunger, drenching sweats, headache, and a particular heaviness that doesn't fit the *Borrelia* profile. *Anaplasma* and *Ehrlichia* respond to doxycycline, so they usually clear inside a standard Lyme course; the symptom signature (high fever, low platelets, transaminase elevation in acute presentation) is often missed because the patient is already on doxycycline by the time labs come back.

The practical implication is that a patient who isn't responding to *Borrelia*-targeted treatment is owed a co-infection workup by a clinician with experience in this territory. Peat's metabolic framework still applies — restoring host state still matters — but the specific antibiotic regimen has to match the organism.

## What this is NOT

This article is documentation of Peat's framework, not a clinical pathway for self-treatment. Several distinctions matter.

This is **not** the position that all chronic fatigue, all unexplained pain, or all mysterious neurological symptoms are Lyme. Peat's repeated point was the opposite — that hypothyroidism is the more common explanation for the syndrome that gets labeled chronic Lyme. The right move on a fatigued patient with no clear Lyme exposure history and no positive testing is the hypothyroid workup, not the empiric Lyme treatment.

This is **not** a substitute for clinician evaluation. Lyme disease is a reportable infection. Acute Lyme should be treated promptly by a physician. Chronic Lyme, suspected or confirmed, requires coordinated care — ideally with a clinician familiar with both standard infectious-disease practice and Lyme-literate protocols. The tetracycline-class antibiotics referenced here are prescription drugs with real interactions and contraindications (pregnancy, children under eight, calcium-binding co-administration with food, photosensitivity).

This is **not** a recommendation against the standard IDSA short-course antibiotics for confirmed acute cases. Two to three weeks of doxycycline for a fresh bull's-eye rash is, in Peat's framework as in mainstream infectious disease, the right move. The disagreement is downstream — about what to do when the standard course apparently fails.

This is **not** an endorsement of self-administered long-term antibiotics. Years of broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure wrecks gut flora, drives candida and resistant organism overgrowth, depletes mitochondrial function, and can produce a chronic-symptom complex that looks like the disease it was meant to treat. If long-course antibiotics are indicated, a clinician with experience and a parallel rebuild protocol — including the metabolic and gut layers above — is the minimum bar.

## Tracking signals

The signals that say the protocol is working overlap heavily with the signals that say any Peat metabolic restoration is working. Body temperature (oral or axillary in the morning, climbing toward 98.6°F by mid-morning). Pulse rate (rising from waking through the day, settling in the 75-90 range at rest in restored health). Sleep continuity. Energy across the day rather than mid-afternoon crash. Joint pain reduction. Brain fog clearing. Mood stability. Appetite normalizing. Body warmth — particularly hands and feet — returning. The neurological symptoms that mark chronic Lyme (the sense of pressure in the head, the cognitive slowing, the disordered sleep) tend to improve in tandem with these metabolic markers more reliably than they improve with antibiotic monotherapy.

Antibody titers on serial testing are a contested signal. They can drop with successful treatment, persist long after clearance, or rise transiently during Jarisch-Herxheimer-type reactions to bacterial die-off. Symptom resolution and metabolic-marker recovery are more clinically meaningful than serology trends.

## What to avoid

**Years of broad-spectrum antibiotics without parallel rebuild.** The bias of the chronic-Lyme-clinic industry is to keep the patient on rotating antibiotic combinations indefinitely. Without the metabolic and gut layers running in parallel, this iatrogenic load can produce a clinical picture indistinguishable from chronic Lyme itself.

**Restrictive elimination diets driven by symptom flares.** The mast-cell-feeling reactivity that often accompanies chronic Lyme produces apparent food sensitivities that drive patients toward narrower and narrower diets. Carbohydrate restriction in particular is a classic trap — the cortisol-prolactin-serotonin cascade gets worse, not better, on a low-carb chronic-illness diet. The Peat-frame move is to restore thyroid and address gut endotoxin, not to remove foods.

**Chronic NSAID use other than [[aspirin]].** The COX-2-selective and broad-spectrum anti-inflammatories suppress the resolution phase of inflammation. Aspirin is the Peat-favored anti-inflammatory because it spares prostacyclin and supports oxidative metabolism; ibuprofen, naproxen, and celecoxib do not share that profile.

**Cold-plunge biohacking.** See [[cold-plunge-dont-do-it]]. The dopamine spike that ice-bath proponents celebrate is the adrenaline-and-cortisol cascade the chronically inflamed body least needs. Cold inputs make hypothyroid metabolism worse. Heat — sauna at tolerable temperatures, hot baths, sun exposure, a warm climate when accessible — is the directionally correct intervention.

**Treating "Lyme" without ruling out hypothyroidism first.** The single most consistent Peat point on this topic. The patient walking into a Lyme clinic deserves a thyroid workup before the antibiotic protocol begins.

## Counter-arguments

**IDSA short-course-only orthodoxy.** The mainstream infectious-disease position is that 14 to 28 days of doxycycline cures the vast majority of Lyme cases, that "chronic Lyme" in the sense of persistent active infection is not supported by controlled trials, that the Klempner trial and successor RCTs found no benefit from extended antibiotic courses for PTLDS, and that the residual symptom syndrome reflects post-infectious immune dysregulation treatable with rehabilitation and time. Peat's framework partially agrees (host metabolic state matters, antibiotic monotherapy isn't enough), partially disagrees (mainstream medicine's failure to treat hypothyroidism in PTLDS patients leaves them stuck), and aligns on the specific point of opposing years-long antibiotic protocols.

**The "chronic Lyme is psychosomatic" position.** A subset of mainstream physicians and skeptics hold that chronic-Lyme-syndrome patients are somatizing — that the diagnosis is a label for medically unexplained symptoms in suggestible patients. Peat's position was emphatically not this. He took the symptoms seriously; his disagreement with the chronic-Lyme-clinic world was about the treatment, not the suffering. The framework here treats the chronic state as a real metabolic-immune-bacterial complex, not as a psychological projection.

**The chronic-Lyme-clinic industry counter-criticism.** A counter-current within the ILADS world acknowledges that not every clinician operating in this space is acting in good faith. Years of IV antibiotics, expensive specialty testing of contested validity, and treatment plans that never end have produced documented harm. Peat's instinct to look for hypothyroidism first, to use antibiotics in short courses, and to rebuild the host rather than indefinitely attack the organism is partial alignment with this internal critique.

**The "antibiotics damage everything; treat with herbs only" alternative-medicine position.** A subset of the chronic-illness community rejects pharmaceutical antibiotics entirely in favor of herbal protocols (Buhner, Cowden, Stephen Harrod Buhner's *Healing Lyme*). The herbal protocols have documented activity against *Borrelia* in vitro and some clinical traction. Peat's position was not anti-antibiotic — he liked the tetracyclines specifically and used them — but he was hostile to multi-year drug protocols. A short tetracycline course combined with metabolic restoration is the centrist position his framework supports; herbal-only treatment was not something he endorsed in his recorded transcripts, though he also did not directly oppose it.

## Data gaps

Mainstream chronic-Lyme RCTs are scarce and the major ones (Klempner 2001, Krupp 2003, Fallon 2008) have small samples, heterogeneous endpoints, and conclusions that depend heavily on how PTLDS is defined. The persistence question is mechanistically incompletely resolved — biofilm formation, intracellular sequestration, persister-cell phenotypes, and round-body morphological variants have all been demonstrated in vitro but their clinical significance is contested. The role of co-infections in driving "chronic Lyme" presentations is plausibly central but quantitatively undocumented in standard infectious-disease literature. The host-metabolic-state hypothesis Peat advanced — that thyroid status, PUFA load, and gut endotoxin determine clearance — has near-zero formal trial evidence; it is a clinical-pattern observation, not RCT-validated mechanism.

What we do not have is the trial that would settle the question definitively: a parallel-arm comparison of standard short-course doxycycline alone, extended doxycycline alone, short-course doxycycline plus a structured metabolic-restoration protocol (thyroid, gut, anti-stress diet), and metabolic restoration alone, in patients meeting consensus PTLDS criteria. Until such a trial exists, the framework here is documentation of a clinical position rather than evidence-based recommendation.

The chronic-Lyme-clinic industry has produced extensive case-series literature that mainstream infectious-disease medicine discounts. The mainstream short-course literature has produced RCTs that the Lyme-literate world considers underpowered to detect the patient populations they treat. The two sides have largely stopped exchanging evidence. A patient navigating this terrain is left to triangulate.
