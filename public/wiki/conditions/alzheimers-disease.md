# Alzheimer's Disease

Someone once proposed that Alzheimer's disease is "[[diabetes]] of the brain." Ray Peat found the comparison apt but incomplete. Like diabetes, Alzheimer's involves cells starving for energy despite available fuel. Like diabetes, the real culprits are impaired oxidative [[metabolism]], excess [[estrogen]], accumulated [[PUFA]], and the cascading inflammatory responses these produce. But the brain's unique vulnerability—its high concentration of DHA, its extraordinary metabolic demands, its dependence on [[progesterone]] and [[pregnenolone]] for stability—means that the metabolic failure manifests as cognitive destruction rather than blood sugar dysregulation.

## The Estrogen Problem

The single most revealing fact about Alzheimer's disease is its sex ratio. As Peat noted in a [[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-autoimmune-and-movement-disorders|2012 episode]], "Women have two to two-and-a-half times the incidence of Alzheimer's as men." Despite this, the medical establishment spent decades promoting estrogen as brain-protective—a position that required ignoring both the epidemiological evidence and the basic biology of what estrogen does to neurons.

Estrogen excites neurons. As Peat described in a [[2011-07-04-its-rainmaking-time-its-rainmaking-time-july-2011|2011 interview]], estrogen "excites the brain like cocaine"—stimulating glutamate receptors, increasing intracellular [[calcium]], and promoting the excitotoxic damage that destroys neurons over time. Progesterone does the opposite, calming neural activity through GABA enhancement and NMDA receptor inhibition.

The most striking evidence comes from aromatase-knockout mice. As Peat recounted in a [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode on dementia and progesterone]], mice genetically unable to produce the aromatase enzyme—meaning they cannot convert testosterone to estrogen—do not develop dementia. The implication is direct: estrogen production is necessary for the development of the disease.

By age forty, most people reach their peak ratio of estrogen to progesterone. As progesterone declines, estrogen's excitotoxic effects go unopposed, and the slow destruction of neurons accelerates. The "normal" cognitive decline of aging in Peat's framework is largely the consequence of this shifting hormonal ratio—not an inevitable feature of biological aging.

## DHA and Excitotoxicity

The brain concentrates DHA—a highly unsaturated omega-3 fatty acid—more than any other tissue. With [[aging]], DHA levels in the brain increase rather than decrease, making neural tissue progressively more vulnerable to [[lipid peroxidation]]. As Peat described in a [[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-autoimmune-and-movement-disorders|2012 episode]], DHA breaks down to form acrolein, which "attaches to tau protein involved in Alzheimer's filament formation." DHA also "activates excitotoxic processes and increases glutamate in the brain."

This connects the dietary advice to avoid fish oil directly to Alzheimer's prevention. The very supplement promoted for "brain health" concentrates the fatty acid whose breakdown products are implicated in the disease's characteristic pathology—tau tangles and amyloid plaques.

## Iron and the Aging Brain

[[Iron]] accumulates in the brain with age, catalyzing the free radical reactions that drive lipid peroxidation of DHA and other unsaturated fats. As Peat discussed in his newsletter *Homeostasis and Aging*, iron and calcium both accumulate during aging and stress, promoting excitatory damage. Bicarbonate helps keep iron in its inactive form, which is one reason adequate [[carbon dioxide]] production—maintained by good oxidative metabolism—protects the brain.

The combination of rising iron, rising DHA, declining progesterone, and rising estrogen creates a convergence of pro-degenerative factors in the aging brain. Each factor amplifies the others: estrogen promotes iron retention; iron catalyzes DHA peroxidation; DHA breakdown products damage mitochondria; damaged mitochondria cannot produce the pregnenolone and progesterone that would oppose estrogen and stabilize neural function.

## Reversal, Not Just Prevention

Peat reported seeing dramatic cognitive improvements with hormonal intervention. In a [[2011-07-04-its-rainmaking-time-its-rainmaking-time-july-2011|2011 interview]], he described "seeing demented people become normal in days with thyroid, progesterone, and pregnenolone." In his [[2012-01-22-politics-and-science-politics-science-progesterone-part-1|2012 progesterone lecture]], he recounted a 52-year-old woman rendered "hopelessly demented" by epilepsy and anti-seizure drugs who, after absorbing progesterone, stopped seizing, recovered her cognition, and returned to university to earn a master's degree with straight A's.

These cases suggest that at least some of what is diagnosed as irreversible dementia may represent metabolic suppression rather than structural destruction—neurons too energy-depleted to function but not yet dead. The therapeutic window depends on intervening before excitotoxic damage becomes permanent, which is why Peat emphasized prevention through sustained metabolic support: adequate [[thyroid]], progesterone, pregnenolone, and avoidance of the PUFA, estrogen, and iron accumulation that create the degenerative conditions in the first place.

As Peat wrote in his newsletter *Autoimmunity*, "In Alzheimer's disease, brain metabolism is low," and "prevention should concentrate on protecting energy metabolism." The approach is the same as for every degenerative condition in Peat's framework: restore the energy that maintains structure and function, and remove the substances that destroy it.
