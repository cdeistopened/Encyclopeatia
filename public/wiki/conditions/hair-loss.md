# Hair Loss

In the orthodox dermatology textbook, androgenetic alopecia has a single causal arrow: testosterone, converted by 5-alpha reductase to dihydrotestosterone, miniaturizes the follicle in genetically sensitive scalps. The remedy, accordingly, is to block the enzyme (finasteride), block the receptor (spironolactone), or stimulate the deprived follicle directly (minoxidil). Ray Peat regarded this entire framing as a category error — a downstream marker promoted to upstream cause, much as testosterone was once falsely accused of driving prostate cancer.

"I think the belief in testosterone as the cause is similar to the old belief that males got prostate cancer because of testosterone. Since males get a particular kind of baldness, it's easy to blame it on testosterone. But in fact, there is no evidence showing that excess testosterone is responsible for it, any more than excess testosterone causes prostate cancer. The idea of 'increased sensitivity' came in because there was no evidence of too much of the hormone" — [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, July 2011]]. "That fills in for a lack of evidence."

When Peat looked at the hormonal profile of people actually losing hair, the picture inverted. The drivers were [[prolactin]], [[cortisol]], [[estrogen]], and [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]] — all stress signals — riding on a substrate of suppressed [[thyroid]] function. Hair is an energy-expensive tissue with a fast turnover cycle, one of the first things the organism sheds when systemic respiration falters. Treating the follicle as the problem misses what the follicle is reporting.

## The follicle as a thyroid organ

Hair follicles produce hair vigorously or not at all, and the rate-limiting input is oxidative energy. "The hair follicle has to produce energy vigorously. If your systemic energy is being blocked, the skin is one of the first places to experience atrophy" — [[#80： World War 3 ｜ Heavy Metals ｜ Progesterone for Hair Loss ｜ T3 to T4 Thyroid Ratio with Ray Peat|*Generative Energy* #80]] (2022). "You can live with quite a bit of atrophy of your hair, skin, and nails and still keep your lungs and brain working." Hair is peripheral, fast-turning, metabolically expendable. Falling hair is a triage signal, not a hair disease.

Scalp temperature reinforces the point. From [[#27： Thyroid Function & Pulse Rate ｜ Weaponized Culture ｜ Finasteride as an ＂Insane Decision＂|*Generative Energy* #27]] (2022): "Is a balding scalp proportionally colder?" — "That's because it's quiet relative to the other parts of the brain. If you raise your whole energy production, that whole syndrome (earlobe crease, high cholesterol) almost always goes with chronic low temperature."

The link to systemic disease is not metaphorical. "Bald people have [the sign presaging aging of the circulatory system] at a very high frequency. It is an indication of the likelihood of heart disease developing" — same episode. The *Journal of Aging and Health* found premature greying and balding clustered with low bone mineral density (Morton et al., 2007, cited in [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|*Osteoporosis, Aging, Tissue Renewal, and Product Science*]]). Hair, bone, and arterial wall report one disease under three names.

For the master variable, see [[thyroid]]. When oxidative respiration fails, stress hormones rise, [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] replaces [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]], and tissue maintenance collapses peripherally first.

## Prolactin: the molting hormone

If [[thyroid]] is the master variable, [[prolactin]] is the proximate executioner. "In birds, prolactin is known as the molting hormone. In humans, it is the milk-producing hormone, but it also regulates practically every other cell in the body. It has the analogous function of terminating the growth cycle of the hair shaft" — [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]].

The single most important hormonal datum in Peat's hair-loss framework directly inverts the orthodox story: "When you look at the actual hormone situation of people with lots of hair versus those with not much, you see an excess of prolactin and cortisol in the people losing their hair — both men and women. Testosterone actually makes the hair shaft grow thicker and faster. Prolactin tends to cause it to fall out" (same episode).

The "normal" prolactin range hides the problem. "Back in the 70s, they found the healthy range for women was around 12 to 15, and for men, it was about 4 to 7. They've now raised the upper limit for men sometimes as high as 20. But all the men I've talked to who had levels close to 20 — even though they were told they were normal — had developing breasts, or gynecomastia. Fertility is best when a man has a level of 4 to 7" (same episode). The reference range has been bent around population [[estrogen]] exposure. A "normal" prolactin of 18 on a modern lab is a 1970s-era four-fold elevation.

Hirsutism and pattern hair loss cluster around the same hormone. "Both hirsutism and hair loss are associated with high prolactin. A thyroid supplement is a very reliable way to control prolactin in most women. Vitamin A and calcium also help" — same episode. Prolactin is a tissue-remodeling stress signal: it disrupts follicle cycling, accelerating growth where it shouldn't and terminating it where it should be maintained.

The female-pattern case is clean: menopausal thinning, postpartum shedding, post-recovery loss in former bulimics — all track prolactin, not testosterone. From the [[#47： The Power Elite ｜ Vaccines ｜ Hair Loss ｜ UFOs ｜ Surviving The Great Reset with Ray Peat, PhD|*Generative Energy* #47]] (2021): "The whole 'menopause is estrogen failure' idea is a total fraud created by the industry to sell a product. All of the symptoms of menopause can be produced by too much estrogen or too little progesterone." Perimenopausal scalp thinning is unopposed estrogen plus failing progesterone plus rising prolactin. The bulimic case: "high prolactin and high serotonin during those appetite disturbances. It probably started because of hypothyroidism, but at a certain point, hair can start falling when the thyroid is really low and the stress hormones are high" ([[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]]).

## Estrogen, prostaglandins, and the local follicular environment

Estrogen elevates prolactin and itself directly attacks the hair shaft. "Estrogen increases the production of prolactin, and estrogen itself terminates the growth of hair" — [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]]. A striking veterinary observation: "There have been interesting studies on dogs. Certain dogs became completely bald when their owners started supplementing estrogen on their skin" — [[#47： The Power Elite ｜ Vaccines ｜ Hair Loss ｜ UFOs ｜ Surviving The Great Reset with Ray Peat, PhD|*Generative Energy* #47]]. Topical estrogen transferred from owner to pet is enough to denude the animal. Dinkov added: "Balding people tend to have high levels of estrogen and prolactin in the scalp. Both prolactin and estrogen are known to destroy the follicle."

The local mechanism converges on prostaglandins. From the [[hair|hair email]]: "Yes, topical aspirin and caffeine stimulate hair growth... since prostaglandins are involved in atrophy of hair follicles, inhibiting prostaglandins locally could help." Inflammatory prostaglandins from stress-released [[pufa]] are part of what shuts down the follicle — which is why [[aspirin]] is scalp-relevant, not just systemic. The estrogen–prolactin–prostaglandin loop explains why the worst hair-loss windows in women are the high-estrogen, low-progesterone windows: postpartum, perimenopause, oral contraceptive use, HRT.

## DHT is what it looks like, not what it does

The orthodoxy notices something real: pattern baldness correlates with scalp 5-alpha reductase activity, which makes DHT. Peat did not contest the observation, only the direction of the causal arrow.

[[dihydrotestosterone|DHT]] in his framework is one of the body's *stabilizing* steroids — closely related metabolically to [[progesterone]] and [[allopregnanolone]], protective of the brain and the follicle. "Progesterone, dihydrotestosterone (DHT), and all the stabilizing steroids are essential to holding our organism in its shape and determining its future shape — the way it coils and uncoils" — [[#27： Thyroid Function & Pulse Rate ｜ Weaponized Culture ｜ Finasteride as an ＂Insane Decision＂|*Generative Energy* #27]]. Dinkov in the same conversation: "If you give snails a 5-alpha reductase inhibitor (like finasteride), they no longer coil. It completely disrupts their morphogenetic field." The 5-alpha-reduced steroids organize the architecture of the organism.

If DHT is protective, why is it elevated in balding scalps? Peat's reading: it appears as *compensation* — local tissue mounting a defense against the upstream estrogen and prolactin assault. The scalp is androgen-sensitive precisely because androgens are protective there. What the orthodoxy sees as the assassin, Peat saw as the bodyguard arriving too late. This inversion is the single most contested claim in this article. It is also the claim that determines whether finasteride is medicine or self-harm.

## Male pattern versus female pattern: same axis, different expression

The orthodox textbook treats male and female pattern hair loss as different conditions. Peat treated them as different presentations of one metabolic state.

**Male pattern.** Vertex-and-frontal recession maps onto falling [[thyroid]], rising estrogen and prolactin, accumulating PUFA, declining DHEA and progesterone. The visible androgens — beard, body hair — appear robust even as scalp hair fails. The scalp is being attacked by estrogen and prolactin, with androgens partially defending.

**Female pattern.** Diffuse crown thinning without a clear hairline recession. Maps onto unopposed estrogen plus rising prolactin, often layered onto subclinical hypothyroidism. Triggers are predictable: postpartum (progesterone crash plus lactational prolactin), perimenopause (failing ovulation, dominant estrogen), oral contraceptive initiation or discontinuation, diet-induced thyroid suppression. Peat noted in the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]] that hirsutism and scalp thinning often co-occur — different follicle types responding to the same systemic prolactin elevation.

**Alopecia areata.** From the [[hair|hair email]]: "Hypothyroidism is often a factor, and I think the 'autoimmune' process is promoted by endotoxin and nitric oxide from bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine; a pregnenolone supplement can improve response to thyroid supplements. Well cooked mushrooms and bamboo shoots have antiinflammatory, antiseptic effects that can reduce inflammation... If you don't get regular sun exposure, vitamin D supplements might help." Gut-to-immune-to-follicle, same chain as inflammatory bowel disease.

**Telogen effluvium and postpartum shedding.** Acute prolactin and cortisol elevation combined with low-thyroid metabolism. The bulimic-recovery case Peat treated is the prototype: starvation drops thyroid, prolactin and serotonin rise, follicle growth phase terminates, the shed appears two to three months later. Postpartum: progesterone falls precipitously after delivery, prolactin remains elevated for lactation, thyroid shifts. The intervention is substrate restoration ([[sugar]], salt, calories, thyroid), not chasing the shed.

## Counter-arguments

The DHT theory of androgenetic alopecia has the strongest empirical support of any orthodoxy Peat inverted, and the counter-arguments deserve real engagement.

**Castrates do not bald.** James B. Hamilton's 1942 study found no pattern baldness in men castrated before puberty; some developed it when given exogenous testosterone. This is the foundational human evidence for the androgen theory. Peat would not contest the observation, but would note that pre-pubertal castration removes far more than DHT — it removes the entire steroidogenic context. The eunuch's low-estrogen, low-prolactin, low-stress profile is exactly the profile in which pattern baldness does not express. Hamilton's data is consistent with the DHT theory but does not exclude the metabolic-stress theory.

**Finasteride works in trials.** MERCK's Propecia trials showed about 65% of men experienced visible hair retention or modest regrowth over two years versus 7% on placebo. The effect is real. Peat's counter: finasteride works by deranging the steroid system enough to produce a hair-cycle perturbation that incidentally favors the follicle short-term (the "early false puberty" effect), and the persistent sexual, cognitive, and emotional dysfunction documented in post-finasteride syndrome is the unconcealed price. Trial efficacy does not vindicate the proposed mechanism, and short-trial data does not capture long-tail systemic harm.

**Twin studies and GWAS.** Identical twins develop similar baldness patterns; GWAS has identified the androgen receptor locus on the X chromosome plus roughly 280 other loci. The genetic signal is real. Peat's framework at [[genetic-determinism]] distinguishes genetic *predisposition* from genetic *causation*. The same genome on a modern PUFA-loaded, [[xenoestrogens|xenoestrogen]]-saturated substrate produces far more pattern baldness than it would on a different metabolic substrate. Identical twins share metabolic environments alongside genomes. The GWAS data describes who is most susceptible to the modern environment, not who is fated regardless of environment.

**5-alpha reductase Type 2 deficiency.** Men with congenital absence of the enzyme producing scalp DHT do not develop pattern baldness even at advanced ages. This is the orthodoxy's strongest card. The Peat-consistent reading is that these men's atypical steroid profile across the board protects them from the systemic stress signature, not that DHT was specifically destructive — but the observation remains the cleanest natural experiment available and is not fully addressed in Peat's published material.

**JAK inhibitors regrow hair in alopecia areata.** Baricitinib and ritlecitinib produce dramatic regrowth by suppressing the IFN-γ inflammatory cascade attacking the follicle. Clinically impressive and entirely consistent with Peat's framework — alopecia areata is the most clearly inflammatory of the hair-loss conditions. The data validates "inflammation destroys the follicle" without resolving whether prolactin/estrogen or DHT is the upstream driver in androgenetic alopecia.

**Minoxidil's vasodilator mechanism.** Topical minoxidil's efficacy supports the circulation-deficit theory broadly but works through a [[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]] pathway Peat regarded as systemically destructive. The end (scalp circulation) is compatible with his framework; the means is not.

Honest summary: Peat's model integrates a wider set of findings (the prolactin/cortisol elevation, the thyroid suppression, the heart-disease/bone-loss/baldness clustering, the female-pattern data, post-finasteride syndrome) than the DHT theory does. The DHT theory explains the specific anatomical pattern of male androgenetic alopecia and finasteride's trial efficacy more parsimoniously. A fair synthesis is that DHT acts as a final-step effector on follicles already weakened by systemic metabolic stress — but this is not what the orthodoxy claims.

## Data gaps

- **Direct measurement of scalp prolactin in pattern hair loss.** Peat repeatedly asserted that balding scalps show elevated local prolactin and estrogen. The published evidence is partial — scalp PRL receptor expression is documented, local conversion of androgens to estrogen via aromatase is documented, but the head-to-head clinical comparison Peat described has not been done at scale.
- **Long-term outcomes of the Peat protocol.** Anecdotal regrowth reports exist throughout the Peatian online community, but no controlled trial has tested the combined thyroid/progesterone/calcium/vitamin D/PUFA-reduction protocol against minoxidil or finasteride.
- **5-alpha reductase Type 2 deficiency.** The natural-experiment data is the strongest evidence for the orthodox model and has not been engaged seriously in Peat's published material. A Peat-consistent reading exists but is not empirically tested.
- **The "moves to high altitude and regrew his hair" cases.** Peat referenced these as if they were established. The published evidence is limited to small case series and the broader high-altitude longevity literature. A controlled study has not been done and cannot easily be done.
- **Female-pattern hair loss in low-hormone contraceptive users.** Peat tied OCP use to hair loss via the prolactin–estrogen mechanism. The dermatology literature is mixed: some progestin-dominant formulations are associated with hair loss, others are neutral. The relationship deserves cleaner study.
- **Topical T3 dosing.** Compounded topical T3 formulations exist but the systemic absorption, scalp-specific efficacy, and safety window are not well characterized.
- **Whether the "DHT-as-bodyguard" reading survives rigorous testing.** Peat asserted, but did not develop in detail, the claim that scalp DHT is a protective compensation rather than a destructive driver. This is the most contested and most consequential claim in his framework and is the one most in need of careful empirical work.
