# Osteoporosis

The estrogen industry built a billion-dollar business on the claim that bone loss in women results from estrogen deficiency at menopause. Ray Peat considered this one of the most profitable lies in the history of medicine. The evidence, he argued, shows that [[estrogen]] actively promotes bone loss—and that the real protective factors are [[progesterone]], [[thyroid]] hormone, adequate [[calcium]], and the [[carbon dioxide]] produced by efficient oxidative [[metabolism]].

As Peat described in a [[2012-01-29-politics-and-science-politics-science-progesterone-part-2|2012 lecture on progesterone]], there is "no evidence that estrogen improves bone in humans." In beagle dogs—a common pharmaceutical research model—estrogen caused *more* osteoporosis, not less. The industry cherry-picked rodent studies to support estrogen therapy while ignoring the contradictory evidence from animals whose bone physiology more closely resembles humans'.

## Estrogen Destroys Bone

The mechanism is not obscure. Estrogen activates osteoclasts—the cells that break down bone. As Peat explained in a [[2016-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-rheumatoid-arthritis|2016 episode on rheumatoid arthritis]], estrogen "activates osteoclasts," the bone-resorbing cells, "despite journals claiming osteoarthritis comes from estrogen deficiency." The confusion persists because estrogen also stimulates some bone-forming activity—but the net effect, particularly over time, is destructive.

Men develop osteoporosis too, and Peat noted that their bone loss correlates with rising estrogen levels with age—not falling testosterone. The pattern is the same for both sexes: as the ratio of estrogen to progesterone increases, bone density declines.

## The Prolactin Connection

[[Prolactin]] is a major but underrecognized driver of bone loss. As Peat explained in [[2011-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-inflammation-ii|2011]] and [[2011-03-15-east-west-healing-east-west-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone-and-the-warburg-effect|2011]] episodes, "Prolactin causes bone loss and osteoporosis. Estrogen increases prolactin; [[serotonin]] is the mechanism by which estrogen increases prolactin."

This chain—estrogen → serotonin → prolactin → bone loss—means that estrogen therapy for osteoporosis attacks the problem by adding more of one of its causes. Each link in the chain has been individually documented, yet the industry continues promoting estrogen as bone-protective.

The prolactin connection also explains why [[hypothyroidism]] promotes osteoporosis independently of estrogen. When thyroid function is low, TSH rises, and prolactin tends to rise with it. Both hormones impair bone metabolism.

## How Bone Is Actually Built

Bone formation depends on a process that Peat connected directly to oxidative metabolism. As he explained in a [[2014-06-02-its-rainmaking-time-its-rainmaking-time-june-2014|2014 interview]], "T3—active thyroid—stimulates bone cell metabolism, producing CO2 which crystallizes with calcium to form new bone. Hypothyroid women have more osteoporosis."

The mechanism is specific: osteoblasts (bone-building cells) require carbon dioxide to incorporate calcium into the hydroxyapatite crystal matrix that gives bone its mineralized strength. CO2 is produced by efficient mitochondrial oxidation of glucose—the metabolism that thyroid hormone supports. When metabolism shifts toward [[lactic acid]] production—as happens in hypothyroidism, PUFA exposure, and estrogen dominance—less CO2 is produced, and bone formation slows.

Worse, lactic acid actively dissolves bone. [[Parathyroid hormone]] (PTH), which rises whenever dietary calcium is insufficient or [[phosphate]] is excessive, blocks oxygen utilization in tissues, promoting lactic acid production that dissolves bone mineral. The freed calcium then deposits in soft tissues—arteries, kidneys, joints—producing the paradox of simultaneous bone loss and soft tissue calcification.

As Peat described in his newsletter *Peptides, Coherent Adaptation, and Some Terminal Diseases*, optimal CO2 levels—as found at high altitude—cause calcium to be properly controlled, lowering PTH. People living at moderate altitude have lower rates of osteoporosis, heart disease, and cancer—conditions that share the common metabolic signature of inadequate CO2 production.

## [[Vitamin K]] and Calcium Direction

[[Vitamin K]] activates the proteins that direct calcium into bone and keep it out of arteries. Combined with adequate [[vitamin D]] (which promotes calcium absorption) and high dietary calcium (which suppresses PTH), vitamin K completes the system that maintains bone density. [[Aspirin]] contributes by suppressing the inflammatory [[prostaglandins]] that activate osteoclasts—the same prostaglandins produced from [[PUFA]] breakdown that promote bone resorption.

The Japanese studies Peat cited demonstrated that high-dose vitamin K could simultaneously rebuild osteoporotic bone while removing calcium from arteries—proving that the misdirection of calcium is a regulatory problem, not a calcium excess problem.

## The Progesterone Alternative

Progesterone is the authentic bone-protective hormone that estrogen was falsely claimed to be. It supports bone formation through multiple mechanisms: it promotes oxidative metabolism and CO2 production; it opposes estrogen's activation of osteoclasts; it suppresses the prolactin that drives bone resorption; and it maintains the stable cellular energy state that keeps calcium in bone rather than allowing it to leak into soft tissues.

Women who have had multiple pregnancies—accumulating high progesterone exposure—maintain better bone density throughout life. The progesterone produced during pregnancy directly builds bone, while the estrogen industry's synthetic progestins do not replicate this effect.
