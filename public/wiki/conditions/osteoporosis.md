# Osteoporosis

The mainstream account of osteoporosis is built on two ideas Ray Peat spent half a century rejecting: that the disease is a deficiency of calcium, and that the disease is a deficiency of estrogen. From those two starting points follow the entire diagnostic apparatus (DEXA scans), the entire pharmacopeia (bisphosphonates, estrogen replacement, calcitonin, denosumab), and the entire dietary advice ("take a calcium supplement"). Peat read every step of this chain as inverted. Osteoporosis is not a problem of too little calcium intake; calcium-deficient diets *cause* the bone loss by driving parathyroid hormone up. It is not a problem of estrogen withdrawal; estrogen *accelerates* bone loss by raising prolactin, suppressing osteoblasts, and degrading the respiratory metabolism the skeleton depends on. The bones thin while the arteries calcify because the same hormonal current drives both. The mainstream "treatment" of bisphosphonate-poisoned osteoclasts and estrogen-driven cancers is the price paid for misreading the underlying biology.

The hub mechanisms this article rests on are documented elsewhere. The respiratory poisoning by estrogen is at [[estrogen]]; the antagonist actions of progesterone at [[progesterone]]; the catabolic stress hormone PTH at [[parathyroid-hormone]]; the calcium-paradox economy at [[calcium]]; the K-vitamin's role in directing calcium into bone instead of arteries at [[vitamin-k]]; the thyroid floor that the whole metabolic field rests on at [[thyroid]] and [[hypothyroidism]]; the cyclic-progesterone window through perimenopause at [[menopause]] and [[menopause-protocol]]. This page concentrates on the load-bearing distinctives: the inversion of the deficiency narrative, the causal chain that links bone loss to arterial calcification, the diagnostic critique of DEXA, the four-article Peat case against bisphosphonates and estrogen "bone protection," and the practical protocol the framework implies.

## What's Actually Happening to Bone

Bone is living tissue, continuously remodeled. Osteoblasts lay down new collagen matrix that mineralizes into calcium phosphate; osteoclasts dissolve old matrix so it can be replaced. The balance between these two cell populations is the operational definition of bone health. In youth, the balance tilts toward formation. In old age — and in the chronic metabolic stress most adults live in — it tilts toward resorption. The single most concise statement of Peat's reframing appeared in his 2011 article on tissue renewal: "The fact that the collagen content of old bone is lower than in young bone (as a percentage of bone weight) shows that the problem in osteoporosis isn't a lack of calcification, it's a deficiency of tissue renewal, parallel to sarcopenia, the decrease of muscle mass with aging" ([[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|*Osteoporosis, aging, tissue renewal, and product science* (2011)]]). The mainstream framing — that the mineral somehow leaks out of an otherwise-intact protein scaffold — is upside down. The scaffold itself is failing. Mineral content tracks the scaffold's health, not the other way around.

The mineral that does the work is not free calcium but a CO2-bound matrix. Peat described bone in [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|the same article]] as "a reservoir-buffer of carbon dioxide, able to bind huge amounts of the gas into its structure. When carbon dioxide increases in the bloodstream it is at first absorbed rapidly by the bones." The mineral in newly formed bone is calcium *carbonate*, which gradually converts to calcium phosphate. Carbon dioxide is also incorporated into osteocalcin, the bone-binding protein, in a vitamin-K-dependent reaction. Without adequate CO2 in the tissue fluids, the carbonate matrix does not form properly. This is why hyperventilation and respiratory alkalosis — induced by hypothyroidism, by HRT, by calcitonin, by activated vitamin-D analogs — drive bone loss in patterns that look nothing like a simple calcium-intake problem. From [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm* (2006)]]: "Calcitonin, vitamin D-active metabolite, and estrogen-'HRT' treatments can cause respiratory alkalosis (relative hyperventilation), and hypothyroidism produces a predisposition to hyperventilation. Hyperventilation tends to cause calcium loss. In respiratory alkalosis, CO2 (and sometimes bicarbonate) are decreased, impairing calcium retention, and in 'metabolic alkalosis,' with increased bicarbonate, calcium is retained more efficiently and bone formation is stimulated, and its dissolution is suppressed." The chickens-laying-thin-shells-when-they-pant analogy Peat returned to repeatedly is the clearest demonstration that the limiting nutrient in mineralization is the *gas*, not the mineral.

Calcium taken in by mouth does not, by itself, fix calcium deposition. This is the single deepest inversion of the mainstream story. From [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|the same newsletter]]: "In calcium deficiency, parathyroid hormone is increased, and tends to cause the typical changes of aging, shifting calcium from hard tissues to soft, and decreasing the ratio of extracellular to intracellular (excitatory) calcium." The pathway is: low dietary calcium → blood calcium tries to fall → parathyroid hormone rises → bone dissolves to supply the deficit → and the dissolved calcium, now in the wrong place, deposits in arteries, kidneys, mitochondria, and joints. The same diet that protects bones therefore also protects vessels: "The same diet that protects against osteoporosis, i.e., plenty of protein and calcium, etc., also protects against kidney stones and other abnormal calcifications" ([[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*]]). The economy is governed at the upstream regulator — PTH — and the dietary intervention that matters is the one that keeps PTH low. The full PTH mechanism, including its catabolic effects on every other tissue, is treated at [[parathyroid-hormone]].

## Peat's Causal Chain

The hormonal current Peat described as driving osteoporosis is the same current he described as driving aging itself. Each element in the chain is downstream of cellular energy failure and each element accelerates the failure of the next. The chain is sufficiently linked that pulling any one node moves the others.

**Estrogen → prolactin → bone resorption.** Estrogen raises prolactin, and prolactin mobilizes calcium from bone — the same hormone that mobilizes it during lactation continues to do so when chronically elevated in nonlactating women. In [[estrogen-and-osteoporosis|*Estrogen and Osteoporosis* (2000)]] Peat quoted "a former editor of *Yearbook of Endocrinology*" who "had reviewed a series of studies showing that excess prolactin can cause osteoporosis. Then, he presented a group of studies showing how estrogen promotes the secretion of prolactin, and can cause hyperprolactinemia. In that review, he wryly wondered how something that increases something that causes osteoporosis could prevent osteoporosis." The same article notes that "drugs that increase prolactin were found to cause osteoporosis" and that "in the 40 years since the drug industry began its intense promotion of estrogen to prevent and treat osteoporosis, there has been very little attention to the fact that estrogen increases prolactin, which contributes to osteoporosis." The full estrogen-as-respiratory-poison mechanism is at [[estrogen]]; the prolactin chain is sketched at [[prolactin]].

**Estrogen → osteoblast suppression.** Beyond the prolactin loop, estrogen directly suppresses bone formation. From [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*]], citing Kassem et al.: "estrogen is the cause of a variety of tissue atrophies, including the suppression of bone formation." The pharmaceutical-friendly account — that estrogen "prevents bone loss" by inhibiting osteoclasts — is technically correct but misleading. "With the clear evidence that estrogen inhibits the osteoclasts without activating the bone-building osteoblasts, estrogen was said to 'prevent bone loss,'" Peat wrote in [[estrogen-and-osteoporosis|*Estrogen and Osteoporosis*]]. "The inhibition of osteoclast activity by either estrogen or tamoxifen is probably a toxic action, that has been characterized as 'beneficial' by the estrogen industry simply because they didn't have any better argument for getting women to use their products." Inhibiting the cells that remove damaged bone without activating the cells that build new bone produces an older, more brittle skeleton. The DEXA scan registers the slowdown of turnover as "improved density." The skeleton continues to fail.

**PTH → bone-to-soft-tissue calcium shift.** The hormone that does the actual mineral dissolving is parathyroid hormone, and PTH rises in any state of chronic calcium insufficiency, magnesium deficiency, high-phosphate intake (industrial meats, sodas, additives), low CO2, or hypothyroidism. PTH instructs osteoclasts to acidify bone matrix and dissolve mineral. From [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|*Osteoporosis, aging, tissue renewal, and product science*]]: "The parathyroid hormone, which removes calcium from bone, causes lactic acid to be formed by bone cells. Lactic acid produced by intense exercise causes calcium loss from bone, and sodium bicarbonate increases calcium retention by bone. Vitamin K2 blocks the removal of calcium from bone caused by parathyroid hormone and prostaglandin E2, by completely blocking their stimulation of lactic acid production by bone tissues." PTH does not merely "regulate" calcium; it is a catabolic, anti-respiratory hormone that, when chronically elevated, drives the same process in bone (dissolution) and in arteries (calcification) by the same mechanism: a shift to lactic-acid energy production in tissues that should be running on oxygen.

**Cortisol and serotonin → bone loss in parallel.** The same metabolic state that elevates PTH typically elevates cortisol, and elevated cortisol is one of the most consistent drivers of bone loss in the clinical literature. Peat described in [[osteoporosis-harmful-calcification-and-nerve-muscle-malfunctions|*Osteoporosis, harmful calcification, and nerve/muscle malfunctions* (1996)]]: "Around the age of 50, when bone loss is increasing, progesterone and thyroid are likely to be deficient, and cortisol and prolactin are likely to be increased. Prolactin contributes directly to bone loss, and is likely to be one of the factors that contributes to decreased progesterone production." Serotonin entered the picture in 2008 when SSRI antidepressants, after a decade of unchecked use, were found to be producing osteoporosis. From [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|*Osteoporosis, aging, tissue renewal, and product science*]]: "A few years ago, the 'serotonin reuptake inhibitor' antidepressants, already known to increase prolactin by increasing the effects of serotonin, were found to be causing osteoporosis after prolonged use. Estrogen increases serotonin, which besides promoting the secretion of prolactin, also stimulates the production of parathyroid hormone and cortisol, both of which remove calcium from bone, and contribute to the calcification of blood vessels." Serotonin produced in the intestine — by inflammation, by endotoxin, by stress — has since been shown to suppress osteoblast activity directly. The serotonin pathway and the osteoporosis pathway run on the same circuit.

**The paradox of low bone density and high arterial calcification.** The endpoint of the chain is the syndrome that confounds the calcium-supplement school of nutrition: elderly patients whose bones are dissolving while their aortas, kidneys, and brain arterioles are calcifying. The conventional response — withhold calcium to protect the arteries — accelerates both processes by raising PTH further. The Peat-coherent response — restore dietary calcium, suppress PTH, raise CO2, supply vitamin K2, restore progesterone and thyroid — moves calcium *out* of soft tissue and *into* bone. From [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|*Osteoporosis, aging, tissue renewal, and product science*]]: "The association between weakened bones and hardened arteries is now widely recognized, but researchers are being careful to avoid investigating any mechanisms that could affect sales of important drug products, especially estrogen and antidepressants." The two pathologies are not separate. They are the same pathology in two anatomical locations, governed by the same hormonal current.

## The Mainstream Prescription Is Upside-Down

If the chain above is right, every major pillar of mainstream osteoporosis medicine is wrong, and most of them are net-harmful. Peat's four dedicated articles on the disease — published across thirty years — return to the same enumeration.

**Estrogen "bone protection" was always a marketing claim.** The estrogen-for-osteoporosis indication was approved by the FDA in 1972 on the basis of a positive calcium balance — the observation that estrogen-treated women excreted less calcium than they took in, taken as proof that the retained calcium was going into bones. The argument was already known to be wrong at the time. From [[estrogen-and-osteoporosis|*Estrogen and Osteoporosis*]]: "Thirty years ago, when people were already claiming that estrogen would prevent or cure osteoporosis, endocrinologists pointed out that there was no x-ray evidence to support the claim. Estrogen can cause a positive calcium balance, the retention of more calcium than is excreted, and the estrogen promoters argued that this showed it was being stored in the bones, but the endocrine physiologists showed that estrogen causes the retention of calcium by soft tissues. There are many reasons for not wanting calcium to accumulate in the soft tissues; this occurs normally in aging and stress." Once the soft-tissue retention was demonstrated, the calcium-balance argument quietly disappeared from estrogen marketing — replaced by the osteoclast-inhibition story, which substituted one truth (slower turnover) for the marketed claim (more bone). The Women's Health Initiative results in 2002 — showing that Prempro caused breast cancer, heart attacks, dementia, and strokes — collapsed the broader HRT consensus, and in Australia between 2001 and 2006 the annual incidence of hip fractures in women over 60 *decreased by 28.3%* while HRT prescriptions fell by 54.6% ([[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|*Osteoporosis, aging, tissue renewal, and product science*]]). The "estrogen protects bones" claim has not survived the natural experiment of women going off estrogen en masse.

**Bisphosphonates poison the cells that maintain bone.** The drug class that succeeded estrogen as the "first-line treatment" works by killing osteoclasts. The argument is that osteoclasts are the cells dissolving bone, so killing them must protect bone. The argument ignores that osteoclasts are also the cells that *remove damaged bone matrix so it can be replaced with healthy matrix*. A bisphosphonate-treated skeleton accumulates microdamage; the matrix becomes brittle; atypical femoral fractures, osteonecrosis of the jaw, and esophageal complications appear in the trial data. Peat did not write a stand-alone article on bisphosphonates because he considered the whole pharmaceutical approach to be misdirected: "the remedies for osteoporosis are things that everyone should be doing, anyway, so diagnosis makes no difference in what the physician should recommend to the patient" ([[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*]]). The closer-to-physiology approach — suppress PTH with adequate calcium, block PTH's lactic-acid-driven bone dissolution with vitamin K2, restore progesterone and thyroid — addresses the same downstream output (osteoclast over-activity) at the upstream regulator, without killing the cells that the skeleton needs to renew itself.

**Calcium supplementation in isolation drives soft-tissue calcification.** The supplement-aisle remedy — a 1,200 mg calcium pill, often calcium citrate, often without companion magnesium or vitamin K — produces a transient post-prandial calcium spike, a vitamin-D-driven absorption pulse, and not much skeletal benefit. The literature suggesting that isolated calcium supplements may *increase* cardiovascular events is exactly what Peat's framework predicts: a sudden bolus of mineral entering circulation, without the K2 to direct it to bone, deposits in the arteries that are already inflamed. The full economy of dietary calcium — milk and cheese rather than tablets, calcium carbonate from eggshell rather than citrate, calcium-to-phosphate ratio of roughly 2:1 — is treated at [[calcium]]. On the citrate point Peat was specific in the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hair loss, inflammation, and osteoporosis]]: "Citric acid itself causes you to lose calcium in your urine. Calcium carbonate isn't quite as soluble, but eventually, it has a chance to absorb. It is a very effective and safe supplement because the carbon dioxide is the form that stimulates bone formation rather than breaking it down."

**DEXA is a measurement with three problems and a dose.** The dual-photon x-ray absorptiometer is not measuring what physicians and patients believe it is measuring. Peat enumerated the failures in [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*]]. First, even seemingly identical machines give different estimates, and radiologists evaluating the same images reach different conclusions. Second, tissue water and fat content distort the measurement; estrogen, by changing both, can produce apparent bone-density gains that are entirely artifacts of soft-tissue edema. Third, soft-tissue calcification — the syndrome the same hormonal current is producing — can register as bone density on the scan, generating a misleadingly favorable reading from a deteriorating skeleton. Then there is the dose. "X-rays accelerate the rate of bone loss. X-rays do their harm at any dose; there is no threshold at which the harm begins" ([[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*]]). The radiation exposure used to measure bone density may exceed dental-x-ray exposure, particularly in hip and thigh scans, and dental x-rays are independently associated with thyroid cancer. Ultrasound bone-density measurement is accurate, dose-free, and even therapeutic (ultrasound stimulates bone repair). It is rarely used. Peat's blunt summary: "I think it would be reasonable to say that a woman with osteoporosis is usually better off when it's not diagnosed, because of the dangerous things prescribed for it" ([[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*]]).

## Peat's Protocol

The protocol that follows from the mechanism is the same protocol that follows from his framework for aging more broadly. There are six load-bearing pieces. The order matters less than the simultaneity — bone is a slow-turnover tissue and the metabolic field has to be sustained for twelve to twenty-four months for clinical signal to appear.

**Progesterone (women).** This is Peat's first move in any postmenopausal or peri-menopausal woman with bone loss. The mechanism is at [[progesterone]]; the phase-specific dosing through perimenopause is at [[menopause-protocol]]. The headline citation, from [[osteoporosis-harmful-calcification-and-nerve-muscle-malfunctions|*Osteoporosis, harmful calcification, and nerve/muscle malfunctions*]]: "Thyroid and progesterone, unlike estrogen, stimulate bone-building, and are associated with a decreased risk of cancer. It seems sensible to use thyroid and progesterone for their general anti-degenerative effects, protecting the bones, joints, brain, immune system, heart, blood vessels, breasts, etc." Progesterone restores the antagonist signal that estrogen, prolactin, and cortisol are otherwise running unopposed. Progest-E delivered to oral mucosa (3 mg per drop, three to four drops daily) is the dosing Peat described most consistently. In premenopausal women cycle-aware dosing (days 14–28) preserves the normal cycle while supplying the bone-protective signal.

**Thyroid restoration.** Hypothyroidism produces hyperventilation, low CO2, elevated cortisol, elevated prolactin, and elevated cholesterol — every variable that drives bone loss. From [[osteoporosis-harmful-calcification-and-nerve-muscle-malfunctions|*Osteoporosis, harmful calcification, and nerve/muscle malfunctions*]]: "Chronic hypothyroidism causes osteopenia, and in this connection, it is significant that women (as a result of estrogen's effects on the thyroid) are much more likely than men to be hypothyroid, and that, relative to men, women in general are 'osteopenic.'" Many women are told to *stop* thyroid medication when osteoporosis is diagnosed, on the basis of poorly designed studies of thyroxine monotherapy. Peat regarded this as the precise opposite of the correct move. Restoration with a desiccated thyroid preparation or a T4/T3 combination — dosed by basal temperature and pulse, the framework at [[hypothyroid-recovery]] — addresses the upstream defect that none of the bone-specific drugs reach. T3 directly stimulates osteoblast activity and CO2 production in the bone microenvironment.

**Vitamin K2.** The single most under-prescribed nutrient in bone medicine. K2 carboxylates osteocalcin so that osteocalcin can bind calcium to the matrix; it blocks PTH-driven lactic acid in bone cells; it oxidizes estradiol. The full mechanism is at [[vitamin-k]]. The dose range Peat referenced was 1–10 mg of menatetrenone (MK-4) daily, far above the conventional 90–120 μg "adequate intake." Japanese RCTs at 45 mg/day have shown reduced fracture incidence in postmenopausal osteoporosis. K2 with calcium and adequate vitamin D directs mineral into bone instead of arteries.

**Magnesium.** Magnesium deficiency is one of the consistent findings in postmenopausal bone loss. Peat preferred dietary sources (meats, fruits, leaves, coffee) and topical Epsom-salt baths over oral supplements, on the basis that magnesium salts cause bowel irritation. The metabolic case is at [[magnesium]]. Adequate magnesium opposes the intracellular calcium overload that drives the catabolic phase, supports the ATP that osteoblasts need, and lowers PTH alongside calcium.

**Calcium from food, in the right ratio.** Not isolated calcium tablets. Two quarts of milk daily — Peat's standing recommendation — supplies roughly 2,400 mg of calcium against a phosphate background that produces a favorable Ca:P ratio. Cheese is a more calcium-dense option for the lactose-intolerant; eggshell calcium carbonate (rinsed, baked, ground) is the supplemental form Peat endorsed because it releases CO2 on metabolism and so stimulates rather than suppresses bone formation. The full calcium-economy detail is at [[calcium]]. From [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm*]]: "Avoiding fluoridated water and the polyunsaturated oils, and drinking two quarts of milk daily (which will provide only 66 g of protein), and using some other nutrient-rich foods such as eggs and fruits, are probably the basic things to protect the bones. For vitamins, especially K, occasional liver can be helpful."

**Vitamin D, modestly.** Peat treated vitamin D as a hormone with a real but narrow role: enough to keep PTH suppressed and to support calcium absorption, not enough to produce respiratory alkalosis or hypercalciuria. The modal recommendation was 1,000–4,000 IU daily, dosed to a serum 25-OH-D of roughly 50 ng/mL, not the 80–100+ targets some practitioners push. Megadoses of activated vitamin D analogs (calcitriol) cause respiratory alkalosis, which drives bone loss. The full mechanism is at [[vitamin-d]].

**CO2 elevation.** The unique piece of Peat's framework that no mainstream protocol contains. The mineralization economy is governed by tissue CO2 content. Living at altitude, the Japanese hot-spring water studies, bag breathing ([[bag-breathing]]), and supplemental sodium bicarbonate all raise tissue CO2 and shift the calcium economy toward bone. From [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science|*Osteoporosis, aging, tissue renewal, and product science*]]: "Living at high altitude, which strengthens bones, increases thyroid activity and decreases prolactin and parathyroid hormone." Small doses of carbonated mineral water (Gerolsteiner, San Pellegrino) raise bicarbonate. Bag breathing for five-to-ten-minute periods raises CO2 acutely. The mechanism is at [[carbon-dioxide]].

**The auxiliary tier.** Aspirin restores mitochondrial respiration and stimulates bone formation; Peat noted that "aspirin can promote trabecular bone remodeling, improve three-dimensional structure of trabecular bone and increase bone density of cancellous in osteoporotic rats by stimulating bone formation" ([[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science]]). Salt — generous, not restricted — lowers cortisol and adrenaline, both of which drive bone loss. Sugar at bedtime, by suppressing the nocturnal cortisol-and-free-fatty-acid surge that does most of a day's bone resorption, may be one of the highest-leverage night-time interventions. PUFA elimination (the depletion protocol at [[pufa-depletion]]) lowers the inflammatory mediators and restores thyroid function. DHEA, in low doses, has been shown to normalize the postload hypercalcemia of menopausal osteoporosis. Copper, the cofactor for cytochrome C oxidase, is consistently deficient in osteoporotic bone; thyroid increases copper absorption, which is why thyroid is the upstream lever rather than copper itself.

**What is conspicuously absent.** No bisphosphonate. No estrogen. No SSRI. No calcium-citrate megadose. No calcitonin or activated vitamin-D analog. The mainstream pharmacy and the Peat pharmacy do not overlap, because the underlying models of the disease do not overlap.

## Tracking Signal

The slow turnover of bone means a Peat-aligned protocol does not produce a DEXA change on the timescale most patients want. The signal arrives in two layers.

**The metabolic signal arrives first.** Within four to twelve weeks of starting progesterone and thyroid restoration with PUFA reduction, the same patient should see basal temperature rising toward 98.6°F on waking, resting pulse rising into the 75–90 range, hair stopping its fall, cold extremities warming, mood lifting, sleep deepening, and the night-time cortisol surge breaking up. The standard temperature-and-pulse tracking is at [[temperature-tracking]]. If these markers do not move, the protocol is not yet adequate; bone will not recover until they do.

**The structural signal arrives over twelve to twenty-four months.** A repeat DEXA at twelve months in a patient whose metabolic markers have normalized typically shows stabilization rather than dramatic gain. By eighteen-to-twenty-four months, modest density increases (1–3% at the spine, less at the hip) are common on the regimen. The clinically more meaningful signal is fewer fractures, better posture (the late-stage kyphosis of osteoporosis stops progressing), restored height, and — strikingly often — reduction in soft-tissue calcification visible on incidental imaging. Patients on K2 plus calcium plus thyroid frequently see arterial calcium scores stabilize or fall over the same window, evidence that the same intervention that helps bone helps the arteries by the same mechanism.

**Lab markers worth following.** Serum PTH (target the lower half of the reference range, not the middle), 25-OH vitamin D (target ~50 ng/mL), serum magnesium (often artifactually normal; RBC magnesium is more sensitive), prolactin (high prolactin is a direct bone-loss signal that should fall on progesterone and thyroid), and TSH (lower-half reference, with T3 and T4 free measurements to confirm the thyroid restoration is real rather than TSH-suppression artifact). The serum calcium itself is a poor marker of the underlying state; calcium is tightly defended by PTH and will read normal whether bone is dissolving rapidly or not.

## Counter-Arguments

The mainstream rebuttal to the Peat framework on bone is real and deserves engagement, not dismissal. The strongest counter-arguments cluster in four areas.

**The bisphosphonate fracture-reduction RCT literature.** Alendronate, risedronate, and zoledronic acid have produced statistically significant reductions in vertebral and hip fractures in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis in placebo-controlled trials, with effect sizes large enough to support FDA approval. A Peat-coherent response notes that (a) the comparator was placebo, not the Peat protocol; (b) the trials were short (3–5 years) relative to the timescale at which bisphosphonate matrix damage appears clinically; (c) the same trials produced the atypical-femoral-fracture and ONJ signal that the FDA subsequently warned about; (d) the absolute risk reductions are modest (NNT of roughly 100 to prevent one hip fracture over five years); and (e) the trials did not measure arterial calcification, cognitive decline, or the other downstream costs the Peat framework predicts. The fracture-reduction signal is real but it is bought against a portfolio of harms the trials were not designed to detect. Peat would also note — fairly — that no head-to-head RCT of bisphosphonate versus progesterone-plus-thyroid-plus-K2-plus-calcium has ever been funded.

**Estrogen does inhibit osteoclasts.** This is true at the cell-biology level and the Peat framework does not dispute it. The dispute is about whether inhibiting osteoclasts produces a *better* skeleton or a *less-turned-over* skeleton. The same mechanism that inhibits osteoclasts also inhibits the matrix renewal that bone needs to clear microdamage; the resulting skeleton is denser by DEXA but more brittle by mechanical test. The WHI fracture data — modestly reduced fractures in the estrogen arms — has to be weighed against the WHI cancer, cardiovascular, and stroke data, which is why the original protocol was halted. The Australian natural experiment (28.3% hip-fracture *decrease* during a 54.6% HRT *decrease*) is the cleanest available evidence that population-level estrogen withdrawal did not produce the predicted fracture surge.

**Calcium-intake epidemiology is messier than Peat's milk-quart recommendation suggests.** Several large observational studies show no clear association between dietary calcium intake and fracture risk above a modest threshold (~700 mg/day), and some show paradoxical associations of high calcium intake with cardiovascular events. The Peat-coherent response: calcium intake without adequate K2, magnesium, and protein produces exactly the soft-tissue-calcification outcome the studies record, which is precisely why dietary advice for calcium must be paired with the rest of the protocol. The 2,400 mg/day from two quarts of milk includes the protein, the phosphate ratio, the vitamin K2 (in pastured cream), the vitamin A, and the food matrix that the supplement-pill studies cannot reproduce. The epidemiology is consistent with the Peat reading once the missing nutrients are accounted for.

**"Peat is wrong about estrogen and bone."** This is the most common pushback from the bioidentical-HRT and Wiley-protocol communities, which argue that low-dose transdermal estradiol started in early perimenopause does protect bone without the prolactin and breast-cancer costs of conjugated equine estrogens. The Peat-coherent response acknowledges that route and dose matter — oral conjugated estrogens are pharmacologically the worst case — but maintains that even bioidentical estradiol raises prolactin, suppresses thyroid, and degrades the respiratory metabolism the skeleton depends on. The cleanest evidence for the Peat position is the four-year hypothalamic-exhaustion mechanism documented at [[menopause]]: estrogen does not "deficit" at menopause, it cumulatively exhausts the regulatory centers. The right answer is to restore progesterone, not replace estrogen.

## Data Gaps

Peat's framework on bone is unusually empirically grounded for a heterodox position — there are decades of citations across his four dedicated articles — but several gaps remain.

**No head-to-head RCT.** No funded trial has compared progesterone + thyroid + K2 + calcium + PUFA reduction against alendronate at standard doses. The bisphosphonate literature has the FDA mandate behind it; the Peat-protocol literature is observational and clinical-experience-based. A two-year, two-arm RCT with paired DEXA, bone-turnover markers, fracture outcomes, and coronary calcium scores would settle the question. None has been undertaken.

**Optimal progesterone dose is uncertain.** Peat's most-cited range is 10–60 mg of Progest-E daily through the postmenopausal phase, but the optimal dose for bone outcomes specifically — and whether that dose differs from the optimal dose for hot-flash management — is not well characterized. Some practitioners use higher doses (100–200 mg) in the first year of severe osteoporosis with apparent clinical benefit; the trial data to confirm or refute this is absent.

**Vitamin K2 sub-form (MK-4 vs MK-7) and dose.** The Japanese RCT literature uses MK-4 at 45 mg/day. Some Western practitioners favor MK-7 at much lower doses (100–200 μg/day) on the basis of longer half-life. Whether MK-7 at low dose produces the same osteocalcin carboxylation, PTH-driven lactic acid suppression, and estradiol oxidation as MK-4 at high dose is unresolved.

**The role of androgens.** Peat described testosterone and DHEA as bone-protective in passing but never developed the male-osteoporosis case with the rigor he developed the female case. Men's bone loss accelerates with the decline of DHEA and testosterone and the rise of estradiol from increased aromatase activity in aging adipose tissue, but the dosing and route specifics for androgen support in male osteoporosis are not laid out in the Peat corpus.

**Strontium, boron, silicon.** Several trace elements with reasonable mechanistic bone-formation claims appear briefly in Peat's correspondence but are not integrated into a single coherent recommendation. The boron-vitamin-D-magnesium synergy and the strontium-substitution-for-calcium-in-hydroxyapatite story both deserve more work than the corpus currently offers.
