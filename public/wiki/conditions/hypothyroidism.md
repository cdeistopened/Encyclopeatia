# Hypothyroidism

Hypothyroidism is the central condition in Ray Peat's framework. Nearly every other problem he discussed across fifty years of writing and teaching connects back to it: [[cancer]], [[heart-disease|heart disease]], [[depression]], [[infertility]], [[obesity]], [[dementia]], [[diabetes]], chronic fatigue, hair loss, cold hands and feet, brain fog, constipation, edema, high cholesterol, and dozens more. His core claim is not simply that hypothyroidism is common but that it is vastly more common than modern medicine recognizes. Before World War II, when diagnosis relied on symptoms, physical signs, and the measurement of oxygen consumption, roughly 40% of Americans showed evidence of inadequate thyroid function. After a fraudulent blood test compressed that estimate to 5% in the late 1940s, an entire civilization of people suffering from fatigue, weight gain, brain fog, and degenerative disease were told they were normal. That redefinition, Peat argued, was "driven by commercial fraud, not science," and it remains one of the great unacknowledged scandals in the history of medicine.

## The Function of Thyroid Hormone

The thyroid gland sits in the neck, but its influence extends to every cell in the body. As Peat explained in a [[2011-02-22-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-thyroid|2011 interview on thyroid function]], "the basic function of thyroid is to energize cells and to give them enough energy and efficiency to allow them to differentiate so that they don't just concentrate on eating and growing the way plants, amoebas, and fungi do." Thyroid hormone is what separates organized animal life from mere growth. Corals, insects, mollusks, crustaceans, echinoderms use thyroid. Plants and amoebas do not.

The active thyroid hormone is triiodothyronine, T3. When T3 is present in adequate amounts, cells oxidize [[glucose]] completely to [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and water, producing energy efficiently. When T3 is deficient, cells shift toward glycolysis, the inefficient fermentation of glucose into [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]. This shift defines hypothyroidism at the cellular level: the same metabolic deterioration that Otto Warburg identified in cancer cells, and that Broda Barnes identified in hypothyroid patients, are expressions of the same underlying failure of [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]].

Peat was emphatic about the distinction between T4 (thyroxine) and T3. "Thyroxine is often called 'the thyroid hormone,' in the same way that estrogen has been called 'the female hormone,'" he wrote in [[thyroid-misconceptions|"Thyroid: Misconceptions" (1993)]]. "Triiodothyronine, or liothyronine, is functionally the most important thyroid hormone. It is the metabolically active derivative of thyroxine." When thyroxine is added to brain tissue in laboratory experiments, it suppresses respiration. When T3 is added to any tissue, respiration increases. The implications for treatment are enormous and largely ignored.

## The Diagnostic Scandal: From 40% to 5%

The history of thyroid diagnosis is, in Peat's telling, a story of institutional fraud whose consequences persist to this day. Before 1945, doctors diagnosed hypothyroidism by evaluating a constellation of physical signs and symptoms: low basal metabolic rate (measured by oxygen consumption), high cholesterol, low body temperature, slow pulse, cold extremities, falling hair, dry skin, yellow calluses on the palms, constipation, fatigue, weight gain, infertility, depression, poor memory, and slow relaxation of the Achilles tendon reflex. By these criteria, approximately 40% of the American population showed evidence of hypothyroid function.

In the late 1940s, a blood test called the Protein Bound Iodine (PBI) test was introduced and promoted as the scientific alternative to these "obsolete" methods. The PBI test found that 95% of Americans had adequate protein-bound iodine in their blood, reducing the estimated prevalence of hypothyroidism from 40% to 5% overnight. As Peat recounted in a [[2008-09-11-politics-and-science-politics-science-thyroid-and-regeneration|1996 interview]], "when I was in school, all of my fat friends with the traditional symptoms of low thyroid had been taught to say, 'No, I don't have a glandular problem. I'm just lazy and gluttonous.' That was passed through the whole culture in the late 1940s and early '50s."

The PBI test was used for approximately twenty years before it was proven, in the 1960s, to have "essentially no relationship to thyroid function." But by then, the 5% doctrine had become medical orthodoxy. When newer, more meaningful blood tests arrived, their "normal" ranges were calibrated to match the expectation that only 5% of the population could be hypothyroid. As Peat wrote in [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|"TSH, Temperature, Pulse Rate, and Other Indicators" (2007)]], "to make the new TSH measurements fit the medical doctrine, an 8- or 10-fold variation in the hormone was defined as 'normal.' With any other biological measurement, such as erythrocyte count, blood pressure, body weight, or serum sodium, calcium, chloride, or glucose, a variation of 10 to 20% from the mean is considered to be meaningful."

The result is that millions of people with genuine hypothyroid symptoms are told by their doctors that their blood work is "normal." The patients are then treated for the individual consequences of their hypothyroidism: statins for high cholesterol, antidepressants for depression, clomiphene for infertility, antihypertensives for high blood pressure. The unifying cause goes unrecognized.

## Why TSH Is a Poor Diagnostic Tool

TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone), secreted by the pituitary gland, is the standard modern test for thyroid function. Peat regarded it as deeply misleading for several reasons.

First, the "normal" range for TSH was established on the basis of the fraudulent 5% doctrine. In 2003, the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists narrowed the range to 0.3 to 3.0 microunits per milliliter, but even this is too broad. "Over a period of several years," Peat wrote, "I never saw a person whose TSH was over 2 who was comfortably healthy, and I formed the impression that the normal, or healthy, quantity was probably something less than 1.0."

Second, TSH can be suppressed by many things besides adequate thyroid hormone: aging, infection, trauma, prolonged cortisol excess, somatostatin, dopamine, adrenaline, amphetamine, [[caffeine]], and fever. A person can have low or normal TSH and still be profoundly hypothyroid because their stress hormones are suppressing the pituitary's call for more thyroid. "A disease or its treatment can obscure the presence of hypothyroidism," Peat noted, citing Parkinson's disease as a clear example.

Third, and most distinctively, Peat argued that TSH is itself pathogenic. "All the known effects of TSH are harmful," he stated in his [[2011-02-22-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-thyroid|2011 interview]]. TSH acts directly on many cell types beyond the thyroid gland, promoting inflammation, edema, fibrosis, and mastocytosis. It increases inflammatory cytokines. It is lipolytic, raising circulating free fatty acids, which themselves inhibit thyroid function. This creates a vicious circle in which elevated TSH actively contributes to the very condition it is supposedly signaling the body to correct.

Peat pointed to W.D. Denckla's experiments with animals lacking pituitaries, and to naturally pituitary-deficient dwarf mice lacking TSH, prolactin, and growth hormone, which live roughly a year longer than normal mice. "Until there is evidence that very low TSH is somehow harmful, there is no basis for setting a lower limit to the normal range."

## The Real Diagnostic Tools: Temperature, Pulse, and the Achilles Reflex

If blood tests are unreliable, what should a person use instead? Peat's answer drew heavily on the work of Broda Barnes, who practiced in Fort Collins, Colorado, and argued that morning body temperature was the best single indicator of thyroid function. But Peat refined Barnes' method significantly.

Temperature alone can be misleading. Peat observed that in hot, humid weather in Eugene, Oregon, "several obviously hypothyroid people" had normal temperatures. "It takes very little metabolic energy to keep the body at 98.6 degrees when the air temperature is in the nineties." Their pulse rates, however, were very low. He also noted that electric blankets, thick bedding, and ambient warmth could mask a genuinely low metabolic rate. "The combination of pulse rate and temperature is much better than either one alone."

The daily pattern matters as much as any single reading. Peat recommended measuring temperature and pulse before breakfast, after breakfast, and in the afternoon. In a healthy person, both temperature and pulse rise after eating breakfast. In a hypothyroid person compensating with high stress hormones, the opposite can happen: temperature and pulse fall after a meal, because the food lowers stress hormones and reveals the true low metabolic rate that the adrenaline was concealing.

Peat described several diagnostic scenarios in his [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|2013 KMUD interview on Hashimoto's]]. A high morning temperature that falls after breakfast indicates excessive adrenaline masking hypothyroidism. A low morning temperature that falls further after breakfast is "stress plus hypothyroidism." A pulse that rises after eating carbohydrates suggests T3 is being produced by the liver in response to glucose. The ideal response to breakfast is temperature rising while pulse comes down slightly: the carbohydrate is lowering adrenaline while genuine metabolic activity increases.

The numbers Peat considered healthy are notable. "Healthy and intelligent groups of people have been found to have an average resting pulse rate of 85 per minute, while less healthy groups average close to 70 per minute." He cited studies of high school students in which those with the best grades averaged 85 beats per minute at rest, and studies of elderly people on pacemakers whose mental function improved when the rate was raised from 70 to 85. The mainstream assumption that a low resting heart rate indicates cardiovascular fitness was, in Peat's view, a confusion of hibernation with health.

The Achilles tendon reflex test, developed in the 1930s, is a physical examination that most physicians know how to perform but believe to be unreliable. Peat disagreed, specifying that the key measurement is the relaxation rate, not the contraction. When the tendon is thumped and the calf muscle contracts, the speed at which it relaxes reveals the rate of energy regeneration. In a healthy person, "relaxation is faster than the passive return motion of the foot, so the foot swings freely back to its original position, and overshoots slightly, causing a slight swinging action." In a hypothyroid person, "the foot returns as if controlled by a pneumatic door-closer, and settles slowly and precisely into its relaxed position, sometimes with a hesitating, intermittent motion." This slow energy regeneration is the same process that causes muscle cramps, restless sleep, rhythm disturbances in the heart, and the aching legs children experience at the end of an active day.

As Peat told listeners in his [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 KMUD interview on thyroid and PUFA]], the Achilles reflex was suppressed as a diagnostic tool because "the labs and pharmaceutical companies couldn't sell anything. You can use a table knife or a wooden hammer handle to thump the tendon, and it takes about two minutes. It is a very uneconomical business for doctors in the pharmaceutical industry to diagnose the condition so simply."

Other useful indicators Peat discussed include: serum cholesterol (rises in mirror-image to falling metabolic rate), carotenemia (yellow calluses on the palms from unconverted carotene), the speed of the pupillary light reflex, the T-wave on the electrocardiogram, blood levels of lactate, albumin, sodium, and glucose, and the amount of water evaporated per day. A person with adequate thyroid function evaporates roughly two quarts of water daily beyond what is lost in urine. Many hypothyroid people evaporate less than a quart, which means they are retaining water, a state Peat described as being "waterlogged."

## The Symptom Picture

Thomas H. McGavack's 1951 textbook *The Thyroid*, representative of the earlier approach, listed arthritis, irregularities of growth, wasting, obesity, abnormalities of hair and skin, carotenemia, amenorrhea, tendency to miscarry, infertility in both sexes, insomnia or somnolence, emphysema, various heart diseases, psychosis, dementia, poor memory, anxiety, cold extremities, and anemia as known reasons to suspect hypothyroidism. These were not theoretical associations. They were observed, documented, and treated successfully with thyroid supplementation by thousands of physicians before the diagnostic shift.

Peat emphasized that the stereotypical hypothyroid patient (overweight, sluggish, lethargic) represents only one presentation. "A very large group of low thyroid people become hyperactive because of this very high adrenaline compensating for the low blood sugar." A thin person with high blood pressure is unlikely to be evaluated for hypothyroidism, yet the high blood pressure may be caused by elevated adrenaline compensating for hypothyroid hypoglycemia, and the thinness may reflect the catabolic effects of chronically elevated [[cortisol]].

The common symptoms that bring people to articles like this one all trace to the same root cause in Peat's framework:

**Fatigue and brain fog.** When cells produce lactic acid instead of carbon dioxide, energy production collapses. "Just sitting passively for a hypothyroid person can be the same as running at high speed for a healthy person." The brain suffers first.

**Cold hands and feet.** High compensatory adrenaline constricts blood flow to the skin and extremities, preserving circulation for the brain, heart, and lungs. Hands, feet, and nose stay cold even when core temperature appears normal.

**Hair loss.** Listed as a diagnostic symptom in every pre-war thyroid textbook. Sudden, diffuse balding reflects the cascade of cortisol excess secondary to hypothyroidism.

**Weight gain.** The body enters a quasi-hibernation state, efficiently conserving fat while failing to repair tissues. After the first day of caloric restriction, the hypothyroid person loses muscle rather than fat, because the liver has depleted its glycogen and begins converting protein to glucose.

**Depression and anxiety.** Both are common, sometimes alternating in the same person. Depression correlates with low temperature and low metabolic rate; anxiety correlates with the compensatory surge of adrenaline and cortisol.

**High cholesterol.** The mirror-image indicator of falling metabolic rate, recognized since the 1930s. Peat described seeing people with cholesterol of 400 and 500 who "took the active T3 hormone in physiological amounts, roughly 5 micrograms per hour, and in a week they had restored their cholesterol to the normal range of around 200."

**Constipation and edema.** When metabolic rate falls, cells take up water and lose their ability to maintain osmotic gradients. The result is puffy skin, water retention, and sluggish bowel motility.

**Infertility.** Hypothyroid animals develop cystic ovaries. Hypothyroid people accumulate carotene because they cannot convert it to [[vitamin-a|vitamin A]], which is needed to convert [[cholesterol]] to [[progesterone]]. Low thyroid leads to low vitamin A function, then to low progesterone, then to relative [[estrogen]] dominance, which further suppresses thyroid function.

## The Liver: Where Thyroid Meets Estrogen

Perhaps the most important mechanistic insight in Peat's framework is the role of the liver as the site where thyroid function and estrogen metabolism intersect. Approximately two-thirds of the body's active T3 is produced in the liver, not in the thyroid gland itself. The liver converts the relatively inactive T4 (thyroxine) to the active T3, but this conversion requires adequate glucose, selenium, and overall liver health.

Estrogen directly damages the liver's ability to perform this conversion. As Peat explained in his [[2011-02-22-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-thyroid|2011 EastWest Healing interview]], "there is an antagonism focused on the liver between estrogen and thyroid. The thyroid works partly by energizing the liver so it can detoxify everything, including removing 100% of the estrogen that reaches it. If your thyroid is low, the liver loses the ability to detoxify estrogen."

This creates what Peat described as a vicious circle, one of the most clinically important feedback loops in his entire framework: inadequate thyroid function weakens the liver; the weakened liver fails to clear estrogen; the accumulating estrogen further suppresses thyroid secretion from the gland and blocks the liver's conversion of T4 to T3; the resulting deeper hypothyroidism further weakens the liver. Each pass through the loop worsens the condition.

This loop explains why women have five to ten times the rate of thyroid disease that men do. Higher baseline estrogen levels make women more vulnerable to the initial entry into the cycle. It also explains why a single dose of [[progesterone]] can sometimes break the cycle, since progesterone opposes estrogen at every relevant site: it promotes the release of thyroid hormones from the gland (where estrogen inhibits it), it supports the liver's detoxification capacity, and it directly antagonizes estrogen at the cellular level.

The Biskinds, whom Peat cited repeatedly, demonstrated in the 1940s that when the liver was deprived of thyroid, protein, or vitamins, estrogen levels "would skyrocket." This is not a minor physiological detail. It means that the symptoms commonly attributed to "estrogen dominance" in popular health writing are often symptoms of hypothyroidism operating through the liver. Treating the estrogen excess without addressing the thyroid deficiency treats the downstream effect while leaving the upstream cause intact.

## PUFA: The Thyroid Blocker at Every Level

If estrogen is thyroid's primary hormonal antagonist, [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]] are its primary dietary antagonist. Peat's case against PUFA is extensive across many topics, but its connection to thyroid function is especially thorough.

As he stated in the [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 KMUD interview]], PUFA blocks thyroid at three distinct levels: "They inhibit the proteolytic enzymes in the gland itself which are needed to form and secrete the hormone. They bind to the protein in the bloodstream that transports thyroid, preventing transport to the tissues. Finally, the polyunsaturated fats block several of the active sites in the cell where thyroid should bind to enliven the cell." Sarah Murray summarized: "They affect thyroid health at the production location, the transportation through the bloodstream, and at the tissue level." Peat confirmed and added a fourth: "They also act on several other parts of the system, including indirectly on the TSH."

In his [[thyroid-misconceptions|"Thyroid: Misconceptions" article]], he quantified the toxicity: "Linoleic and linolenic acids are roughly twice as toxic to these systems as oleic acid is. The tissue response to the hormone is inhibited by unsaturated fats in proportion to the number of double bonds in the fat."

This means that blood tests can show adequate levels of thyroid hormones while the person remains functionally hypothyroid, because the PUFA in their tissues is blocking the hormones from acting. "Measuring the amount of thyroid in the blood isn't a good way to evaluate adequacy of thyroid function, since the response of tissues to the hormone can be suppressed," Peat wrote. A person eating a diet high in vegetable oils, nuts, and seeds can have normal-appearing blood work and still suffer from every symptom of hypothyroidism, because the polyunsaturated fats prevent the hormones from reaching their targets.

Carotene, the precursor to vitamin A found in carrots and other vegetables, acts as an additional thyroid inhibitor through the same mechanism. "It not only blocks the cellular sites that use vitamin A, but as a polyunsaturated molecule, it blocks the thyroid function everywhere that the vegetable oils do." The yellow-orange calluses on the palms and soles of hypothyroid people represent accumulated carotene that cannot be converted to vitamin A because of insufficient metabolic rate. Peat considered this one of the most reliable old-fashioned diagnostic signs.

The PUFA stored in body fat has a half-life of approximately four years, meaning that even after a person eliminates vegetable oils from their diet, the PUFA already embedded in their tissues continues to suppress thyroid function for years. This is why Peat told listeners that "the average person in their 20s or 30s usually benefits from taking a supplement for at least a few years until they can change the composition of their body."

[[Coconut-oil|Coconut oil]] occupies a special position in this framework. G.W. Crile found that the basal metabolism of people in Yucatan, where coconuts are a staple food, is 125% of that of people in the United States. The saturated fatty acids in coconut oil, especially the short- and medium-chain varieties, "probably tend to simply dilute and weaken the inhibition that is chronically exerted by the polyunsaturated fatty acids."

## The Stress Cascade

Hypothyroidism and chronic stress are nearly synonymous in Peat's framework, because each one produces the other. When thyroid is low, the body cannot maintain blood sugar through efficient oxidative metabolism. The liver fails to maintain its glycogen reserves. Blood sugar falls. The adrenal medulla secretes [[adrenaline]] to force the liver to release its remaining stored sugar. When that is exhausted, the adrenal cortex secretes [[cortisol]] to break down muscle and glandular tissue into glucose.

"When your thyroid is low, you don't have efficiency with oxidative metabolism, so you turn sugar into lactic acid," Peat explained. "Your liver spends energy converting lactic acid back to sugar, so low blood sugar is a constant problem." The falling blood sugar and rising adrenaline signal the brain to increase ACTH and [[prolactin]]. ACTH drives cortisol higher. Prolactin causes breast pain and contributes to bone loss.

The nighttime pattern is especially revealing. "Blood sugar falls at night, and the body relies on the glucose stored in the liver as glycogen for energy, and hypothyroid people store very little sugar. As a result, adrenalin and cortisol begin to rise almost as soon as a person goes to bed." Adrenaline peaks around 1 or 2 A.M., causing some people to wake with a pounding heart. Cortisol peaks around dawn. This insight reframes reactive hypoglycemia and insomnia as consequences of inadequate thyroid function acting through the liver. Broda Barnes made the case in *Hypoglycemia: It's Your Liver, Not Your Mind*.

The cascade also explains the link between hypothyroidism and hypertension. "Hypothyroidism typically increases the viscosity of the blood and raises blood pressure," Peat stated. "A high percentage of people with hypertension are simply hypothyroid." He noted seeing people whose chronic resting pulse of 180 beats per minute normalized to 90 or 100 within weeks of starting thyroid supplementation.

## Cholesterol and Heart Disease

Published studies from the mid-1930s showed that when a person's thyroid gland was surgically removed, cholesterol rose sharply over the following weeks, and when a thyroid supplement was given, cholesterol returned to normal in exact proportion to the increase in oxygen consumption. "If you chart the metabolic rate," Peat explained, "it's a mirror image of the cholesterol."

Elevated cholesterol, then, is not a disease requiring its own treatment but a symptom of inadequate thyroid function. Artificially lowering it with statins does not address the metabolic failure, and because cholesterol is the precursor to [[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], [[DHEA]], and other protective hormones, suppressing it reduces the body's ability to produce these substances. Early studies showed that lowering cholesterol "tremendously increased cancer mortality, total mortality, and increased the death rate from accidents, homicide, and even heart disease and strokes."

When the body cannot convert cholesterol into protective steroids, it defaults to increasing cortisol. The chronic cortisol elevation associated with high cholesterol and low thyroid weakens blood vessels, promotes both bleeding and clotting, accelerates osteoporosis, and drives tissue degeneration. Broda Barnes, through decades of clinical work, "clearly showed the protective action of thyroid against heart disease."

## T4 vs. T3: Why Synthroid Often Fails

Most doctors prescribe synthetic thyroxine (T4, sold as Synthroid or levothyroxine) as the standard treatment for hypothyroidism. Peat considered this practice inadequate for a large proportion of patients, particularly women.

The thyroid gland itself secretes approximately three parts T4 to one part T3. The remaining T3 the body needs (about two-thirds of the total) is produced by the liver through conversion of T4. In healthy young men, giving T4 alone is often sufficient because their livers convert it efficiently. But in women, whose higher estrogen levels impair liver function, and in anyone with compromised liver function from stress, PUFA accumulation, or nutritional deficiency, T4 may not be adequately converted.

The consequences of this failure can be severe. Peat told a striking story in his [[2008-10-31-other-hope-for-health-hope-for-health-thyroid|2008 Hope for Health interview]]: "A woman had been prescribed the equivalent of one grain of thyroxine. She got more hypothyroid, so they kept increasing the dose. When they reached 400 micrograms of thyroxine, she went into a myxedema coma. They took her to the hospital unconscious and kept her alive until someone figured out she wasn't converting T4 to T3. It had turned her brain metabolism off to the point that she was no longer conscious. When they gave her T3, she recovered immediately to full functioning."

In his KMUD interview on Hashimoto's, Peat explained the mechanism by which T4-only therapy can actually worsen hypothyroidism: "If the liver isn't producing T3, and you give that person big doses of thyroxine, you suppress the TSH. This turns off the little bit of active hormone coming out of their thyroid gland, and they go into an increasingly hypothyroid state."

Natural desiccated thyroid (Armour thyroid, or similar preparations from porcine or bovine glands) contains both T4 and T3 in a ratio similar to what the human gland produces. Peat noted in his 1993 article that "every batch is biologically standardized, and studies have shown it to be reliably within 1% of the labeled potency. The best known brand of the supposedly chemically precise levothyroxine, however, was for a long time 30% below the labeled potency." The pharmaceutical narrative that Armour thyroid was poorly standardized and that synthetic T4 was precise was exactly backwards.

For people whose liver conversion is severely impaired, Cytomel (synthetic T3, liothyronine) can provide the active hormone directly. Peat's practical advice, drawn from his [[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]], was that "the active hormone, T3, acts quickly, within a few minutes, and leaves the body quickly (half of it in a day), while the precursor thyroxine is hormonally inactive, and accumulates in the body with a half life of two weeks." He recommended taking T3 in small divided doses throughout the day, roughly matching the body's natural production rate of about 4 micrograms per hour, and always with meals.

## The Progesterone-Thyroid Axis

[[Progesterone]] and thyroid exist in a mutually reinforcing relationship. "Thyroid promotes the formation of progesterone, which in turn promotes the secretion of thyroid hormones," Peat wrote. "Estrogen blocks their release from the thyroid gland, causing the gland to enlarge." The practical implication is that supporting one supports the other, and that deficiency in one tends to produce deficiency in the other.

Progesterone facilitates the proteolytic enzymes in the thyroid gland that are needed to release stored hormone into the bloodstream. Estrogen inhibits these same enzymes. This is why during puberty and pregnancy, when estrogen rises, the thyroid gland commonly enlarges: it is accumulating stored hormone that it cannot release. When estrogen declines (after delivery, or after a change in circumstances), the stored hormone is sometimes released in a burst that produces temporary hyperthyroid symptoms. "A lot of doctors will kill your thyroid with radioactive iodine or cut it out when they see symptoms like that," Peat warned. "But it's usually just a natural adaptive process in which the enlarged gland is returning to normal size."

The connection to [[Hashimotos|Hashimoto's thyroiditis]] is direct. "Estrogen inhibits thyroid secretion, and excess estrogen is involved in most 'autoimmune' disease," Peat wrote. "Supplemental thyroid so easily normalizes estrogen and the immune system that it, with progesterone and pregnenolone and DHEA, should be considered as the cure for autoimmune diseases, including thyroiditis." This is a strong claim, but it follows logically from the estrogen-thyroid-liver feedback loop: if estrogen drives the autoimmune attack on the thyroid, and thyroid hormone enables the liver to clear estrogen, then supplementing thyroid should break the cycle.

## Other Thyroid Suppressors

Beyond PUFA and estrogen, Peat identified several common suppressors of thyroid function. **Goitrogens** in raw cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, mustard greens, beans, lentils, and nuts directly inhibit the gland. **Tryptophan and cysteine**, concentrated in muscle meats, are antithyroid, which is one reason Peat recommended [[gelatin]] as a protein source. **Fluoride** was potent enough that Peat reported from personal experience: "A glass of fluoridated water is enough to knock out a day's portion of the active thyroid hormone." **Excessive exercise** lowers thyroid hormones by accelerating their breakdown. **Fasting and caloric restriction** deplete the liver's glycogen within 12 to 24 hours, shutting down T4-to-T3 conversion as a survival mechanism. **Seasonal variation** is substantial: summer requirements can be as little as a quarter of winter requirements.

These factors are additive. Peat described a syndrome that captures many health-conscious people: "Many health conscious people become hypothyroid with a synergistic program of undercooked vegetables, legumes instead of animal proteins, oils instead of butter, carotene instead of vitamin A, and breathless exercise instead of a stimulating life."

## Practical Approach

A therapeutic trial was the standard method of confirming a hypothyroid diagnosis before 1945, and Peat endorsed it as still valid. If a person's symptoms improve as their temperature, pulse rate, and food intake normalize on thyroid supplementation, the diagnosis is confirmed.

**Monitoring.** Keep a chart of temperature and pulse at waking, after breakfast, and in the afternoon for at least two weeks when beginning supplementation. Thyroxine has a half-life of about two weeks, so the effect of any daily dose increases over time.

**Dosing.** Individual requirements vary enormously. "I've known people who temporarily needed 500 mg of Armour even in the summer, but usually the summer requirement is a fourth of the winter requirement. For some people, 15 mg of Armour was enough, and for some 1 mcg of Cytomel was an effective dose."

**T3 supplementation.** If Cytomel is used directly, small amounts throughout the day with meals approximate the physiological production rate. "The body produces about 4 mcg of T3 per hour, so taking more than that can interfere with regulatory processes."

**Supporting the liver.** Adequate glucose from fruit, juice, and sugar (not starch), along with selenium, vitamin A from animal sources, and the avoidance of PUFA all support T4-to-T3 conversion. "A piece of fruit or a glass of juice or milk between meals, and adequate animal protein in the diet is sometimes enough to allow the liver to produce the hormone."

**Recovery of glandular function.** Contrary to the fear that supplementing thyroid will permanently suppress the gland, Peat observed the opposite. "I've seen several people who were hypothyroid who, when they took the amount that brought their oxygen consumption up to the right level, had recovery of their own thyroid function and could stop taking it." If the gland is temporarily suppressed, full function returns within two to three days of stopping.

## Subclinical hypothyroidism — mainstream vs Peat

Mainstream endocrinology has a category called "subclinical hypothyroidism" that occupies a peculiar place in its diagnostic scheme. It applies to patients whose free T4 falls within the lab's reference range but whose TSH sits between roughly 4.0 and 10 microunits per milliliter. Above 10, the patient is "overtly hypothyroid" and gets a Synthroid prescription. Below 4 (or 2.5–3 in updated American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists guidance), the patient is "normal." In the 4-to-10 window, the textbook advice is to wait and recheck in six months, and to treat only if the TSH continues to rise or if the patient develops "clinical" symptoms. The Tuzcu paper Peat cited in his [[cholesterol-longevity-intelligence-and-health|"Cholesterol, Longevity, Intelligence, and Health" newsletter]] tied subclinical hypothyroidism directly to elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and fasting hyperinsulinemia — meaning that even by mainstream standards, "subclinical" is already producing measurable inflammation and metabolic damage.

Peat dismantled this framework from two directions. The first is that the entire reference range is artifactual. As discussed in the [[#why-tsh-is-a-poor-diagnostic-tool|Why TSH Is a Poor Diagnostic Tool]] section above, the upper bound was set to make the post-1950 prevalence of hypothyroidism match the politically required 5% figure, not to identify the level at which the hormone stops being pathogenic. "Over a period of several years, I never saw a person whose TSH was over 2 was comfortably healthy," he wrote in [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|"TSH, Temperature, Pulse Rate, and Other Indicators" (2007)]]. By that standard, the entire "subclinical" range is overt hypothyroidism that has been definitionally laundered into normality.

The second direction is that the concept of "subclinical" assumes blood chemistry tells the truth and symptoms are noise — exactly inverted from the framework that produced the diagnosis of hypothyroidism before 1950. Peat told Sarah Johanneson Murray in his [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 KMUD interview]] that diagnosis "in the 1930s" was made by oxygen consumption, basal temperature, the Achilles relaxation rate, and the constellation of physical signs catalogued in McGavack's *The Thyroid*. A patient with cold hands, falling hair, a midday pulse of 60, a temperature of 96.8°F, high cholesterol, constipation, and a TSH of 3.5 is, by the pre-war methods that Peat considered the only validated standard, fully hypothyroid. The blood test is not adding information. It is providing pharmaceutical-grade cover for ignoring the patient.

Peat's email correspondence on Hashimoto's makes the corollary explicit. When a reader asked about TPO antibodies in a Type 1 diabetic with TSH of 3.9, he wrote: "I think the high TSH explains the antibodies, and a combination of T4 and T3 is usually all that's needed; it usually takes a few months after suppressing TSH for the antibodies to decrease" ([[autoimmune-diseases|Autoimmune Diseases email exchanges]]). A TSH of 3.9 is "normal" in current American labs and "subclinical" at best in the few clinics that have adopted the 2003 guideline. To Peat, it was a tissue-damaging stimulus that needed to be suppressed.

The failure mode of "wait until it's clinical" is that the wait itself is the disease. Every month a patient sits with TSH of 4 or 5, the elevated TSH is acting directly on bone marrow, fibroblasts, mast cells, vascular smooth muscle, and the thyroid gland itself, raising IL-18 and other inflammatory cytokines, promoting calcification of arteries, driving the gland into the inflamed state diagnosed years later as Hashimoto's, and feeding back through lipolysis to release [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] that block T3 at the tissue level. The patient also continues to accumulate the downstream damage that hypothyroidism causes: rising cholesterol, falling progesterone, accumulating estrogen, deepening insulin resistance, hair loss, infertility risk, and the gradual replacement of muscle with cortisol-driven gluconeogenesis. The "subclinical" category, in Peat's framework, is not a holding pattern. It is the active phase of degenerative disease, billed as healthy.

The practical implication is symmetrical with the existing therapeutic-trial advice: treat the patient, not the number. If symptoms point to hypothyroidism, temperature and pulse confirm low metabolic rate, and the Achilles reflex relaxes slowly, the appropriate response is a low-dose trial of [[thyroid|thyroid hormone]] regardless of where the TSH falls in the lab's range. The trial itself answers the diagnostic question. (Prescription access is the obvious constraint here, discussed in the [[thyroid#ndt-brand-comparison|NDT brand comparison]] in the thyroid article.)

## Why TSH lies

The deepest reason TSH should not be treated as a thyroid-status readout is that it isn't a thyroid measurement. It is a pituitary signal whose level reflects the integrated influence of stress hormones, time of day, sleep quality, recent food intake, infection, age, and several drugs that have nothing to do with thyroid sufficiency. A single AM TSH draw captures one point in a continuously varying signal, samples it during the hour TSH is naturally lowest, and ignores the fact that the same number can mean very different things depending on what else is happening in the body.

### Pulsatility and the diurnal pattern

TSH is secreted in pulses approximately every two to three hours, with the amplitude of those pulses changing across the day. The pulses ride on top of a robust diurnal rhythm: TSH is lowest in the late afternoon (roughly 4:00 to 6:00 PM) and peaks during sleep, typically between 11:00 PM and 4:00 AM. The nocturnal surge can be two to three times the afternoon nadir in the same individual, on the same day. Standard clinical practice draws blood between 7:00 and 10:00 AM, fasting, which catches TSH on its way down from the overnight peak — somewhere between its 24-hour high and its mid-afternoon low, with no information about where in the descent the draw landed.

Peat described the physiological coupling that makes the nocturnal surge non-arbitrary. "During the night, at the time adrenaline and free fatty acids are at their highest, TSH usually reaches its peak," he wrote in the [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|2007 newsletter on TSH]]. "TSH itself can produce lipolysis, raising the level of circulating free fatty acids." The night surge participates in the same stress physiology that drives the [[#the-stress-cascade|nighttime adrenaline-cortisol peak]] described above. A patient whose TSH appears acceptable at 9:00 AM may have spent the night with TSH four times higher, mobilizing fatty acids that themselves block T3 action at the tissue level. The morning draw certifies normality for a window during which TSH was, in fact, doing its inflammatory work.

The Coiro paper Peat cited (J Investig Med 2002, "The nocturnal serum thyrotropin surge is inhibited in patients with adrenal incidentaloma") demonstrates that the nocturnal surge is itself a measurable variable and that cortisol-secreting tumors flatten it. The diurnal pattern is real, suppressible by cortisol, and routinely sampled at exactly the time of day designed to obscure it.

### Stress-induced suppression — TSH normal, hypothyroid anyway

The second mechanism by which TSH lies is that many things besides adequate T3 suppress it. Peat catalogued the list in the [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|2007 newsletter]]: "Aging, infection, trauma, prolonged cortisol excess, somatostatin, dopamine or L-dopa, adrenaline, amphetamine, caffeine and fever can lower TSH, apart from the effect of feedback by the thyroid hormones, creating a situation in which TSH can appear normal or low, at the same time that there is a real hypothyroidism." A patient under chronic stress will have a cortisol-suppressed TSH; the clinician sees the number and concludes the thyroid is fine; the patient continues to deteriorate. Peat's phrase for the doctors who chase this number was that they were "treating the TSH," not the patient.

The point applies across the full range of TSH readings. A "low-normal" TSH of 0.8 in a stressed patient is not the same physiological state as a TSH of 0.8 in a patient running on adequate thyroid. Without a paired free T3 and a basal metabolic readout (temperature, pulse), the number is empty of information.

### The reverse T3 problem

When the body is stressed — by surgery, illness, starvation, low-calorie dieting, prolonged endurance exercise, chronic infection, or acute trauma — it stops converting T4 into active T3 and starts converting it into [[reverse-t3|reverse T3]] instead. Reverse T3 has the same molecular weight as T3 but with the iodines rearranged into a non-functional configuration. It is not just inactive. It binds to the same cellular sites as T3 and actively blocks them, antagonizing T3 wherever they compete.

The enzymatic mechanism Peat described in his 2019 *Jodellefit* episode on cortisol is that cortisol (and the other stress hormones) shifts the deiodinase enzymes that act on T4 away from making T3 and toward making rT3. "All of the stress hormones interfere with the conversion of T4 to T3. Instead of making the effective, respiration-promoting T3, they convert thyroxine into Reverse T3. They dispose of the precursor and turn it into an interfering, inactive suppressor of respiration." The same shift is documented in the Kabadi-Premachandra paper Peat cited from *J Am Geriatr Soc* (1984): in uncontrolled type II diabetics, "serum T3 levels declined and rT3 levels rose ... with worsening of the metabolic control" ([[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context"]]). And Nauman et al. (1980, 1984), referenced in the 2007 TSH newsletter, showed adrenaline directly increases reverse T3 production.

Diagnostically, this means a patient under stress can show:

- TSH normal (suppressed by cortisol, adrenaline, dopamine)
- T4 normal or even high (the precursor is accumulating because conversion has shunted to rT3)
- T3 low (the active hormone isn't being made)
- rT3 elevated (the antagonist is being made instead)

And present clinically with every symptom of hypothyroidism. Standard panels that include only TSH and free T4 will miss this entirely. The patient is told their thyroid is fine. The mechanism — that stress has flipped the deiodinase switch — is invisible on the test.

Peat's email correspondence frames the practical implication: "Yes, [reverse T3] is probably induced by stress, with cortisol inducing the type of deiodinase that makes the inactive rT3. A low sugar diet can cause chronically high cortisol. If you are eating enough fruit and protein, I think the T3 of natural thyroid will help to correct the stress/inflammatory metabolism that is connected with the reverse T3" ([[thyroid|Thyroid email exchanges]]). Adequate carbohydrate (which lowers cortisol via the liver's glycogen supply) plus exogenous T3 (which bypasses the conversion step entirely) addresses both the upstream stress and the downstream block.

### Free T3, free T4, and the binding-protein confusion

Peat considered "free" hormone measurements another layer of artifact. "Free T3" and "free T4" are calculated from the dialyzable fraction of total hormone, on the assumption that only unbound hormone is biologically active. Peat disputed the assumption. "Free T3 or free T4 is a laboratory concept, and the biological activity of T3 doesn't necessarily correspond to its freedom in the test," he wrote in the [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|2007 newsletter]]. "T3 bound to its transport proteins can be demonstrated to enter cells, mitochondria, and nuclei. Transthyretin, which carries both vitamin A and thyroid hormones, is sharply decreased by stress, and should probably be regularly measured as part of the thyroid examination." A stressed patient may have low transthyretin, depressed binding capacity, and a free-T3 number that looks acceptable while total cellular T3 delivery is collapsing. The test's "free" fraction obscures the loss of carrier capacity.

### What a healthier interpretation looks like

The Barnes temperature-and-pulse protocol, refined by Peat into a multi-time-point measurement, is fundamentally a workaround for the unreliability of TSH. Where blood tests sample one moment of one variable in a system governed by pulsatility, diurnal rhythm, stress hormones, and protein binding, the temperature-pulse approach samples the integrated downstream effect of all of those at multiple points in the day — and it samples the variable that actually matters, which is whether cells are producing heat (and by implication CO2, and by further implication ATP).

The pre-meal and post-meal readings, described in the [[#the-real-diagnostic-tools-temperature-pulse-and-the-achilles-reflex|Real Diagnostic Tools]] section above, are diagnostic precisely because they reveal what TSH and free T4 cannot: whether a normal-looking morning reading is being held up by adrenaline. Food lowers stress hormones. If temperature and pulse fall after breakfast, the morning reading was a stress artifact and the patient's true metabolic rate is lower. If they rise, the patient has metabolic reserve. No blood test gives this information.

The minimum useful panel, if blood work is being ordered at all, is: TSH, free T3, total T3, free T4, reverse T3, [[cholesterol]] (mirror image of metabolic rate), [[prolactin]] (elevated by serotonin-driven pituitary stimulation), and a midday cortisol. In Peat's framework, what matters is the ratio of free T3 to reverse T3 (should be high), the trajectory of cholesterol relative to baseline, and whether TSH and prolactin are both suppressed by adequate thyroid status. A "comprehensive thyroid panel" that includes only TSH and free T4 is incomplete to the point of being misleading.

## NDT crash course — quick reference

The full brand-by-brand comparison, the dosing schedule, the magnesium load-in, the seasonal adjustment, and the no-prescription workaround all live in the [[thyroid#ndt-brand-comparison|thyroid article's NDT section]]. What follows is the abridged quick reference for someone arriving at hypothyroidism for the first time.

**What "NDT" means.** Natural Desiccated Thyroid is dried, powdered pig thyroid (occasionally beef) standardized for hormone content and pressed into tablets. It contains T4 and T3 in roughly the 4:1 ratio a healthy human gland secretes, plus traces of T2, T1, and (historically) calcitonin. The grain-equivalent unit is the standard: 1 grain = 60–65 mg dried glandular = roughly 38 mcg T4 + 9 mcg T3.

**Why NDT rather than T4-only.** Synthetic T4 (levothyroxine, Synthroid) gives the body only the precursor and depends entirely on the liver converting it to T3. In women, in anyone with PUFA-loaded tissues, in anyone with high estrogen, in anyone with low glycogen reserves, that conversion fails — and the failure is invisible on a TSH test that gets dutifully suppressed by the high circulating T4. NDT provides the active hormone directly, so the liver's conversion capacity becomes less of a bottleneck.

**Brands Peat actually used:**

- **Cynoplus** (Mexican, T4 + T3 combo, 100 mcg T4 + 25 mcg T3) — his daily default
- **Cynomel** (Mexican, T3 only, 25 mcg liothyronine) — for direct T3 supplementation
- **Cytomel** (US, T3 only, 5/25/50 mcg) — trusted historically, post-reformulation uncertain

**Brands he was skeptical of:** post-1990 Armour (he wrote "I haven't seen anything that compares well with the original Armour"), Erfa, NatureThroid (discontinued 2020), and any U.S. desiccated product after the calcitonin-extraction reformulation in the 1990s. NP Thyroid (Acella) was recalled for sub-potency in 2020. His underlying rule: validate any tablet against temperature and pulse over two weeks before trusting the label.

**Starting dose.** Peat's repeated guidance: "An eighth of a tablet of either [Cynoplus or Cynomel] is a good starting dose" — roughly 12 mcg T4 + 3 mcg T3, or 3 mcg T3 alone. For Armour-equivalents: half a grain (30 mcg T4 + 7.5 mcg T3). Taken with a meal so absorption is slow. This is twenty to thirty times lower than a typical prescription starting dose, and the difference matters: a large fasting T4 bolus produces a liver counter-response that wipes out the T3 for the rest of the day.

**Divided dosing.** "It should be taken in small divided amounts with meals," Peat wrote in the [[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]. The body's natural T3 output is about 4 mcg per hour. Splitting the dose into three or four meal-paired portions, with a small bedtime dose to support sleep, approximates that pattern. A single morning bolus does not.

**Titration signal.** Not TSH. Morning, midday, and afternoon temperature and pulse, plus the post-meal change (see [[broda-barnes]]). Achilles relaxation rate as a cross-check. Healthy targets: midday oral 98.0–98.6°F, resting pulse 80–85 bpm, hands warm.

**What to watch in the first weeks.**

1. *Adrenaline unmasking.* T3 lowers stress hormones, which can briefly worsen the morning temperature reading even while the system is improving. "T3, by lowering stress, sometimes reveals a low basal metabolic rate, that was hidden by high stress hormones" ([[thyroid|email exchanges]]). The signal is the post-meal direction: if midday temperature is rising and morning temperature is falling, the trajectory is correct.
2. *Magnesium depletion.* Rising metabolic activity burns through magnesium faster than diet usually replaces it. Symptoms — palpitations, racing pulse, restless sleep — get misread as "too much thyroid" when the actual issue is magnesium running short. Two quarts of milk daily, or magnesium glycinate, before increasing the thyroid dose.
3. *Two-week cumulative window.* Because T4 has a two-week half-life, the full effect of any combination-product dose change does not appear for two weeks. Changes made faster than every two weeks are guesses, not titration.
4. *Seasonal variation.* Summer requirement is often a quarter of winter requirement. Dose held flat across seasons will overshoot in July or undershoot in January.

**The prescription/legal layer.** In the United States, all forms of thyroid hormone are prescription-only. The Mexican sourcing Peat used personally (via mymexicandrugstore.mx and similar) and the Anti-Aging Systems route are workarounds people pursue, but they are workarounds — they sit outside U.S. medical care and outside FDA potency standards. Self-medicating without a physician is a real risk for anyone with cardiac disease, adrenal insufficiency, or pregnancy, and the magnesium-load-in and titration discipline are not optional. Working with a clinician who understands the difference between T4 monotherapy and physiological combination dosing is the route Peat recommended when it was available. When it isn't, the rule he came back to was: validate every product, every batch, against temperature and pulse for two weeks before trusting it.

## The Hashimoto's question

Hashimoto's thyroiditis — the diagnosis that fires when a patient's anti-thyroid-peroxidase (TPO) or anti-thyroglobulin antibodies test positive — has become one of the most common reasons women in the United States are told they have an autoimmune disease. The mainstream framework treats the antibodies as evidence of an immune system mistakenly attacking healthy thyroid tissue, and the standard treatment is to wait until the gland fails sufficiently to push TSH above 10, then prescribe lifetime levothyroxine. Peat rejected almost every step in this chain.

His starting position, from the [[thyroid-misconceptions|1993 newsletter]]: "Hashimoto's thyroiditis is often diagnosed, without appropriate evidence. Even when correctly diagnosed, the knowledge isn't relevant to treatment." The antibodies are not the disease. They are the immune system's response to tissue damage that has already happened, and the question that matters is what caused the damage.

His answer, repeated across multiple sources, was that the damage is TSH-driven. In the [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on Hashimoto's]], he laid out the sequence: "It has been well established in the last several years that TSH itself causes many of the symptoms blamed on hypothyroidism. It acts on the bone marrow and many other tissues, including the liver, increasing inflammatory cytokine chemicals. So, TSH in itself is a basic problem of hypothyroidism." The chronically elevated TSH that defines "subclinical" hypothyroidism is the same TSH that, sustained over months and years, inflames the gland, recruits white blood cells, drives tissue destruction, and ultimately produces the antibody-positive picture diagnosed as Hashimoto's.

The estrogen layer compounds the TSH layer. Hashimoto's is five to ten times more common in women, and the explanation Peat gave runs through the [[#the-liver-where-thyroid-meets-estrogen|estrogen-thyroid-liver vicious circle]] documented earlier in this article. Estrogen inhibits the proteolytic enzymes that release thyroid hormone from the gland, causing the gland to enlarge under TSH stimulation without secreting effectively. Estrogen impairs the liver's conversion of T4 to T3. Estrogen drives the pituitary to produce more TSH. The combination produces a goiter under chronic stimulation — exactly the picture Hashimoto himself described a hundred years ago. "Estrogen inhibits thyroid secretion, and excess estrogen is involved in most 'autoimmune' disease," Peat wrote in [[thyroid-misconceptions|"Thyroid: Misconceptions"]]. "Supplemental thyroid so easily normalizes estrogen and the immune system that it, with progesterone and pregnenolone and DHEA, should be considered as the cure for autoimmune diseases, including thyroiditis."

The treatment implication is symmetrical with the diagnostic frame. If the antibodies are a downstream consequence of TSH-driven inflammation, suppressing TSH should resolve them. Peat's email correspondence makes the protocol explicit. When a reader described a Type 1 diabetic with positive TPO antibodies and TSH of 3.9, he replied: "I think the high TSH explains the antibodies, and a combination of T4 and T3 is usually all that's needed; it usually takes a few months after suppressing TSH for the antibodies to decrease" ([[autoimmune-diseases|Autoimmune Diseases email exchanges]]). In the 2013 KMUD interview, he described the same trajectory: "The autoimmune condition is an excited, inefficient state. If TSH keeps rising, it drives the gland harder until it causes thyroiditis. The immune system comes in to clean up the damaged cells. If you supplement enough thyroid to lower the TSH, you stop producing the antibodies. The condition corrects itself after several months."

A second mechanism — gut leakiness — runs in parallel. "Hypothyroidism is one thing that commonly causes leaky gut, as well as leaky liver, muscles, heart, thyroid gland, etc. The immune system reacts to the leakiness, and although it might be an effect, rather than a cause, the presence of antibodies is sometimes said to show autoimmunity" ([[autoimmune-diseases|Autoimmune Diseases email exchanges]]). Restoring metabolic rate restores barrier function. The intestinal permeability that releases food and bacterial antigens into circulation reverses as soon as cells have enough ATP to maintain tight junctions. The antibodies — to gluten, to TPO, to thyroglobulin, to dozens of other targets — fall as the underlying leakiness resolves.

What changes about the protocol if antibodies are present? In Peat's framework: very little. The intervention is still adequate thyroid supplementation in physiological divided doses, with attention to estrogen reduction (raw carrot, progesterone, liver support), PUFA reduction, magnesium repletion, and the standard metabolic monitoring through temperature and pulse. The antibody titer does not change the dosing strategy. It is a lagging indicator that confirms, several months in, whether the underlying inflammatory driver has been removed.

What Peat actively pushed back against was the surgical and radioactive-iodine response to thyroid enlargement. In the [[2008-09-11-politics-and-science-politics-science-thyroid-and-regeneration|2008 *Politics and Science* episode]], he described the natural cycle of an estrogen-enlarged gland releasing its stored hormone during pregnancy unwinding or estrogen reduction: "Sometimes there will be an episode of hyperthyroid function as that stored hormone is released. That will often scare the patient's doctor if they see their pulse running at 120 to 125 beats per minute at rest, and their hands being pink, warm, and sweaty. That's classical hyperthyroidism. A lot of doctors will kill your thyroid with radioactive iodine or cut it out when they see symptoms like that. But it's usually just a natural adaptive process in which the enlarged gland is returning to normal size. Some people have told me that they never felt better in their lives than when they were in their month or two of hyperthyroidism. Unfortunately, that's the opportunity for surgeons and radiologists to destroy the thyroid." The radioactive-iodine ablation that has become routine for Hashimoto's with hyperthyroid phases is, in Peat's framework, an irreversible response to a self-resolving process.

The argument can be summarized as a single inversion: mainstream medicine treats the antibodies as the disease and the gland as the victim. Peat treated TSH and estrogen as the disease, the antibodies as cleanup, and the gland as a structure that can recover its function once the inflammatory drivers are suppressed. The protocol that follows from each framing produces dramatically different outcomes — a lifetime of T4 monotherapy in the first case, a resolution of the antibody titer and partial recovery of glandular function over several months in the second.
