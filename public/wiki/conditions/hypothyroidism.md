# Hypothyroidism

If Ray Peat is right, the majority of Americans are hypothyroid and do not know it. Standard blood tests miss the condition because they measure the wrong things in the wrong way, while the clinical method that actually worked—measuring oxygen consumption—was abandoned decades ago in favor of tests that serve the laboratory industry. In a [[2019-10-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-brain-barriers|2019 episode]], Peat estimated that "by the standard of oxygen consumption, 30% of Americans were hypothyroid in 1940; now it is more like 60%." The hidden epidemic of low [[thyroid]] function underlies conditions as diverse as [[obesity]], [[depression]], heart disease, infertility, and dementia—all treated as separate diseases when they share a common metabolic root.

## The Diagnostic Problem

The core problem with thyroid diagnosis is that modern medicine relies on the TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) test, which measures the pituitary gland's response to thyroid levels rather than the thyroid's actual metabolic effects. A person can have symptoms of severe hypothyroidism—fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, hair loss, depression—while their TSH falls within the reference range.

The confusion runs deeper. As Peat explained in a [[2019-07-18-one-radio-network-one-radio-network|2019 interview]], many people get diagnosed with *hyperthyroidism* because their TSH is low, but their symptoms are typical of hypothyroidism. "Doctors actually tell patients they are 'both,'" he noted—a contradiction that reveals the inadequacy of the test rather than a paradox in the patient.

The older method—measuring basal metabolic rate through oxygen consumption—directly assessed what thyroid hormone actually does: drive oxidative [[metabolism]]. [[Broda Barnes]], practicing in Colorado, simplified this further by demonstrating that basal body temperature correlated reliably with metabolic rate. As Peat recounted in a [[2011-02-22-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing|2011 interview]], "Broda Barnes found temperature was adequate for diagnosing. He thought waking temperature should be about 97.8°F, rising to about 98.6°F after breakfast."

Peat recommended self-monitoring with temperature and pulse as practical diagnostic tools. In a [[2019-11-12-jodellefit-jodellefit-listener-qa-calories-cortisol-cellulite|2019 interview]], he explained: "After breakfast, temperature should reach 37°C / 98.6°F and pulse should be about 80-85 bpm." Temperature reflects metabolic heat production; pulse reflects cardiac output driven by thyroid. Both low readings together strongly suggest inadequate thyroid function.

## The Hidden Syndrome

The range of conditions that hypothyroidism produces is staggering, because thyroid hormone drives virtually every cellular process. Peat's clinical observations repeatedly demonstrated this breadth.

In a [[2012-05-18-politics-and-science-politics-science-autoimmune-and-movement-disorders|2012 interview]], he described how "the first five people I met with an MS diagnosis all had classical symptoms of hypothyroidism. When they took thyroid, their symptoms totally disappeared." Multiple sclerosis, in these cases, was hypothyroidism presenting as neurological disease.

In a [[2011-07-12-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-qa-i|2011 discussion]], he added that "the first five or six people I saw with MS diagnosis recovered instantly when they took thyroid." Pituitary enlargement from prolactin-secreting tumors—pressing on the optic nerve and causing visual disturbances—was another presentation that resolved with thyroid correction.

Hypothyroidism increases blood viscosity and raises blood pressure. As Peat explained in a [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 episode]], "A high percentage of people with hypertension are simply hypothyroid; thyroid supplementation can lower blood pressure." The mechanism is direct: low thyroid produces thick, sluggish blood that the heart must pump harder to circulate.

Other manifestations include: water retention and edema (the body holds water when it cannot produce enough metabolic heat), hair loss, constipation, cold extremities, menstrual irregularity, infertility, elevated [[cholesterol]] (thyroid drives cholesterol conversion to protective steroids), fatty liver, sodium wasting (as Peat's newsletter noted, "CO2 deficiency is connected with inability to retain adequate sodium"), and [[depression]]—which in many cases is simply the subjective experience of inadequate cellular energy.

## Why Hypothyroidism Is Increasing

The modern epidemic of hypothyroidism reflects the convergence of several factors that Peat identified throughout his work:

**[[PUFA]] consumption** suppresses thyroid function at multiple levels—blocking hormone secretion from the gland, binding transport proteins in the blood, and blocking receptor sites in cells. The massive increase in seed oil consumption since the 1940s parallels the increase in hypothyroidism.

**[[Estrogen]] exposure** from environmental sources (plasticizers, birth control residues in water, soy products) interferes with thyroid function. As Peat noted, "women have 5 or 10 times as much thyroid disease as men. The reason is that estrogen interferes with the liver's ability to convert thyroxine (T4) to the active T3 hormone."

**Iodine displacement** by environmental halogens (fluoride in water, bromide in flour and flame retardants, chlorine in water treatment) competes with iodine for thyroid uptake.

**Diagnostic criteria** that define the "normal" range too broadly, leaving millions of people with clearly symptomatic hypothyroidism untreated because their lab values fall within the reference range.

## The T4-to-T3 Conversion Problem

The thyroid gland primarily produces T4 (thyroxine), which must be converted to T3 (triiodothyronine) by the liver and other tissues to become metabolically active. This conversion fails under several common conditions: liver dysfunction, estrogen excess, PUFA accumulation, nutritional deficiencies (selenium, zinc, vitamin A), and—circularly—hypothyroidism itself.

As Peat explained in his email correspondence, "Hypothyroid people's blood serum can have a T4:T3 ratio of 50:1 or 100:1" when the liver fails to convert T4. The normal ratio, reflected in pig and cow thyroid glands, is about 3:1 to 4:1. This means a person can have adequate T4 production while being severely deficient in the active T3 that cells actually use.

This conversion failure explains why synthetic T4 (levothyroxine/Synthroid)—the standard medical treatment—fails many patients. They receive T4, their TSH normalizes, but their symptoms persist because the underlying conversion problem was never addressed. Adding T3 directly, or using desiccated thyroid (which contains both T4 and T3 in physiological ratios), often produces dramatic improvement.

Peat's newsletter on biological balance described a case where T3 was given to a man with serious liver cirrhosis: "The man woke without a hangover for the first time—T3 restored liver function enough to process alcohol." The active thyroid hormone directly restored liver function that synthetic T4 alone could not.

## Treatment: The Practical Framework

Peat's approach to hypothyroidism involved more than prescribing thyroid hormone—though that was often necessary.

**Desiccated thyroid** (Armour, or similar preparations from pig or cow thyroid) provides T4 and T3 together in a natural ratio. Peat discussed these preparations frequently, noting that old Armour thyroid made from beef was similar in T4:T3 ratio to human thyroid. Modern Armour uses pork thyroid, which is equally compatible.

**T3 supplementation** (Cytomel or compounded triiodothyronine) addresses the conversion problem directly. When the liver cannot convert T4 to T3, providing T3 bypasses the bottleneck. Peat's newsletter on mood and metabolism described how "in extremely adrenergic states, T3 causes both temperature and heart rate to decrease as it restores metabolic efficiency"—an initially counterintuitive response that occurs because T3 calms the stress hormones (adrenaline, cortisol) that were compensating for inadequate thyroid.

**Temperature and pulse monitoring** guides dosing better than blood tests. The goal is a waking temperature approaching 97.8°F, rising to 98.6°F after breakfast, with a resting pulse around 80-85 bpm. As Peat advised, "use temperature and heart rate to judge thyroid supplement dosage."

**Supporting factors** include adequate [[cholesterol]] (the raw material for steroid synthesis that thyroid enables), [[vitamin A]] (required for thyroid receptor function), adequate protein (to provide the transport proteins thyroid binds), and reduced PUFA intake (to remove the metabolic brake on thyroid action).

**Not everyone needs supplementation.** As Peat wrote in response to a question about athletes: "No, if the diet and level of activity are right, it shouldn't be necessary." The goal is to remove the factors suppressing thyroid function—PUFA, estrogen, nutritional deficiencies—and supplement only when those corrections prove insufficient.
