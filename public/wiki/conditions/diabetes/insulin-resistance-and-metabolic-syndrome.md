# Insulin Resistance and Metabolic Syndrome

*Mainstream endocrinology treats insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome with carbohydrate restriction, metformin, GLP-1 agonists, statins for the dyslipidemia, antihypertensives for the blood pressure component, and — at later stages — exogenous insulin. This article reconstructs Peat's inversion of the standard causal arrow: hyperinsulinemia is the first problem, the "resistance" is the body's protective adaptation, and the cluster is a chronic free-fatty-acid state rather than a freestanding syndrome. This page is a synthesis of Peat's framework, not medical advice; readers with a metabolic-syndrome or pre-diabetes diagnosis should not stop metformin, insulin, or any other prescribed medication based on what is written here, and dietary changes belong in coordination with a clinician who can track glucose, A1c, lipids, and blood pressure as the substrate environment shifts.*

In Ray Peat's reading, the cluster of findings mainstream medicine has spent forty years assembling under the names *insulin resistance*, *metabolic syndrome*, *Syndrome X*, *Reaven's syndrome*, *prediabetes*, and *non-alcoholic fatty liver disease* is the long-running clinical phenotype of a body that has switched fuels. The mechanism is the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] — free fatty acids displacing glucose oxidation at the pyruvate dehydrogenase step — and the molecular accounting belongs there. What lives here is the chronic, observable face of that switch: the visceral belly that supplies the fatty acids; the liver that takes the first hit; the lipid panel that documents the substrate change; the clotting markers that reflect the chronic FFA stress; the diagnostic thresholds that have crept downward each decade to enlarge the patient population; the hyperinsulinemia that precedes the resistance and inverts the conventional causal arrow. Three things make this article different from the existing [[randle-cycle|randle-cycle]] and [[insulin|insulin]] pages. It is pitched at the *clinical phenotype* level, not the mechanism level. It carries the unique Peat inversion that hyperinsulinemia is the *first* problem and the resistance is the body's *protective adaptation*. And it integrates the cluster — IR, fatty liver, PCOS, dyslipidemia, PAI-1, "pre-diabetes" — as one underlying state expressed in different tissues, rather than as a syndrome of independently co-occurring risk factors.

## The Reaven construct and Peat's critique

Gerald Reaven's 1988 Banting Lecture introduced *Syndrome X*: the observation that insulin resistance, glucose intolerance, hyperinsulinemia, elevated VLDL triglycerides, reduced HDL cholesterol, and hypertension travel together and confer cardiovascular risk beyond what any single risk factor predicts. The construct was renamed *metabolic syndrome* and added visceral obesity to the diagnostic criteria. By the early 2000s it had become a billable diagnosis in most national health systems. Reaven's empirical observation that the components cluster together is correct. The mechanism he proposed — that insulin resistance is the upstream defect that produces all of the other components — is, in Peat's reading, the inverted half of a story that had been documented in the 19th century and partly assembled by Selye in the mid-twentieth.

The Peat critique works on three levels. The first is descriptive: Reaven gave the cluster a name and then a syndrome label, which made it a thing to *have* rather than a state to *be in*. "High blood sugar is often seen along with high blood pressure and obesity in Cushing's syndrome, with excess cortisol, and these features are also used to define the newer metabolic syndrome," Peat wrote in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues* (2012)]]. The components Reaven assembled into a syndrome were the same components that Selye, Cushing, and the stress physiologists had been describing since the 1930s as the chronic-stress phenotype: cortisol-driven gluconeogenesis, adrenaline-driven free-fatty-acid mobilization, estrogen-driven growth-hormone elevation, hypothyroid-driven slowing of substrate oxidation. Naming the cluster a syndrome moved it from "chronic stress state" to "freestanding metabolic disorder," and the syndrome label obscured the underlying physiology even as it organized clinical attention around the cluster.

The second level is mechanistic. Reaven proposed insulin resistance as the upstream defect. Peat reverses it: insulin resistance is the adaptive consequence of a more upstream state in which free fatty acids have blocked glucose oxidation and forced the body to chronically elevate insulin. "Diabetics typically have elevated lactate," Peat wrote in the [[diabetes|email exchange on diabetes]], "which shows that glucose doesn't have a problem getting into their cells, just getting oxidized." The cell takes up glucose. The cell cannot finish oxidizing it because PUFA-derived free fatty acids have shut down pyruvate dehydrogenase. The unburned glucose ferments to lactate. Insulin is elevated chronically because the pancreas reads the elevated blood glucose and pours out insulin trying to drive it into cells that cannot use it. The insulin is doing nothing wrong; the substrate environment is wrong. The "resistance" is the body's homeostatic refusal to amplify the futile signal. Calling it the *cause* of the syndrome is reading a thermostat as the source of a fire.

The third level is the syndrome-naming critique itself. Peat repeatedly returned to the pattern by which medical doctrine consolidates around named entities that organize attention but obscure mechanism. The diabetes-naming history — wasting disease → sugar disease → blood-glucose threshold → A1c threshold — is the same pattern that produced Syndrome X and metabolic syndrome. Each renaming brings a new diagnostic threshold, a new population of patients, and a new commercial opportunity, while the underlying state Peat saw in 1970 has not changed. See [[diabetes]] for the full disease-naming history; the metabolic-syndrome chapter is its modern continuation.

## Hyperinsulinemia precedes resistance — the causal-arrow inversion

The single most consequential Peat-frame inversion of the conventional account is the direction of causality between hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance. Mainstream endocrinology runs the chain in this direction: insulin resistance develops first (genetics, sedentary lifestyle, abdominal obesity) → the pancreas compensates by secreting more insulin → hyperinsulinemia ensues → eventually beta-cell capacity fails → frank diabetes. The Peat-frame chain runs the other way: PUFA accumulation in tissue lipids and estrogen-driven elevation of growth hormone produce free-fatty-acid release into the blood → free fatty acids block glucose oxidation at PDH → blood glucose rises because cells cannot oxidize what they take up → insulin is secreted chronically and abundantly to push glucose into substrate-blocked cells → the chronic insulin elevation is itself a stressor → tissues develop "resistance" as a protective response to suppress fat synthesis and growth signaling that would otherwise run away under sustained hyperinsulinemia → eventually beta-cell exhaustion follows the chronic over-stimulation.

The evidence Peat marshaled for this inversion has three legs. The first is the contraceptive observation. When the oral contraceptive Enovid was introduced in the early 1960s, women on it developed signs of diabetes including decreased glucose tolerance, and Spellacy and Carlson, observing the effect, proposed that the underlying mechanism was an elevation of circulating free fatty acids — "free fatty acids can block the Krebs cycle, with relative insulin action resistance resulting," and the danger of oral contraceptives was "one of prolonged pancreatic stimulation" ([[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones* (2006)]]). The estrogen-driven elevation of growth hormone produced the FFA elevation; the FFA elevation produced the insulin resistance; the chronic pancreatic stimulation eventually produced the diabetes. Hyperinsulinemia came first, not last.

The second leg is the visceral-obesity evidence. The Colberg et al. 1995 study in the *Journal of Clinical Investigation* (cited in the brief at lines 446–452) demonstrated that women with visceral obesity have skeletal muscle insulin resistance, but not because of Randle substrate competition in the conventional sense — they had *reduced* postabsorptive FFA utilization by muscle. The muscle was not preferring fat over glucose; it had simply lost the capacity to oxidize either efficiently. Tchernof and colleagues, working the same year, showed that men with visceral fat accumulation had reduced testosterone and altered adrenal steroid metabolism *along with* the glucose intolerance and hyperinsulinemia. The hormonal abnormality and the insulin elevation were companions of the visceral fat depot, not consequences of insulin resistance.

The third leg is Bjorntorp's 1995 paper, "Insulin resistance: The consequence of a neuroendocrine disturbance?" — explicitly proposing that insulin resistance is downstream of a neuroendocrine state, and that the decreased capillary density observed in diabetic muscle "may be of importance for the apparent insulin resistance." The tissue is not receiving enough oxygen because the capillary network is sparser. It cannot complete oxidation. It develops the appearance of resistance to a hormone whose job is to push more substrate into cells that already cannot oxidize what they have. The receptor-defect frame has the etiology backwards.

Peat synthesized this in the 2006 newsletter: "The picture that I think explains many of the features of diabetes is that an energy deficit produces an alarm state, causing increased production of adrenalin and cortisol. Adrenalin mobilizes fat from storage, and the free fatty acids create a chronic problem involving 1) blocked ATP production, 2) activation of the protein kinase C system (increasing tension in blood vessels), 3) inhibition of thyroid function with its energetic, hormonal, and tissue-structure consequences, 4) availability of fats for prostaglandin synthesis, and 5) possibly a direct effect on clot dissolving, besides the PAI-1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor) effect seen in diabetes" ([[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]). The hyperinsulinemia in this chain is not the disease and not the cause. It is the body's attempt to push glucose into a tissue that has lost the capacity to burn it, in the presence of a fat-mobilization system that has been chronically activated. The resistance is the body's attempt to keep the insulin signal from making the situation worse. See [[insulin]] for the substance-level treatment of insulin as an estrogen-like stressor and the "hyperinsulinemia as stress" argument.

The clinical implication is that the standard therapeutic target — *lowering insulin resistance to allow more insulin signaling* — moves in exactly the wrong direction. The chronic insulin stimulation is what the body has been protecting itself from. The intervention that addresses the upstream defect is fatty-acid displacement: lower FFA by suppressing lipolysis (niacinamide, sugar, low-PUFA diet), restore substrate competition in favor of glucose (thyroid restoration, [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|PDH]] reactivation), and let the hyperinsulinemia resolve as a consequence of the cell's regained oxidative capacity. This is the inverse of the standard care path.

## Visceral obesity as the FFA-source organ

The clinical observation that abdominal fat — the "fat waist" — predicts diabetes more reliably than total body weight or BMI is one of the most replicated findings in metabolic epidemiology. The Peat-frame interpretation is anatomical and specific: visceral adipose tissue is the body's chronic free-fatty-acid factory, and its product is dumped directly into portal circulation where it reaches the liver before being diluted by systemic blood. This positions visceral adipose as a private FFA pipeline to the liver, distinct in physiology from subcutaneous adipose, which empties into systemic venous circulation and arrives at the liver only after dilution.

The biology of the visceral depot makes it a different organ from subcutaneous fat. Visceral adipocytes are more responsive to catecholamines, less responsive to insulin's antilipolytic effect, and more sensitive to cortisol's lipolytic priming. They store more PUFA in proportion to their volume than subcutaneous depots, because the PUFA-rich fats are preferentially retained in metabolically active tissue. When stress hormones rise — which happens chronically in the hypothyroid, estrogen-elevated, hyperinsulinemic patient — visceral fat releases free fatty acids preferentially, and those fatty acids include the polyunsaturated species that are most effective at blocking the Krebs cycle. Tchernof and colleagues' 1995 paper on visceral adipose tissue and glucose tolerance documented the importance of the depot, and Peat returned to it as the anatomical specificity of the FFA-source critique ([[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]).

The clinical phenotype that follows from this anatomy is consistent. The visceral-obese patient presents with portal-vein-delivered free fatty acids → hepatic insulin resistance → hepatic gluconeogenesis that cannot be suppressed by insulin → fasting hyperglycemia → fatty liver as the liver attempts to handle the FFA load by re-esterification → systemic hyperinsulinemia in response to the fasting hyperglycemia → eventual systemic insulin resistance as the body protects against the chronic insulin elevation. The systemic features of metabolic syndrome — high triglycerides, low HDL, high blood pressure, glucose intolerance — are downstream of this hepatic substrate-switching event, not co-equal components of a syndrome.

The implication for therapy is that visceral fat reduction matters in a way that subcutaneous fat reduction does not. The conventional measure of obesity, BMI, conflates two anatomically distinct fat depots with different metabolic outputs. The waist-to-hip ratio, the waist circumference, and the visceral fat fraction measured by imaging are better predictors of metabolic dysfunction than total weight because they isolate the depot that is doing the metabolic damage. The Peat-frame intervention — saturated-fat-replacement of PUFA, sufficient sugar to suppress lipolysis, thyroid restoration to raise basal metabolic rate, sufficient protein to maintain lean mass — produces preferential loss of visceral fat in clinical experience, because the visceral depot is the one that is being defended by the chronic stress signaling that the intervention dissolves.

## Fatty liver, NAFLD, and the substrate-switching organ

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is the third or fourth largest pseudo-epidemic that mainstream hepatology has assembled in the last forty years. The current US estimates put NAFLD prevalence at twenty-five to forty percent of adults, with NASH (the inflammatory, fibrotic subtype) at four to eight percent. The disease is, in mainstream framing, a separate entity that happens to co-occur with obesity, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome and that progresses to non-alcoholic cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma in a fraction of cases. In the Peat-frame reading, NAFLD is not a separate entity. It is the visible bookkeeping of the substrate switch in the organ that has to do the bookkeeping.

The liver in a body running chronically on free fatty acids has to handle the FFA load. It does this by re-esterifying free fatty acids into triglycerides and storing them locally, by exporting triglycerides as VLDL, and by oxidizing some fraction in mitochondria when the mitochondrial machinery is still intact. As PUFA accumulates in the liver's own membranes, mitochondrial function declines, the cell loses the capacity to oxidize what it stores, and the triglyceride accumulation becomes chronic — fatty liver. The inflammation that produces NASH is the response to chronic PUFA peroxidation in the hepatocyte and to chronic endotoxin exposure from a permeable gut, and the fibrosis that follows is the standard reaction of stressed tissue accumulating collagen as cells fail.

Peat directly addressed the fructose-causes-fatty-liver claim that has been the public-health framing for the last fifteen years: "Many people lately have been told, as part of a campaign to explain the high incidence of fatty liver degeneration in the U.S., supposedly resulting from eating too much sugar, that fructose can be metabolized only by the liver. The liver does have the highest capacity for metabolizing fructose, but the other organs do metabolize it" ([[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues* (2012)]]). The fructose-as-hepatotoxin claim relies on the false premise that fructose is uniquely hepatic; the actual evidence shows that fructose is a normal substrate that the liver handles preferentially because the liver is good at it, and that fructose can bypass the FFA-induced block at phosphofructokinase that traps glucose oxidation in stress states. Fructose, in the diabetic state, is more useful than glucose because it is not subject to the same blockade.

The non-drinker cirrhosis phenotype — chronic fatty liver progressing to cirrhosis without alcohol — is the late stage of this chronic substrate-switching state. It looks like alcoholic cirrhosis because alcoholic cirrhosis is itself a substrate-switching disease (alcohol blocks glucose, fat, and amino acid oxidation; ethanol-induced fatty liver is the same pattern produced by a different inhibitor). The shared pathway is the chronic block of substrate oxidation, the resulting accumulation of fat in the liver, and the fibrotic response to chronic cellular stress. See [[liver]] for the general liver-function frame and [[liver-restoration|liver-restoration]] for the protocol; the diabetes-specific load on the liver is the particular fatty-liver phenotype described here.

Fatty liver, in this reading, is not a disease to be treated separately from the rest of the metabolic-syndrome cluster. It is the same disease expressed in the liver that is also expressed in visceral fat, in muscle's lost oxidative capacity, in the dyslipidemic panel, and in the elevated PAI-1. The intervention is the same — PUFA depletion, sugar sufficiency to suppress lipolysis, thyroid restoration, and gut decontamination to reduce hepatic endotoxin. Standard NAFLD treatment with weight-loss counseling and GLP-1 agonists addresses surface features without touching the substrate-switching root.

## The PCOS overlap

Polycystic ovary syndrome — treated at length at [[pcos|PCOS]] from the gynecological and androgen-excess angle — also lives in the metabolic-syndrome cluster from the diabetes-side angle. The overlap is anatomical and physiological, not coincidental: PCOS patients carry elevated insulin, elevated androgens, ovarian morphological changes, and frequent visceral obesity. The mainstream account treats the insulin elevation as a downstream consequence of skeletal-muscle insulin resistance. The Peat-frame account integrates PCOS into the hyperinsulinemia-precedes-resistance story: estrogen-driven growth-hormone elevation drives FFA release; chronic FFA exposure produces hyperinsulinemia; the hyperinsulinemia drives ovarian theca-cell androgen production; androgens aromatize peripherally back to estrogen; ovarian follicles arrest because the FSH-LH ratio required for ovulation cannot resolve under the substrate-switching state. The PCOS patient is a metabolic-syndrome patient with the female reproductive axis in the foreground.

The clinical consequence is that PCOS treatment in the Peat frame is the same as metabolic-syndrome treatment — lower the free fatty acids, restore thyroid function (the most-suppressed axis in the PCOS patient), and address the estrogen excess at its sources (gut, liver, aromatase activity in visceral fat). Standard metformin-plus-oral-contraceptive treatment addresses neither root: metformin reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis without touching FFA exposure, and the oral contraceptive worsens the underlying estrogen burden while suppressing ovulation entirely. See [[pcos]] for the full ovarian-axis treatment; what lives here is the framing of PCOS as a special case of the metabolic-syndrome cluster.

## Diabetic dyslipidemia as a symptom of substrate-switching

The standard diabetic dyslipidemia pattern — elevated triglycerides, reduced HDL cholesterol, small dense LDL particles, normal or modestly elevated total cholesterol — is, in mainstream lipidology, a separate lipid disorder that co-occurs with diabetes and confers cardiovascular risk independently of glucose control. The Peat-frame reading is that the dyslipidemic panel is the predictable lipid output of a body running on free fatty acids. It is not a separate disease and not an independent risk factor; it is the lipid bookkeeping of the substrate switch.

The mechanism is direct. When a body chronically oxidizes fat rather than glucose, the liver receives a high free-fatty-acid load and re-esterifies the fatty acids into triglycerides. The triglycerides are exported as VLDL. VLDL particles, when triglyceride-rich, exchange triglycerides for cholesterol esters with HDL via cholesteryl ester transfer protein, depleting HDL cholesterol and producing triglyceride-rich HDL particles that are cleared more rapidly. The same exchange with LDL produces triglyceride-rich LDL that is then hydrolyzed by hepatic lipase to small dense LDL — the atherogenic remnant. The full pattern (high TG, low HDL, small dense LDL) is mechanistically downstream of one event: the high free-fatty-acid load entering the liver.

This reframes the clinical question. The lipid panel is not telling the clinician about a separate "lipid disorder" treatable with a statin or fibrate; it is telling the clinician about the body's substrate environment. The intervention is the one that addresses the substrate-switching itself: PUFA depletion, sugar sufficiency, thyroid restoration. As the substrate switch resolves, the lipid panel normalizes. Statin treatment of diabetic dyslipidemia treats the bookkeeping while leaving the cause intact. See [[free-fatty-acids]] for the substance-level treatment of FFA as the upstream lever.

## PAI-1 and the hypofibrinolytic clotting phenotype

Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 is the master regulator of the fibrinolytic system — the enzymatic cascade that dissolves fibrin clots. PAI-1 inhibits tissue plasminogen activator and urokinase, the enzymes that activate plasmin and break down clots. In the metabolic-syndrome / insulin-resistance state, PAI-1 is chronically elevated, and the body's capacity to dissolve clots is correspondingly suppressed. This is the molecular basis for the well-documented hypercoagulable state of diabetes and metabolic syndrome.

Three 1995 papers, all cited in the diabetes brief, established the phenotype. Vague, Raccah, and Scelles in *International Journal of Obesity* documented "hypofibrinolysis is observed among obese subjects and it has been shown that an excess of plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI 1) the main regulator of the fibrinolytic system, is closely associated to other components of the insulin resistance syndrome, namely, excessive body weight, high waist to hip ratio, elevated blood pressure, hyperinsulinemia and hypertriglyceridemia." Udvardy, Posan, and Harsfalvi in *Thrombosis Research* showed that platelet-rich clots from insulin-dependent diabetic patients were resistant to lysis "along with the highest PAI-1 activity concentration ratio of the platelet lysates, compared to plasmatic levels." Ceriello and colleagues in *Blood Coagulation and Fibrinolysis* demonstrated that the hyperglycemia-induced overproduction of PAI-1 in endothelial cells could be prevented by antioxidant defense — glutathione reduced PAI-1, supporting "the hypothesis that oxidative stress may play an important role in the pathogenesis of diabetic complications."

The Peat-frame integration of these findings is direct. The hyperinsulinemic, hyperglycemic, PUFA-loaded body is a chronically oxidatively stressed body, and the endothelium of that body responds by elevating PAI-1. The clotting tendency that follows is not an independent thrombotic disorder; it is the vascular bookkeeping of the substrate-switching state. The clinical correlate is the elevated risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, deep venous thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism in the metabolic-syndrome / diabetic population. Estrogen is the relevant aggravating factor — estrogen suppresses vascular plasminogen activator activity and elevates clotting factor IX, both of which compound the PAI-1 effect ([[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]). The female diabetic on oral contraception or postmenopausal hormone replacement carries a particularly high thrombotic load because two PAI-1-elevating signals (the FFA stress and the estrogen) are stacked.

The intervention is the same intervention. Antioxidant defense (vitamin E, glutathione precursors, the basic anti-stress diet), free-fatty-acid suppression, estrogen reduction, and thyroid restoration each reduce the upstream drivers of PAI-1 elevation. Aspirin (used in the diabetes protocol) addresses the platelet side of the clotting phenotype but does not reduce PAI-1 itself; the deeper intervention is substrate restoration.

## Pre-diabetes, HbA1c, and the diagnostic theater

The diagnostic creep that brought "pre-diabetes" into mainstream clinical practice is the modern continuation of the disease-naming history described in the [[diabetes|hub]]. The diagnostic threshold for diabetes itself has tightened progressively: the original ADA cutoff for fasting plasma glucose was 140 mg/dL; in 1997 it dropped to 126; the impaired-fasting-glucose category was created with thresholds of 110–125; in 2003 the lower bound dropped to 100; in 2010 the ADA endorsed HbA1c thresholds (6.5% for diabetes, 5.7–6.4% for "pre-diabetes"). Each successive tightening expanded the labeled population by tens of millions in the United States alone.

The Peat-frame critique is twofold. First, the thresholds are themselves continuous with the disease-naming creep that has run since 1848 — the disease is whatever the current threshold says it is, and the threshold has been moved repeatedly toward more patients. Second, HbA1c does not measure what it is taken to measure: it reflects average blood glucose weighted toward recent glucose and is confounded by red-cell turnover, iron status, hemoglobin variants, and non-glucose-dependent glycation. Two patients with identical mean glucose can produce different HbA1c values; iron-deficiency anemia inflates it, hemolysis or recent blood loss deflates it.

The deeper point is that even a perfect HbA1c measurement reports the wrong target. The underlying disease state is the chronic substrate-switching, and the clinical question is whether the body is oxidizing glucose. Temperature, pulse, postprandial respiratory quotient, fasting lactate, and fasting free-fatty-acid concentration are better markers of substrate-switching than HbA1c because they measure the actual metabolic state rather than a smoothed three-month glucose average. The patient told they have "pre-diabetes" on the basis of an A1c of 5.9% is being told something about an arbitrary threshold rather than something about their physiology. The pharmacological response addresses the number while leaving the substrate environment untouched.

## Transhydrogenase and estrogen in the metabolic-syndrome cluster

The transhydrogenase enzymes — the nicotinamide-nucleotide transhydrogenase on the inner mitochondrial membrane and the pyridine-nucleotide transhydrogenases in the cytosol — catalyze hydrogen transfer between NAD/NADH and NADP/NADPH pools. Peat returned to this enzyme family repeatedly because it is the molecular junction at which estrogen ties directly to the substrate-switching state. Estrogen is uniquely effective at activating transhydrogenases, and the activation shifts metabolic energy from oxidative respiration toward glycolysis. In tissue with intact respiration, the shift is reversible and the transhydrogenase can be flipped the other way as oxygen and substrate availability improve. In tissue with chronic free-fatty-acid exposure and PUFA-substituted cardiolipin, the shift becomes persistent, and the cell remains stuck in glycolytic mode — the [[warburg-effect|Warburg state]] applied to non-cancer tissue.

The relevance to metabolic syndrome is direct. The estrogen-driven elevation of growth hormone, documented since the 1960s contraceptive observations, is the proximate driver of free-fatty-acid release. The free-fatty-acid release activates protein kinase C and the transhydrogenase mechanism that estrogen has primed. The combined effect is to lock the cell into a substrate-switching state in which glucose cannot be efficiently oxidized, lactate accumulates, and the body continues to elevate insulin in a futile attempt to push glucose into cells whose oxidative apparatus has been disabled. "Estrogen and the polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), linoleic and linolenic acid, alike activate the protein kinase C (PKC) system of cellular activation," Peat wrote in the 2006 newsletter. "Many of the functions of PUFA are similar to the functions of estrogen (e.g., antagonism to thyroid function, promotion of age pigment/lipofuscin), so this information showing that they both act similarly on the same basic regulatory pathway is important."

This explains the strong female-skewed epidemiology of metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes when measured in the postmenopausal and contraceptive-using populations. Peat wrote in the same newsletter that "Women are much more likely than men to develop diabetes," and the mechanism he proposed is the estrogen-GH-FFA chain operating chronically over decades. The transhydrogenase activation by estrogen is the molecular event that ties the female reproductive endocrinology to the metabolic-syndrome phenotype, and it operates by the same chemistry that ties estrogen to the cancer phenotype. The cancer-side treatment is at [[cancer]] and the [[cancer/estrogen-and-cancer]] sub-article; the metabolic-syndrome-side treatment is here. They are the same underlying mechanism with different downstream tissue expressions.

The therapeutic implication is that estrogen-lowering interventions matter for metabolic syndrome in a way the standard endocrinology has not registered. The interventions Peat advocated for cancer protection — gut decontamination to reduce the enterohepatic recirculation of estrogen, liver support to improve estrogen clearance, sufficient progesterone or pregnenolone to restore the progesterone-to-estrogen ratio, low PUFA to reduce aromatase substrate in fat tissue, thyroid restoration to raise the SHBG concentration that sequesters free estradiol — are simultaneously interventions for metabolic syndrome. The metabolic-syndrome patient on hormone replacement therapy is being pushed in the wrong direction by the same intervention that pushes the cancer patient in the wrong direction. The unified intervention is consistent with the unified physiology.

## Counter-arguments

**The low-carb camp's framing.** A large body of clinical and popular work over the last twenty-five years — Atkins, Lustig, Taubes, Phinney, Volek, the ketogenic-diet community, the carbohydrate-insulin model of obesity — holds that carbohydrate consumption is the proximate driver of hyperinsulinemia, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome, and that carbohydrate restriction (especially restriction of fructose and refined sugar) is the therapeutic lever. Several clinical trials show short- and medium-term improvements in insulin sensitivity, triglycerides, HDL, and glucose markers on low-carb diets. The Peat-frame response is partial agreement and substantial disagreement. Partial agreement: the modern American diet that combines high-PUFA seed oils with chronic snacking on starchy and sweetened processed food is bad, and removing the snacking and the seed oils produces real metabolic improvement that is wrongly attributed to the carbohydrate reduction. Substantial disagreement: the mechanism by which low-carb diets improve metabolic markers in the short term is the substitution of saturated fat for PUFA and the temporary forced metabolic adaptation to fat as a fuel; the longer-term consequence is the chronic stress signaling that ketogenic states demand (chronically elevated cortisol, suppressed thyroid, raised free fatty acids), with the predictable side effects of insomnia, anxiety, declining libido, declining cold tolerance, and eventual recurrence of insulin resistance. The clean test is what happens after five to ten years on a ketogenic diet; the literature on long-term outcomes is sparse because long-term adherence is poor, but the cases that have been documented show the expected pattern.

**The GLP-1 era.** The arrival of semaglutide, tirzepatide, and the broader GLP-1 receptor agonist class in clinical practice has produced striking weight loss and improvement in glycemic markers in trials. Mainstream endocrinology has framed this as the resolution of the obesity-metabolic-syndrome problem and the validation of insulin resistance as the central pathology. The Peat-frame response is that GLP-1 agonists work by inducing nausea, suppressing appetite, slowing gastric emptying, and producing a chronic caloric deficit. The metabolic improvements that follow are the metabolic improvements that follow any sustained caloric deficit, and they are accompanied by the muscle loss, the gallstone formation, the gastroparesis, and the bone density decline that follow any sustained caloric deficit. The five-to-ten-year horizon will tell whether the weight loss is sustainable after drug discontinuation and what fraction of patients develop the late complications. The mechanism does not address PUFA accumulation, does not address the substrate-switching state, and does not address the upstream estrogen-FFA-transhydrogenase chain. Peat died before the GLP-1 era and did not write on these drugs; the brief treatment in the [[diabetes-protocol|diabetes-protocol]] article addresses coordination with conventional treatment for patients already on them.

**The lipid hypothesis of diabetes.** A subset of the conventional account treats diabetic dyslipidemia as the upstream cardiovascular risk factor and frames statin therapy as the central intervention for the diabetic patient. The Peat-frame response is that the lipid panel is downstream bookkeeping rather than upstream cause, and that statin therapy lowers cholesterol synthesis at the cost of mevalonate-pathway products (CoQ10, dolichol, isoprenoids) that the diabetic patient cannot afford to lose. The cardiovascular event reduction in trials is modest, the side-effect profile (myalgia, diabetes induction, cognitive complaints) is substantial, and the underlying substrate environment is unaddressed. The dyslipidemia resolves when the substrate-switching resolves.

**The receptor-defect framing.** Mainstream molecular endocrinology has spent decades searching for the receptor-level or post-receptor-signaling defect that produces insulin resistance — PI3K signaling, Akt phosphorylation, GLUT4 translocation, adipokine cascades. The Peat-frame critique is that the receptor signaling looks abnormal because the substrate environment is abnormal: the receptor is being signaled chronically into a cell whose oxidative apparatus has been disabled, and the appearance of receptor-level "defect" is the cell's appropriate refusal to amplify a futile signal. The Bjorntorp 1995 observation about capillary density is the canonical empirical instance — the resistance is anatomical and substrate-environmental, not receptor-level.

## Data gaps

The mechanism articulated here rests on biochemistry, physiology, and clinical observation accumulated over a century, but formal RCT evidence for the Peat-frame intervention package (PUFA depletion, sugar sufficiency, thyroid restoration, gut decontamination, estrogen reduction) is sparse. Most supporting evidence is mechanistic, observational, or drawn from the 1940s–1970s nutritional literature — the 1947 Houssay coconut-oil-diabetes experiment, the Lazarow alloxan-protection studies, the 1990s FFA-insulin-resistance literature. A modern multi-arm RCT comparing the Peat-frame intervention against standard care does not exist and is unlikely to be funded.

Specific empirical gaps: the dose-response relationship between dietary PUFA reduction and tissue PUFA turnover (the published linoleic-acid adipose half-life clusters around two years, but the diabetic-adipose figure may differ); the contribution of gut endotoxin to hepatic versus systemic insulin resistance; the relative contributions of estrogen, growth hormone, prolactin, and cortisol to chronic FFA elevation across the female lifespan; whether estrogen-driven transhydrogenase activation is reversible in tissue that has been chronically estrogen-elevated for decades; and the relationship between the visceral fat depot's PUFA loading and its FFA output rate.

Safety framing: nothing in this article is medical advice. The Peat-frame intervention is an adjunct to be coordinated with the patient's existing medical care, not a replacement for it. Patients on insulin, metformin, GLP-1 agonists, or other diabetes medications should not modify those treatments without their prescribing physician's involvement. The substrate-restoration approach changes the body's metabolic state, and that change has to be matched with appropriate adjustment of medications whose dose was set under the prior state.
