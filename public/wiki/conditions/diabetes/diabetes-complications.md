# Diabetes Complications

The conventional diabetic-complications narrative reads as a cascade of damage produced by chronic hyperglycemia: high blood glucose glycates proteins, glycated proteins damage capillaries, damaged capillaries blind retinas and starve nerves and ulcerate feet and scar kidneys. Tight glycemic control prevents the cascade; that is the institutional reading of DCCT, UKPDS, and a half-century of complication-prevention orthodoxy. Peat's inversion of that reading is the central argument of this article. The complications are largely complications of the treatment, not of the underlying state. The chronic exogenous insulin produces the lipogenic and counter-regulatory cascade that mobilizes free fatty acids from PUFA-loaded stores; those free fatty acids, year after year, are what damages the retinal capillaries, the peripheral nerves, the renal glomeruli, the cardiac myocyte, the small arteries of the feet. The chronic glucose-deprivation of the cellular interior — the diabetic liver cannot keep glycogen on hand, and without liver glycogen cannot convert T4 to T3 — drops the patient into chronic hypothyroidism, and the hypothyroidism produces the soft-tissue calcification, the capillary leakiness, the widely spaced capillaries, the slow connective-tissue degeneration that mainstream pathology then attributes to "diabetes" as such. The disease is real. The complications, in their characteristic modern pattern, are mostly iatrogenic.

Three pieces of evidence anchor the inversion and recur through this article. First, the non-diabetic apes given insulin developed the same "complications of diabetes" seen in human patients — retinopathy with antibodies to insulin lodged in the retina — establishing that insulin treatment alone, on a body that did not have the disease, produced what is called the disease's late picture ([[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]). Second, [[people/broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]] gave all of his diabetic patients a thyroid supplement and reported that *none* of them developed the expected circulatory, renal, retinal, or neuropathic complications — establishing that the complications are not inherent to the diabetic state but contingent on the chronic hypothyroidism the diabetic state induces. Third, Hammes and colleagues in *Diabetologia* (1996) gave streptozotocin-diabetic rats fish oil for six months expecting protection against retinopathy, and instead found a 75 percent *increase* in occluded acellular capillaries in the retina — the strongest single rebuttal of AHA fish-oil-for-diabetes advice in the experimental literature. The three findings sit together. They are why the Peat-frame approach to diabetic complications is not "tighter glycemic control" but "remove the PUFA, restore the thyroid, support the energy production, do the smallest possible insulin dose, and the complications largely do not develop."

This article is the iatrogenesis case at organ-system resolution. The hub at [[conditions/diabetes|diabetes]] frames the inversion; the [[diabetes/insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome|insulin-resistance-and-metabolic-syndrome]] companion handles the chronic Type 2 phenotype; the [[diabetes-protocol|diabetes-protocol]] article gives the actionable clinical stack. What follows is the complication portfolio — the apes study, the neuropathy mechanism and nerve-restoration protocol, the retinopathy data including Hammes 1996, the nephropathy and microalbuminuria cluster, the cardiovascular and microcirculatory pattern, the diabetic foot and gangrene pathway, the diabetic cardiomyopathy that mirrors Meerson's stress-injured heart, the Broda Barnes counterfactual, the tracking signals, and the counter-arguments mainstream cardiology and endocrinology will raise.

## The non-diabetic-apes-given-insulin study

The single piece of evidence Peat returned to most often is the observation that non-diabetic apes developed "complications of diabetes" when given insulin. From [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]] (2006): "When non-diabetic apes were given insulin treatments, they developed some of the same 'complications of diabetes' that are seen in humans, and antibodies to insulin were found in their retinas, suggesting that some 'complications of diabetes' were complications of insulin treatment. Patients were seldom well informed of the arguments against the use of insulin, but the justification for the new genetically engineered human insulin is precisely that it avoids immunological damage." The animals did not have the disease; they received its treatment; they developed its late picture. The mechanistic chain — confirmed in detail by Poulaki et al. *J Clin Invest* 2002 (see retinopathy below) — is that exogenous insulin acutely lowers blood sugar, the resulting hypoglycemia triggers a counter-regulatory adrenaline-and-cortisol surge, the surge mobilizes free fatty acids from PUFA-loaded fat stores, and chronic exposure of capillary endothelium and tissue mitochondria to oxidizable PUFA drives the slow-grade peroxidative damage that produces the complications. The retinal antibodies to insulin point at a second mechanism — the immunological injury that recombinant-human-insulin marketing tacitly admitted by framing the new product as "non-immunogenic." Animal insulin had been producing antibody-mediated damage to retinas for half a century. The fix was a different insulin molecule, not a re-examination of whether chronic insulin therapy was the right idea.

The study does not show — and Peat did not claim — that all diabetic complications are insulin-mediated. Type 1 patients in ketoacidosis require insulin for acute survival; that is not the question. The question is whether the moderate chronic hyperglycemia-targeted insulin therapy administered to Type 2 patients for decades, often introduced when oral agents fail, is responsible for a meaningful fraction of the complication burden attributed to "uncontrolled diabetes." The apes study, the mortality data (the U.S. diabetes mortality rose from 16.0 to 26.4 per 100,000 from 1920 to 1944 across institutional adoption of insulin), and the Broda Barnes clinical counterfactual together suggest that the iatrogenic fraction is substantial. Insulin should have prevented the acute ketoacidotic deaths that previously killed diabetics, so the rate should have dropped. Instead it rose.

The implication is what the diabetes hub names "alongside, not instead of." Insulin is appropriate for acute decompensation, ketoacidosis, and frank Type 1. The goal in chronic management is the smallest dose compatible with metabolic stability while the upstream insults — PUFA, estrogen, endotoxin, hypothyroidism — are addressed. Stopping insulin without practitioner coordination is dangerous and is not what any companion in this hub advises. See [[insulin]] for the substance-level treatment of insulin as estrogen-like stressor.

## Diabetic neuropathy

The orthodox account of diabetic neuropathy is that chronic hyperglycemia damages peripheral nerves through some combination of glycation, sorbitol accumulation via the polyol pathway, microvascular insufficiency, and oxidative stress. The clinical picture is loss of sensation in the feet ("stocking" distribution), progressive autonomic dysfunction, eventually motor involvement. The standard intervention is tighter glycemic control and symptomatic management of pain (gabapentin, duloxetine, tricyclics).

Peat's reading, given most clearly in the [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor: Diabetes II*]] episode that titles itself "how to restore and protect nerves," reframes the lesion as an energy-failure lesion in nerve cells and their supporting glial cells. The mechanism: "When cells are living on fatty acids, the fatty acids intrinsically disturb the metabolism of phosphate, turn off glucose energy production, and activate nitric oxide, the respiratory inhibitor." The cell is energy-starved because its substrate-switching apparatus has been shoved into the wrong mode — see [[mechanisms/randle-cycle|randle-cycle]] for the full substrate-switching account — and the energy deficit shows up in the nerve as failure to make the cholesterol and the neurosteroids that protect and maintain the axon. "In nerves, one of the effects of failing energy is the inability to make cholesterol and convert cholesterol into neurosteroids. When a nerve or its supporting glial cells are injured by anything — including lack of glucose or oxygen — they stop producing the defensive steroids from cholesterol and begin producing estrogen. The diabetic brain and nerves have more aromatase (the enzyme which converts androgens to estrogens) than a normal person." The shift from neurosteroid to estrogen synthesis is not a mainstream-recognized feature of diabetic neuropathy; it is one of the most distinctive Peat-frame mechanistic claims. The estrogen "keeps things excited and stressed," the local inflammatory and excitotoxic signals continue, and the nerve progressively loses its insulating myelin and its conducting function.

The actionable nerve-restoration stack is the same five-piece intervention that the hub's "Restore respiration" move sketches, plus two neurosteroid-specific additions. Thyroid restores the cellular oxidation that the FFA block is preventing — without restored oxidation, no other intervention compounds. [[substances/hormones/progesterone|Progesterone]] supplies the neurosteroid the injured nerve has stopped making — Peat in the 2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor: Particles* episode noted that "many animal studies show that vitamin D and progesterone work together to restore nerves" for both peripheral and central nerve regeneration. [[substances/drugs/aspirin|Aspirin]] blocks the prostaglandin cascade and reduces the lipase activity that is mobilizing the offending fatty acids. [[substances/vitamins/niacinamide|Niacinamide]] inhibits the adipose lipase directly. The B-vitamin cluster — thiamine, biotin, B12, pantothenic acid — supplies cofactors for the respiratory enzymes that the FFA block has compromised. Magnesium restores cellular Mg-ATP and prevents the calcium-uptake cascade that the energy-deficient cell falls into. [[substances/drugs/methylene-blue|Methylene blue]] supports mitochondrial electron transport directly. The 2014 transcript places progesterone and thyroid at the center of the neurosteroid-restoration logic; the protocol article elaborates the dose ranges and sequencing.

The key reframe is that diabetic neuropathy is not an irreversible glycation lesion. It is, in this framework, a chronic energy-deficit lesion in a nerve that has stopped producing its own defensive steroids. Remove the FFA block, restore the cellular energy, supply the neurosteroid precursors, and the nerve can recover function — slowly, over months to years, but recoverably. The diabetic-protocol article carries the operational detail; the [[conditions/diabetes/diabetes-protocol|diabetes-protocol]] section on nerve restoration anchors at this episode.

## Diabetic retinopathy and the Hammes 1996 study

Diabetic retinopathy is the leading cause of blindness in working-age adults in developed countries, and the orthodox story is the same as for neuropathy: chronic hyperglycemia damages retinal capillaries through glycation and microvascular injury; tighter glycemic control prevents progression; the modern interventions are anti-VEGF injections, laser photocoagulation, and vitrectomy. The Peat-frame reading flips the causation. The damage is not predominantly glycative — it is peroxidative damage to retinal capillaries chronically exposed to mobilized free fatty acids from PUFA-loaded fat stores, accelerated by exogenous insulin and the counter-regulatory cascade it provokes.

The mechanistic capstone in the mainstream literature is the Poulaki et al. paper in *J Clin Invest* (2002), cited in the [[diabetes-research|diabetes research brief]]: **"Acute intensive insulin therapy exacerbates diabetic blood-retinal barrier breakdown via hypoxia-inducible factor-1alpha and VEGF."** The authors demonstrated that acute intensive insulin therapy markedly increases VEGF expression in the retinae of diabetic rats and markedly increases blood-retinal barrier breakdown. As they wrote: "To our knowledge, these data are the first to identify a specific mechanism for the transient worsening of diabetic retinopathy, specifically blood-retinal barrier breakdown, that follows the institution of intensive insulin therapy." The clinical observation that retinopathy can worsen in the months after intensive insulin is initiated has been recognized in the diabetic-ophthalmology literature for decades; it has been called the "early worsening" phenomenon and treated as a transient phenomenon to be ridden out. Poulaki identified the mechanism: the insulin itself drives the VEGF surge that breaks down the blood-retinal barrier. This is precisely the iatrogenesis case in mechanistic detail.

The strongest single rebuttal of AHA fish-oil-for-diabetes advice in the experimental literature is Hammes, Weiss, Fuhrer, Kramer, Papavassilis, and Grimminger, *Diabetologia* 39(3), 251–255, 1996, "Acceleration of experimental diabetic retinopathy in the rat by omega-3 fatty acids." The authors gave streptozotocin-diabetic rats 750 mg Maxepa (14% EPA, 10% DHA) five times per week for six months, hypothesizing that omega-3 anti-inflammatory effects would inhibit retinopathy. The biochemical effects were as expected: EPA roughly doubled in total fatty acids; the thromboxane ratio collapsed from 600 to 50. The retinal effects were not. Pericyte loss was unaffected. The formation of acellular occluded capillaries — the lesion that defines proliferative diabetic retinopathy in this model — was *increased by 75 percent* in the fish-oil group (115.1 ± 26.8 vs. 65.2 ± 15.0 acellular segments per mm²). The authors' conclusion: "We conclude from this study that dietary fish oil supplementation may be harmful for the diabetic microvasculature in the retina."

The finding sits inside a broader literature Peat collected. Vessby and Boberg *J Intern Med* (1990) showed MaxEPA supplementation in Type 2 diabetics tended to increase blood glucose and decrease peripheral insulin sensitivity. Vessby et al. *Diabet Med* (1992) confirmed PUFA "may impair blood glucose control" — fasting glucose +15 percent, midday glucose +17–18 percent on PUFA vs. saturated fat. The pattern is consistent: omega-3 supplementation in diabetic models accelerates the very complications it is given to prevent. The mechanism is straightforward: fish oils are highly oxidizable; the diabetic state is already a state of elevated lipid peroxidation; adding more substrate accelerates the damage.

The clinical implication is that AHA-style fish-oil advice given to diabetic patients on the basis of cardiovascular extrapolations from non-diabetic populations is not grounded in the diabetic-complication experimental literature. The Peat-frame retinal protection stack is the opposite: remove dietary PUFA, substitute saturated fats, support [[substances/vitamins/vitamin-e|vitamin E]] and other antioxidants, restore thyroid, supply adequate dietary sugar. Progress is slow because adipose PUFA turnover is slow ([[protocols/pufa-depletion|pufa-depletion]]).

## Diabetic nephropathy

Diabetic nephropathy is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease in the United States. The orthodox account is that chronic hyperglycemia damages glomerular filtration through glycation, hemodynamic stress, and TGF-beta-mediated mesangial expansion, progressing through microalbuminuria, proteinuria, and falling GFR. The Peat-frame reading parallels the retinal case: the dominant lesion is peroxidative damage to glomerular capillaries chronically exposed to mobilized FFA, accelerated by exogenous insulin.

Rungby et al. *Acta Endocrinologica* (1992): "In the kidney, lipid peroxidation was increased after one week of diabetes; insulin treatment reduced the level of lipid peroxidation to levels lower than seen in controls." The acute insulin effect lowers peroxidation; the chronic story is more complicated because chronic insulin therapy reshapes the lipid environment in ways that accelerate peroxidation through the FFA counter-regulatory loop. Plante et al. *Metabolism* (1999), "The blood vessel, linchpin of diabetic lesions," describes the structural pattern: increased vasa vasorum arteriole diameter, enhanced albumin permeability, poor lymphatic drainage — consistent with hypothyroid-tissue capillary leakiness ("Hypothyroidism is known to include deposition of mucopolysaccharides in tissues, increased permeability of capillaries with leakage of albumin out of the blood," Peat).

Microalbuminuria, in this reading, is the first measurable sign of the same generalized capillary leakiness hypothyroidism produces; it is renal-specific because urine is what gets sampled. Jensen et al. *Clinical Science*: "Microalbuminuria reflects a generalized transvascular albumin leakiness in clinically healthy subjects." The implication is that microalbuminuria progression should respond to interventions that restore capillary integrity (thyroid, magnesium, the saturated-fat shift) rather than only to interventions that lower glomerular filtration pressure (ACE inhibitors, ARBs). ACE inhibitors do slow progression, but they treat the hemodynamic consequence rather than the underlying permeability defect.

The Broda Barnes counterfactual applies with full force here. Barnes's thyroid-supplemented diabetics did not develop the renal degeneration, because thyroid restored the cellular oxidation, capillary integrity, and hepatic glycogen capacity that the chronic FFA-driven state had compromised. The intervention stack is the same five-piece move that anchors the rest of the portfolio: thyroid, PUFA reduction, adequate sugar, estrogen reduction (women), endotoxin reduction. See [[concepts/blood-sugar|blood-sugar]] and [[substances/hormones/cortisol|cortisol]] for background.

## Cardiovascular complications

Diabetic cardiovascular disease is the largest single mortality contributor in the diabetic population. The orthodox account is that hyperglycemia drives atherogenesis through glycation of lipoproteins, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction; intensive glycemic control plus statins plus blood-pressure control reduce events. Peat's reading reframes diabetic cardiovascular disease as a variant of the broader cardiovascular picture (see [[conditions/heart-disease|heart-disease]]) in which the dominant lesions are (1) calcification of blood vessels driven by the chronic hypothyroidism, magnesium loss, and intracellular-calcium accumulation that the diabetic state produces; (2) hypertension driven by protein-kinase-C activation by the same PUFA and estrogen that drive the systemic state; (3) PAI-1-mediated hypofibrinolysis (Ceriello, Udvardy, Vague); and (4) the diabetic cardiomyopathy detailed in the next section.

From [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]: "One of the problems associated with diabetes is the calcification of blood vessels, though now there is more emphasis on fatty degeneration. Other blood vessel problems include hypertension, and poor circulation in general, leading to gangrene of the feet, impotence, and degeneration of the retina." The pattern of soft-tissue calcification — arterial walls, joint capsules, the lens — is, in this reading, a hypothyroid-tissue pattern the diabetic state accelerates rather than a glucose-driven pattern as such. The osteoporosis-with-soft-tissue-calcification axis is unified: calcium leaves the bones under chronic stress and parathyroid-hormone elevation; the same calcium deposits in arterial walls and atherosclerotic plaques. The intervention is the same that prevents the bone loss — thyroid, magnesium, reduced chronic stress.

PAI-1 is elevated in diabetic populations as part of the lipogenic-stress cluster (insulin, estrogen, and cortisol each elevate it), producing a chronic hypofibrinolytic state. The intervention is the upstream stack; mainstream anticoagulation does not address it. Grynberg *Diabetes Metabolism* (2001) describes the diabetic cardiomyocyte: "characterised by an over consumption of FA... requires a reduced FA contribution to ATP production." This is the Randle cycle at cardiac-tissue resolution — the diabetic heart burning fat when it should be burning glucose, with the resulting inefficiency, lipid-peroxidation load, and membrane-PUFA-driven arrhythmogenic instability.

## Diabetic foot, gangrene, and the microcirculatory pattern

The diabetic foot is the proximate cause of most non-traumatic lower-extremity amputations in the developed world. The orthodox account is combined neuropathy (loss of sensation) plus microvascular insufficiency plus immunological compromise producing non-healing ulcers that progress to gangrene. The Peat-frame reading retains the multifactorial structure but reframes the microvascular insufficiency as a hypothyroid-tissue-perfusion pattern rather than a glucose-driven small-vessel disease.

The most distinctive structural observation Peat made is the wider capillary spacing in diabetic tissue. From [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|the 2006 newsletter]]: "In muscles, and probably in other tissues of diabetics, capillaries are more widely spaced, as if the basal oxidative requirement were lower than normal. However, mitochondria contain more respiratory enzymes, as if to partly compensate for the poor delivery of oxygen to the cells." The diabetic muscle bed is, structurally, the muscle bed of an organism with chronically lower oxidative demand — exactly what chronic hypothyroidism produces. The compensatory mitochondrial-enzyme density increase is the energy-starved cell trying to extract more from what reaches it.

Apply that framework to the foot. Capillaries are more widely spaced; oxygen diffuses farther; cellular ATP is lower; cells are more susceptible to peroxidative damage from chronic FFA exposure. The hypothyroid skin and connective tissue have mucopolysaccharide deposition and capillary leakiness. A small injury — callus, blister, minor cut — is harder to heal because tissue is at the edge of its energetic capacity. The wound becomes chronic, ulcerates, infects, and with already compromised vascular supply, becomes the entry point for gangrenous progression that ends in amputation.

The intervention is to restore perfusion and cellular energy. Thyroid restores oxidative demand and the capillary structure that supports it over months and years. PUFA reduction removes the peroxidative substrate from membranes. Adequate sugar sustains hepatic glycogen. Local wound care, antibiotics for established infection, and off-loading are necessary in acute disease; the systemic intervention that prevents recurrence is the same that prevents the rest of the portfolio.

## Diabetic cardiomyopathy and the Meerson parallel

The most striking single mechanistic parallel Peat drew in [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|the 2006 newsletter]] is between the diabetic heart and F. Z. Meerson's experimentally stress-injured heart. "F. Z. Meerson's description of the stress-injured heart is very similar to the general changes that occur in chronic diabetes. He found that the stressed heart becomes rigid and unable to contract completely, or to relax completely. Excess calcium enters cells, and fatty acids are mobilized both locally and systemically, and both of these tend to damage the mitochondria. In diabetes, fatty acids are mobilized and oxidized instead of glucose, and calcium enters cells, increasing their rigidity and preventing relaxation of muscles in blood vessels." The diabetic cardiomyopathy that mainstream cardiology has begun to recognize as a distinct entity — diastolic dysfunction, impaired relaxation, eventually systolic decline — is, on Peat's reading, the cardiac expression of the same chronic stress lesion that Meerson induced experimentally with stress alone.

The mechanism in this reading is unified across the diabetic complication portfolio. Free fatty acids mobilize from PUFA-loaded stores under the chronic adrenaline-and-cortisol drive that the FFA-induced energy deficit perpetuates. The fatty acids block pyruvate dehydrogenase ([[mechanisms/pyruvate-dehydrogenase|pyruvate-dehydrogenase]]) and prevent glucose oxidation. The cell cannot keep its ATP up. ATP without magnesium is unstable; magnesium leaves the cell; calcium enters. The intracellular calcium accumulation makes the muscle rigid — unable to contract completely, unable to relax completely. The same fatty acids generate prostaglandins ([[mechanisms/prostaglandins|prostaglandins]]) and other lipid mediators that perpetuate the inflammatory state. The damage compounds across years. The diabetic heart, the diabetic muscle, the diabetic vessel, the diabetic kidney — same lesion at the cellular level, different organ presentation.

The "presence of an excess of free unsaturated fatty acids, and of calcium, in cells makes me think of the insoluble soap that these substances form in other situations, including the intestine. It seems that this could form a harmful deposit in cells, blocking many metabolic processes." The insoluble-soap conjecture — calcium-PUFA complexes accumulating in cells — is one of the more speculative things Peat said in this newsletter, but it points at a class of cellular deposits that the membrane-lipidomics literature has begun to document under different names (lipotoxic ceramide species, intramyocellular lipid accumulation). The general direction — that PUFA in the presence of intracellular calcium overload forms toxic deposits — is consistent with the wider lipid-peroxidation framework. See [[mechanisms/lipid-peroxidation|lipid-peroxidation]] for the underlying mechanism.

The intervention for diabetic cardiomyopathy is the same intervention as for the other organ-system complications. Restore the substrate switch — get the heart burning glucose instead of fat — by removing the FFA drive (PUFA reduction, niacinamide, aspirin, thyroid, adequate sugar). Restore the magnesium-ATP-calcium balance (magnesium supplementation, thyroid, reduce stress). The structural changes are slow to reverse; the metabolic shifts are quicker. The clinical signature of improvement is the same as in the broader heart-disease framework: temperature and pulse rise toward normal, diastolic function improves over months, exercise tolerance recovers.

## Broda Barnes — "no diabetic of mine ever developed the complications because I gave them thyroid"

The single most consequential clinical observation in this entire article, and the one Peat returned to most often, is [[people/broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]]'s decades-long clinical practice of giving thyroid supplementation to his diabetic patients and observing that they did not develop the expected complications. From [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|*Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones*]]: "Hypothyroidism is known to include deposition of mucopolysaccharides in tissues, increased permeability of capillaries with leakage of albumin out of the blood, elevated adrenalin which can lead to increased production of cortisol, decreased testosterone production, high risk of heart and circulatory disease, including a tendency to ulceration of the extremities, and osteoporosis, all of which are recognized 'complications of diabetes.' Broda Barnes gave all of his diabetic patients a thyroid supplement, and found that none of them developed the expected complications of diabetes." The list is striking because it overlaps almost completely with the standard mainstream list of diabetic complications — the same capillary leakiness, the same vascular disease, the same foot ulcers, the same osteoporosis-with-soft-tissue-calcification axis. Barnes saw the complications as expressions of the hypothyroidism that the diabetic state produces, and the clinical implication followed immediately: treat the hypothyroidism, and the complications do not develop.

From the [[people/broda-barnes-research|Broda Barnes research brief]], the more compact statement Peat made: "The circulatory complications of diabetes were avoided in Broda Barnes' diabetic patients, who received thyroid supplements. In diabetes, glucose is unable to enter cells, and the body's response resembles that of hypoglycemia, secreting adrenaline and mobilizing fats from storage. Since a certain amount of intracellular glucose is needed before the liver can form T3, diabetics, like hypoglycemics, or like anyone during starvation, are unable to activate the thyroid hormone." This is the mechanistic core of the Barnes observation. The diabetic liver cannot make T3. Without T3, every tissue progressively drops into a hypothyroid state. The hypothyroid state produces the complications. Supplementing thyroid bypasses the failure of hepatic T4-to-T3 conversion and prevents the hypothyroid tissue cascade.

Barnes's case series is not a randomized controlled trial; it is in his books (*Hypothyroidism: The Unsuspected Illness*, 1976) and decades of clinical writing. The mainstream diabetic-endocrinology literature has not engaged with the claim, in part because hypothyroidism diagnosis and treatment have been narrowed (TSH-only diagnosis, levothyroxine-only treatment) in ways Barnes anticipated and rejected. The claim is, strictly, anecdotal — but it is anecdote across thousands of patients across decades, in alignment with a mechanistic argument that holds together. The Peat-frame protocol treats Barnes's observation as the clinical anchor for the thyroid intervention. See [[substances/hormones/thyroid|thyroid]], [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroidism]], and [[protocols/hypothyroid-recovery|hypothyroid-recovery]].

## Tracking and monitoring

The mainstream complication-screening panel — annual dilated retinal exam, urinary microalbumin-to-creatinine ratio, monofilament foot exam, ECG and lipid panel, eGFR — is appropriate and should not be abandoned. The Peat-frame addition is the upstream metabolic signal the mainstream panel does not capture: body temperature on waking and after breakfast, resting pulse — the basic indicators of whether the cellular oxidation that prevents complications is recovering. Pulse should reach 75–85 bpm at rest in a well-fed warm adult; waking temperature 97.8–98.6 °F, midday 98.6 °F. If these improve across months, the long-term complication trajectory is improving regardless of what HbA1c is doing in the short term.

HbA1c is informative but secondary. The damage mainstream endocrinology attributes to glycation is, in Peat's reading, largely PUFA-derived lipid-peroxidation damage that happens to attach to the same proteins. A patient on a Peat-frame protocol may see HbA1c remain elevated for months while temperature, pulse, energy, and clinical symptoms all improve — and this can be the right trajectory. Stopping insulin or metformin to chase a lower HbA1c without addressing the upstream environment is dangerous.

Complication-specific signals worth adding: **vibration sense and monofilament testing** at the feet six-monthly (recovery of vibration sense is the most actionable nerve-restoration endpoint); **microalbumin-to-creatinine ratio** six-monthly (a falling ratio is the renal-permeability signal); **resting and postprandial pulse pressure** monthly (widening pulse pressure flags early diastolic dysfunction before echocardiogram); **cold extremities and skin-temperature gradient** daily by hand (a cold foot in a warm room is a vascular signal); **vision changes**, prompt examination — the transient retinopathy worsening on initiation of intensive insulin (Poulaki mechanism) is recognized; management is ophthalmologist coordination, not panic-driven insulin discontinuation.

The clinical signature of recovery is the hub's: temperature up, pulse up, energy up, cold extremities resolving, sleep improving, hair and nails growing better.

## Counter-arguments

DCCT (1993) and UKPDS (1998) are the two landmark trials that anchor the mainstream tight-glycemic-control orthodoxy. Both showed that intensive glycemic control reduced microvascular complications (retinopathy progression, microalbuminuria, neuropathy) in Type 1 and Type 2 populations — roughly 35–76 percent reductions across endpoints in DCCT, roughly 25 percent per percentage-point of HbA1c reduction in UKPDS. The macrovascular endpoints were more equivocal, with cardiovascular benefit emerging in the legacy-effect analyses. These are the data on which the mainstream complication-prevention paradigm rests.

The Peat-frame response is not to deny the findings. The microvascular endpoints did improve with intensive control. The response is to read the trials in light of the iatrogenesis argument. First, the DCCT comparator was conventional insulin therapy versus intensive insulin therapy — both arms were on insulin, and the question the trials answered was "how much insulin," not "is insulin the right intervention." Second, the early-worsening phenomenon (the Poulaki mechanism) shows that initiation of intensive therapy can transiently *worsen* retinopathy, indicating that the insulin-retina relationship is more complicated than long-term endpoints suggest. Third, the patients in both trials were on the standard high-PUFA Western diet and were given none of the upstream interventions — thyroid, PUFA reduction, adequate sugar, estrogen reduction — that the Peat-frame protocol prioritizes. The trials show that on a high-PUFA background diet, with insulin as the only metabolic intervention, more aggressive glucose-lowering reduces microvascular damage. They do not show that aggressive glucose-lowering is the optimal intervention given alternative upstream interventions; that comparison has never been made.

ACCORD (2008) is the trial that most directly complicates the mainstream story. The intensive-control arm (HbA1c target <6.0%) was stopped early because of a 22 percent increase in all-cause mortality and a 35 percent increase in cardiovascular mortality versus the standard-control arm. The intensive-arm patients had more hypoglycemic episodes, more weight gain, and worse cardiovascular outcomes. ACCORD is the trial result that fits most cleanly with the iatrogenesis argument: pushing glycemic control to near-normal with the standard pharmacological cascade in a high-risk diabetic population *increases* mortality. The mainstream response has been to attribute the result to overly aggressive management with too much hypoglycemia; the Peat-frame response is that the trial revealed the iatrogenic ceiling of pharmacological glycemic control.

The mainstream will also point at the consistent observational association between higher HbA1c and worse complications across cohort studies. The association is real, but consistent with multiple causal interpretations. In the Peat-frame reading, both the elevated HbA1c and the complications are downstream of the same upstream insults; reducing the HbA1c pharmacologically without addressing the upstream insults treats the marker without addressing the cause, and the complication trajectory continues. The DCCT and UKPDS results show that this pharmacological intervention does, on average, slow complication progression somewhat. The Peat-frame claim is that addressing the upstream insults would reduce complication progression substantially more — and the Broda Barnes clinical observation is the strongest existing evidence for that claim.

The fish-oil-for-diabetes recommendation deserves separate treatment because it is mainstream advice most directly contradicted by the experimental literature. The AHA recommendation that diabetic patients consume 1 g/day or more of EPA/DHA was based on extrapolation from cardiovascular-protection studies in non-diabetic populations. The diabetic-specific experimental literature (Hammes 1996, Vessby 1990, Vessby 1992) shows the opposite effect on diabetic endpoints. Recent large trials of omega-3 supplementation (VITAL, ASCEND, STRENGTH) have shown small or null effects on cardiovascular endpoints in diabetic subgroups. Pelikanova et al. *Metabolism* (2001) documented elevated highly-unsaturated omega-6 fatty acids in diabetic serum phospholipids — diabetic patients are already in a state of elevated PUFA exposure relative to controls. Adding more is not, on the experimental data, the right intervention.

## Data gaps

Missing from the Peat-frame case on complications, principally, is the prospective randomized trial of a Peat-frame intervention against mainstream intensive control with complication endpoints as the primary outcome. Barnes's clinical case series, compelling at the level of accumulated observation, is not in the form mainstream literature accepts as definitive. Hammes 1996 and the Vessby studies are animal and small-cohort human; they have not been replicated in large randomized trials. The mechanistic chain — chronic insulin therapy → counter-regulatory FFA mobilization → chronic peroxidative damage to capillary endothelium → late complications — is supported by individual studies (Poulaki 2002 most cleanly) but has not been demonstrated as the dominant causal pathway in human populations.

The PUFA-depletion timescale remains open. Adipose linoleic-acid half-life is two to three years; full depletion takes years to decades. The trajectory of complication regression under a fully implemented Peat-frame protocol — how much existing damage to retinas, nerves, and kidneys is reversible, on what timescale — is not documented in the detail a clinician needs to give a calibrated prognosis.

The integration with mainstream pharmacology is incomplete. Metformin, GLP-1 agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors all have documented effects on complication endpoints; how those drugs interact with a Peat-frame upstream intervention is not characterized. The clinical signature is "alongside, not instead of," but the optimal combination has not been studied.

The role of the gut [[mechanisms/endotoxin|endotoxin]] and dysbiosis cluster in diabetic complications, particularly diabetic foot infections and the broader inflammatory state, is suggestive but underspecified. The diabetic-cardiomyopathy literature has begun to converge with the metabolic-substrate-switching framework — FFA over-consumption in the diabetic myocardium, early diastolic dysfunction, the lipotoxic-cardiomyopathy concept — but the integration is partial and therapeutic implications are not yet drawn through to clinical recommendations.
