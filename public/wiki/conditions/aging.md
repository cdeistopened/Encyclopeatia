# Aging

Aging is not a genetic program ticking down to a predetermined conclusion. In Ray Peat's framework, it is an accumulating metabolic failure—the progressive result of impaired cellular respiration, rising [[estrogen]], stored [[PUFA]], and declining protective hormones. "Degenerative brain diseases of aging parallel cancer, arthritis, pancreatitis, hepatitis, kidney disease," Peat explained in a [[2012-05-18-politics-and-science-politics-science-autoimmune-and-movement-disorders|2012 interview]]—"the same processes in different proportions of energy supply and irritation." Aging is not many diseases but one process expressed in many tissues.

## The Metabolic Decline

At its core, aging represents a shift from efficient oxidative [[metabolism]] to inefficient, stress-driven fermentative metabolism. A young, healthy organism oxidizes glucose completely in the mitochondria, producing carbon dioxide, water, and abundant ATP. With age, this process degrades. Cells begin producing more [[lactic acid]] and less carbon dioxide—the same metabolic shift that characterizes [[cancer]], [[inflammation]], and [[stress]].

Peat identified this pattern early in his career. His newsletter "Energy, Entropy and Estrogens in Aging" outlined the thesis: aging is fundamentally a decline in oxidative metabolism, driven by the accumulation of substances that inhibit respiration. The parallels between aging and estrogen exposure—both producing the same metabolic signature—were not coincidental but causal.

The consequences cascade. As oxidative metabolism declines, cells take on water (edema), produce inflammatory mediators, and lose their differentiated functions. The brain, which has the highest metabolic rate of any organ and depends most critically on efficient respiration, shows these effects first—manifesting as cognitive decline, mood disorders, and eventually dementia. As Peat noted in a [[2022-01-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-january-2022-kmud|2022 episode]], the concept of "inflammaging" captures this: "cell structure damage, excitation, inflammation—the inability to make enough energy."

## PUFA: The Slow Poison

The accumulation of [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]] in tissues may be the single most important driver of aging. Unlike saturated fats, which are metabolically stable, polyunsaturated fats undergo spontaneous oxidation at body temperature, generating free radicals, aldehydes, and the cross-linked proteins that form [[lipofuscin]]—the brown "age pigment" that accumulates in skin, brain, heart, and every other tissue.

This accumulation is relentless and progressive. As Peat explained in his PUFA research, "by the time a person is 45, the body is really soaked in polyunsaturated fats." The brain tells the story most clearly: "If you look at the aging brain, the amount of PUFA steadily increases from the so-called deficient state at birth up to a heavy overload state in old age and dementia." A healthy infant's brain is highly saturated; a demented elder's brain is loaded with oxidized DHA and arachidonic acid.

The damage compounds because PUFA suppresses [[thyroid]] function at multiple points—blocking hormone secretion, transport, and cellular reception. As thyroid declines, metabolism slows further, and the body becomes less capable of clearing the stored PUFA. This is the vicious cycle of aging: PUFA suppresses metabolism, and suppressed metabolism allows more PUFA to accumulate.

Peat's newsletter on lungs, shock, and aging noted that the omega-3 fats promoted as health supplements are actually "the most easily oxidized of the common fatty acids. With stress, such as premature birth, and with aging, these fats produce free radical decomposition products at a high rate." The supplement industry's promotion of fish oil to the elderly accelerates the very process it claims to prevent.

## The Estrogen Shift

Around age 40, most people reach their lifetime peak of [[estrogen]] relative to [[progesterone]]. This shift defines the transition from youth to decline. As Peat explained in a [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|2013 episode on aging and energy]], "almost everyone around the age of 40 has the highest ratio of estrogen to progesterone."

The mechanism is self-reinforcing. As progesterone production declines, estrogen accumulates in tissues because progesterone is needed to release it. Blood tests can be misleading—serum estrogen may appear to fall while tissue estrogen actually rises. "The fact that you don't find it in the blood is simply because estrogen sticks inside the cells in proportion to the deficiency of progesterone," Peat explained.

Estrogen drives aging through the same mechanisms by which it promotes cancer: impairing cytochrome oxidase, shifting cells from oxidative to fermentative metabolism, promoting water uptake and cellular excitation, and activating aromatase in every tissue. After menopause, when ovarian production declines, estrogen formation shifts to fat cells, brain tissue, and inflamed tissues throughout the body.

The parallel extends to men. As testosterone declines and thyroid function slows, more testosterone gets converted to estrogen through aromatase. In a [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 episode]], Peat described how "slow thyroid shifts testosterone to estrogen conversion—that's where degenerative problems start."

## Iron, Calcium, and the Degenerative Triad

Beyond PUFA and estrogen, Peat identified iron accumulation and calcium dysregulation as critical aging factors. Iron accumulates steadily with age (especially in men and post-menopausal women), and excess iron catalyzes the oxidation of PUFA, generating free radicals and accelerating lipofuscin formation. Peat discussed these themes extensively in his work on estrogen, iron, and degenerative aging.

Calcium, normally maintained within cells at extremely low concentrations by active energy-dependent processes, begins accumulating in soft tissues as metabolism declines. This [[calcification]] stiffens blood vessels, damages joints, and impairs organ function. The process is not caused by dietary calcium excess—Peat was a strong advocate of adequate calcium intake—but by the failure of cellular energy systems to maintain proper calcium gradients.

The triad of PUFA oxidation, estrogen dominance, and iron-calcium dysregulation creates a self-amplifying degenerative process. Each element worsens the others: estrogen increases PUFA uptake, PUFA oxidation products activate inflammatory iron pathways, and declining energy production allows calcium to flood cells.

## The Free Radical Theory—Corrected

Denham Harman proposed in the 1950s that free radicals cause aging, and this theory has dominated gerontology for decades. Peat accepted the observation while rejecting the mainstream interpretation. The problem is not free radicals per se but the substances that generate them inappropriately—primarily polyunsaturated fats.

As Peat's newsletter on determinism versus process thinking explained, Harman "proposed that a high rate of living shortens the lifespan in the same way that ionizing radiation or a toxic excess of oxygen does, by accelerating the formation of free oxygen radicals that cause cumulative damage." But Harman's theory was corrupted by the antioxidant supplement industry into a simplistic narrative: take antioxidants, neutralize free radicals, slow aging.

Peat's correction is that the source of harmful free radicals matters more than their neutralization. A person with low tissue PUFA generates far fewer destructive free radicals regardless of antioxidant supplementation. The strategy is not to fight oxidative damage after it occurs but to remove the substrate that makes it inevitable.

## Lactate, the Brain, and Progressive Deterioration

The shift from oxidative to fermentative metabolism increases lactic acid production throughout the body. As Peat described in a newsletter on lactate metabolism, "chronic exposure to a slight excess of lactate will cause progressive deterioration of blood vessels in the brain as it does in other parts of the body." The damage is cumulative and silent—years of slightly elevated lactate gradually destroys the vascular infrastructure that the brain depends on.

This explains why cognitive decline often accelerates after periods of stress, illness, or starvation—events that spike lactate production. It also explains the protective effect of adequate dietary sugar, which keeps lactate production low by providing glucose for direct oxidation rather than fermentation.

Before clinical dementia develops, the condition Peat referenced as "reductive stress" is already measurable—a state where tissues are less well oxidized, amino acids are being depleted to fuel gluconeogenesis, and ammonia buildup promotes further glycolytic metabolism. The conventional framing of Alzheimer's as a genetic disease of protein misfolding misses that the misfolding is caused by the metabolic environment—particularly the accumulation of oxidized PUFA in neural tissue.

## Reversibility: The Anti-Aging Framework

If aging is metabolic rather than genetic, it is at least partially reversible. Peat's approach centers on restoring the conditions that maintain oxidative metabolism:

**[[Thyroid]] support** is primary. Adequate thyroid hormone drives every step of oxidative metabolism, from glucose uptake to mitochondrial respiration to carbon dioxide production. Many people over 40 are functionally hypothyroid without meeting the narrow diagnostic criteria of standard tests.

**[[Progesterone]]** opposes estrogen's aging effects, supports mitochondrial respiration, and protects the brain. Women who have had multiple pregnancies—accumulating residual progesterone-producing cells—live significantly longer on average.

**PUFA elimination** removes the substrate for lipid peroxidation and age pigment formation. The tissue turnover takes approximately four years for complete replacement, but benefits begin accumulating immediately.

**Adequate sugar and frequent meals** prevent the stress-hormone mobilization of stored PUFA. When blood sugar drops, cortisol and adrenaline rise to liberate fatty acids—if those fatty acids are polyunsaturated, the damage is renewed. Keeping glucose available suppresses this cycle.

**[[Aspirin]]** and **[[vitamin E]]** provide acute protection against the PUFA and estrogen already in tissues—aspirin by blocking inflammatory prostaglandins, vitamin E by interrupting lipid peroxidation chains.

**Light exposure** supports mitochondrial function. Peat emphasized that adequate light, particularly red and near-infrared wavelengths, supports cytochrome oxidase activity—the same enzyme that estrogen and PUFA impair.

The aim is not to "stop the clock" but to shift the metabolic balance back toward the oxidative, high-energy state that characterizes youth. As Peat demonstrated through his framework, aging and its diseases are not separate conditions requiring separate treatments but manifestations of a single metabolic regression that can be addressed at its root.
