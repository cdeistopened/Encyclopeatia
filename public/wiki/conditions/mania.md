# Mania (Manic Phase)

> Parent: [[bipolar]]. See also [[mental-health-cluster]].

## Bottom line

Despite the surface presentation (high energy, racing thoughts, reduced sleep need, euphoria, grandiosity), mania in Peat's frame is a **stress state**, not a high-energy state. It is driven by elevated adrenalin and cortisol, not by genuine cellular energy availability. The clue Peat gives is that the depressive phase shares the same adrenergic profile — both phases of the cycle have **high morning body temperature** that falls after breakfast, opposite to simple hypothyroidism.

## The energy-vs-excitation distinction

A key Peat lens: high subjective activation does not mean high cellular ATP. Adrenalin can drive movement, speech, and racing thought while cells are simultaneously running on glycolysis (lactate), depleting glycogen, and underproducing CO₂. This is the same pattern as panic, withdrawal-induced agitation, and hyperthyroid storm — high signal, low energy.

> "Hallucination suggests that there is distortion in their meaning, probably when energy isn't being produced as fast as it's used." — Peat, *Hallucinations* email

Mania's hallucinatory or grandiose-thinking edge fits this: signal-to-energy mismatch.

## Specific levers for the manic phase

Most of these come from extrapolating Peat's general frame to the acute manic state. Read them as hypotheses, not protocol — acute mania needs psychiatric care.

1. **Stabilize blood sugar** — frequent salty/sugary meals; the Peat-prescribed "salt and sugar lower adrenalin" rule is directly applicable to a manic patient running on adrenergic overdrive.
2. **Sleep restoration** — increased body temperature improves deep slow-wave sleep; hot bath, warm feet, salt + carbs at bedtime. Sleep deprivation worsens mania in mainstream clinical observation, which agrees with Peat.
3. **Anti-adrenergic minerals** — sodium adequacy, magnesium (calcium antagonist, retained in proportion to thyroid activity), calcium (anti-fermentation, anti-PTH, anti-stress).
4. **Anti-serotonin support** — progesterone (Peat: "Progesterone, like LSD, can inhibit the firing of serotonergic nerves"); cyproheptadine where appropriate.
5. **Reduce endotoxin** — fermentable fiber → gut serotonin → CNS effects; daily raw carrot is Peat's standard tool here.
6. **Avoid amphetamines / cocaine / SSRIs** — all push the same axis Peat sees as the problem.
7. **Lithium** — works in the Peat frame as a sodium-mimic that suppresses adrenal adrenalin release and antagonizes serotonin (see below).

## Why lithium fits the Peat frame for mania

Peat's mechanism for lithium is unusually friendly to its psychiatric use:

- Sodium-analog with stronger electrical charge → mineral metabolism stabilizer
- "Chronic consumption of lithium blocks the release of adrenalin from the adrenal glands"
- Extensive antiserotonin effects — "inhibiting its release from some sites, and blocking its actions at others"
- Forms a complex with ammonia, which mimics serotonin in fatigue
- "Has been demonstrated to restore the energy metabolism of mitochondria"

All of these match Peat's general therapeutic targets. So lithium is one of the few mainstream psychiatric drugs Peat does not critique. His only caveat is the diagnostic-first stance: before high-dose lithium, check whether thyroid + nutrition + mineral metabolism are correctable.

## What Peat would *not* do for mania

- **SSRIs** — can trigger mania in bipolar patients (mainstream-acknowledged); Peat's broader serotonin critique reinforces this.
- **L-tryptophan / 5-HTP** — direct serotonin precursors.
- **L-tyrosine** — pushes dopamine, contraindicated in active mania per even mainstream sources.
- **Long fasting / very-low-carb** — pushes free fatty acids, cortisol, adrenalin; opposite of stabilizing.
- **High-PUFA diet** — same logic.

## Counter-arguments

- Acute mania is a psychiatric emergency. The Peat lever set is appropriate as adjunct, not as monotherapy.
- The "mania = stress state" framing, while internally consistent, is not how mainstream psychiatry models the disorder; it does not account for genetic loading or specific responsiveness to lithium and atypical antipsychotics.
- Manic patients often refuse food and sleep; Peat's "salty/sugary meals + warmth" advice presumes patient cooperation.
- Lithium's narrow therapeutic window means "low-dose lithium as a nutrient" is meaningfully different from "lithium as a mood stabilizer," and Peat's permissive framing risks blurring this.

## Data gaps

- No formal study of high-morning-temperature pattern as mania-vs-depression-vs-hypothyroid discriminator.
- No clinical evidence that progesterone or pregnenolone alone can resolve manic episodes.
- Acetazolamide (which Peat lists for psychosis) has tiny scattered case-report evidence in mania, no RCT.
