# Hashimoto's Thyroiditis

Hashimoto's thyroiditis is the most common autoimmune diagnosis in the developed world and the most common reason a younger woman walks out of an endocrinologist's office with a lifetime prescription for synthetic thyroxine and the news that her own immune system is destroying a healthy organ. Ray Peat rejected almost every step of that summary. In his framework, Hashimoto's is not primary, not properly autoimmune in the sense most clinicians use the word, and the antibodies are not the cause of the damage. They are the smoke from a fire that has been burning, often for years, fueled by chronic [[tsh|TSH]] elevation, excess iodine, gut-derived [[endotoxin]], [[estrogen]] dominance, [[selenium]] deficiency, and the membrane fragility produced by [[pufa|polyunsaturated fats]]. The productive work is upstream.

The framework inverts the textbook causal direction. The textbook says a malfunctioning immune system attacks thyroid peroxidase and thyroglobulin, destroying the gland. Peat said a gland under metabolic and hormonal stress leaks its own proteins, and the immune system performs its ordinary janitorial function on the debris. The titer rises because cleanup is ongoing. Stop the upstream injury, and the cleanup tapers off. This explains why women have five to ten times the rate men do, why patients with high titers can feel fine on labs, why antibodies persist for years before clinical failure, why high-dose iodine can flip a latent case into a flare, and why suppressing TSH with thyroid medication often brings antibodies down over months.

Pair with [[hypothyroidism]] for the broader condition, [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for the lifestyle layer, and [[thyroid-dosing]] for medication titration.

## TPO and Thyroglobulin Antibodies: What They Mean and What They Don't

Anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) and anti-thyroglobulin (anti-Tg) define a Hashimoto's diagnosis. TPO attaches iodine to tyrosine residues inside the gland to produce T4 and T3. Thyroglobulin is the scaffold protein on which iodination happens. Both live inside the follicle and neither belongs in circulation. Modern endocrinology reads their presence as evidence the immune system has identified the thyroid as foreign. Peat read it differently. "Usually the antibodies just mean that the thyroid gland is inflamed," he wrote in [[autoimmune-diseases|a direct email exchange]], "and increased TSH can be responsible for that." The titer is a backward-looking inflammation marker, not a forward-looking disease marker. Anti-TPO appears at detectable levels in ten to fifteen percent of adult women; most are euthyroid and remain so.

The cross-reactivity findings undermine the primary-autoimmune model from another direction. In [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|the 2013 KMUD interview]] that gave this article most of its scaffolding, Peat described work showing "a great cross-reaction between thyroid antibodies and joint antibodies. That explains why people with thyroiditis and hypothyroidism so often have arthritis and joint problems." If the antibody were a precise weapon aimed at thyroid tissue, it would not also bind joint cartilage. The cleanest demonstration of the housekeeping interpretation comes from rabbit joint experiments Peat cited: foreign cartilage produces few antibodies, while structurally damaged native cartilage produces a terrific immune reaction. Damage, not foreignness, is the driver. "Any organ that gets drained or overworked tends to leak some of its proteins. The immune system's function is largely to clean up any mess."

In [[2019-12-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-top-contrarian-on-health|his 2019 *One Radio Network* interview]] Peat framed the historical lineage as Metchnikoff versus Ehrlich: Ehrlich worked for the chemical industry and created the antibody-as-weapon theory; Metchnikoff, an embryologist, saw the immune system as maintenance, removing damaged tissues. Peat sided with Metchnikoff. The antibody is part of repair.

## The Iodine-Overload Trigger

The most consequential clinical implication of Peat's framework is his warning about iodine. The popular alternative-health treatment — Brownstein, Abraham, the "Iodine Project" — is milligram-range supplementation: Iodoral at 12.5 mg, Lugol's at 12.5 to 50 mg, occasionally up to 100 mg. The US RDA is 150 micrograms. These doses are several hundred to a thousand times that figure. Peat considered them dangerous in general and specifically dangerous for anyone with thyroiditis. "Lugol's solution is sometimes helpful for an inflammation, but it's risky when there might be a thyroid problem. Short term use of iodide is safe at a few milligrams per day, but chronic intake of even one mg per day increases the risk of thyroiditis." See [[iodine]] for the full dose-response.

The mechanism is the Wolff-Chaikoff effect. When iodide concentrations inside the follicle rise above a threshold, the gland's hormone-synthesis machinery shuts down as a defensive reflex. In a healthy gland the shutdown is transient. In an inflamed gland — already infiltrated with lymphocytes, already producing antibodies, already operating near its capacity — the shutdown can become semi-permanent, and the iodine flood directly damages the gland by overdriving its peroxidase chemistry. In the 2013 KMUD interview Peat described the Japanese picture: "Many people in Japan eat so much iodine that they get thyroiditis from blocking the functions of the gland. That classically results in increasing the TSH to overcome the blockage by excess iodine." High-iodine populations show elevated rates of Hashimoto's compared with iodine-sufficient-but-not-saturated controls.

Risk is not uniform. A thyroid already stressed by estrogen excess, PUFA-induced membrane fragility, low selenium, and chronic TSH drive flares when iodine arrives in milligram quantities. A thyroid in good metabolic order absorbs the insult better. The practical conclusion: if antibodies are elevated on a recent panel, or family history of autoimmune thyroid disease exists, keep iodine to the trace amounts in iodized salt, dairy, and occasional seafood. Skip kelp, dulse, and seaweed products. Do not start Lugol's. The window Peat considered safe for chronic intake was roughly 150 to 1,000 micrograms per day.

## The Gut-Thyroid Axis

The intestinal contribution runs through two mechanisms: gut-derived endotoxin driving systemic inflammation, and absorption of antigenic protein fragments through a permeable wall.

Bacterial endotoxin — the lipopolysaccharide shed from gram-negative gut bacteria — is the centerpiece of Peat's disease model. In the [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-hormones-behind-inflammation|2025 KMUD interview on inflammation]] he tied it to autoimmunity: "If you're overloaded with estrogen, your blood vessels tend to be leaky. That increases the risk of exposure to endotoxin, which intensifies that leakiness and affects all of your cells." Once endotoxin reaches the liver, the inflammatory cascade runs — Kupffer cells release cytokines, aromatase up-regulates (converting androgens to estrogen), serotonin and histamine rise. See [[endotoxin]].

The second mechanism is intestinal permeability. "Hypothyroidism is one thing that commonly causes leaky gut," Peat wrote in [[autoimmune-diseases|the autoimmune email file]], "as well as leaky liver, muscles, heart, thyroid gland, etc. The immune system reacts to the leakiness, and although it might be an effect, rather than a cause, the presence of antibodies is sometimes said to show autoimmunity." Hypothyroidism slows membrane maintenance including the intestinal barrier. Antigenic fragments cross into the bloodstream. The immune system responds. Some antibodies cross-react with thyroid proteins. Hashimoto's, in this reading, is partly a downstream effect of hypothyroidism, not only its cause.

Gut work belongs in any serious thyroiditis protocol — daily raw carrot salad with vinegar or coconut oil, well-cooked mushrooms, bowel motility, elimination of inflammatory foods, occasional [[activated-charcoal|activated charcoal]] for acute endotoxin loads. See [[gut-restoration]] and [[endotoxin-reduction]]. On gluten, Peat acknowledged the contribution without making it central: "Celiac disease has been increasing right along with the exposure to estrogen." Gluten is a real irritant for some. The prime movers remain estrogen, endotoxin, PUFA, and the metabolic environment that lets the gut barrier fail.

## Selenium and Glutathione Protection

The thyroid is the most selenium-dense tissue in the body by weight. The deiodinases use selenium to convert T4 to active T3. Glutathione peroxidase uses selenium to neutralize the hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxides the gland generates as byproducts of its own iodination chemistry. Without adequate selenium, the gland's antioxidant defenses fail; lipid peroxides accumulate; membrane integrity declines; and the conditions for chronic inflammation and follicular damage are established.

Inland soils across the United States, central Europe, parts of New Zealand, and much of China are selenium-depleted. Several randomized trials in deficient populations have shown 200 mcg per day reduces anti-TPO titers in Hashimoto's patients over six to twelve months — moderate effect, reproducible. Peat's framework accommodates it: the supplement restores antioxidant capacity, damaged proteins clear faster, steady-state cleanup antibody production falls.

Six medium oysters supply 150 to 200 mcg of selenium along with zinc, copper, iodine, and B12. Brazil nuts deliver a day's worth in one or two nuts but vary widely between batches. The supplement, 100 to 200 mcg per day of selenomethionine, is the backstop. Doses above 400 mcg per day for extended periods produce toxicity. See [[selenium]]. Glutathione recycling depends on selenium-containing enzymes and runs low in hypothyroid patients; the food template in [[hypothyroid-recovery]] supports synthesis through gelatin (glycine), eggs (cysteine), and B vitamins.

## Peat's Framework: PUFA, Estrogen, Cold, Chronic Stress

Beyond iodine, gut, and selenium, Peat located the proximate drivers in four overlapping systemic problems.

**PUFA-induced membrane fragility.** As linoleic acid from seed oils accumulates in follicular membranes, cells become more susceptible to oxidative damage from the gland's own peroxide production, more prone to protein leakage, more sensitive to Wolff-Chaikoff suppression. The iodolipid signaling Peat cited — the alpha-iodohexadecanal the gland forms when it iodinates fatty acids — depends on whatever fats are available. A PUFA-loaded gland produces more pro-inflammatory mediators. Eliminating PUFA and allowing months-to-years of membrane turnover lowers risk. See [[pufa]].

**Estrogen dominance.** Women have five to ten times the male rate; Peat pointed at estrogen. "Thyroid cells contain estrogen 'receptors,' and estrogen inhibits thyroid secretion," he wrote in [[thyroid-misconceptions|*Thyroid: Misconceptions* (1993)]], "and excess estrogen is involved in most 'autoimmune' disease." Estrogen interferes with the liver's T4-to-T3 conversion, suppresses hormone release at the gland, raises inflammatory eicosanoids, increases intestinal permeability, and acts on T and B cells to shift antibody production toward inflammatory patterns. The female predominance is the ratio of estrogen exposure, not a sex-linked autoimmune vulnerability. See [[estrogen]] and [[estrogen-detox]].

**Cold and chronic stress.** Cold exposure raises cortisol and adrenaline, lowers T3 conversion, worsens every variable on the Hashimoto's picture. Cold tolerance is a *result* of recovery, not a method for producing it. See [[cold-plunge-dont-do-it]]. Cortisol and adrenaline also suppress TSH release, inhibit deiodinase conversion, and antagonize hormone at the receptor. A patient grinding through a high-cortisol life will not see antibodies fall regardless of how careful the diet is. See [[cortisol]] and the stress pillar in [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

## Peat's Treatment Protocol

The Peat-aligned treatment reads nothing like the standard endocrinology playbook. No levothyroxine monotherapy at TSH-targeted doses, no immunosuppressant, no antibody-targeted intervention. The protocol is upstream, layered, and patient. [[hypothyroid-recovery]] documents the full lifestyle layer; [[thyroid-dosing]] documents medication titration. The Hashimoto's-specific overlay:

**Suppress TSH protectively with adequate thyroid hormone.** "When TSH is too high for a long time, it causes inflammation in the gland, and the antibodies are in reaction to that. A combination of T4 and T3 is usually all that's needed; it usually takes a few months after suppressing TSH for the antibodies to decrease." The medication is desiccated natural thyroid (Armour, Cynoplus, NP Thyroid) or a T4-T3 combination, dosed in microgram portions with food, titrated against waking and afternoon temperature and pulse rather than TSH alone. Levothyroxine monotherapy can work for men and for women whose liver conversion is unimpaired, but the typical Hashimoto's patient — a woman with elevated estrogen interfering with T4-to-T3 conversion — is left tissue-hypothyroid despite normal TSH. See [[thyroid-dosing]].

**Selenium, 100 to 200 mcg daily.** Selenomethionine or oysters, ocean fish, modest Brazil nuts. The supplement with the best evidence base for lowering anti-TPO.

**Gelatin, one heaping tablespoon daily.** Glycine balances muscle-meat amino acids, supports liver glucuronidation (which clears excess estrogen), damps the inflammatory edge of high-protein eating. See [[gelatin]].

**The anti-stress diet.** Whole milk, orange juice, ripe fruit, eggs, weekly liver, white rice or potatoes with butter, coconut oil, gelatin, iodized salt to appetite, a bedtime snack. PUFA eliminated. Raw goitrogens cooked. Soy avoided. See [[anti-stress-diet]] and [[core-diet-protocol]].

**Gut decontamination.** Daily raw carrot salad, well-cooked mushrooms, bowel motility, no industrial seed oils, no undercooked starches. See [[gut-restoration]] and [[endotoxin-reduction]].

**Progesterone, pregnenolone, DHEA for estrogen-driven cases.** Peat in [[thyroid-misconceptions|*Thyroid: Misconceptions*]]: "Supplemental thyroid so easily normalizes estrogen and the immune system that it, with progesterone and pregnenolone and DHEA, should be considered as the cure for autoimmune diseases, including thyroiditis." Topical natural progesterone in modest physiological doses, titrated against cycle phase and symptom. See [[progesterone]].

**Minimize iodine if antibodies are high.** Iodized salt and occasional seafood are fine. Kelp, dulse, seaweed snacks, Iodoral, and Lugol's are not. See [[iodine]].

Antibody titers fall over months, not weeks. Symptoms often improve first. The marker of success is the morning temperature, the resting pulse, the warmth of the hands, the steadiness of mood.

## The Iodine Controversy

The split within alternative health on iodine is one place Peat collides hard with another internally coherent contrarian position. The high-dose camp — Brownstein, Abraham, the "Iodine Project" — holds the modern population is profoundly deficient and the right treatment is milligram-range Lugol's or Iodoral. They cite Japanese coastal diets, marshal case reports, and include selenium co-supplementation to buffer Wolff-Chaikoff risk.

Peat differed on every premise. Iodine deficiency was largely solved in the US by iodized salt in 1924. The Japanese coastal data is evidence of *thyroiditis* risk, not a model to emulate, since high-iodine prefectures show elevated rates of Hashimoto's. Wolff-Chaikoff is a real chronic risk in supplementing populations, not an acute transient. The asymmetry of error mattered: refusing unnecessary high-dose iodine costs nothing; taking it into a vulnerable gland can convert a latent condition to overt disease. For a patient already showing antibodies, the recommendation was unambiguous: stay inside the dietary window. The selenium-and-thyroid-hormone-and-anti-estrogen route does the same work with less flare risk.

## Tracking Signal

Labs at baseline and every three to six months: anti-TPO, anti-Tg, TSH, free T4, free T3, reverse T3. Bedside, at least twice weekly morning and afternoon: oral or armpit temperature, resting pulse, finger and toe temperature, plus subjective markers (sleep depth, dream recall, hair, mood, libido, cold tolerance).

Bedside markers move first. Within two to six weeks, morning temperature creeps up by half a degree, resting pulse stabilizes, hands warm. TSH falls within weeks if medication is in play. Free T3 rises slowly. Reverse T3 falls as stress hormones come down. Antibody titers are slowest — six to twelve months, sometimes longer. Patients who measure only antibodies get discouraged and quit; patients who track bedside markers see recovery in real time.

The most informative single measurement is the temperature-and-pulse response to breakfast. The ideal: morning temperature low or moderate, rising by a degree after a carbohydrate-and-protein breakfast, while the pulse comes down slightly from its overnight adrenaline-elevated state. The opposite — high morning temperature falling after breakfast, pulse staying high — indicates adrenaline-driven warmth masking a more depleted state. See the diagnostic scenarios in [[hypothyroidism]].

## What to Avoid

**High-dose iodine.** Iodoral, Lugol's at milligram doses, kelp tablets, seaweed as daily food, fucus, "thyroid support" supplements containing milligram-range iodine. Stay inside 150 to 1,000 micrograms per day from iodized salt, dairy, and occasional seafood.

**Soy in any form.** The 1991 Ishizuki study Peat cited showed thirty grams of soybeans per day for three months produced TSH elevation and goiter in healthy adults. For a thyroiditis patient, soy is one of the most provocative foods in the supermarket. See [[soy]].

**Raw cruciferous vegetables daily.** Raw cabbage, raw kale, raw broccoli, raw cauliflower. The goitrogenic compounds are largely deactivated by cooking. The kale-smoothie habit reliably drives Hashimoto's the wrong direction.

**Chronic cold exposure.** Cold plunges, ice baths, cold showers. The catecholamine surge feels invigorating; the sustained cost is a setback.

**Fasting and skipped meals.** Twelve to twenty-four hours without food shuts down T4-to-T3 conversion as a survival response. Eat every three to four waking hours.

**Long-duration cardio.** Marathon training, HIIT, two-a-days, fasted morning cardio. Long cortisol exposure accelerates thyroid hormone breakdown.

**Industrial seed oils and fish oil.** Months-to-years of strict avoidance allow tissue PUFA to slowly clear. See [[pufa]] and [[seed-oils]].

## Counter-Arguments

**Modern endocrinology.** The dominant model: Hashimoto's is a genuine HLA-linked autoimmune disease in which T and B cells mount a coordinated attack; treatment is levothyroxine replacement titrated to normalize TSH. Supported by histological lymphocytic infiltration, HLA-DR3/DR5 associations, family clustering, twin concordance, the response of severe Graves' to immunosuppression, and a large levothyroxine literature. The strongest mainstream critique of Peat is that he discounted genetic and histological evidence in favor of a metabolic story that does not by itself account for the genetic associations. The Peat-aligned response is that the loci confer susceptibility, not destiny, and susceptibility expresses only with upstream drivers (estrogen, PUFA, iodine excess, endotoxin). The genes set the threshold; the environment crosses it. Defensible synthesis, not knockdown rebuttal.

**Immunosuppressant therapy.** Some endocrinologists treat aggressive Hashimoto's with low-dose naltrexone or broader immunosuppression. Patients report falling antibodies and stable thyroid function. The Peat-aligned reading is that immunosuppression suppresses the cleanup symptom rather than addressing the cause, and that underlying gland damage continues silently. Long-term outcomes are not well studied.

**The Brownstein high-dose-iodine camp.** Discussed above. They have a coherent literature, case reports, and selenium co-supplementation. The Peat-aligned position acknowledges some patients tolerate high-dose iodine and disputes the safety of the default for the population. Not resolvable from first principles. A patient leaning toward the high-dose route should start with extensive selenium supplementation, careful antibody monitoring, and willingness to stop if antibodies rise.

**Molecular mimicry / gluten-driven model.** The functional-medicine literature (especially Kharrazian) emphasizes structural similarity between gluten fragments, gut bacterial proteins, and thyroid proteins as the trigger. The Peat framework overlaps in the gut-permeability section and disputes the centrality of gluten, since most gluten-tolerant adults never develop Hashimoto's despite lifelong exposure. The mimicry contribution is probably real for a subset, not the prime mover.

**"Antibodies are real damage."** Critics argue that anti-TPO antibodies actively destroy thyroid tissue through complement-mediated cytotoxicity and ADCC. The cellular evidence for active antibody-mediated destruction is genuine. The Peat-aligned response is that antibody-mediated destruction, where it occurs, is the continuation of cleanup of already-damaged tissue rather than the initiating insult — a subtle distinction the experimental literature does not cleanly resolve.

Peat's framework is more internally coherent than its critics often credit and makes correct predictions in places the mainstream model does not (iodine sensitivity, female predominance, joint cross-reactivity, the antibody-to-overt-disease lag, responsiveness to anti-estrogen and selenium). It has empirical gaps (genetic associations, histological infiltrates, partial response of severe cases to immunosuppression). It is a working framework, not a closed case.

## Data Gaps

The dose-response between iodine intake and thyroiditis risk is described qualitatively in Peat's correspondence but not quantitatively at the population level. The exact threshold, the modifying effect of selenium status, and the role of pre-existing antibody titers are not well characterized.

The role of viral triggers, especially Epstein-Barr, is acknowledged in mainstream literature and partially dismissed in the Peat framework as a downstream consequence of the same inflammation that drives autoimmunity. Whether viral reactivation is a real independent trigger modifiable by antiviral approaches is unresolved.

The remission rate on a fully implemented Peat protocol has not been documented systematically. Individual cases abound; no register tracks success rates across a defined population.

The specific contribution of PUFA load to Hashimoto's risk, as opposed to its contribution to hypothyroidism in general, is inferred from biochemistry rather than measured in human studies.

The interaction between thyroid medication choice (T4 alone, T4+T3, desiccated) and antibody trajectory is not well studied. Peat's framework predicts desiccated thyroid or T4+T3 should outperform levothyroxine monotherapy for antibody reduction in typical female patients; case reports support that, but no large RCT has tested it.

**Safety note.** Adjunct framework, not a replacement for medical care. Severe hypothyroidism, especially with cardiac symptoms or pregnancy, requires monitored medication. Patients on existing thyroid medication should not change doses without physician supervision.
