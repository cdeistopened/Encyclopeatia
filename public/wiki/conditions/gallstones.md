# Gallstones

The orthodox story places cholesterol at the center. Bile becomes "supersaturated," crystals nucleate, stones form, the storage organ gets removed — roughly 600,000 cholecystectomies a year in the United States, one of the most common adult operations. The textbook risk factors ("female, fat, forty, fertile, fair") are presented as fixed demographics rather than as the signature of a metabolic state.

Peat's framework re-reads the same observations. Cholesterol is in the stone because the stone is made of cholesterol that fell out of solution — not because the patient was carrying too much of it. The five-F demographic is the demographic of estrogen dominance and hypothyroidism. The supersaturation is downstream of a gallbladder that is not contracting — a [[bile]] that sits, concentrates, and crystallizes because nothing moves it out. Surgery removes the storage organ; it does not address the cholestasis that produced the stones, which is why the symptoms so often persist as *post-cholecystectomy syndrome*.

For the upstream organ see [[liver]]; for the protective hormone, [[progesterone]]; for the driver, [[estrogen]]; for the metabolic ceiling, [[hypothyroidism]]; for the membrane-stiffening driver, [[pufa]]. The protocol layer lives at [[liver-restoration]] and [[estrogen-detox]].

**Safety up front.** Acute cholecystitis — fever, severe right-upper-quadrant pain unrelieved by position change, vomiting, jaundice, gallstone pancreatitis — is a surgical emergency. None of what follows applies to that situation. This article concerns the years of upstream physiology that produce the stones, and the post-acute management of patients whose stones are known but quiescent.

## Bile composition and stone formation

Bile carries [[cholesterol]], bile acids (cholic and chenodeoxycholic, synthesized from cholesterol), phospholipids (mainly phosphatidylcholine, which keeps cholesterol micellar), conjugated bilirubin, conjugated [[estrogen]] and other hormones the liver is excreting, and the Phase II metabolites that leave the hepatocyte through the canalicular membrane.

Cholesterol is essentially insoluble in water. Bile keeps it dissolved only because the bile acids and phospholipids hold it inside mixed micelles. Three things have to be in proportion: enough bile acids and phospholipids relative to the cholesterol load, enough flow to keep bile moving, and enough contractile rhythm to empty the gallbladder periodically.

Cholesterol stones form when one or more of these go wrong. The cholesterol-to-bile-acid ratio rises (cholesterol secretion up, or bile acid synthesis down). The bile sits long enough to concentrate (the gallbladder normally concentrates bile five- to ten-fold by reabsorbing water). Crystals nucleate, grow, aggregate. Cholesterol stones account for roughly 80% of cases in industrialized populations; pigment stones (calcium bilirubinate) and brown stones (from bacterial deconjugation) involve different physiology and are not this article's main concern.

The bile chemistry that produces cholesterol stones — high cholesterol, low bile acid, low motility — is the chemistry of estrogen excess and hypothyroidism, accelerated by PUFA loading, low-fat dieting, and prolonged fasting.

## Why women outnumber men

The two- to three-to-one female-to-male ratio is one of the most reliable findings in gastroenterology. The orthodox literature attributes it to "hormones" without specifying which ones. Peat was specific.

From the 2014 *Comparison of Progesterone and Estrogen* newsletter: "Excess estrogen (and decreased thyroid hormone) linked with breast/cervical cancer, tripled rate of gallbladder disease." Tripled. Estrogen does three relevant things at the gallbladder.

First, it **raises biliary cholesterol** — upregulating hepatic HMG-CoA reductase and cholesterol secretion into bile, while suppressing CYP7A1 (the rate-limiting enzyme of bile acid synthesis). The bile leaves the liver already supersaturated relative to its bile-acid content.

Second, it **slows contraction.** From the 2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on skin cancer and cholesterol: "The gallbladder is very sensitive to low thyroid function or high estrogen in relation to progesterone. Progesterone relaxes it; estrogen tends to cause spasms. The sick reaction to fats is because the thresholds are lowered by the high estrogen-to-progesterone ratio. The key to it is almost always low thyroid function." Estrogen-driven spasm and progesterone-deficient relaxation produce a gallbladder that cannot empty smoothly and is hyperreactive to fatty stimuli. The "fat intolerance" women describe in their thirties and forties is a clinical sign of this state, not an indication to remove fat from the diet.

Third, estrogen **increases biliary inflammatory susceptibility.** From the 2019 *Jodellefit* digestion interview: "In the gallbladder, receiving chemicals like estrogen and endotoxin causes inflammation. If estrogen is high — such as in pregnancy — cholesterol tends to accumulate, forming sludge or stones. Thyroid deficiency is extremely closely associated with gallbladder problems because of the lowered detoxifying function of the liver." Pregnancy is the natural experiment: estrogen rises tenfold, gallbladder emptying slows, sludge and de novo stones appear through the second and third trimester.

The [[progesterone]] side matters equally. Progesterone relaxes smooth muscle of the gallbladder wall and the sphincter of Oddi the way smooth muscle is supposed to relax — rhythmically, coordinated with contraction. The pre-menstrual luteal phase, when progesterone falls and estrogen has acted unopposed for two weeks, is a recognized window for biliary colic. Women on combined oral contraceptives — estrogen plus a synthetic progestin that does not act on the gallbladder the way native progesterone does — have measurably elevated cholesterol-stone rates. So do women on post-menopausal estrogen replacement. The patient population is not mysterious. It is the population running unopposed estrogen against a hypothyroid liver.

## The low-fat-dieting iatrogenesis

Here is where Peat's framework directly contradicts mainstream public health. **Chronic low-fat dieting causes gallstones.** Not "is associated with" — *causes*. The mechanism is uncontroversial inside gastroenterology but has never made it into popular advice.

The gallbladder contracts in response to fat in the duodenum. The duodenal I-cells release cholecystokinin (CCK) when fat or protein hits the small intestine; CCK travels to the gallbladder, the muscle contracts, the sphincter of Oddi relaxes, bile propels into the duodenum to emulsify the meal's fat. If no fat is eaten, no CCK is released, and the gallbladder does not contract. Bile sits.

A gallbladder that does not contract concentrates its bile. Water reabsorption across the gallbladder epithelium is constant; the cholesterol-to-bile-acid ratio rises locally far above whatever the liver delivered. Crystals nucleate. Sludge forms. With weeks to months of low-fat eating, sludge progresses to stones.

This is the mechanism behind the documented "very low calorie diet" and "weight loss surgery" gallstone epidemic: 30 to 50% of patients on aggressive caloric restriction or post-bariatric weight loss develop sludge or stones within months. Modern GLP-1 prescribing literature now lists gallstones as an expected adverse event of rapid weight loss on Ozempic, Wegovy, and tirzepatide. The mechanism is the same as the mechanism behind chronic moderate low-fat dieting: a gallbladder that does not contract enough makes stones.

The correct instruction is the opposite of conventional advice: **eat enough fat — at every meal — to make the gallbladder contract.** A pat of butter, a tablespoon of [[coconut-oil]], a few egg yolks, a piece of cheese, the fat naturally in milk. The dose does not need to be heroic. The point is rhythm, not volume.

## Cholestasis: the upstream driver

"Cholestasis" is the gastroenterology term for impaired bile flow. It is the upstream lesion of which gallstones are one downstream expression; fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption, pale stools, elevated alkaline phosphatase and GGT, and pruritus are the others. Five Peat-recognized causes converge on the same canalicular bottleneck.

**[[PUFA]] accumulation in hepatocyte and canalicular membranes.** Polyunsaturated fats stored in the liver peroxidize slowly to reactive aldehydes and stiffen the canalicular membrane where the bile-acid export pumps (BSEP, MRP2) live. The transporters work less efficiently. Bile acid secretion falls; the bile that does leave is enriched in cholesterol relative to bile acids. This is the multi-year cholestasis driver behind much of what mainstream gastroenterology calls "idiopathic" or "metabolic-associated" liver disease.

**Estrogen-driven cholestasis.** Pregnancy, oral contraceptives, and estrogen replacement are the classic three causes of *intrahepatic cholestasis* — itching, jaundice, elevated bile acids that resolve when the estrogenic input stops. Estrogen directly inhibits hepatocyte bile-acid export.

**Hypothyroidism.** The thyroid governs hepatocyte oxidative metabolism and Phase I and II detoxification capacity. A hypothyroid liver under-produces bile, under-conjugates substances that need to leave through bile, and contracts the gallbladder weakly. Broda Barnes documented the temperature-gallbladder association decades ago; Peat repeated it consistently. From the 2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode: "There's a great tendency for hypothyroid people to have gallbladder disease and trouble digesting fats. The best thing to do for gallbladder disease is to improve thyroid function." This is the single intervention with the largest expected effect.

**Prolonged fasting and intermittent fasting.** The same CCK-deprived state that chronic low-fat dieting produces is what extended fasting produces over hours. Sixteen hours without food leaves the gallbladder uncontracted for sixteen hours. Even the Johns Hopkins gallstones patient-education page notes that fasting "can contribute to the development of gallstones, which can develop when bile stored in the gallbladder hardens into stonelike matter." This is the same physiology applied to intermittent fasting as currently marketed.

**Rapid weight loss from any cause.** Bariatric surgery, GLP-1 agonists, very-low-calorie diets, ketogenic regimens that drive large weekly weight change — all produce mobilization of adipose-stored cholesterol and PUFA the liver must handle, while reducing the dietary fat that would drive contraction. The mechanism is identical to chronic low-fat dieting, sharpened by the adipose mobilization.

## Peat's protocol

Address the upstream metabolism, give the system time to remodel, intervene minimally when the underlying physiology has been corrected. The protocol below belongs alongside [[liver-restoration]] and [[estrogen-detox]].

**Regular fat at every meal.** Butter, coconut oil, egg yolks, cheese, the fat in milk and cream, moderate beef and lamb fat. The dose need not be heroic — a pat of butter, a tablespoon of coconut oil in coffee, two eggs at breakfast. Three to five eating events per day, each containing some fat, each calling for biliary emptying.

**Optimize thyroid.** Low resting body temperature, slow pulse, cold extremities, afternoon energy crash, constipation, dry skin, elevated cholesterol despite a "good" diet — these point to the most consequential intervention available. See [[thyroid-dosing]] and [[hypothyroid-recovery]]. A patient with stones who restores normal thyroid function will frequently see "fat intolerance" resolve within weeks, the lithogenic index of subsequent bile fall, and existing stones stop progressing — sometimes shrink — over months to years.

**Reduce estrogen load.** The four levers live at [[estrogen-detox]]: progesterone supplementation where appropriate (transdermal Progest-E for luteal-phase deficiency or symptomatic post-menopause), [[carrot-salad]] daily to interrupt enterohepatic estrogen recirculation, thyroid optimization to drive hepatic clearance, and removal of xenoestrogen exposure (plastics, soy isoflavones, hormone-treated meat and dairy). A woman in her thirties with gallstones is almost always running unopposed estrogen against a hypothyroid liver.

**Bile-flow herbs, cautiously.** Sarah Johanneson Murray, in the same 2011 episode where Peat described the thyroid-gallbladder link: "Gentian, Burdock root, and Dandelion root are very bitter herbs that can stimulate the flow of bile. Swedish Bitters is a traditional European tonic that has those types of herbs." Peat did not contradict her. Bitter herbs work through tongue and gut receptors that trigger reflex CCK release and bile flow. They support — they do not substitute for — the upstream work, and any active bile-flow stimulant carries some risk of mobilizing a stone into a duct it cannot pass.

**Daily [[aspirin]].** Anti-inflammatory at the gallbladder (suppressing the [[prostaglandins]] that drive biliary inflammation and spasm), bile-thinning, and supportive of the broader anti-estrogen, pro-thyroid state. A standard daily dose (81 to 325 mg with food), paired with [[vitamin-k]] to maintain carboxylation reactions aspirin slightly disturbs.

**Fat-soluble vitamins.** [[Vitamin-e]] (mixed tocopherols, 100 to 400 IU daily) protects the unsaturated fats the bile is handling from peroxidation. [[Vitamin-d]] (from sunlight; 2,000 to 5,000 IU supplemental as a hedge). [[Vitamin-k]] (K1 from cooked greens, K2 from butter, cheese, egg yolks). These vitamins are themselves bile-dependent for absorption — the regular-fat-at-every-meal instruction is the prerequisite for getting any of them in.

**AVOID prolonged fasting and very-low-fat diets.** This is the single largest behavioral change. No intermittent fasting longer than the overnight sleep. Three meals and two to three snacks daily, never going more than four hours between eating events during waking hours. A bedtime snack containing some fat — milk, cheese, a small bowl of ice cream — prevents the long overnight cholestasis that would otherwise concentrate morning bile.

**AVOID [[pufa]].** Polyunsaturated fats from corn, soy, cottonseed, safflower, sunflower, canola, and grapeseed oil are the longest-arc contributor to cholestasis. Adipose half-life is roughly two years; full tissue clearance about four. The new input must stop now. See [[pufa-depletion]] for the longer-arc protocol.

## Cholecystectomy critique

Six hundred thousand cholecystectomies a year is a remarkable number, and the long-term outcomes literature is less reassuring than the operative literature suggests. *Post-cholecystectomy syndrome* — persistent right-upper-quadrant pain, fat intolerance, chronic diarrhea, dyspepsia — affects 10 to 40% of patients after gallbladder removal. The storage organ is gone, but the upstream cholestasis that produced the stones is intact. Bile now drips continuously into the duodenum rather than releasing in coordinated boluses; fat digestion is impaired; bile acids reach the colon at higher concentrations and produce diarrhea; the sphincter of Oddi dysregulates; some patients develop common-bile-duct stones in the years after surgery.

The Peat reading is direct. The gallbladder is not vestigial. It is the bile reservoir, the contractile element of biliary emptying, the coordinator of bile release with meals. Removing it because it produced stones is removing the organ the dysfunctional metabolism damaged, while leaving the dysfunctional metabolism intact.

Surgery is the right intervention for acute obstruction or impending perforation. It is the wrong intervention for asymptomatic or mildly-symptomatic stones. Most asymptomatic stones never become symptomatic — the lifetime cumulative risk of needing surgery for incidentally-discovered stones is well under 20%, and mainstream gastroenterology already manages those expectantly. The mildly-symptomatic stones — occasional biliary colic without complications — are where the Peat framework has the most to offer: treat the upstream metabolism, watch the symptoms over months and years that the thyroid, estrogen, and bile chemistry re-normalize, reserve surgery for actual indications.

Stone impacted in the cystic duct with developing cholecystitis is a surgical emergency. Choledocholithiasis with cholangitis is a surgical emergency. Gallstone pancreatitis is a surgical emergency. None of those are negotiable. What the framework denies is the *default* — the assumption that the appearance of stones on an ultrasound automatically writes the prescription for an operation.

## Tracking signal

**Body temperature** trending up from the 97s toward 98.2–98.6°F is the single best signal that thyroid function is recovering. See [[temperature-tracking]].

**Fat tolerance.** The patient who could not eat butter or eggs without right-upper-quadrant cramping at month one finds them tolerable at month three and unremarkable at month six.

**Stool color and consistency.** Pale, fatty, or floating stools indicate ongoing fat malabsorption from cholestasis. Brown, formed stools indicate bile is flowing at the volume and rhythm digestion requires.

**Lab signals.** GGT and alkaline phosphatase trend down over months. ALT and AST follow. HDL up, triglycerides down. SHBG up.

**Imaging.** Ultrasound at six to twelve months can confirm existing stones have not grown and may show shrinkage. New sludge formation should stop.

**Symptom log.** Frequency, severity, duration of biliary colic episodes, and precipitating foods. Fewer, milder, shorter episodes is the most important patient-level signal.

## What to AVOID

- **Low-fat or "fat-free" eating patterns.** Fat-free dairy, dressing-free salads, dry chicken, the "clean eating" template stripped of butter and yolks.
- **Intermittent fasting, prolonged fasting, time-restricted eating.** The "16:8" window, the 24-hour fast, the multi-day "cleanse." Each long fasting interval is a cholestatic interval.
- **Crash diets and rapid weight loss programs.** Slow gradual weight loss with adequate fat is the only safe trajectory.
- **Oral contraceptives and high-dose estrogen replacement** where alternatives exist.
- **PUFA-rich cooking oils** — corn, soy, cottonseed, safflower, sunflower, canola, grapeseed, "vegetable oil."
- **Acetaminophen as the routine analgesic.** Hepatotoxic at doses below package guidance and glutathione-depleting. Use [[aspirin]] instead.
- **Statins as the response to "high cholesterol"** from a gallstone-related lipid pattern. The cholesterol number is downstream of the same hypothyroid-estrogenic metabolism; HMG-CoA reductase inhibition does not address it.
- **"Gallbladder cleanses" and olive-oil-and-lemon-juice flushes.** A mobilized stone can lodge in the common bile duct and produce the very emergency the cleanse was meant to prevent.

## Counter-arguments

**Modern hepatology and cholecystectomy-as-default.** Mainstream gastroenterology treats cholesterol stones as a cholesterol problem amenable to either ursodeoxycholic acid or, dominantly, surgical removal. The expected efficacy at relieving acute biliary symptoms is high; the rate of significant post-cholecystectomy syndrome is debated but generally placed under 20% in the surgical literature. The Peat framework accepts the acute-emergency portion of this consensus and disputes the everyday application — the operation for mildly-symptomatic or incidentally-discovered stones — on the grounds that the upstream metabolism is fixable and the organ is not vestigial.

**The Mediterranean-diet camp.** Observational data favor Mediterranean-pattern eating (olive oil, fish, nuts, vegetables, moderate wine) for gallstone outcomes, which the camp attributes to the unsaturated fats. The Peat reading attributes the favorable outcome to the *presence* of fat at most meals (driving contraction) rather than to fatty-acid composition, and would predict the same contractile benefit — with less PUFA loading — from a saturated-fat-rich diet of butter, coconut oil, eggs, and dairy.

**AHA low-saturated-fat advice.** The AHA's decades of recommending reduced saturated fat is built on the lipid hypothesis of cardiovascular disease, not on gallbladder considerations, and does not address the iatrogenic cholestasis Peat describes. The two streams of advice are incompatible at the patient level: AHA tells the high-cholesterol patient to eat less fat; Peat says less fat produces gallstones and worsens the hypothyroidism that raised the cholesterol.

**Ultra-low-fat reversal-of-heart-disease programs.** Ornish, Esselstyn, and Pritikin produce documented coronary regression on near-fat-free protocols. The gastroenterology literature broadly confirms a substantial increase in gallstones among long-term adherents — the trade-off is rarely discussed in the Ornish literature, and is central in the Peat reading.

**Bariatric surgery and GLP-1 programs.** Modern weight-loss medicine produces gallstones in 25 to 50% of patients, often managed with prophylactic ursodeoxycholic acid during active loss. The Peat framework would argue a slower weight-loss trajectory with adequate dietary fat would produce both better metabolic endpoints and no gallstone epidemic.

## Data gaps

- **No quantitative trial data.** Peat's clinical observation that thyroid optimization plus estrogen reduction plus regular-fat-eating reverses the gallstone-prone state has not been replicated in a randomized trial. The framework rests on mechanism and clinical impression.
- **No specific dosing on bile-flow herbs.** Murray's recommendation of gentian, burdock, dandelion, and Swedish Bitters is given without dose, frequency, or duration.
- **No detailed position on ursodeoxycholic acid (Actigall).** The mainstream pharmacologic alternative to surgery is not directly addressed in the Peat corpus I can locate.
- **No specific protocol for pregnancy-onset gallstones.** The general protocol applies, but trimester-specific specifics (thyroid dosing, progesterone form, carrot-salad in trimester three) are not detailed.
- **No clear position on pediatric gallstones**, which involve specific contexts (hemolytic disease, TPN, cystic fibrosis) Peat did not address.
- **No stone-composition stratification.** Cholesterol stones are the framework's natural target. Pigment stones (calcium bilirubinate) and brown stones (bacterial deconjugation) involve different upstream physiology not addressed in the available corpus.
