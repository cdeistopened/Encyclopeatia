# Where Peat Evolved

Ray Peat wrote for forty-seven years. The earliest surviving pieces are 1967 fluoride letters to the *Eugene Register-Guard*; the last interviews aired in 2022. Across that span he wrote roughly 180 newsletters, sat for more than 250 polished radio episodes, fielded thousands of emails, and published two books. The framework that emerged is dense and internally consistent — thyroid, [[pufa|PUFA]], [[estrogen]], [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]], [[progesterone]], [[sugar]], [[learned-helplessness]] — and it is the consistency that makes Peat-online treat him as monolithic.

But the framework did move. Some positions hardened. Some softened. Some topics were invisible in 1985 and central by 2015. Some early nuances were absorbed by the community as more absolute than Peat actually said. An encyclopedia of a living thinker that doesn't track this evolution is hagiography, and Peat himself would have objected to that flatly — his methodology was empirical, his self-correction was visible in print, and he changed his mind in public.

This page documents what moved. For the biographical arc, see [[ray-peat]]. For the year-by-year publication chronology, see [[timeline]]. Each shift below is anchored in specific corpus citations, early and late, and each closes with the practical implication for anyone reading the framework today. Where the shift is uncertain — where the change might be framing rather than substance — that uncertainty is stated, not papered over.

The structure: eleven verified shifts, then a list of what didn't move, then a brief note on how the Peat-online community tends to read these shifts (often, the late position is taken as canonical, but not always rightly).

## 1. PUFA tolerance — soft refusal to hardline

**Early position (1985–1991):** Peat's earliest sustained anti-fat writing already names polyunsaturated oils as harmful, but the framing is calibrated. The 1985 newsletter referenced in [[sales-pitch-for-linseed-oil|"Sales Pitch for Linseed Oil" (Feb/Mar 1989)]] cites "interactive toxicities of unsaturated oils and iron" and discusses unsaturated fats as substances that "inhibit many enzymes" and "disrupt mitochondrial energy production." The argument is rigorous, but the tone reserves room for context — the harm is dose-related, iron-catalyzed, vitamin-E-modifiable, and discussed in the language of "many people are still falling for the false establishment story." Linseed oil is the named offender; "the unsaturated oils" function as a category, not a binary contaminant.

**Late position (2007–2020s):** By [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic|"Unsaturated Vegetable Oils: Toxic" (Feb 2007)]], the framing has hardened. The title itself is now categorical. The opening line links unsaturated oils to "acquired immunodeficiency" comparable to radiation damage. In [[fats-and-degeneration|"Fats and Degeneration" (Dec 2009)]] and the late radio interviews, the operative metric becomes "PUFA depletion" — a half-decade-long project of replacing tissue PUFA with saturated and monounsaturated fat. The implicit message shifts from "avoid the new vegetable oils" to "every PUFA molecule in your body is a future lipid peroxidation event."

**The shift:** Real but partly cosmetic. The mechanism Peat described in 1985 is the same mechanism he described in 2015. What changed is the rhetorical confidence and the practical advice. In 1985 he expected the public to encounter unsaturated oils mainly through visible seed-oil products; by 2007, after a decade of fish-oil marketing and the dietary-fat reformulation of processed food, he saw the problem as ubiquitous and the appropriate response as more drastic. The framework didn't pivot — the world pivoted around it, and Peat's recommendation hardened to match.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** Compare [[sales-pitch-for-linseed-oil]] (1989) and [[darkness-water-osteoporosis]] (1985), where PUFA is one harm among several, with [[unsaturated-vegetable-oils-toxic]] (2007), [[fats-and-degeneration]] (2009), and [[the-great-fish-oil-experiment]] (2007), where it is treated as the dominant degenerative factor in modern disease.

**Implication for protocol:** The late hardline is reasonable as a default. But the early framing — that PUFA harm is iron-catalyzed, dose-related, and vitamin-E-modifiable — is still mechanistically correct, and it explains why some Peat-curious readers tolerate trace PUFA exposure (occasional fish, an olive-oil dressing, a restaurant meal) without obvious decompensation. Treating PUFA as a binary contaminant ("a single drop ruined the diet") is a community over-extrapolation; treating it as a metabolic load to minimize is closer to what Peat actually said.

## 2. Coconut oil — moderate enthusiasm to community over-extrapolation

**Early position:** Coconut oil is essentially invisible in the 1985–1995 newsletter corpus. The first sustained treatment is the [[coconut-oil|2006 newsletter]] titled simply "Coconut Oil," which describes Peat's own experience — adding "about an ounce" per day to a normal diet, watching his weight drop from 185 to 175 pounds over six months. He explicitly notes that components of coconut oil "have been found to have remarkable physiological effects" but cautions against making "any such claims for the natural coconut oil" precisely because of regulatory exposure.

**Late position (2008–2020s):** The radio interviews escalate. In the [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 thyroid and oils episode]] Peat says "butter and coconut oil are really the only very safe fats." In the 2011 *It's Rainmaking Time* interview he tells listeners to take "a tablespoon of coconut oil" with every meal because the metabolic effect "only lasts for an hour or so." The community took this further: by the mid-2010s, the "drink coconut oil by the spoonful" advice was circulating as Peat doctrine.

**The shift:** Mostly community amplification, partly a real escalation. Peat's own 2006 prescription was an ounce a day. By 2011 it was a tablespoon per meal — roughly the same total dose, redistributed. The "spoonful straight from the jar, multiple times a day" advice that became Peat-online folklore was not what Peat said. He said it should be used in food, eaten with carbohydrates, in service of replacing seed oils — not as a metabolic accelerant taken neat.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** [[coconut-oil]] (2006) is the foundational text. Compare with the radio transcripts cited above. There is essentially no pre-2000 newsletter treatment of coconut oil — it was a late entry to Peat's vocabulary, probably influenced by Mary Enig's 1995 papers and the rebuilding of the saturated-fat case in the late 1990s.

**Implication for protocol:** The therapeutic effect Peat described — momentary competitive displacement of unsaturated fats at the mitochondrion — is real, dose-related, and works at a tablespoon. The community version (raw coconut oil consumed as a supplement, multiple ounces daily) is over-extrapolated and can produce its own problems (digestive distress, ketogenic metabolic shifts Peat would have flagged as stress signals). The early enthusiastic-but-modest framing is what to follow.

## 3. Aspirin dosing — preventive use to aggressive therapeutic

**Early position (1986–1991):** In the early newsletters, aspirin appears almost in passing — a household analgesic, used by patients in case reports ([[hormone-balancing-natural-treatment-and-cure-for-arthritis|"Hormone Balancing" (1986)]] notes a patient "no longer needed aspirin" after progesterone treatment), discussed as one of the drugs people self-medicate with ([[biological-balance-and-addictions|"Biological Balance and Addictions" (Jun 1991)]] names it alongside coffee, tobacco, and alcohol as "normally the only drugs they have to experiment with"). There is no dedicated newsletter on aspirin in the 1985–2000 corpus and no high-dose recommendation in that period.

**Late position (2006–2020s):** [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|"Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer" (2006)]] is the consolidation point. By then Peat is describing aspirin as a substance with effects "broadly analogous" to progesterone, recommending its "preventive and therapeutic" use, and citing case after case of high-dose use in cancer, dementia, and acute stress. The vault article on [[aspirin]] documents the late escalation in his own practice: 1,000 mg/day personal baseline, 6 grams or more daily for active cancer treatment, 325–500 mg every 4–6 hours during acute flu, dissolved in warm water with vitamin K cover.

**The shift:** Real, large, and consequential. The 1985–1991 Peat treated aspirin as a useful drug among many. The 2006-onward Peat treated it as one of the four or five most important interventions available, comparable in scope to thyroid hormone or progesterone. The shift maps to the accumulation of the COX-2 literature in the late 1990s, the cancer-prevention aspirin trials of the 2000s, and Peat's own evolving view that "stability and instability" at the cellular level (his phrase) was the right frame for understanding why a 19th-century drug had such broad protective effects.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** Compare any 1985–1995 newsletter, where aspirin is incidental, with [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer]] (2006), which positions it centrally. The dosing escalation is documented in the [[aspirin|vault aspirin page]] from email exchanges and late radio transcripts.

**Implication for protocol:** This is one of the shifts where the late position is probably right but also where the late position requires more caution than the early one did. High-dose aspirin without vitamin K cover is dangerous. The community sometimes adopts the dose without the cover. The mature Peat position is "aspirin + vitamin K + dissolved-in-warm-water + with-food" as an integrated protocol, not "more aspirin is always better."

## 4. Methylene blue — invisible to central

**Early position (1985–2000):** Methylene blue is essentially absent from the 1985–2000 newsletter corpus. A corpus-wide search of the 180-newsletter archive finds zero substantive treatments. The drug existed; Peat knew about it (anyone who studied bioenergetics knew about the Ehrlich-malaria story); but he did not write about it as a recommended substance.

**Late position (2014–2022):** From roughly the 2014 [[2014-11-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-nitric-oxide|nitric oxide *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] forward, methylene blue becomes one of Peat's most frequently discussed substances. He treats it as a chemical substitute for the role thyroid hormone normally plays — an electron carrier capable of bypassing damaged mitochondrial respiratory complexes. The [[methylene-blue|vault page]] documents the full late-career integration: depression at 1 mg, dementia adjunct, cancer adjunct with red light, sepsis indication, hard SSRI contraindication.

**The shift:** This is not an evolution of position — it is the addition of a topic that didn't exist in his earlier vocabulary. The mechanistic frame (mitochondrial respiration, electron transport, redox cycling) was always there; methylene blue was simply not part of his pharmacology until the 2010s revival of the antidepressant and Alzheimer's research literature gave him reason to engage with it. Atamna's 2008 cytochrome c oxidase paper and the TauRx Alzheimer's program (2016–2018) are the proximate causes.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** Almost entirely in late radio: the 2014, 2015, and later [[ask-the-herb-doctor]] episodes; [[generative-energy|Generative Energy]] episodes 35, 36, and 41; the 2015 [[politics-and-science]] evolution episode. Zero newsletter treatments before 2014.

**Implication for protocol:** Methylene blue is in the framework but it is a late addition, and the dose-response curve Peat described is narrow. The sub-milligram dose he favored is closer to homeopathic than to the high-dose biohacker market that emerged in the early 2020s. The SSRI/MAOI contraindication is non-negotiable. Anyone reading "Peat recommended methylene blue" should know that he recommended it cautiously, late, and with a specific dose ceiling.

## 5. Vitamin D — early scared off, late "single most important thing"

**Early position (1985):** In [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|"Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis" (April 1985)]], vitamin D appears only in passing — listed among substances that can "cause respiratory alkalosis" alongside calcitonin and estrogen "HRT" treatments. The newsletter's whole emphasis is on red light, prolactin, and CO2 as the regulators of calcium metabolism; supplemental vitamin D is treated with the suspicion typical of the 1950s–1980s public-health debates about vitamin D toxicity. Peat himself has explicitly described how he was "directed away from Vitamin D because there was such a taboo about taking anything more than 200 or 300 units per day" in his student years.

**Late position (2016–2022):** By the [[2016-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vitamin-d|2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* vitamin D episode]] and especially the [[2021-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2021-kmud|September 2021 episode]], Peat is calling vitamin D "probably the single most important thing for Americans." He recommends "5,000 units a day for an adult." He reframes the calcification literature — long used to argue *against* vitamin D — as evidence that vitamin D *deficiency* is what drives soft-tissue calcification through chronically elevated PTH.

**The shift:** Real, well-documented, and one of the largest reversals in his late career. The mechanism that made him cautious in the 1980s (the 1950s "vitamin D causes calcification" scare) was the same mechanism he later argued ran the other direction. The proximate cause was the 1990s–2000s PTH literature, the kidney-dialysis evidence on parathyroidectomy, and Peat's own re-reading of the calcification mechanism through the lens of carbon dioxide regulation.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** [[darkness-water-osteoporosis]] (1985) — cautious, indirect. The [[vitamin-d|vault vitamin D article]] documents the 2016 and 2021 episodes as the late consolidation; the shift had completed by then.

**Implication for protocol:** The late position is right and the early caution is correctable. But the dose recommendation comes with a magnesium and calcium dependency Peat emphasized: vitamin D alone, without sufficient dietary calcium and magnesium, can mobilize calcium from bone in ways that worsen the very problem it's meant to fix. The community sometimes supplements vitamin D in isolation; Peat's late framework treats it as one leg of a tripod with [[calcium]] and [[magnesium]].

## 6. Calcium recommendations — modest to "two quarts of milk a day"

**Early position (1985):** [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|"Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis" (April 1985)]] is the earliest sustained calcium-and-bone newsletter. It centers on prolactin, light, and the diurnal cycle. The mechanism is sophisticated — Peat already has the "calcium in the wrong places" frame — but the practical recommendation is implicit. There is no quantitative milk target. Calcium is discussed as one of several minerals that respond to hormonal regulation, not as a substance to be loaded into the diet.

**Late position (2009–2020s):** [[calcium-and-disease-hypertension-organ-calcification-shock-vs-respiratory-energy|"Calcium and Disease" (2009)]] and especially [[milk-in-context-allergies-ecology-and-some-myths|"Milk in Context" (Feb 2011)]] consolidate a new prescription: roughly two quarts of milk per day, providing approximately 2,000 mg of dietary calcium. The vault [[calcium]] article documents that Peat himself "drank up to a gallon of milk per day for over thirty-five years" — which means his personal practice ran ahead of the public recommendation, but the public recommendation only solidified in the late 2000s.

**The shift:** Real. The mechanism (PTH suppression as the central anti-aging lever) was developing through the 1990s but the quantitative dietary target — the "two quarts" floor — is a 2009–2011 consolidation. The proximate causes were the 1990s–2000s McCarron literature on calcium and blood pressure, the multiple-sclerosis-and-PTH studies, and the kidney-dialysis parathyroidectomy evidence.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** Compare [[darkness-water-osteoporosis]] (1985) — no milk target — with [[milk-in-context-allergies-ecology-and-some-myths]] (2011), where the "small frequent meals, milk as a convenient calcium delivery vehicle, anti-PTH effect" prescription is explicit.

**Implication for protocol:** The two-quart floor is a late-Peat target and is reasonable for someone whose digestion tolerates dairy. But the early framework — that calcium regulation is hormonal, light-sensitive, and prolactin-mediated — gives ground for the case that not every Peat reader needs to chase the two-quart number if they're getting adequate dietary calcium from other sources (cheese, eggshell calcium, well-cooked greens). The number is a default, not a non-negotiable.

## 7. Fish oil — low-PUFA category to actively harmful

**Early position (1980s–1990s):** Fish oil is treated alongside other unsaturated oils — the [[sales-pitch-for-linseed-oil|1989 Linseed Oil letter]] references "Dale Alexander made a career of the idea that cod liver oil will grease arthritic joints" with skepticism, but doesn't single fish oil out as uniquely dangerous. Cod liver oil's vitamin A and D content is noted; the polyunsaturated content is a concern but one harm among several. The 1992 [[failures-of-immunity|"Failures of Immunity"]] and 1995 [[aging-ovaries-not-the-eggs|"Aging Ovaries"]] newsletters treat fish oil as a marginal topic.

**Late position (2007):** [[the-great-fish-oil-experiment|"The Great Fish Oil Experiment" (2007)]] is one of Peat's most categorical newsletters. The title itself is loaded. The opening argues that the FDA "neglected to evaluate" fish oil's "antithyroid, immunosuppressive, lipid peroxidative, light sensitizing, and antimitochondrial effects." The case is structured as a public health indictment — fish oil isn't merely a polyunsaturated oil with the standard risks; it is actively more immunosuppressive than seed oils, more readily oxidized in tissue, and being inappropriately promoted to children through fortification programs.

**The shift:** Real and dated. The 1989–2000 period treated fish oil as one PUFA source among many. The 2007 newsletter elevates it to a specific public health threat. The trigger was the 2000s shift in commercial fish-oil marketing — the Omega Protein school-lunch contracts, the Andrew Weil-style mainstream-medicine endorsement, the FDA "generally regarded as safe" determination on DHA for baby formula — all of which prompted Peat to write a dedicated, sharper newsletter than the early framework would have produced.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** Compare incidental mentions in [[sales-pitch-for-linseed-oil]] (1989) and the early 1990s newsletters with the categorical treatment in [[the-great-fish-oil-experiment]] (2007).

**Implication for protocol:** This is one of the shifts where the late position is right and the early framing under-stated the case. Fish oil concentrates EPA and DHA — the most peroxidation-prone fatty acids — in a way that ordinary dietary PUFA exposure does not. The 2007 hardline is mechanistically sound.

## 8. Iron — broad caution to "the major life-shortening factor"

**Early position (1985):** Iron is mentioned in early newsletters as one of several heavy metals whose accumulation causes free radical damage. [[sales-pitch-for-linseed-oil|The 1989 letter]] notes that ascorbic acid tablets "may be contaminated with enough iron and/or other catalysts to produce a toxic amount of free hydroxyl radicals." Iron's role in lipid peroxidation is acknowledged but it is not yet positioned as the central catalyst.

**Late position (1997–2022):** [[iron-cumulative-danger|"Iron: Cumulative Danger" (June 1997)]] is the consolidation point — the dedicated newsletter where Peat lays out the iron-accumulation curve, the WHO Africa supplementation disaster, the blood-donor longevity finding, and the case that iron drives aging more than calories do. [[irons-dangers|"Iron's Dangers" (2006)]] expands the case. By the late radio interviews and email exchanges, Peat is recommending blood donation, iron-blocking with coffee at meals, and specific tests (iron saturation, ferritin) before any iron supplementation — and arguing that "the iron content of food has been identified as the major life-shortening factor, rather than the calories" in calorie-restriction studies.

**The shift:** A consolidation rather than a reversal. The 1985 framework already named iron as harmful in excess. The 1997 newsletter made the case categorical and quantitative. The shift is partly his integration of the 1990s research on iron and Alzheimer's, the heart-disease literature (the 1980s Salonen ferritin studies), and the African WHO supplementation outcomes.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** [[iron-cumulative-danger]] (1997) is the pivot. The early 1980s newsletters mention iron incidentally; from 1997 forward it is a recurring focal topic.

**Implication for protocol:** The late position — iron as a primary degenerative driver — is mechanistically grounded. The framework hasn't moved, only sharpened. The community sometimes over-corrects, avoiding red meat or eggs as if they were toxic iron sources; the late Peat himself ate steak, eggs, and shellfish. The intervention he recommended was blood donation and iron-blocking food choices, not dietary asceticism.

## 9. Stress framework — cortisol-centric to CRH-and-estrogen-as-mediators

**Early position (1985–1991):** [[energy-entropy-and-estrogens-in-aging|"Energy, Entropy and Estrogens in Aging" (Aug 1985)]] and [[biological-balance-and-addictions|"Biological Balance and Addictions" (June 1991)]] both frame stress through cortisol. The mediators named in 1991 are "adrenalin and cortisol and growth hormone." [[natural-immunity-and-viral-infections|"Natural Immunity and Viral Infections" (Aug/Sept 1988)]] discusses cortisol's catabolic and pro-inflammatory effects but does not yet have CRH as a primary actor.

**Late position (2011–2022):** [[endotoxin-stress-depression|"Endotoxin Stress Depression" (May 2011)]] is the consolidation. CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone) appears as the primary actor: "CRH is more directly associated with depression than cortisol is, and it by itself activates many inflammatory processes." Estrogen is named as the substance that "increases [CRH's] activity." The frame has shifted from "cortisol is the stress hormone" to "CRH is the upstream signal, estrogen and serotonin and endotoxin amplify it, cortisol is downstream."

**The shift:** Real and important. The early framework was already non-mainstream — Peat was unusual in the 1980s for treating cortisol as a stress mediator with both protective and destructive functions, not as a simple "stress hormone." But the explicit CRH-as-upstream-actor frame is a 2000s consolidation. The proximate cause was the 1990s–2000s neuroendocrinology research on CRH receptors in mast cells, the gut, and the brain — research that gave Peat a mechanistic substrate for what he had previously described through cortisol alone.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** Compare [[energy-entropy-and-estrogens-in-aging]] (1985) and [[biological-balance-and-addictions]] (1991) — cortisol-centric — with [[endotoxin-stress-depression]] (2011) and [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women-depression-energy-stress|"Estrogen and Brain Aging" (Oct 2009)]], where CRH is the through-line.

**Implication for protocol:** This is mostly a refinement of the same framework, not a change in recommendations. The interventions Peat recommended in 1991 (thyroid, progesterone, pregnenolone, sugar, salt, light) are the same interventions he recommended in 2015. The mechanism by which they work — what they actually suppress — was reframed from "cortisol axis" to "CRH-serotonin-endotoxin-estrogen integrated stress response," but the practical advice didn't move.

## 10. Estrogen-as-stress-mediator — central to even-more-central

**Early position (1985–1991):** [[energy-entropy-and-estrogens-in-aging|"Energy, Entropy and Estrogens in Aging" (1985)]] and [[estrogen-in-1990|"Estrogen in 1990" (April 1991)]] already treat estrogen as one of the master variables. The 1985 newsletter explicitly names estrogen as causally upstream of "Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease and other 'senile' changes in nerve function" plus circulatory, bone, and respiratory degeneration. The 1991 newsletter is an indictment of the estrogen industry's promotion of HRT. So the centrality is there from the start.

**Late position (2009–2020s):** The late Peat treats estrogen as so central that almost no degenerative condition is discussed without it. The [[endotoxin-stress-depression|2011 stress newsletter]] explicitly names estrogen as a primary CRH amplifier. The 2014 [[comparison-of-progesterone-and-estrogen|"Comparison of Progesterone and Estrogen"]] interview frames estrogen as opposed to progesterone in essentially every domain of cellular function. The late radio interviews integrate estrogen into the iron, [[serotonin]], [[prolactin]], [[nitric-oxide]], inflammation, and cancer mechanisms — making it almost a unified field theory of degenerative disease.

**The shift:** This is the shift that is hardest to call. Estrogen was already central in 1985 — it didn't become central. But it absorbed more domains. By 2015 it is the named mediator of stress, iron loading, calcium misplacement, serotonin elevation, nitric oxide excess, and the inflammatory cascade. The early framework had it as one of three master variables (thyroid, estrogen, progesterone); the late framework treats it as the explanation that connects them all.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** The 1985–1991 corpus already has the estrogen frame. The late expansion is visible across [[endotoxin-stress-depression]] (2011), [[estrogen-and-brain-aging-in-men-and-women-depression-energy-stress]] (2009), and the [[comparison-of-progesterone-and-estrogen]] (2014) consolidation.

**Implication for protocol:** Peat's recommendations on progesterone, anti-aromatase nutrition, and the avoidance of xenoestrogens are stable across decades. The shift is one of explanatory weight, not protocol. Readers should know that the late Peat's habit of routing nearly every disease through estrogen is partly a sharpening of focus and partly an editorial choice — the early Peat would also have flagged hypothyroidism, PUFA, and endotoxin as load-bearing in those same cases. The community version that flattens everything to "estrogen excess" loses information the early framework retained.

## 11. The vegan / vegetarian / carnivore framing

**Early position (1985–1995):** Peat's anti-vegetarian position is stable from the earliest newsletters. [[bse-mad-cow-scrapie-etc-stimulated-amyloid-degeneration-and-the-toxic-fats|"BSE, Mad Cow, etc." (2007)]] notes the Fore women's vegetarianism as a contributor to kuru. [[calcium-and-disease-hypertension-organ-calcification-shock-vs-respiratory-energy|"Calcium and Disease" (2009)]] argues that 1960s recommendations to substitute vegetables for milk to avoid strontium-90 actually delivered more strontium per gram of calcium. [[the-bean-syndrome|"The Bean Syndrome"]] is a sustained case against legume-heavy diets. The anti-vegetarian frame is present from the start.

**Late position (2018–2022):** The carnivore wave (Shawn Baker, Mikhaila Peterson) is largely post-Peat's late consolidation. Peat himself never endorsed an all-meat diet. His framework was always milk-and-fruit-centered, with meat as a supporting protein source, gelatin/collagen for the glycine balance, and dairy as the central nutritional vehicle. The late interviews are explicit that an all-meat diet would shift the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in the wrong direction and miss the [[fructose]] and lactose substrate his metabolic theory required.

**The shift:** This isn't a shift in Peat's position — it's a marker of how the community evolved around him. The early Peat opposed vegetarianism; the late Peat opposed both vegetarianism and the emerging carnivore wave; the community sometimes reads "Peat = anti-vegan" as endorsement of carnivore, which it isn't. The framework is consistent. The misreading is what's new.

**Where it appears in the corpus:** The anti-vegetarian frame is in [[the-bean-syndrome]] and the early newsletters. The anti-keto/anti-carnivore implications are in [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context" (Sept 2009)]] and [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|"Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes" (June 2012)]], where Peat explicitly says "there isn't anything wrong with a high carbohydrate diet."

**Implication for protocol:** This is the place where the community most needs to read Peat's actual words. The framework is not carnivore-adjacent. The framework is sugar-positive, anti-keto, dairy-central, meat-as-supplement. Anyone applying Peat through a low-carbohydrate or carnivore lens has departed from what Peat actually wrote.

## What didn't shift

A note for honesty: several core positions were stable across all forty-seven years and are not in this ledger. Documenting evolution requires documenting also what didn't evolve. The bedrock:

- **Thyroid as the master variable.** Present in the 1971 cold-inactivated-enzymes paper, the 1975 *Generative Energy* essays, the 2009 fats newsletter, the 2021 radio interviews. The mechanism Peat described in 1971 — thyroid hormone activating cytochrome oxidase, raising CO2 production, lowering lactate — is the same mechanism he described in 2021.
- **The anti-estrogen frame.** Stable from the 1971 [[estrogen-stimulated-pathway-changes-and-cold-inactivated-enzymes|cold-inactivated enzymes]] paper through the 2014 [[comparison-of-progesterone-and-estrogen|progesterone/estrogen comparison]]. The substance list of "estrogenic" exposures expanded (xenoestrogens added in the 1990s; serotonin reframed as estrogenic in the 2000s); the framework didn't.
- **Progesterone as the protective steroid.** The 1984 [[ray-peat-patent-1984|US patent 4,439,432]] for progesterone-in-tocopherol is the technological consolidation of a position Peat held from his 1969 dissertation forward. Forty-seven years of consistency.
- **CO2 as protective; lactate as failure signal.** Present from the 1973 [[energy-and-structure-in-biological-water|"Energy and Structure in Biological Water"]] paper through the [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|2009 "Lactate vs. CO2"]] consolidation and the late interviews. Mechanism and recommendation both stable.
- **Sugar as fuel, not poison.** The 1985 [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|"Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis"]] paper already names sugar as fuel; the 2009 [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context]] and 2012 [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes]] consolidations are refinements, not reversals. The anti-keto, pro-sucrose, pro-fructose position is stable.
- **"Genes are records, not causes."** Stable from the 1980 [[hereditys-hot-debate|"Heredity's Hot Debate"]] piece through the 2012 [[genes-carbon-dioxide-and-adaptation]] essay. The position predates Peat's biological-physics work; it was already mature in the *Science News* piece.
- **Fluoride opposition.** Stable from the 1973 [[fluoride-pollution]] newspaper letter through the late interviews. Fifty years of consistent opposition.
- **The biological-physics frame.** Gilbert Ling, Albert Szent-Györgyi, Otto Warburg, Hans Selye, Broda Barnes — the same intellectual lineage is named in 1975 and in 2021. Peat added researchers but never replaced the foundational lineage.
- **The anti-orthodoxy stance.** From the 1973 fluoride letters through the late critiques of pharmaceutical marketing, Peat's position on the corruption of medical science, the capture of regulatory agencies, and the suppression of inconvenient research is stable. Specific cases changed; the meta-position didn't.

These are the bedrock. The shifts above are second-order refinements within a framework whose load-bearing elements held for forty-seven years.

---

## How the Peat-online community reads the shifts

The Peat-online community tends to read the late position as canonical. This is usually right but sometimes wrong, and the failure mode is consistent enough to flag.

The community is right that the late aspirin position is more developed than the early one — the integration of vitamin K cover, the cancer-dose data, and the warm-water dissolved-with-food preparation are all late refinements that matter. The community is right that the late vitamin D position is more accurate than the 1980s caution. The community is right that the late iron position is the consolidated case.

The community over-extrapolates the late PUFA hardline into a binary contamination model that loses the dose-response, iron-modifiable, vitamin-E-modifiable nuances of the early framework. The community over-extrapolates the late coconut oil recommendation into "spoonfuls straight from the jar" advice that Peat didn't give. The community sometimes reads the late "estrogen as central mediator" frame as license to flatten every disease to "estrogen excess," which loses the hypothyroidism, PUFA, and endotoxin co-factors the early framework kept in play. The community sometimes reads the late anti-vegetarianism as endorsement of carnivore, which is the most consequential misreading — Peat's framework is sugar-positive and milk-centered, not keto-adjacent.

The framework is a moving target, not a fixed catechism. The mature reader holds the early and late positions in mind, recognizes which way the shift moved, and uses both as data when applying the framework to specific cases. That's what an encyclopedia of an evolving thinker is for.
