# Hardin Jones

Hardin Blair Jones (1914–1978) was a University of California, Berkeley biophysicist and medical statistician whose statistical analyses of cancer-survival data, presented to the American Cancer Society in 1956 and elaborated in the 1969 *Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences*, argued that the cancer establishment's "cure-rate" claims were built on lead-time bias, over-diagnosis, and the unrepresentative selection of healthier patients into treatment cohorts — and that, when his data were stratified honestly, untreated cancer patients lived as long as or longer than treated ones across most of the common tumor types he examined. Ray Peat cited Jones repeatedly as the original statistical critic of the cancer industry — the figure who, working from inside a major research university, exposed the methodological machinery by which a failing therapeutic enterprise advertised itself as winning a war. The line Peat quoted most often, from a 1956 Jones address: "If one has cancer and opts to do nothing at all, he will live longer and feel better than if he undergoes radiation, chemotherapy or surgery, other than when used in immediate life-threatening situations." The line is shocking. The statistical case behind it, which Jones built over two decades at the Donner Laboratory of Medical Physics, was not.

## Biography

Jones was a Berkeley man across his whole career. He took his doctorate in medical physics at UC Berkeley in 1940, joined the Donner Laboratory of Medical Physics — the laboratory founded by Ernest O. Lawrence to apply the new tools of radioisotope tracing and accelerator physics to biology and medicine — and rose to become its assistant director and a full professor of medical physics and physiology. The Donner Laboratory was, by mid-century, one of the major American centers of radiobiology; its principal investigators worked on the metabolic effects of radiation, the use of cyclotron-produced isotopes in clinical medicine, and the human physiology of high-altitude flight, atomic exposure, and aging. Jones's own training in physics and statistics, combined with the laboratory's access to large clinical datasets from the Atomic Energy Commission's medical-effects program and the postwar radiology literature, gave him a vantage that few critics of cancer treatment then possessed: he was inside the apparatus, not outside it, and he had the statistical tools to read the apparatus's own publications adversarially.

His published work spanned several lines. He wrote on the biological effects of ionizing radiation across the human lifespan; on smoking and lung cancer (where he was among the early voices arguing the relationship was real and dose-dependent); on the aging process itself, in which his statistical bent led him to formulate human longevity in terms of a depleted "vital reserve" and a force of mortality whose mathematical signature could be measured even when the underlying biology was incomplete. His name appears on hundreds of technical reports filed with the AEC and the Donner Laboratory, on textbook chapters about radiology and aging, and on the cancer-statistics papers for which Peat cited him. He served on the editorial board of the *Journal of Gerontology*. He died in 1978 in a swimming accident off the coast of California — a death that came at the height of his cancer-statistics controversy and that, in the readings of his sympathetic biographers, foreclosed the longer arc of work he might have done.

The biographical detail Peat returned to was the institutional position. Jones was not a fringe figure. He was a faculty professor at a major university, working at a laboratory whose funding came from the United States government, presenting his findings at the official cancer-society meetings of his era. That his critique was made from inside the system, and that the system absorbed his name without absorbing his argument, is what made him, for Peat, the cleanest case study of how institutional capture suppresses inconvenient statistics without ever needing to confront them.

## The 1956 ACS-meeting paper

The paper that became the foundation of Jones's reputation among cancer-statistics critics was an address delivered at an American Cancer Society meeting in 1956 and subsequently elaborated in a 1956 *Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences* paper titled "Demographic Consideration of the Cancer Problem." The argument was statistical, not clinical. Jones took the published five-year survival data for the major solid tumors of the era — breast, colon, prostate, uterine, gastric, lung — and broke the cohorts apart along two axes that the ACS's own summary statistics aggregated away: stage at diagnosis and whether the patient had received the standard treatment (surgery, radiation, or both, depending on the cancer type).

The disaggregation produced two findings the official summaries had concealed. First, when patients were stratified by stage at diagnosis, the median survival of patients who had refused or been denied conventional treatment was, across most of the cancer types Jones analyzed, no shorter than the median survival of treated patients at the same stage — and in several tumor types (notably breast and prostate, the cancers with the highest indolent fractions) it was longer. Second, the "five-year survival" figures the ACS publicized as therapeutic success counted as cures patients who had been diagnosed and treated at progressively earlier stages of disease, with no demonstrated improvement in overall mortality. The patient diagnosed at stage I and surviving five years was being counted as a "cure" against a denominator (all diagnosed patients) that increasingly favored early-stage detection regardless of any change in the underlying biology.

Jones laid out a specific list of statistical confounds that the cure-rate enterprise rested on: (1) **lead-time bias** — earlier diagnosis lengthens "survival from diagnosis" without changing the date of death; (2) **length bias** — slower-growing tumors are over-represented in screen-detected cohorts because the fast-growing ones outrun the screening interval, so the cohort entering "treatment" is preselected for indolence; (3) **selection bias in treatment cohorts** — patients accepted into surgical or radiation series were systematically healthier (younger, fewer comorbidities, better-nourished) than the all-comers denominator the no-treatment patients came from, so the treated cohort's outcomes were partly the outcomes their baseline health predicted; (4) **stage-migration bias** (later named the Will Rogers phenomenon by Alvan Feinstein in 1985, but described by Jones decades earlier) — improvements in staging move some patients from a worse stage to a better stage and inflate the survival of both stages without affecting any individual; (5) **definition drift** — the expanding category of "cancer" (including pre-invasive and *in situ* lesions reclassified into the cancer denominator) increased the apparent cure rate without curing anyone.

The 1956 paper was respectful in tone and devastating in implication. Jones did not call for the abandonment of treatment. He called for honest reporting — stratified survival data, intention-to-treat cohorts, age- and stage-matched controls, and an explicit accounting of the statistical confounds his analysis had identified. The American Cancer Society's response, which Peat documented in [[breast-cancer|*Breast Cancer* (2006)]], was to absorb the critique by adopting new "age-adjustment" procedures that, on Peat's reading, made the underlying inflation worse rather than better — standardizing future cohorts against the 1940 U.S. population (which had a disproportionately old age structure because the Depression had suppressed births), thereby making any modern cohort look younger and apparently more survivable than the standard. The publicized "declining cancer mortality" of the 1990s, Peat argued, was built on this manipulation; absolute cancer deaths in the United States rose from 505,300 in 1990 to 536,900 in 1994, faster than the population grew, during the years the ACS was advertising a 0.5%-per-year mortality decline.

## The 1969 NYAS paper

In 1969 Jones published a more comprehensive treatment of the same argument in the *Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences*, under the title "A Report on Cancer." The 1969 paper extended the 1956 analysis in three ways. It incorporated thirteen additional years of data, allowing longer follow-up of cohorts that had been only five or seven years out at the time of the original presentation. It expanded the cancer-type coverage to include cancers (pancreatic, hepatic, certain leukemias) where Jones's earlier work had been thin. And it engaged directly with the radiation-therapy literature, which had grown substantially in the postwar decades and which the official cohort statistics presented as one of the great cancer-medicine successes.

The 1969 conclusion was sharper than the 1956 one. Stratified by stage and matched for baseline health, the treated and untreated cohorts in Jones's reading showed no statistically distinguishable survival advantage for treatment in most solid-tumor categories. In some categories — Jones cited cervical, breast, and gastric cancer specifically — the treated cohorts did worse on long follow-up than the untreated, an effect Jones attributed to the cumulative toxicity of radiation and the early use of cytotoxic chemotherapy. The exceptions Jones acknowledged were essentially the exceptions Peat acknowledged half a century later in [[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]: the acute leukemias, where the cancer cells' lineage fragility made cytotoxic therapy genuinely productive; certain testicular and lymphomatous cancers, where multi-agent chemotherapy was beginning to produce durable remissions; and the surgical debulking of obstructing or compressive solid masses, where the benefit was mechanical rather than disease-modifying.

The 1969 paper also extended the institutional critique. Jones documented the reporting conventions by which the cancer-research enterprise advertised its successes: the use of "five-year survival from diagnosis" rather than "five-year survival from disease onset"; the exclusion of patients lost to follow-up; the differential reporting of trials with positive results versus those with negative results; the role of pharmaceutical company funding in shaping which questions got asked. The frame Peat later used — that the cancer industry is in the business of treating cancer, not eliminating it ([[breast-cancer|*Breast Cancer*]]) — is Jones's frame, articulated in a venue (the New York Academy of Sciences) and a journal (its *Transactions*) of legitimate scientific standing.

## The statistical-fraud thesis

The thesis Jones gave the cancer-statistics critique was that "cure rate" as the term was used in the postwar cancer literature was not a measure of therapeutic effect; it was a measure of three other things — lead time, length bias, and over-diagnosis — being mistaken for therapeutic effect. The fraud Jones described was not necessarily knowing or deliberate at the individual level; clinicians who saw their treated patients survive five years honestly believed they were curing patients. The fraud was structural. The denominators were drifting; the cohorts were unmatched; the endpoints were defined to favor the apparent success of intervention; and the publishing apparatus rewarded positive findings while burying negative ones.

The single most damaging element of the argument, in Peat's reading, was the lead-time-bias point. Earlier diagnosis, by itself, lengthens the apparent survival "from diagnosis" without changing the date of death. If a tumor is diagnosed at age 60 and the patient dies at 65, the five-year survival statistic counts the patient as a survivor. If the same tumor in the same patient had been diagnosed at age 55 (by a screening program), the patient still dies at 65 — but now the "ten-year survival" statistic counts the patient as a survivor. The numerator changes; the date of death does not. Multiplied across hundreds of thousands of patients in the breast-mammography era and the prostate-PSA era, lead-time bias is enough to produce the entire "declining cancer mortality" headline without any individual patient being saved.

Length bias — the over-representation of slow-growing tumors in screen-detected cohorts — compounds the effect. A breast-cancer screening program detects more tumors per unit time than a clinical-diagnosis-only stream, but the additional tumors are disproportionately the indolent ones that would never have killed the patient. Including them in the cure-rate denominator inflates the apparent cure rate without curing anyone, because the indolent tumors were always going to be "survived." The 1990s and 2000s ductal-carcinoma-*in-situ* (DCIS) explosion that Peat documented in [[breast-cancer|*Breast Cancer*]] — a 1000+% increase in DCIS diagnosis with no corresponding change in breast-cancer mortality — is the case study Jones's 1956 framework predicted in advance.

Selection bias was Jones's third pillar. Patients accepted into surgical or radiation series were not randomly drawn from the cancer population. They were younger, healthier, better-nourished, and more functional than the all-comers denominator. The treated cohort's outcomes were partly the outcomes their baseline health predicted. To attribute the outcome difference between treated and untreated patients to the treatment, Jones argued, required matching on baseline health — and when the matching was done, the apparent treatment effect collapsed across most solid-tumor categories.

## How mainstream oncology suppressed the work

Jones's critique was not censored. It was something more sophisticated than that. It was *absorbed without consequence*. His papers were published in legitimate journals, indexed in the National Library of Medicine, available to any researcher who looked for them. The American Cancer Society did not denounce him; it did not need to. The discipline simply continued reporting cure rates by the methods Jones had criticized, year after year, while the careers and grant lines of statistical critics terminated quietly. Jones himself remained a Berkeley professor in good standing until his death, but no school of "Hardin Jones cancer statistics" formed at Berkeley or anywhere else. His students did not become the next generation of cancer statisticians. The methodological reforms he called for — stratified survival reporting, intention-to-treat denominators, matched cohorts, explicit accounting of lead-time and length bias — were not adopted in the cancer-society publications of his lifetime, and have been adopted only partially since.

The mechanism of the suppression was selection, not censorship. Researchers whose work supported the cure-rate narrative received grants, journal placements, and institutional advancement; researchers whose work questioned the narrative did not. The frame Peat used in [[regulatory-capture]] — that the apparatus rewards friendly findings and starves hostile ones, and that the cumulative effect over decades is to produce a literature that looks like a consensus but is in fact a survivor bias — describes the Hardin Jones case exactly. There is no record of any cancer-society response to the 1956 paper or the 1969 *Transactions* paper that engaged with the statistical substance. The literature continued as if the critique had not been made.

Dean Burk, Otto Warburg's NCI collaborator (see [[otto-warburg]] for the connection to the Warburg framework), made the same critique from the chemical-biology side, with the addition of a personal characterization: "They lie like scoundrels," he said of the American Cancer Society, in remarks Peat quoted in [[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix* (2015)]]. The two men were working from different angles — Burk from inside the National Cancer Institute, with the laboratory perspective that Warburg's lineage had given him; Jones from inside Berkeley, with the statistical perspective the Donner Laboratory had given him — and they reached the same conclusion about the same institutional behavior in the same decade. Neither man's critique altered the institutional behavior.

## Peat's use

In Peat's writing, Hardin Jones serves three functions. First, he is the *original statistical critic* — the figure to whom Peat could point when readers asked whether the cancer-industry critique was a fringe view. It was not; it was a view held by a Berkeley professor of medical physics, articulated in legitimate scientific venues, ignored rather than refuted. Second, Jones supplies the *quantitative armature* for the broader cancer-industry case that Peat builds in [[breast-cancer|*Breast Cancer*]], [[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix*]], and [[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]] — the lead-time-bias mechanism, the length-bias mechanism, the over-diagnosis mechanism are all Jones's machinery, repurposed for a later generation's tumors. Third, Jones is the *case study for [[regulatory-capture]] in cancer specifically* — the demonstration that a major American university professor, working in a legitimate field with legitimate data, could publish a critique of a multi-billion-dollar therapeutic enterprise and have the critique absorbed without consequence by the enterprise it indicted.

The Peat quote that distills Jones's argument — the line about untreated cancer patients living longer and feeling better — appears in the cancer corpus of vault research materials drawn from the Peat newsletters and emails. The line is rhetorical at the surface and statistical underneath. It is the conclusion Jones reached after twenty years of cohort analysis, condensed into a single sentence the lay reader can act on.

## What Jones did NOT claim

Several misreadings of Jones's work circulate in the popular anti-cancer-treatment literature, and Peat was careful to mark them as misreadings.

Jones did **not** claim that all cancer treatment is harmful. He acknowledged surgical debulking of obstructing or compressive masses, the use of radiotherapy in defined palliative settings, the productive use of cytotoxic chemotherapy in acute leukemias and certain lymphomas, and the role of surgery in early-stage solid tumors where the local removal of the mass relieved real symptoms. His critique was methodological, not categorical.

Jones did **not** claim a metabolic theory of cancer. The Warburg framework that anchors Peat's cancer writing (see [[warburg-effect]] and [[otto-warburg]]) is biochemical; Jones's framework is statistical. The two are complementary — Jones's lead-time-bias mechanism explains why the cure-rate statistics inflate over time, and Warburg's respiratory-defect framework explains why the underlying biology is unimproved by cytotoxic therapy — but Jones did not himself argue the biochemical case.

Jones did **not** advise individual patients to refuse treatment. His writings were directed at the professional cancer-statistics literature, not at lay patients. The popular line that has been built around the "live longer and feel better" quote — that any patient should categorically refuse oncology care — is a misuse of a statistical argument as personal medical advice. The 1956 paper and the 1969 *Transactions* paper were written for clinicians and biostatisticians.

Jones did **not** deny that some specific cancer therapies work. The acute lymphoid leukemias, the testicular cancers, certain lymphomas, the surgical management of localized solid tumors in healthy patients — Jones's framework accommodates all of these as legitimate exceptions, the same way Peat's framework does. The argument was about the population statistics for the common solid tumors, not the universal denial of every therapeutic claim.

The distinction matters because the strongest version of Jones's argument is the one Peat actually used: that the cancer industry's *statistical reporting machinery* is broken, and the breakage produces a systematic over-attribution of natural-history outcomes to therapeutic effect. The weak version — that all conventional treatment is iatrogenic and should be refused — is not Jones's claim and is not Peat's.

## Connection to Dean Burk

Dean Burk was Otto Warburg's longtime collaborator and a senior chemist at the National Cancer Institute. His critique of the cancer establishment, made in the same decades as Jones's, came from the laboratory side: Burk argued that NCI was systematically declining to fund research on cancer mechanisms that did not fit the somatic-mutation framework, and that the cure-rate statistics the American Cancer Society advertised were not supportable from the underlying data. The "they lie like scoundrels" line ([[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix*]]) was Burk's, not Jones's, but the two men's critiques converged.

The Burk-Jones pairing matters because it shows the critique was not idiosyncratic. Two senior American researchers in two different institutional positions — one at NCI, one at Berkeley — using two different methodologies — chemistry and statistics — independently arrived at the same conclusion about the same institutional pathology in the same time window. Both critiques were absorbed without consequence by the institutions they criticized. The phrase Peat used to capture the joint observation was that the cancer industry "denigrated Warburg for commercial reasons" ([[otto-warburg]]); the same pattern applied to Jones, whose statistical critique was inconvenient for the same commercial reasons.

The Burk-Jones-Warburg triangle is the core of Peat's *historical* case for the cancer-industry critique. The contemporary case (Sauer-Dauchy 1987 on energy restriction accelerating metastasis, Schlappack-Zimmermann-Hill 1991 on glucose deprivation and acidosis as metastasis-promoters, the radiation-bystander literature, the Gompertz-Laird actuarial-curve critique) extends the same argument into the 1990s and 2000s. Jones is the bridge — the figure who shows that the critique was made cleanly and publicly fifty years before the contemporary evidence arrived.

## Why he matters today

The modern cancer-screening over-diagnosis debate has, on Peat's reading, vindicated Jones's framework in three specific cancer categories.

**Breast DCIS** — ductal carcinoma *in situ*, the pre-invasive lesion that mammography detects in volume. The 1000+% increase in DCIS diagnosis since the introduction of mammography, with no corresponding change in breast-cancer mortality, is exactly the pattern Jones predicted: an expanding numerator of "cancer" diagnoses with no improvement in deaths. The American College of Physicians and several international screening bodies have, since 2010, formally acknowledged that some DCIS diagnoses represent over-diagnosis — lesions that would not have progressed to clinical disease in the patient's lifetime.

**Prostate cancer with PSA screening** — the prostate-specific antigen era produced a parallel explosion in diagnosis with no corresponding reduction in prostate-cancer mortality. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force in 2012 recommended against routine PSA screening on the grounds that the harms (over-treatment, surgical complications, hormonal-therapy side effects) exceeded the demonstrated benefit at the population level — a position the urology profession resisted but did not refute. The "active surveillance" strategy that has emerged as the preferred management for low-risk prostate cancer is, in effect, the Jones recommendation arriving fifty years late.

**Thyroid cancer** — the South Korean experience of the 2000s, in which ultrasound screening expanded thyroid-cancer diagnosis by 15-fold without altering mortality, is the clearest single case of screening-driven over-diagnosis in the modern literature. The "epidemic of papillary thyroid cancer" that screening produced was an epidemic of diagnosis, not disease.

The vindication of Jones's framework in these three specific categories has not produced a general reform of cancer-statistics reporting. The five-year-survival metric remains the dominant cancer-outcome measure in popular communication; stage-stratified intention-to-treat reporting remains the exception rather than the norm; the institutional pressure to advertise progress remains unchanged. But the underlying methodological points Jones made in 1956 are no longer fringe. They are mainstream epidemiology, taught in biostatistics curricula, cited in every careful cancer-screening review. The man's specific name is rarely attached to them; the arguments are his.

## Counter-arguments

The strongest objection to Jones's framework is empirical: the *specific* therapeutic advances of the post-1970 oncology era have produced real, measurable, RCT-confirmed survival improvements that the 1969 *Transactions* analysis could not have anticipated. Childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia survival rose from under 10% in the 1960s to over 90% by 2010. Testicular cancer survival rose to over 95% with platinum-based therapy. Hodgkin lymphoma is now largely a curable disease. Trastuzumab in HER2-positive breast cancer, imatinib in chronic myeloid leukemia, the various tyrosine kinase inhibitors in defined molecular subsets — each is a documented therapeutic advance with credible RCT data behind it. Jones's framework, applied honestly to the modern era, has to acknowledge these as exceptions. Peat did acknowledge them (see [[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]).

The immunotherapy era — checkpoint inhibition with anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4, and CAR-T cell therapies — has produced durable remissions in melanoma, lung cancer, and several other settings beginning around 2014. These cannot be attributed to lead-time bias; they are randomized-controlled improvements in overall survival in patients with metastatic disease. Jones's framework, like Peat's, has to accommodate them as a real therapeutic advance, while noting that they work precisely because they release immune surveillance rather than because they destroy tissue.

The "Jones's cohorts were biased" critique runs the other direction. Jones built his analysis from clinical cohorts that were themselves not randomized — patients who refused treatment, or were denied treatment, or were diagnosed in eras when treatment was not standardly offered. The untreated cohorts were not necessarily healthier than the treated cohorts; they may have been older, sicker, more rural, or differently insured in ways Jones's stratification did not fully control for. The contemporary objection is that Jones's untreated-survival-equals-treated-survival finding was itself a statistical artifact, produced by the unrandomized selection of his cohorts. The objection is real, but it cuts both ways: the treated cohorts were also unrandomized, with their own selection biases, and the original ACS cure-rate statistics that Jones criticized rested on cohorts no better controlled than his.

The modern survival-curve data — the SEER database, the major cooperative-group trials, the decades of registry follow-up — does show statistically real improvements in stage-stratified survival for many cancers since Jones's era. The question is how much of the improvement is real therapeutic advance and how much is the residue of the lead-time-and-length-bias machinery Jones identified. The answer, careful epidemiologists now acknowledge, varies by cancer. For testicular cancer and childhood ALL the improvement is overwhelmingly real. For prostate cancer it is overwhelmingly lead-time bias. For breast cancer it is a mixture, with some real improvement (adjuvant chemotherapy in node-positive disease, trastuzumab in HER2+ disease) on top of substantial over-diagnosis. The Jones framework is not the final word on any particular cancer's progress, but it remains the operative framework for distinguishing real therapeutic advance from statistical illusion.

## Data gaps

Jones's primary papers — the 1956 ACS-meeting address and the 1969 *Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences* paper — are not as easily available as the cancer-society literature they critiqued. The Donner Laboratory's full archive of Jones's technical reports is held at Berkeley but has not been digitized in full; the AEC reports from the 1950s and 1960s require archival access. A modern reanalysis of Jones's original cohort data, with contemporary statistical tools and the longer follow-up available now, has not been published.

The biographical record on Jones is thinner than the importance of his work warrants. The standard cancer-statistics histories (Mukherjee's *Emperor of All Maladies*, the various biographies of Sidney Farber and Mary Lasker) mention him only in passing. There is no full-length biography. The circumstances of his 1978 death have not been the subject of serious biographical work, and the question of whether his late writings (which had moved further from the institutional consensus) would have been published in his lifetime had he lived is unanswered.

The connection between Jones and Burk is documented circumstantially — they were senior figures in the same decades making related arguments — but their personal correspondence, if any, has not been published. Whether they coordinated their critiques, whether they read each other's work, whether they corresponded with Warburg (whose Berlin institute Burk had worked in for years) is unknown.

The modern revisionist epidemiology that has vindicated Jones in specific cancers (DCIS, prostate over-diagnosis, thyroid screening) does not consistently credit him. The Welch-Black over-diagnosis literature, the Esserman-Thompson breast-screening reanalyses, the Bleyer-Welch screening critiques — all of which use methods Jones articulated — proceed largely without reference to the 1956 or 1969 papers. The transmission of Jones's framework into modern biostatistics has been by methodological diffusion rather than by citation, and the historiographical record is correspondingly thin.
