# Otto Warburg

Otto Warburg won the Nobel Prize in 1931 for discovering the nature of the respiratory enzyme—the cytochrome oxidase system that allows cells to use oxygen. He then spent the rest of his career arguing that [[cancer]] results from damage to this very system, and watched as American biology systematically buried his work in favor of genetic theories. Ray Peat considered Warburg one of the most important scientists of the twentieth century, and the suppression of his metabolic theory of cancer one of the great tragedies of modern medicine.

As Peat recounted in a [[2018-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-skin-cancer-part-2|2018 episode]], he was "deeply interested in Warburg's work" when starting graduate school. But American biochemists were already turning against Warburg, despite his Nobel Prize, because his central claim—that cancer is a metabolic condition, not a genetic mutation—threatened the entire direction of the emerging molecular biology establishment. It took fifty years, Peat noted, for mainstream researchers to begin revisiting what Warburg had discovered nearly a century earlier.

## The Respiratory Theory of Cancer

Warburg demonstrated in 1923 that cancer cells produce [[lactic acid]] from [[glucose]] even when adequate oxygen is present—the phenomenon now called the [[Warburg effect]]. His explanation was direct: cancer cells have damaged [[mitochondria]], specifically damaged cytochrome oxidase, which prevents them from fully oxidizing glucose. Unable to use oxygen efficiently, they revert to fermentation.

This was not a secondary feature of cancer but its defining characteristic. As Peat described in a [[2012-02-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cancer-treatment|2012 episode on cancer treatment]], Warburg showed that knocking out cytochrome oxidase creates the cancer metabolism, and that anything that weakens this enzyme—[[PUFA]], [[estrogen]] excess, thyroid deficiency, ionizing radiation, chemical carcinogens—can initiate the metabolic shift toward malignancy.

Warburg identified the carcinogens broadly. As Peat summarized from his newsletters, Warburg showed that "chemical carcinogens, radiation, prolonged culture without adequate oxygen, and even vitamin B1 or B2 deficiency would promote first the characteristic cancer metabolism." The common factor was never a specific genetic mutation but any insult to respiratory capacity.

## The Intellectual Lineage

Peat placed Warburg within a tradition of scientists who understood cancer as an energy problem rather than a genetic one. The lineage runs from W.F. Koch—who argued in the early 1900s that cancer, allergy, and infection all result from defective tissue oxidation—through Warburg to [[Albert Szent-Gyorgyi]], who extended the framework into a theory of biological organization based on electronic charge transfer.

For all three, as Peat explained in a [[2001-01-02-politics-and-science-politics-and-science-suppression-of-cancer-treatments|2001 lecture on the suppression of cancer treatments]], cancer and seizures and allergies were examples of an "activated state" with excess electrons. Oxidation—the controlled removal of electrons through the respiratory chain—was the answer to restoring the stable resting state of healthy tissue. Cell division, they argued, is the biological default; it was oxidative respiration that made multicellular life possible by allowing cells to stop dividing and specialize.

This framing—proliferation as primitive default, differentiation as achievement of respiratory competence—inverts the genetic theory completely. Cancer is not cells acquiring new powers through mutation; it is cells losing the respiratory capacity that kept them differentiated and quiet.

## Why Warburg Was Suppressed

Peat attributed Warburg's marginalization to specific institutional forces. In his newsletter *100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*, he described how post-1950 American biology was reorganized in ways "incompatible with Warburg's older ideas." The Rockefeller Foundation and related institutions promoted molecular biology and genetics as the future of biomedical research, directing funding away from metabolic and bioenergetic approaches.

The stakes were enormous. If cancer is fundamentally a metabolic problem—solvable through nutrition, thyroid support, and avoidance of respiratory poisons—then the multi-billion-dollar cancer treatment industry built on chemotherapy, radiation, and targeted genetic therapies is addressing consequences rather than causes. Peat argued that this is precisely what happened, and that Warburg's marginalization was not an honest scientific disagreement but a structural reorganization of biology to serve industrial interests.

James Watson—co-discoverer of DNA and hardly a critic of molecular biology—offered an inadvertent vindication late in his career. As Peat noted in a [[2016-05-20-exploring-alternatives-exploring-alternatives-may-2016|2016 interview]], Watson said that if he were starting over to understand cancer, "he would study biochemistry, not molecular biology" and would need to learn the Krebs cycle. After decades of genetic research, even the founders of the DNA paradigm recognized that cancer's mysteries lay in metabolism.

## Peat's Extension

Peat did not merely advocate for Warburg's ideas—he extended them by identifying the specific agents that damage respiration in modern life. Warburg knew that carcinogens damaged cytochrome oxidase but could not have foreseen the massive increase in dietary [[PUFA]] consumption that began in the mid-twentieth century. Peat connected the dots: polyunsaturated fats, through [[lipid peroxidation]], directly poison the mitochondrial respiratory enzymes Warburg identified as cancer's primary target. The modern epidemic of metabolic and degenerative disease reflects exactly the kind of widespread respiratory damage Warburg's theory would predict.

Similarly, Peat integrated Warburg's cancer metabolism with [[Hans Selye]]'s stress physiology and [[Broda Barnes]]'s clinical thyroid work, building a unified framework in which cancer, heart disease, diabetes, and neurodegeneration all share a common origin in impaired oxidative metabolism. Warburg saw the cellular mechanism; Peat placed it in its full biological and historical context.
