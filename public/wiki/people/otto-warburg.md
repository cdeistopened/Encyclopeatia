# Otto Warburg

Otto Warburg was the German biochemist whose work — beginning in 1920 and producing two pieces of Nobel-grade discovery — established that cancer cells produce lactic acid even in the presence of adequate oxygen, an observation Ray Peat treated as the foundational fact of twentieth-century cell biology and the great suppressed insight of modern medicine. For Peat, every strand of his framework runs back to Warburg. The conviction that cells "revert to a slime metabolism" when respiration is blocked, that [[carbon-dioxide]] is the product whose absence defines disease, that [[lactic-acid]] is "the polar opposite of carbon dioxide," that [[thyroid]] supplementation is medicine because it activates the oxidative system, that [[PUFA]] is the slow chronic poison because it suppresses cytochrome oxidase — none of these are coherent without Warburg standing behind them. "Since reading Warburg's publications in the late 1960s and early 70s, and doing my own research on tissue respiration, I have been convinced that Warburg was on the right track in seeing mitochondrial respiration as the controlling influence in cell differentiation, and in seeing cancer as a reversion to a primitive form of life based on a 'respiratory defect,'" Peat wrote in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality* (2016)]].

What makes Peat's relationship to Warburg distinctive is that he treated the post-war suppression of Warburg's actual claim as the central commercial-political crime of mainstream cancer biology, and the current "rehabilitation" of the Warburg name as a second, subtler dishonesty: observation rescued, interpretation quietly inverted, patentable therapies preserved. The whole [[oxidative-metabolism]] frame is, in part, an effort to restore Warburg to himself.

## The 1923 Discovery and the Pasteur Inversion

The discovery Peat returned to most often dates to 1923, though Peat sometimes credited it to 1920. Warburg, working in Berlin, observed that thinly sliced tumor tissue suspended in a buffered medium consumed glucose at an extraordinary rate and converted most of it to lactic acid — and that, unlike normal tissue, it kept doing so even when oxygen was abundant. "From the 19th century until the second quarter of the 20th century, cancer was investigated mainly as a metabolic problem. This work, understanding the basic chemistry of metabolism, was culminating in the 1920s in the work of Otto Warburg and Albert Szent-Györgyi on respiration. Warburg demonstrated as early as 1920 that a respiratory defect, causing aerobic glycolysis, i.e., the production of lactic acid even in the presence of oxygen, was an essential feature of cancer," Peat wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2 in Wounds, Sickness, and Aging*]]. The parenthetical mattered: "the formation of lactic acid is normal and adaptive when the supply of oxygen isn't adequate to meet energy demands, for example when running."

The discovery only acquires its bite against the Pasteur effect that preceded it. In the nineteenth century, Pasteur recognized a regulatory process in yeast: introduce oxygen into a fermenting culture, and the fermentation stops. Cells, given the choice between completing the oxidation of sugar to CO2 and stopping halfway at lactate or ethanol, will choose the complete oxidation if oxygen is available. The Pasteur effect was assumed to be universal. Warburg's contribution was to show that one tissue — cancer — broke the rule. "This aerobic glycolysis, the production of lactic acid in the presence of oxygen, was defined by Otto Warburg as the characteristic cancer metabolism," Peat wrote in [[homeostasis-and-aging|*Homeostasis and Aging* (1999)]]. "The 'respiratory defect' in which the Pasteur effect (suppression of lactic acid formation in the presence of oxygen) fails to operate, consists largely of the failure to produce carbon dioxide in the mitochondria."

This is the move that shaped everything downstream. Cancer was not, in Warburg's view, a disease of cell-division genes. It was a disease of the respiratory apparatus — a cell that had lost the ability to oxidize glucose all the way to CO2 and water, and settled for the older, less efficient route of fermentation. As Peat told Danny Roddy on the [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|July 2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on the metabolism of cancer]]: "Frederick Koch in the U.S., Otto Warburg, and Albert Szent-Györgyi were all seeing the creation of aspiring organisms as developing and progressing through the use of oxygen. They saw that when you interfere with this flow of electrons by doing something that blocks the ability to use oxygen, you revert to basically a slime metabolism — a very primitive, ancient, under-organized way of using energy. All three of them saw cancer as using a primitive, pre-oxidative metabolism."

The mechanism is detailed at [[warburg-effect]]; the broader frame at [[oxidative-metabolism]]. The point of this article is the man and his place in the lineage Peat reconstructed.

## The 1931 Nobel Prize — For the Wrong Discovery

A puzzle running quietly through Peat's writing is that Warburg's Nobel Prize, awarded in 1931, was not for the cancer work. It was for the discovery of what Warburg called the *Atmungsferment* — the respiratory enzyme, later identified as cytochrome c oxidase, the terminal enzyme that hands electrons to oxygen. Warburg had isolated the activity, characterized its carbon-monoxide inhibition, and used differential CO sensitivity in light versus dark to identify it as a heme-containing protein. The Nobel Committee cited him for this. The cancer hypothesis, more famous and more contested, went unrecognized in Stockholm.

Peat noted the distinction directly in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism* (2016)]]: "Warburg, around the same time, suggested that his 'respiratory enzyme' might be acting on particles that served as catalysts, the way charcoal might. This was the work for which he received the 1931 Nobel Prize, not the insightful, but holistic, study of cancer metabolism." The Prize honored Warburg's narrowest, most chemically isolable result — the identification of the enzyme — while passing over his broader claim about what the enzyme was for. Patents and pharmaceutical mechanism rewarded the part of Warburg's work that could be turned into a target. The part that implicated diet, hormones, and the cell's redox state as a whole — the part Peat cared about — was left for the next century to rediscover and misuse. Warburg's later work, in the 1940s and 1950s, was widely considered Nobel-grade a second time; he never received a second Prize. Peat treated the silence as part of the larger suppression.

## "The Cure of Human Cancer" — Warburg's Actual Therapeutic Program

The Warburg whom Peat read was not the abstract observer of aerobic glycolysis that modern cancer biology has reconstructed. Warburg drew explicit therapeutic implications: if the defect is respiratory, the cure is whatever restores respiration — the B vitamins, hormones, and minerals the cytochrome chain requires to run.

The epigraph Peat chose for his July 2016 newsletter is Warburg's own: "The cure of human cancer will be the resultant of biochemistry of cancer and of biochemistry of man." Peat developed the corollary in his own voice: the factors that lower stress hormones, increase carbon dioxide, and lower lactate, ammonia, and free fatty acids are "vitamin B1 (to increase CO2 and reduce lactate), niacinamide (to reduce free fatty acids), sugar (to reduce cortisol, adrenaline, and free fatty acids), salt (to lower adrenaline), thyroid hormone (to increase CO2)" — [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]]. Each item supports a step in the respiratory chain whose failure defines the cancer state.

Warburg's own emphasis on the B vitamins as anti-cancer agents — thiamine (B1) for pyruvate decarboxylation, niacinamide (B3) as the NAD precursor maintaining the cytoplasmic redox ratio, riboflavin (B2) as the FAD precursor without which the respiratory complexes cannot pass electrons — was for Peat the clearest evidence that the suppressed Warburg had a complete therapeutic vision, not merely a chemical curiosity. The B vitamins are co-factors at the precise enzymatic steps Warburg spent his career characterizing. To know cytochrome oxidase as Warburg knew it was to know that copper-deficient, riboflavin-deficient, thiamine-deficient, and PUFA-poisoned cells were all running the same broken machine. The treatment was to fix the machine. The mainstream's later choice — to look for "precisely targeted poisons" that would kill the cells running the broken machine without harming the cells running it correctly — was a refusal of the framework, not an extension of it. Peat sharpened the contrast with a Warburg quote he returned to: "No way can be imagined that is scientifically better founded to prevent and cure a disease, the prime cause of which is an impaired respiration."

## The Lineage: Koch, Szent-Györgyi, Burk

Warburg was not alone, and one of Peat's regular emphases was that the respiratory-defect theory of cancer was a multi-investigator consensus before it was officially erased. "While Warburg was investigating the roles of glycolysis and respiration in cancer, a physician with a background in chemistry, W.F. Koch, in Detroit, was showing that the ability to use oxygen made the difference between health and sickness, and that the cancer metabolism could be corrected by restoring the efficient use of oxygen," Peat wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]]. "He argued that a respiratory defect was responsible for immunodeficiency, allergy, and defective function of muscles, nerves, and secretory cells, as well as cancer. Koch's idea of cancer's metabolic cause and its curability directly challenged the doctrine of the genetic irreversibility of cancer that was central to governmental and commercial medical commitments."

The third figure was [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Albert Szent-Györgyi]], the 1937 Nobel laureate whose electron-flow framing of life paired with Warburg's respiratory-defect framing of cancer (the distinction Peat repeatedly drew between Warburg's mitochondria-as-localized-energy-source and Szent-Györgyi's respiration-holds-structural-proteins-in-electronic-state framings — both in [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]] — is developed at [[albert-szent-gyorgyi]]). Szent-Györgyi's later water-and-protein work, and his interactions with [[gilbert-ling|Gilbert Ling]], became another channel through which Warburg's framework reached Peat.

Within Warburg's own laboratory, the figure who most concerned Peat was Dean Burk, "who was a collaborator of Otto Warburg's for many years," as Peat wrote in [[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix*]]. Burk worked at the National Cancer Institute and put on record his judgment of the American Cancer Society's claims about cancer statistics: "They lie like scoundrels." Burk's testimony mattered because it placed an American insider, with no German wartime baggage, in the same intellectual position Warburg held — and showed the same suppression operating in his own country. The "scoundrels" line was epidemiological, not personal: it referred to the statistical machine, which Peat documented in the 1970s by tracing how previously precancerous diagnoses were silently reclassified as "cancers cured," that buried the failure of post-Warburg cancer medicine.

## The Post-War Suppression

The story Peat told about what happened to Warburg's work after the Second World War is one of the most consistent strands in his writing. The Pacific and Atlantic wars had isolated German scientists from American grant systems; Warburg, "of the famous Jewish banking family, continued his work in Germany with the support of the government, despite his open opposition to Nazism," Peat wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]]. "In the years after the war, nothing positive could be said in the US about his work on cancer."

Peat treated this as commercial rather than scientific. "The metabolic interpretation of disease that had been making progress for several decades was suddenly submerged when government research financing began concentrating on genetic and viral interpretations of disease." The redirection was simultaneous across disease categories — heart disease became cholesterol-driven, mental illness became neurotransmitter-driven, cancer became gene-driven — and the common factor was migration onto patentable molecular targets and away from physiological frameworks that pointed at diet, sunlight, hormones, and minerals. "For about 70 years, the cancer establishment denigrated Warburg for commercial reasons," Peat wrote in his [[cancer|cancer email]]. From the late 1940s, when post-war American medicine consolidated around the NIH funding structure, to the rediscovery of "cancer metabolism" in the late 2000s, Warburg was a nearly forbidden citation.

The mechanism of suppression was selection, not censorship. Researchers who pursued metabolic frameworks found their grants harder to renew, their students harder to graduate. The frameworks were preserved in corners — Peat learned them at the University of Oregon in the late 1960s, and many of the figures he cited were Eastern European or Russian, working in different funding ecologies — but they vanished from mainstream Anglophone training.

## Lactate as Sign Versus Cause: Peat's Friendly Amendment

The one substantive disagreement Peat had with Warburg was minor in metaphor and large in implication, and he was careful about both. "Otto Warburg apparently thought of lactate as simply a sign of the respiratory defect that characterizes cancer. V.S. Shapot at least hinted at its possible role in turning on the catabolic reactions leading to cancer cachexia (wasting). I think a good case can be made for lactate as the cause of the respiratory defect in cancer, just as it is usually the immediate cause of the respiratory derangement of hyperventilation on the organismic level," Peat wrote in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality*]].

The amendment matters because it converts Warburg's diagnostic observation into a self-reinforcing pathological process. If lactate is merely the smoke, the cure is to repair the fire of respiration and the smoke will lift. If lactate is also the kindling, the cure must remove it directly. Peat's clinical recommendations — bag-breathing to raise CO2 and acidify the blood and shift the equilibrium away from lactate accumulation, methylene blue and DCA to reactivate pyruvate dehydrogenase, niacinamide and thiamine to support the steps lactate has bottlenecked, sugar to displace fatty-acid oxidation that worsens the lactate yield — make sense only if lactate is a driver and not merely an indicator. "Lactate should stand out as a polar opposite of carbon dioxide," he told Roddy in *Generative Energy* episode 35. "I think a basic medical test should be the level of lactate in your blood versus total carbon dioxide. The higher lactate gets, the closer you are to being dead."

Peat presented the amendment as an extension of Warburg's framework, not a correction of it. Warburg had identified the defect; Peat was filling in the feedback loop that explained why it was so hard to reverse and what specifically had to be done to break the cycle.

## The Modern Rehabilitation and Its Distortions

Beginning in the early 2000s, "Warburg" began appearing again in mainstream cancer literature, and Peat treated this rehabilitation with more anger than the original suppression had elicited. The reason was that the modern usage took Warburg's observation while inverting his interpretation. "It's nice that some cancer researchers are now remembering Warburg, but unfortunately they are usually just fitting the fact of cancer's aerobic glycolysis into the genetic mutant cell paradigm, thinking of the respiratory defect as just another opportunity for killing the evil deviant cancer cell, rather than looking for the causes of the respiratory defect," he wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]].

The fashionable framing, associated with researchers like Matthew Vander Heiden and Lewis Cantley, holds that aerobic glycolysis is not a defect at all. It is an adaptive choice by metabolically competent cancer cells, because the precursors of glycolysis (ribose for nucleotides, glycerol for membranes, amino acids for protein synthesis) are exactly what a rapidly dividing cell needs. On this view, the Warburg effect is the chosen metabolic posture of cells with intact mitochondria. The implication for therapy is that one can target the glycolytic machinery — pyruvate kinase isoform M2, hexokinase, glucose transporters — and starve the tumor of its preferred substrate without addressing respiration at all.

Peat treated this as a category error. "Many people are now writing about 'cancer metabolism,' especially Otto Warburg's 1923 discovery of cancer's 'aerobic glycolysis,' but most of them are taking his ideas out of context, to make them fit into the idea that the prime cause of cancer is a genetic defect, whose metabolic consequences might make it possible to kill the mutated cells without harming the rest of the organism," he wrote in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]]. "When Warburg observed the production of large amounts of lactic acid by cancer cells in the presence of oxygen, and described it as 'impaired respiration,' with an absence of the 'Pasteur effect,' he was placing the cancer problem exactly at the center of the question of the nature of life."

The blunter line came in his [[cancer|cancer email]]: "The present use of his name by mainline researchers for immature ideas about cancer physiology shows the quality of biomedical education in the US and Europe." The Warburg-as-anabolic-adaptation school had taken his vocabulary while abandoning his diagnosis — and the targeted-glycolysis therapies it promised remained clinically thin, while the simple measures Warburg's framework actually pointed to (thyroid, B vitamins, CO2, sugar, sunlight) stayed at the margins.

## Warburg in Peat's Own Research

Peat read Warburg as a graduate student at the University of Oregon in the late 1960s, and the encounter is reported in [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]] as a turning point in his own work. "Although I had investigated the association of estrogen with cancer, and knew from my own experience with migraines that stress, diet and hormones interacted in powerful ways, when I began to investigate the oxidative metabolism of the uterus I didn't realize that it would involve a convergence of several of my main interests. I was familiar with Otto Warburg's famous idea that cancer is caused by a 'respiratory defect,' and I knew that aged tissue has a diminished respiratory capacity," he wrote.

The PhD thesis grew out of this convergence. Peat measured oxygen consumption and CO2 production of uterine tissue under various hormonal conditions, and found that estrogen acted on the uterus in much the way Warburg had described a tumor: depressing complete oxidation, raising lactate, creating a state of "estrogen dominance" that resembled the cancer metabolic state. "The idea of many factors acting in the same direction, and tending to have a cumulative effect, seemed to me to have a general biological significance. It seemed to say something about the nature of Warburg's 'respiratory defect' in cancer. The thyroid hormone, which governs respiration, is suppressed by estrogen, and by unsaturated fats." The thesis was the seed of the entire framework — estrogen, PUFA, thyroid, and aging all converging back on Warburg's respiratory defect.

Harry Rubin's later cell-culture work sealed the priority claim against the genetic theory: "Harry Rubin's studies of cells in culture have expanded Warburg's picture of the process of cancerization, showing that genetic changes occur only after the cells have been transformed into cancer," Peat wrote in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality*]]. The metabolic shift came first; the mutations followed. The implication that the cancer state was reversible — that restoring respiration could un-cancer a cell, as some of Koch's clinical reports had claimed — was a direct attack on the genetic-irreversibility dogma that justified the entire chemotherapy industry.

## Warburg in the Triad of Peat's Heroes

Across forty years, the figures Peat cited as foundational are remarkably stable: [[broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]] for the clinical reality of hypothyroidism, [[hans-selye|Hans Selye]] for the stress-cascade, [[gilbert-ling|Gilbert Ling]] for the cell-water-protein framework, [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Albert Szent-Györgyi]] for the electron-flow conception of life — and Warburg, recurring more often than any of the others, for the respiratory-defect theory of cancer. In his [[immunity-hormones-and-yeast-infections|*Immunity, Hormones and Yeast Infections* (1985)]] newsletter, Peat listed "Albert Szent-Györgyi, Otto Warburg, Max Gerson, and Broda Barnes" as researchers who contributed supporting evidence for the importance of respiration in resistance to disease. The pairing with Gerson is telling: Gerson was a clinician who treated cancer with juice, coffee enemas, and a high-potassium low-sodium diet, and reported many remissions. Warburg gave Gerson the theoretical framework — restore respiration, restore differentiation — and Peat treated the unfashionable Gerson reports as supporting rather than discrediting the underlying theory.

The respiratory-defect theory was not just a hypothesis about cancer. It was a claim about what life is and what disease is. "Each stage of evolutionary history has its characteristic metabolic chemistry. By protecting and promoting mitochondrial respiration we are contributing to our own evolution," Peat wrote, extending Szent-Györgyi. Warburg had seen the bottom of the slide. Peat spent his career trying to keep his readers off it.

## Counter-arguments

- **The genetic theory of cancer.** Modern oncology overwhelmingly treats cancer as a disease of accumulated somatic mutations. Genome sequencing of thousands of tumors has demonstrated extensive mutational signatures, characteristic patterns by tissue and exposure type, and clonal evolution under treatment. The standard interpretation holds that driver mutations precede and cause the metabolic shift Warburg observed, not the reverse. Peat's reading of Rubin's culture experiments — that the metabolic shift precedes the mutations — is a minority position in mainstream oncology and has not been confirmed by subsequent large-scale sequencing work.
- **The Vander Heiden / Cantley "anabolic adaptation" framing.** The dominant current interpretation in metabolic-cancer biology is that aerobic glycolysis is not a defect but a biosynthetic choice. Tumor cells with demonstrably intact and abundant mitochondria still favor lactate production because the glycolytic intermediates feed nucleotide, lipid, and amino-acid biosynthesis required for rapid division. Peat's claim that mitochondrial respiration is genuinely impaired in cancer cells is contested.
- **The Nazi-protection biographical reading.** Some historians argue that the post-war silence around Warburg reflected the difficulty of celebrating a German researcher whose institute had been protected by the Nazi regime (because Hitler feared cancer), rather than a commercial conspiracy against the metabolic framework. Peat's commercial-suppression framing is one historical reading; the political-biographical reading is another.
- **The lactate-as-fuel rehabilitation.** George Brooks and others have reframed lactate over the last three decades as a normal inter-organ fuel — shuttled between glycolytic and oxidative tissues, used by the heart and brain as a preferred substrate. On this view, the "lactic acid is poison" framing Peat extended from Warburg is overstated. Peat would presumably distinguish acute exercise-driven lactate (transient, oxygen-debt context) from chronic resting elevation (the cancer signature), but this distinction is not always sharp in his writing.
- **Warburg's own later contradictions.** Warburg's positions on diet and supplementation in the 1950s were idiosyncratic and not entirely consistent with the program Peat reconstructs. The "Warburg" who appears in Peat's writing is more systematic than the historical Warburg sometimes was.
- **DCA and methylene blue have not delivered.** Peat treated dichloroacetate as a confirmation of the framework — a drug that reactivates pyruvate dehydrogenase. Subsequent clinical trials have been mixed at best. The respiratory-restoration strategy has produced fewer clinical wins than the framework would predict, and this gap is not directly addressed in Peat's writing.

## Data gaps

- **The role of mitochondrial DNA mutations.** Peat acknowledged that mitochondrial genes can mutate, but treated this as downstream of physiological stress. Inherited mitochondrial disease (Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy, MELAS, MERRF) suggests primary mitochondrial gene defects do produce respiratory dysfunction without prior physiological insult, and how Peat would reconcile this with the strict "physiology first" reading is unclear.
- **Warburg's own data and the modern reproducibility question.** Many of Warburg's specific quantitative claims have not been systematically retested with modern instrumentation. Whether his core observations replicate at modern resolution is partly known and partly not.
- **Dose-response of B-vitamin therapy in cancer.** Peat consistently recommended thiamine and niacinamide in cancer states, but the formal dose-response work that would establish where supplementation begins and stops helping has not been done in any rigorous clinical setting.
- **The PET-scan paradox.** PET scans show most tumors are bright on FDG-PET, confirming avid glucose uptake. But the relationship between uptake and the actual respiratory state of the tumor varies considerably by tumor type. The clinical implications for dietary glucose restriction remain disputed, and Peat's framework against it is grounded in physiology but not in modern PET data.
- **Warburg's relationships with Koch and Gerson.** Peat treated all three as one school. Whether Warburg corresponded with or endorsed Koch's clinical work is not documented in Peat's writing, and the historical record is thin.
- **Pediatric cancer.** Childhood cancers (Wilms tumor, neuroblastoma, leukemias) appear in patients without plausible PUFA accumulation or chronic stress exposure. Whether the respiratory-defect framework accommodates these is not directly addressed.
- **Aged mitochondria vs. cancer mitochondria.** Peat treated aging and cancer as the same defect on different timescales, but the molecular signature of the aged mitochondrion (mtDNA deletions, ETC assembly defects) and the cancer mitochondrion (often functional but rewired) are not the same.
