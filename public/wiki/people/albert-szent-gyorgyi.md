# Albert Szent-Györgyi

Albert Szent-Györgyi (1893–1986) was the Hungarian biochemist whose Nobel Prize in 1937 — awarded for the isolation of vitamin C and for prior work on the biological oxidations that became the citric acid cycle — is the smallest part of why Peat read him for half a century. Across forty years of newsletters, interviews, and books, Peat returned to Szent-Györgyi more often than to any other twentieth-century scientist, and treated him as the deepest intellectual ancestor of the entire bioenergetic framework. [[Otto Warburg|Warburg]] gave Peat the respiratory defect; [[Gilbert Ling|Ling]] gave him the structured cytoplasm; [[Broda Barnes|Barnes]] gave him the clinical signature; [[Hans Selye|Selye]] gave him the stress cascade. Szent-Györgyi gave him the unifying physical picture that ties all of those together: life is the flow of electrons through a structured protein-water medium, the flow itself organizes the medium, and disease is what happens when the flow slows or the medium melts.

The "highest respect" Peat said Szent-Györgyi reserved for the unfashionable parts of medicine — the [[W.F. Koch|Koch]] line of free-radical therapy, the heretical claim that cancer is a metabolic-electronic disease rather than a genetic one — is the same respect Peat reserved for Szent-Györgyi. He is the figure Peat turned to when he wanted to remind a reader that the textbook is not the world, that pigments inside the body have electronic-metabolic functions rather than decorative ones, that the "Hopkins electron pool" stored in cellular proteins is the actual currency of life. "The basic texture of research consists of dreams into which the threads of reasoning, measurement and calculation are woven," runs the Szent-Györgyi epigraph Peat chose for his Q1 2022 newsletter [[carcinogenic-metabolism|*Carcinogenic Metabolism*]]. Peat returned to that line because it described his own method.

What makes Szent-Györgyi distinctive within Peat's pantheon is the breadth of the bridge he built. He demonstrated experimentally that ATP relaxes muscle and that muscle can contract without splitting it. He proposed in 1941, in a single short *Science* paper, that cellular proteins might function as semiconductors — a claim solid-state physics would not catch up to for thirty years. He built the framework of electron-donor-acceptor pairs ("D-A pairs") that contraction, division, and consciousness might all express. He named the categories — retine, promine, the dicarbonyl methylglyoxal — that he believed cancer reflected the failure of. He spent his American decades at Woods Hole pursuing what he called "submolecular biology" — the claim that biological function lives below the level of the molecule, in the behavior of the electrons that move between molecules — while the molecular biology consensus of Watson, Crick, and the gene-protein dogma swept past him. Peat's framework is what Szent-Györgyi's submolecular biology looks like applied to clinical medicine.

## Biographical Sketch: Hungary, Paprika, Cambridge, Woods Hole

Szent-Györgyi was born in Budapest in 1893 into a family of three generations of scientists on his mother's side. He served as a medic in the First World War, was wounded, deserted (he had decided the war was a fraud), and finished medical school in 1917. The interwar decades were a long migration through Europe's biochemical laboratories: Bratislava, Prague, Berlin, Hamburg, Leiden, Groningen. He spent four years at Cambridge in the late 1920s under Frederick Gowland Hopkins, whose conception of "biological oxidation" as a chain of dehydrogenations passed from substrate to substrate would shape Szent-Györgyi's career. The Hopkins influence runs throughout Peat's later citations: the "Hopkins electron pool" Peat referenced in his 1989 *Townsend Letter* essay [[szent-gyorgyi-energy|*Szent-Györgyi's Energy*]] is the Cambridge inheritance.

The vitamin C work began at Cambridge with a reducing agent Szent-Györgyi isolated from adrenal cortex and named "hexuronic acid." Returning to Hungary in 1930 to take the chair of medical chemistry at Szeged, he obtained large quantities of the same substance from paprika — the Hungarian red pepper grown around Szeged in industrial quantities. By 1932 he had identified hexuronic acid as the antiscorbutic factor and renamed it ascorbic acid. The 1937 Nobel Prize was for vitamin C and for the work on the dicarboxylic acids of the cycle that Hans Krebs would complete the same year as the citric acid cycle. Szent-Györgyi's contribution was the sequence fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate → succinate and the demonstration that these acids functioned catalytically — small amounts were enormously effective in stimulating muscle respiration — implying they cycled rather than being consumed.

The Nobel money he donated to the Finnish war effort during the Winter War of 1939–40. The Szeged years overlapped with the rise of the Hungarian Arrow Cross and the German occupation. Szent-Györgyi worked actively in the anti-Nazi resistance during the war, served as a secret emissary to the Allies through Istanbul in 1943, and was personally hunted by the Gestapo and Hitler. The story of his hiding from arrest, the asylum offered by the Swedish embassy, and the rescue of Hungarian Jews from his laboratory is documented in his own memoirs and biographies. After the war, occupied Hungary turned Stalinist; the Soviet-aligned regime offered Szent-Györgyi the presidency of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences, and his refusal led to a forced emigration. He arrived in the United States in 1947.

His American decades were spent largely at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Woods Hole, Massachusetts, with funding eventually channeled through the Albert Szent-Györgyi National Foundation for Cancer Research (later the National Foundation for Cancer Research) — the funding stream that the NIH was unwilling to provide for his "submolecular" work. The Woods Hole years produced the major theoretical works Peat cited repeatedly: *Bioenergetics* (1957), *Introduction to a Submolecular Biology* (1960), *The Living State* (1972), and *Electronic Biology and Cancer* (1976). He died in 1986 at age 93. The shadow of his death appears in Peat's 1989 *Townsend Letter* essay, which begins: "Since Albert Szent-Györgyi died I have been thinking about his idea that the 'Hopkins electron pool,' stored in cellular proteins provides the energy for cell division."

## Key Contributions: From Ascorbic Acid to Submolecular Biology

The contributions Peat cited fall into four overlapping bodies of work, all of which Peat treated as expressions of one underlying intuition about how life uses electrons.

### Vitamin C and biological oxidation

The discovery of ascorbic acid was the part of Szent-Györgyi's work that the Nobel Committee found legible. The deeper claim he never abandoned, and that Peat developed in detail, was that ascorbic acid was not simply an antiscorbutic vitamin but a catalyst at the level of the respiratory chain itself. "Early in his research Szent-Györgyi believed that vitamin C was one of the catalysts supporting oxidative metabolism in animals," Peat wrote in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism* (July 2016)]]. "The ordinary ascorbic acid (which contains one carbonyl group, activated by its conjugation with double bonded carbons) is, in the cell, oxidized to dehydroascorbate, a tricarbonyl, with a relatively stable free radical intermediate state, semidehydroascorbate. The oxidized form greatly predominates in healthy cells, but in cancer cells the reduced form is increased. This corresponds to the generally less oxidized state of cancer cells, or of normal cells that are in an excessively excited state, relative to the availability of oxygen."

This passage compresses an enormous claim. Vitamin C, for Szent-Györgyi and for Peat, was a redox indicator, not just a scurvy preventive. The ratio of dehydroascorbate (oxidized) to ascorbate (reduced) in a tissue reports the redox state of the cytoplasm. A healthy cell, running full oxidative metabolism, sits with the dehydroascorbate form dominant — because the electrons that vitamin C donates are being picked up and used by an actively respiring system. A cancer cell, a stressed cell, a hypoxic cell, accumulates the reduced ascorbate form because there is no working electron acceptor downstream. This is the same logic Peat applied to the NAD+/NADH ratio in [[oxidative-metabolism]]: the ratio of oxidized to reduced cofactors *is* the cell's metabolic state, and vitamin C is one of the cofactors. The pop-nutrition picture of ascorbic acid as a "scavenging antioxidant" had the polarity backward in exactly the way Peat thought twentieth-century nutrition science had inverted Szent-Györgyi's framework — and he made the inversion explicit in *Generative Energy* episode 35: "Dehydroascorbate is the predominant form in a healthy working cell, and that shifts in the direction of ascorbic acid (reduced form) in cancer and stress cells."

### The fumarate-malate-oxaloacetate-succinate sequence

The other half of the Nobel work was the partial reconstruction of the cycle of small dicarboxylic acids that Krebs completed. Szent-Györgyi demonstrated that adding fumarate or malate to minced muscle dramatically stimulated oxygen consumption — disproportionately to the amounts added — and concluded that the acids were catalysts that cycled. The catalytic role of these substrates is the foundation of the citric acid cycle and therefore of all aerobic energy metabolism. For Peat, the philosophical significance of the result was as great as its biochemical content: small amounts of a substance, acting catalytically rather than stoichiometrically, can govern the energy production of an entire cell. This is the form of every Peatian recommendation. Thyroid is dosed in micrograms and changes the metabolism of every cell in the body. Carbon dioxide acts as a Lewis acid at concentrations that gas-exchange physiology treats as trivial. Methylglyoxal — Szent-Györgyi's later candidate for the anti-cancer dicarbonyl — would act at concentrations far below those of ordinary metabolites. The catalytic vision of biochemistry, as opposed to the stoichiometric vision, is one of the things Peat learned from reading the Szeged work.

### ATP, actomyosin, and the electronic theory of muscle contraction

In the late 1930s and 1940s, working with his collaborator Bruno Straub, Szent-Györgyi isolated and characterized the muscle protein system, distinguishing actin from myosin and demonstrating that the contractile filament is the actomyosin complex. The experimental moment Peat returned to repeatedly was the demonstration that ATP causes a precipitated myosin filament to contract — and that, after death, the disappearance of ATP causes the contracted-stiff state of [[rigor-mortis|rigor mortis]]. "Szent-Gyorgyi observed that, although ATP was involved in the contraction of muscles, its post-mortem disappearance caused the contraction and hardening of muscles known as rigor mortis. When he put hardened dead muscles into a solution of ATP, they relaxed and softened. The relaxed state is a state with adequate energy reserves," Peat wrote in his article [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]]. This is the experimental observation that Peat used, throughout his clinical writing, to argue that the relaxed state — of a muscle, of a vessel, of a person — is the energetically replete state, and that the cramped, tight, stiff state is the state of energy depletion. The Charlie horse, the menstrual cramp, the angina, the rigid arteriosclerotic vessel: all are local rigor mortis, the muscle's ATP-deprived posture.

Even more important for Peat was the 1949 observation that muscle could contract *without* splitting ATP — that the ATP-mediated effect on the contractile proteins was not the hydrolysis of a "high-energy bond" but the electronic effect of ATP's binding to the protein. "In the 1940s he was exploring the role of ATP in muscle contraction, and observed that muscle can contract without splitting ATP (Szent-Gyorgyi, 1949). Gilbert Ling's work shows that it's the electron-withdrawing effect of ATP that explains its effects, rather than its internal bond energy," Peat wrote in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]]. This is the direct lineage from Szent-Györgyi to [[gilbert-ling|Ling]] to Peat. ATP as a cardinal adsorbant, not as a "fuel" — the entire reframing of bioenergetics that distinguishes Peat's metabolic medicine from textbook biochemistry — was Szent-Györgyi's 1949 observation, generalized.

After his move to the United States, Szent-Györgyi made what should have been a discipline-defining demonstration. "After Szent-Gyorgyi moved to the U.S., in 1947, he demonstrated the effect of muscle cytoplasm on the behavior of fluorescent substances, which was analogous to that of ice, until the muscle was stimulated. During contraction, the fluorescent material behaved as it would in ordinary liquid water. This effect involved the stabilization of the excited state of electrons. This single demonstration should have caused biologists to abandon the membrane theory of cellular excitation, and to return to basic physics for their understanding of cell behavior. The implications of Szent-Gyorgyi's work were enormous for biology and medicine, and even for the understanding of semiconductors, but most of the world was hypnotized by a simple textbook model of cell membranes," Peat wrote in [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]]. The water inside a resting muscle is structured — it behaves like ice, in the sense that it constrains the rotational freedom of dissolved fluorescent dyes — and during contraction, the structure melts toward bulk water. This was the experimental marriage of the muscle work to what would later become Gilbert Ling's framework for the entire cell.

### Semiconduction and submolecular biology

The 1941 Koranyi Memorial Lecture, published in *Science* the same year, contained the single sentence that should have rewritten cell biology and didn't: living proteins might conduct electrons the way solid-state semiconductors conduct holes and electrons. "Albert Szent-Gyorgyi was probably the first person to seriously investigate the semiconducting properties of living material. Since he was aware of W.F. Koch's idea of a free radical catalyst to support oxidative metabolism, his suggestion in 1941 that cellular proteins could function as electrical conductors (or semiconductors) was very likely based on his research in cellular respiration, as well as on his work with muscle proteins," Peat wrote. The semiconductor industry was a decade away from its founding triumphs. Szent-Györgyi had reasoned from first principles — from the regular spacing of conjugated double bonds in protein backbones, from the inductive effects of bound substrates, from the demonstrable speed of nervous conduction — to the conclusion that the protein-water-ion system of the cell was an electronic device.

The framework he built around this claim was what he called, in 1960, *submolecular biology* — the study of biological function at the level below the molecule, where electrons rather than molecules are the units of analysis. "Szent-Gyorgyi also demonstrated that the combination of properly balanced electron donors and electron acceptors (D-A pairs) would cause a muscle to contract. He compared this to 'doping' an inorganic superconductor, to regulate its electronic behavior," Peat wrote in *Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*. The analogy to doped semiconductors is not loose. Szent-Györgyi argued that the cell's protein-water lattice could be doped, in the literal solid-state sense, by the electron-donor and electron-acceptor small molecules that bound to it — and that the macroscopic functions of contraction, secretion, conduction, and division were the electronic states the doping produced.

## Where Peat Draws on Szent-Györgyi

Across Peat's corpus, Szent-Györgyi is invoked at four specific load-bearing junctures. Together they form most of what makes Peat's framework distinct from a generic metabolic-medicine position.

### Electrons as the currency of life

The most general debt is the framing of life as a flow of electrons. This is Peat's deepest commitment, and it is Szent-Györgyi's frame. "Frederick Koch in the U.S., Otto Warburg, and Albert Szent-Györgyi were all seeing the creation of aspiring organisms as developing and progressing through the use of oxygen. They saw that when you interfere with this flow of electrons by doing something that blocks the ability to use oxygen, you revert to basically a slime metabolism — a very primitive, ancient, under-organized way of using energy," Peat said on the [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|July 2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on the metabolism of cancer]]. The shared insight of the three figures was not three different cancer theories. It was one picture of life as electron flow, expressed at three different scales: Warburg at the mitochondrion, Koch at the level of clinical therapy, Szent-Györgyi at the level of the cellular protein lattice. Peat treated them as one school.

The structural claim — that the flow of electrons is what builds the cell, not just what powers it — is what Peat referred to repeatedly as "energy creates structure." It was Szent-Györgyi's claim before it was Peat's. His 1937 Nobel lecture opens with the line Peat used as a touchstone: "A living cell requires energy not only for all its functions, but also for the maintenance of its structure. Without energy life would be extinguished instantaneously, and the cellular fabric would collapse," as Peat reconstructed it in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]]. "Many years later he explained that he saw cell structure as an integral conductive/semiconductive system, and cellular movement and other functions, as consequences of the flow of energy through that system." The cell is not a machine that runs on energy; it *is* the energy flow, with its apparent structure as the residue. Peat developed the same view in his own voice throughout [[oxidative-metabolism]], but the conceptual debt is to the Hungarian.

### Retine, promine, and the electron donor-acceptor framing of cancer

In the 1950s and 1960s, Szent-Györgyi proposed that two classes of small molecules govern cell division: *retine*, which inhibits growth and which he extracted from healthy tissues, and *promine*, which stimulates growth and which he extracted from rapidly dividing tissues. Cancer, in this framework, was a deficiency of retine relative to promine — the balance between the electron-donor and electron-acceptor compounds in the cell had shifted toward division. "In the 1950s and 1960s, Szent-Gyorgyi began talking openly about life processes in terms of a very precise balance between oxidation and reduction which kept systems of electrons in cells in a special free-radical-like state, oscillating between 'donor' and 'acceptor' molecules. He believed that a deficiency of the naturally occurring oxidative dicarbonyl molecule methylglyoxal might be a cause of cancer," Peat wrote in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]].

The candidate molecule Szent-Györgyi identified for retine was [[methylglyoxal]], a small dicarbonyl produced as a side-product of glycolysis. Its dicarbonyl structure made it electron-withdrawing — a cardinal adsorbant in [[gilbert-ling|Ling's]] sense — and in Szent-Györgyi's model it would pull electrons out of the cell's protein lattice, holding the lattice in the oxidized state that prevents division. Methylglyoxal-deficient cells, by this account, would dwell in the reduced state that division requires. The retine-promine framework, and the methylglyoxal candidate specifically, was treated as a fringe idea by mainstream cancer biology and quickly buried after Szent-Györgyi's death. Peat resurrected it in his 2016 cancer essay and noted that recent literature had begun to confirm it: "There is still interest in his theory, for example 'The anti-cancer effect of methylglyoxal is now well established in the literature' (Pal, et al., 2016), but that molecule can also be toxic, depending on the condition of the cell." The toxicity qualification matters — methylglyoxal at high concentrations is the glycation agent responsible for much diabetic damage — but the Szent-Györgyi insight that the same molecule, at homeostatic concentration, holds the cell in its non-dividing redox state is, in Peat's reading, basically correct.

The retine-promine framework also reshapes the Koch lineage. Where Warburg located the cancer defect in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, and Koch located it in the failure to use oxygen at the tissue level, Szent-Györgyi located it in the redox balance of the cell's protein-bound electrons. "I think Otto Warburg and Albert Szent-Gyorgyi were among the few who thought that Koch might be talking about something real. Szent-Gyorgyi acknowledged having 'the highest respect' for Koch's work, but he was clearly aware of the dangers involved in premature holism," Peat wrote in *100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*. The three frameworks are not in competition; they are different views of the same defect.

### Structured water and the protein-water lattice

The third debt is the demonstration that water inside a resting cell behaves differently from bulk water — that the cytoplasm is structured, in the technical phase-physics sense, and that the structure is what binds ions, holds excited-state electrons, and maintains the cell's resting electrical potential. The fluorescence-in-muscle experiment of 1947 was the canonical demonstration, and it joins Szent-Györgyi's lineage to what would become [[gilbert-ling|Ling's]] Association-Induction Hypothesis. Peat explicitly paired the two figures: "If people want to get a perspective on what that means — a new attitude towards what life is — the works of Albert Szent-Györgyi and Gilbert Ling are the best places to start," he told listeners on the [[2014-06-02-its-rainmaking-time-it-s-rainmaking-time-energy-protective-materials|2014 *It's Rainmaking Time* episode]]. The structured-water claim, with its corollaries about cellular swelling in disease, bound versus free ions, and the resting electrical potential as a phase-equilibrium consequence rather than a pump-driven gradient, is developed at length in [[gilbert-ling]]. What Szent-Györgyi contributed was the experimental marriage of water structure to muscle function — the demonstration that the structure of the cell's water *changes* during a normal physiological event, and that the change is electronic.

### Pigments, hormones, and the global response system

Peat returned often to Szent-Györgyi's late insistence that pigments inside the body — usually treated as decorative or epiphenomenal — were in fact electronically active and metabolically central. "(Though Szent-Györgyi for many years emphasized that pigments often exist invisibly inside organisms, and probably have electronic-metabolic functions, rather than the decorative function usually associated with pigments)," Peat wrote in [[szent-gyorgyi-energy|*Szent-Györgyi's Energy* (1989)]]. The pigment claim joins to Peat's emphasis on red light, on the cytochromes (which are themselves pigmented heme proteins), on the absorbance spectra of mitochondrial enzymes, and on the photosensitivity of biological systems. A cell, in Szent-Györgyi's framework, is an electronic device with optical properties — pigments are not aesthetic accidents but the absorbing antennas and electron-shuttle compounds of the system.

The framework also reshapes endocrinology. "When I began teaching endocrinology, some of my students didn't want to hear about anything except lock and key endocrinology, in which a hormone signals certain cells that have a suitable receptor for that hormone. But the studies of Hans Selye and Albert Szent-Gyorgyi made it clear that Pavlov's global, holistic approach to the organism in its environment was the soundest scientific basis for physiology, including endocrinology. A cell's response to a hormone depended on the state of the cell. Nutrients and metabolites and hormones and neurotransmitters all modify the cell's sensitivity to its surroundings," Peat wrote in his article [[gelatin-stress-longevity|*Gelatin, Stress, Longevity*]]. The receptor-and-ligand picture of hormone action — which dominated American endocrinology after the Second World War — is, in this account, what you get when you drop Szent-Györgyi's framework and read hormones as keys to membrane locks. The Szent-Györgyi framework reads them as electronic perturbations of an integrated cooperative system.

Szent-Györgyi himself worked on hormones late in life. "Szent-Gyorgyi spent most of his career studying muscles — from the anal sphincter to pigeon breast to tense goats. One of his most interesting experiments investigated the effects of estrogen and progesterone on the heart muscle. He showed that estrogen excess prevents the increase of stroke volume as the speed increases, but that progesterone increases the stroke volume as the speed increases," Peat wrote in his article [[adaptive-substance-creative-regeneration|*Adaptive Substance, Creative Regeneration, Mainstream Science, Repression and Creativity*]]. The estrogen-progesterone result is a small experiment but emblematic — the same hormone-axis Peat would spend decades on, examined through Szent-Györgyi's electronic lens, on the same organ (heart muscle) that the muscle work had earlier illuminated.

The other late synthesis Peat highlighted was Szent-Györgyi's anti-genetic-dogma framing of cancer alongside Warburg's. "Warburg and Szent-Gyorgyi, in thinking about cancer, emphasized that growth is the primordial function of all cells, and that the differentiated functions of complex organisms involve restraints of that primitive function, imposed by a system that has developed through time," Peat wrote in [[adaptive-substance-creative-regeneration|*Adaptive Substance*]]. Cancer is not a perversion that some genetic accident has installed in a normal cell. It is the underlying program of cellular life — division — re-emerging when the inhibitory, differentiating, electron-organizing apparatus that complex organisms have built on top of it fails. This is the same insight Peat applied to aging, to wound healing, to embryonic development, and to the question of why cancer rates rise with age.

## Specific Texts Peat Cites Repeatedly

Peat referenced Szent-Györgyi's primary works directly across the corpus. The four that appear most often:

- **The 1937 Nobel Lecture** ("Oxidation, Energy Transfer, and Vitamins"). The "energy maintains structure" passage Peat quoted in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]] is from this lecture.
- ***Bioenergetics*** (1957). The first synthesis of the electron-flow framing; the source of the framework that frames Peat's own [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]] title.
- ***Introduction to a Submolecular Biology*** (1960). The treatise stating the case for biology at the level below the molecule. Peat refers to "submolecular biology" using Szent-Györgyi's exact phrase.
- ***Electronic Biology and Cancer*** (1976). The late synthesis of the retine-promine and methylglyoxal framework, joined to the semiconduction picture. The cancer-as-electron-defect argument Peat developed in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism]] is largely a gloss on this book.

The 1949 paper on muscle contraction without ATP splitting (Szent-Gyorgyi, 1949) is cited in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism]]. The 1941 *Science* paper on semiconduction in proteins is referenced throughout [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation]] without specific citation but with detailed description of its content. The American Heart Association lecture on estrogen, progesterone, and stroke volume is referenced in [[adaptive-substance-creative-regeneration]].

Peat's own book [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy: Restoring the Wholeness of Life*]] (1994) is, in significant part, a synthesis of the Szent-Györgyi framework with the [[gilbert-ling|Ling]] framework and Peat's own work on uterine oxidative metabolism. The title is a deliberate echo of *Bioenergetics*. "I think Otto Warburg and Albert Szent-Gyorgyi were among the few who thought that Koch might be talking about something real," Peat wrote in *100 Years of Cancer Metabolism* — and *Generative Energy* is what it looks like to take that "something real" and build a clinical framework on it forty years after the field had abandoned the project.

## Counter-arguments

- **Mainstream rejection of submolecular biology.** The framework Szent-Györgyi spent his American decades developing — biology at the level of electron behavior in protein-water lattices, semiconduction in living tissue, retine and promine as governing cancer — was largely ignored by the molecular biology consensus that consolidated around DNA, RNA, and protein after Watson and Crick. The standard view is that "submolecular biology" was a respected old man's late hobby, lacking the testable predictions and mechanistic clarity of gene-centered biochemistry. Whether the framework was wrong or merely premature for the instrumentation of its era remains contested. The reproducibility of the specific quantitative claims (semiconduction parameters in dehydrated proteins, the electronic effects of D-A pairs on contraction) has been spotty and is not part of any modern mainstream consensus.

- **The retine-promine framework has not been confirmed.** Szent-Györgyi's specific candidate molecules for retine and promine, and the methylglyoxal hypothesis in particular, have not received the experimental confirmation that would have rehabilitated his cancer framework. The Pal et al. (2016) paper Peat cited shows methylglyoxal has *some* anti-cancer activity in some preparations, but the dose-response, toxicity profile, and clinical applicability remain unsettled. Mainstream oncology has not adopted methylglyoxal therapy, and the gap between Szent-Györgyi's framework and the genome-driven mainstream is wider in 2026 than it was in 1976.

- **The 1941 semiconduction paper was speculative.** Szent-Györgyi's original proposal that proteins conduct electrons over substantial distances was an extrapolation from limited measurements. Subsequent solid-state physics established that dry proteins do have semiconducting properties, but the conditions in living tissue — high water content, ionic strength, temperature — significantly alter these properties. Whether biologically relevant long-range electron transfer through protein lattices occurs at the scale Szent-Györgyi proposed is contested. Modern mitochondrial biology accepts short-range electron transfer through the respiratory complexes; the broader claim that the entire cytoplasm functions as a semiconductor doped by small molecules is not part of any current consensus.

- **The structured-water claim has been overextended.** Szent-Györgyi's fluorescence-in-muscle experiment is a real result, but later popularizations — particularly Gerald Pollack's "fourth phase of water" — have made claims about structured water that have weak quantitative grounding and have damaged the credibility of the underlying framework by association. The careful version of the claim (that water near proteins has different rotational mobility than bulk water) is uncontroversial; the strong version (that this constitutes a distinct phase of water with macroscopic biological consequences) is contested.

- **The "Pavlov / Selye / Szent-Györgyi holistic triad" reading is Peat's reconstruction.** The historical relationships among Pavlov, Selye, and Szent-Györgyi were not as tight as Peat's framing sometimes suggests. Pavlov died in 1936; Selye and Szent-Györgyi were contemporaries but worked in different research traditions. The triad as a coherent "school" is more Peat's organizing concept than a self-identified group.

- **Vitamin C as a respiratory catalyst.** Szent-Györgyi's early claim that ascorbic acid functions catalytically in respiration has been partially folded into modern biochemistry (vitamin C is a cofactor for several hydroxylases and a redox-active small molecule), but the strong form of the claim — that the dehydroascorbate / ascorbate ratio is the diagnostic redox marker of the cell — is not standard. Modern redox biology focuses on glutathione and NADPH ratios; ascorbate's role is treated as one component among many.

- **Pediatric and developmental cancer.** Szent-Györgyi's retine-promine framework, like Warburg's respiratory defect framework, struggles to account for pediatric malignancies that arise in cells with no plausible accumulated metabolic insult. The framework is most persuasive for adult solid tumors developing on a background of accumulated stress and PUFA exposure.

## Data gaps

- **The mechanistic chain from D-A pairs to specific clinical interventions has gaps.** Szent-Györgyi gave Peat the framework but not the dosing. How much methylglyoxal, of what purity, by what route, for what tumor type, in what background metabolic state, has never been worked out at clinical scale. Peat's recommendations (CO2, thyroid, B vitamins, sugar) are derived from the framework rather than from direct retine-promine measurements.

- **The structured-water-to-disease bridge has not been measured in patients.** The clinical predictions of the framework — that cancer tissue has more disordered water, that aged tissue has less structured cytoplasm, that recovery involves re-structuring of the protein-water phase — were partially confirmed by Damadian's MRI work (water in cancer tissue relaxes differently from healthy tissue) but have not been developed into routine clinical measurements. The diagnostic potential of cellular water structure has not been realized.

- **Szent-Györgyi's relationship to Koch is documented only through Szent-Györgyi's own remarks.** "The highest respect" line is a single Szent-Györgyi quote. Whether the two men corresponded, whether Szent-Györgyi reviewed or replicated Koch's clinical reports, what specifically he respected in Koch's work, is not documented in Peat's writing and the primary historical record is thin.

- **Late-career Szent-Györgyi positions were not always consistent.** The 1960s and 1970s writings ranged across cancer, consciousness, philosophy, and politics. The "Szent-Györgyi" Peat reconstructs is more systematic than the historical Szent-Györgyi sometimes was. Distinguishing the load-bearing claims from late speculative essays requires careful reading.

- **The structured-water work was not replicated by mainstream laboratories at the time.** The 1947 fluorescence experiment was published in *Bioenergetics* and elsewhere but did not become a standard demonstration in cell biology textbooks. Whether this reflects deliberate dismissal, technical difficulty in replication, or genuine ambiguity in the result is partly known and partly not.

- **Methylglyoxal's dual role — anti-cancer at low concentration, glycation agent at high — needs a quantitative dose-response framework.** Peat acknowledged the toxicity caveat but did not develop the homeostatic-concentration framework that would make Szent-Györgyi's hypothesis clinically actionable.

- **The Hungarian-language primary literature is largely uncited.** Szent-Györgyi wrote substantial work in Hungarian during the Szeged years that has not been translated. Whether material exists there that would alter the framework Peat reconstructed from the English-language sources is not known.
