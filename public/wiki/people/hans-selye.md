# Hans Selye

Hans Selye gave medicine a word it desperately needed and then watched as the profession stripped that word of its meaning. His concept of biological [[stress]]—the body's nonspecific response to any demand placed upon it—should have unified medicine around a single physiological framework. Instead, "stress" became a vague catchall for psychological tension, and the precise biochemical cascade Selye described was largely forgotten. Ray Peat built directly on Selye's foundation, extending and correcting it with insights Selye himself had not fully grasped.

Selye's intellectual lineage matters. As Peat traced in a [[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|2014 interview on stress and trauma]], the concept emerged from George Crile's earlier work on surgical shock—the observation that severe physiological insult produces a characteristic syndrome regardless of what caused it. Selye formalized this into the General Adaptation Syndrome: alarm, resistance, exhaustion. Any stressor—cold, injury, infection, emotional crisis, toxic exposure—triggers the same sequence of adrenal activation, metabolic mobilization, and eventual depletion.

## The Estrogen Discovery

One of Selye's most important findings, and one Peat returned to repeatedly, was the relationship between [[estrogen]] and the stress syndrome. As Peat explained, "for Selye, an excess of estrogen was enough to create the whole shock syndrome." This was not a peripheral observation—it placed estrogen at the center of stress physiology rather than limiting it to reproductive biology.

Selye demonstrated that estrogen could independently produce the tissue changes associated with severe stress: thymus shrinkage, adrenal enlargement, stomach ulceration, and the metabolic shifts characteristic of the alarm phase. This finding anticipated Peat's lifelong argument that estrogen is fundamentally a stress hormone, not the benign "female hormone" of popular understanding.

## Catatoxic and Syntoxic Steroids

Selye's classification of steroid hormones into "catatoxic" and "syntoxic" categories became a cornerstone of Peat's therapeutic framework. Catatoxic steroids—including [[progesterone]] and [[pregnenolone]]—protect the body by actively blocking the effects of toxins and stressors. Syntoxic steroids—including [[cortisol]]—protect by suppressing the body's own responses, creating tolerance at the cost of long-term damage.

Peat considered this distinction essential. Progesterone does not merely dampen the stress response the way cortisol does; it actively counteracts the substances causing the stress. This is why Peat described progesterone as having "a broad variety of antihistamine effects" and why he recommended it for conditions ranging from brain injury to cancer—it operates as a catatoxic protector, neutralizing threats rather than simply numbing the body's reaction to them.

## Serotonin and Susceptibility

Selye and his associates produced findings on [[serotonin]] that Peat considered decades ahead of their time. As Peat noted in his newsletter *Serotonin, Coherence, and Aging*, "fifty to sixty years ago Hans Selye and his associates were showing that increased serotonin made specific organs and tissues more susceptible to stress and degenerative diseases, such as ulceration and sclerosis."

This observation runs directly counter to the modern psychiatric consensus that serotonin is a beneficial neurotransmitter whose levels should be maintained or increased. Selye's research showed the opposite: serotonin sensitized tissues to damage. Peat built an extensive critique of SSRI antidepressants and serotonin-promoting interventions on this foundation, arguing that Selye had identified serotonin as a stress mediator long before medicine decided to promote it.

## Where Selye Fell Short

Peat's relationship to Selye was not uncritical. When asked about Selye's concept of a finite "adaptation energy"—the idea that organisms are born with a limited reserve that stress gradually depletes—Peat argued that Selye "wasn't looking far enough." The missing variables, according to Peat, were the cumulative buildup of [[iron]] and [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]] across a lifetime.

Both iron and PUFA accumulate with age, and both amplify the damage from each new stressor. Iron catalyzes free radical reactions; PUFA provide the substrate for [[lipid peroxidation]] chain reactions. The appearance of a finite adaptation reserve is really the consequence of progressively more inflammatory tissue composition. A person who avoids PUFA accumulation and manages iron levels does not exhaust their capacity to adapt in the way Selye's framework predicts—they simply face less damage from each stress exposure.

This correction preserves Selye's core insight—that stress produces cumulative damage—while replacing the mysterious "adaptation energy" with identifiable biochemical mechanisms. [[Estrogen]] accelerates both iron retention and PUFA-mediated damage, connecting Selye's own estrogen findings to the progressive vulnerability he observed but could not fully explain.

## The Stress-Resistant Organs

Selye's work also revealed that not all tissues respond equally to stress. As Peat discussed, "the heart and the brain are the most stress-resistant organs"—moderate stress causes thymus shrinkage and intestinal damage, but the heart and brain maintain function longer. This observation connects to the high concentrations of protective steroids (particularly progesterone) in brain tissue and to the heart's preferential access to metabolic substrates under stress.

The hierarchy of stress vulnerability helps explain why degenerative diseases tend to affect peripheral tissues, the gut, and the immune system before they reach the brain and heart—and why, when they do reach those organs, the consequences are most severe.
