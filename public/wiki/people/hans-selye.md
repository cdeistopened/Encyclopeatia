# Hans Selye

Hans Selye was, in Ray Peat's framework, the man who taught twentieth-century medicine to see disease as a *response* rather than a *thing* — to recognize that the same handful of cellular events lay underneath conditions that mainstream nosology insisted were unrelated. From his first observation in 1936 that rats subjected to any noxious agent showed the same triad of changes (enlarged adrenals, shrunken thymus, gastrointestinal ulceration) through his forty-year program at the Université de Montréal, Selye built the conceptual scaffold on which Peat's entire stress physiology rests. The vocabulary Peat used in every interview — "stress," "general adaptation," "shock and antishock," "exhaustion," "alarm reaction" — was Selye's vocabulary. The mechanisms Peat traced underneath it ([[oxidative-metabolism|respiratory failure]], [[lactic-acid|lactate accumulation]], [[free-fatty-acids|free-fatty-acid mobilization]], the progressive depletion of the protective steroid pool) were Selye's mechanisms refined by another two generations of work. And the central clinical error Peat spent decades correcting — the narrowing of Selye's "anti-stress cortical hormones" into a single molecule, [[cortisol]], to be exploited pharmacologically as prednisone and its derivatives — was a misreading of Selye's own data that Selye himself, by the end of his career, had begun to walk back.

"Hans Selye's studies of stress weren't widely accepted until about 40 years after their publication, but some of the main investigators of the fatigue phenomenon are still practically unknown in the universities, many years after they published their work," Peat wrote in [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]]. That sentence is the whole of Peat's posture toward Selye in two clauses — gratitude for the framework, irritation at its institutional reception, and a continued interest in the workers (Meerson, Szent-Györgyi, the Soviet stress school) whose extensions of Selye's program never made it across the Atlantic. The vault entries on [[learned-helplessness]], [[cortisol]], [[stress]], [[progesterone]], and [[oxidative-metabolism]] cannot be read without Selye standing behind them. He is the figure on whose shoulders the rest of the architecture rests.

## Biographical sketch

Selye was born János Hugo Bruno Selye in Komárom, Hungary (then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire) in 1907, the son of a military surgeon. He studied medicine at the German University of Prague, took his MD in 1929 and his PhD in organic chemistry in 1931, and arrived in North America at the bottom of the Depression. After a Rockefeller fellowship at Johns Hopkins he settled at McGill University in Montreal in 1932, where as a junior researcher in the histology department he stumbled on the observation that became his life's work. Trying to characterize a new ovarian hormone he had isolated, he injected unrefined extracts into rats and noticed that every batch — including the inert controls — produced the same constellation of effects: enlarged adrenal cortex, atrophied thymus and lymph nodes, bleeding ulcers in the stomach and duodenum. The "hormone" was an artifact. The triad was the discovery. Whatever stressor he applied — cold, surgical trauma, foreign-body irritation, hormone overdose, immobilization — the same three changes appeared.

In 1936 he sent a 74-line note to *Nature* titled "A syndrome produced by diverse nocuous agents." This was the founding document of stress research. He moved to the Université de Montréal in 1945 to head a newly-created Institut de Médecine et de Chirurgie Expérimentales, where he ran one of the largest endocrine laboratories in North America for the next thirty-seven years. He died in Montreal in 1982. He had published more than 1,700 papers and forty books, of which *The Stress of Life* (1956) and *Stress without Distress* (1974) reached the largest popular audiences, and *Calciphylaxis* (1962) and the multi-volume *Hormones and Resistance* (1971) the deepest specialist ones. The English word "stress" in its biological sense — separating eustress from distress, alarm from resistance from exhaustion — is his coinage. The fact that "stress" had a precise pre-existing meaning in engineering, where it referred to the *force* applied to a material rather than the material's *response* to it, was an irritation he acknowledged but never resolved. Peat noted the imprecision once and accepted the term anyway, because by then the alternative had no existence.

The biographical detail Peat returned to was not Selye's institutional affiliation but his cosmopolitan formation — Hungarian birth, Prague training, English-language publication out of French-speaking Quebec — and the Pavlovian-holistic intellectual lineage it carried. "The studies of Hans Selye and [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Albert Szent-Györgyi]] made it clear that Pavlov's global, holistic approach to the organism in its environment was the soundest scientific basis for physiology, including endocrinology," Peat wrote in [[gelatin-stress-longevity|*Gelatin, Stress, Longevity*]]. "A cell's response to a hormone depended on the state of the cell. Nutrients and metabolites and hormones and neurotransmitters all modify the cell's sensitivity to its surroundings." Selye's framework was a continuation of Central European systems thinking, not an American lock-and-key reductionism, and that fact mattered for what could be done with it.

## Key contributions: the General Adaptation Syndrome

Selye's central construct was the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS), the stereotyped three-phase response by which any organism reacts to any sustained noxious stimulus. The phases were named and dated early — alarm reaction, stage of resistance, stage of exhaustion — and the histological signature was the adrenal-thymus-gut triad that had launched the program in 1936.

The **alarm reaction** is the acute mobilization. Heart rate rises, blood pressure rises, glycogen breaks down, fatty acids mobilize from storage, the adrenal medulla pours out catecholamines, and within hours the adrenal cortex hypertrophies under ACTH stimulation to produce the corticoids that sustain the response. The thymus and lymphoid tissues begin to shrink. If the stressor is brief, the alarm reaction subsides and the organism returns to baseline.

The **stage of resistance** is the period during which the organism has adjusted its physiology to tolerate the continuing stressor — body temperature stays elevated, the adrenal cortex maintains a higher tonic output, the immune system operates at reduced capacity but the organism functions. This is the phase Selye thought of as adaptive in the proper sense: the system has changed its operating point to absorb the load.

The **stage of exhaustion** is the failure mode. After enough time under the stressor, the same physiological changes that defined the alarm reaction reappear — but now the adrenal cortex, instead of hypertrophying, begins to fail. Tissues atrophy. Endocrine output falls. The organism becomes susceptible to infection, ulceration, autoimmune phenomena, and death. Selye attributed the failure to adrenal-cortical exhaustion: the cortex had simply been overworked.

This was the framework Peat both inherited and corrected. In [[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]] Peat stated the inheritance with unusual completeness:

> One of the most broadly substantiated principles in biology is that a great variety of harmful causes all lead to a few forms of biological harm — the concept of the stress reaction shows the powerful implications of the principle. Stress, no matter what the specific cause, has a particularly destructive effect on three organ systems: The nervous system, the immune system, and the reproductive system. Inflammation, lipid peroxidation, tissue atrophy, the "calcium catastrophe" (when almost anything goes wrong, calcium can transmit and amplify and extend the problem, but isn't itself the source of the problem), mitochondrial decay, and similar events help to define the stress reaction in greater detail.

The principle that diverse causes converge on a small number of cellular events is Selye. The list of converging events — inflammation, lipid peroxidation, tissue atrophy, the calcium catastrophe, mitochondrial decay — is Peat's enumeration of what Selye's "exhaustion" looks like underneath the histology. Selye had named the syndrome; Peat was filling in its molecular content.

The other framework piece Peat inherited verbatim was Selye's claim that **some hormones are antagonistic to others.** "Selye believed that some hormones are antagonistic to each other. A few of the oppositions that he identified have been thoroughly researched, especially the catabolic/anabolic functions of glucocorticoids and androgens, and the shock/antishock functions of estrogen and progesterone, respectively" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). The hormone-pair as a unit of analysis — cortisol against testosterone, [[estrogen]] against [[progesterone]], [[adrenaline]] against the protective steroid pool — is Selye's. The pharmacological enthusiasm for single-molecule interventions (give prednisone, give estradiol, give an SSRI) ignored Selye's basic insight that hormones do not act in isolation: every signal is read in the context of its antagonist's tone.

## "Stress" as a scientific term — and what Selye actually meant by it

Peat noted repeatedly that the popular conception of stress as a psychological phenomenon ("feeling stressed") was a degeneration of Selye's original technical meaning. For Selye, stress was *the organism's nonspecific response* to any demand placed on it — physiological, biochemical, immunological, psychological. The stressor could be anything; the response was stereotyped. Two important consequences followed.

First, the stress response was not optional and not inherently pathological. Selye coined the term **eustress** for the adaptive form (the runner's increased cardiac output, the immune system's response to a brief infection, the post-exercise hypertrophy of muscle) and **distress** for the maladaptive form (the same response when prolonged, repeated, or running against a substrate that cannot bear it). The popular reduction of stress to "what's bad for you" lost half the framework.

Second, the response was *non-specific in its initial phase but specific in its end-organ damage.* A hypothyroid person under stress would manifest the damage differently than a euthyroid one; a person with [[PUFA]]-loaded tissues would peroxidize lipids more than a person on saturated fats; a postmenopausal woman without [[progesterone]] would suffer the catabolic effects of cortisol more than a woman with intact luteal-phase progesterone production. Selye's GAS was a chassis on which the specific clinical syndromes were mounted, and the substrate state of the patient was what determined which syndrome appeared.

Peat used Selye's nonspecific-response framework constantly. The cluster of conditions that mainstream medicine treats as separate diagnostic entities — [[learned-helplessness|depression]], chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia, autoimmune flares, recurrent infection, the postmenopausal cluster — are, in Peat's reading, manifestations of a single underlying state that Selye would have called late-stage GAS exhaustion and Peat would call protective-steroid depletion combined with [[oxidative-metabolism|respiratory failure]]. "Stress, no matter what the specific cause, has a particularly destructive effect on three organ systems: The nervous system, the immune system, and the reproductive system" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]) is Peat using Selye's grammar without modification. The cure is also Selye's, in principle: remove or reduce the stressor, supply the substrate, and the response normalizes.

## Where Peat extends or amends Selye: progesterone as the original anti-stress steroid

The single most important amendment Peat made to Selye's framework concerned the identity of the "anti-stress hormones of the adrenal cortex." Selye had attributed the resistance-phase capacity to the adrenal cortex broadly, and after the isolation of cortisone in 1948–49 (Hench, Kendall, and Reichstein at the Mayo Clinic, Nobel Prize 1950) the field collapsed into treating cortisone as the canonical anti-stress hormone. Prednisone followed. The pharmaceutical exploitation of "Selye's hormones" began.

Peat treated this as a category error of the first magnitude. In [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]] he stated the correction directly:

> Selye's work with the diseases of stress, and the anti-stress hormones of the adrenal cortex, helped many scientists to think more clearly about the interaction of the organism with its environment, but it has led others to focus too narrowly on hormones of the adrenal cortex (such as cortisol and cortisone), and to forget the older knowledge about natural resistance. There are probably only a few physicians now practicing who would remember to check for hypothyroidism in an arthritis patient, or in other stress-related conditions. Hypothyroidism is a common cause of adrenal insufficiency, but it also has some direct effects on joint tissues.

The "older knowledge about natural resistance" that Peat was protecting was specifically the role of [[progesterone]] and [[pregnenolone]] — steroids made in the adrenal cortex (and in the gonads and the brain) that sit upstream of cortisol in the synthetic pathway and downstream of cortisol in functional opposition. Pregnenolone is the precursor from which both the glucocorticoid arm (progesterone → 17-hydroxyprogesterone → 11-deoxycortisol → cortisol) and the mineralocorticoid arm and the androgenic arm all descend. Progesterone is the intermediate. Both are antagonistic to cortisol's catabolic action at the tissue level — they stabilize cells, suppress excitotoxicity, oppose estrogen, induce cholinesterase, and protect the thymus from the very destruction Selye had observed.

Selye himself had touched the progesterone story. His 1941 work on the anesthetic properties of high-dose progesterone — what he called the "steroid anesthesia" — demonstrated that progesterone at pharmacological doses produced reversible unconsciousness, indicating a direct GABAergic effect on brain function that no glucocorticoid produced. His later work on **calciphylaxis** (1962), the calcification disorder he believed underlay many degenerative conditions, was performed against a background of progesterone-and-pregnenolone protection that he documented in detail. But the synthesis between Selye's stress framework and Selye's own steroid pharmacology never reached the clinical mainstream. Peat's contribution was to insist on the connection. As he put it in [[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]: "the shock/antishock functions of estrogen and progesterone, respectively" were among the "few of the oppositions [Selye] identified [that] have been thoroughly researched." Estrogen produces shock — the very condition Selye's "alarm reaction" partly characterizes. Progesterone reverses it. The pharmaceutical industry had made cortisone into the only anti-stress hormone in the medical imagination, but Selye's own data pointed at progesterone first.

The clinical consequence is what Peat described in the [[cortisol|cortisol article]]: "All of these protective steroids block the effect of cortisol. You can take a cortisol pill or prednisone and have very little harm from it if you're fairly saturated with progesterone, DHEA, and testosterone, because they prevent the action as well as taking care of some of the things that cortisol would produce." A patient saturated with the protective steroids tolerates cortisol; a patient depleted of them is destroyed by even normal cortisol levels. The pharmaceutical use of prednisone in patients with no concurrent progesterone or pregnenolone support — the standard of care in rheumatology, gastroenterology, and pulmonology — is, in Peat's reading, an iatrogenic re-creation of Selye's exhaustion phase.

## Beyond cortisol: Meerson, mitochondria, and the bioenergetic refinement

The second large amendment Peat made was to take Selye's "exhaustion" — which Selye had attributed to adrenal cortical failure — and trace it down to the mitochondrion. The intermediary in this work was the Soviet physiologist F. Z. Meerson, whose laboratory in Moscow extended Selye's program through the 1960s and 1970s and whose work was almost entirely unknown in the Anglophone clinical world. Peat encountered Meerson through the Russian literature and treated his work as the necessary bioenergetic supplement to Selye's endocrine framework.

In [[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]] Peat made the correction explicit:

> Hans Selye showed that the thymus shrinks very early in the stress reaction. In his understanding of the process, when adaptation was followed by the "exhaustion phase," the adrenal glands had simply become exhausted from overuse. F. Z. Meerson's work showed that cortisol, and the free fatty acids mobilized by stress, have a toxic influence on the mitochondrial energy production system. Both cortisol and the free fatty acids block the efficient use of glucose for producing energy, creating a diabetes-like condition. The exhaustion problem caused by excessive stress is generalized, not just a matter of adrenal insufficiency.

This is the bioenergetic refinement of Selye in one paragraph. The exhaustion is real but is not located in the adrenal gland. It is located in every cell whose mitochondria have been damaged by the prolonged exposure to elevated cortisol and free fatty acids that the stress response itself produces. Selye's "exhaustion" maps onto what Peat (following Meerson) treats as a generalized mitochondrial respiratory failure — the same shift toward glycolysis and [[lactic-acid|lactate]] production that [[otto-warburg|Warburg]] had identified in cancer, here driven by sustained stress hormones rather than by primary respiratory enzyme defects.

The continuation was equally important: "Meerson's work created the basis for understanding several degenerative processes, especially the phenomenon of 'excitotoxicity,' in which the combination of excessive stimulation and deficient energy supply damages or kills cells" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). Excitotoxicity is the cellular-level statement of why Selye's GAS becomes terminal — the cells that fire while energy-starved kill themselves. The same physiology underlies the brain damage of chronic stress, the heart damage of catecholamine excess, the muscle damage of cortisol-driven gluconeogenesis, and the gastrointestinal bleeding that Selye had observed in 1936 without yet understanding its mechanism.

The third Selye observation that Peat amended with Meerson was the role of [[gelatin|gelatin]]. In [[gelatin-stress-longevity|*Gelatin, Stress, Longevity*]] Peat wrote: "Although Hans Selye observed as early as the 1930s that stress causes internal bleeding (in lungs, adrenals, thymus, intestine, salivary and tear glands, etc.), the medical establishment, which has the opportunity to see it after surgery, burns or other trauma, and following strokes and head injuries, prefers to explain it by 'stomach hyperacidity,' as if it were limited to the stomach. At the time Selye was observing stress-induced bleeding, standard medicine was putting gelatin to use — orally, subcutaneously, and intravenously — to control bleeding. But since Selye's work relating the thrombohemorrhagic syndromes to stress wasn't known at that time, gelatin was thought of as a useful drug, rather than as having potentially far-reaching physiological effects, antagonizing some of the agents of stress-induced tissue damage." The pharmaceutical industry had separated the bleeding (Selye's observation) from the treatment (gelatin) and lost both. Peat restored the connection: stress causes a thrombohemorrhagic syndrome systemically, gelatin (and the glycine it provides) antagonizes the catabolic pathways that produce it, and the connection between the two had been visible in 1930s clinical practice and lost by the time the 1970s pharmaceutical model took over.

## Specific Selye experiments Peat cites repeatedly

Three Selye experiments recur across Peat's corpus as load-bearing pieces of the framework.

**The rat-tube experiment and foreign-body carcinogenesis.** Around mid-century Selye implanted small glass tubes under the skin of rats and observed what happened to the tissue inside them. The rats encapsulated the tubes in fibrous connective tissue (the standard foreign-body reaction), and a filament of cells then grew down the channel of the tube from the capsule, joining the two ends. The filament cells were isolated from the rat's circulating fluids — they could exchange only with the small volume of lymph that pooled inside the capsule. Selye observed that these isolated cells aged very rapidly: within weeks, they showed the histology of cells from an old animal, while the surrounding capsule remained normal. But when he periodically drained the fluid from the capsule and let it refill with fresh lymph, the filament cells retained their youthful appearance for the rat's entire life. "Even when the rats died of old age, the filaments still appeared to be young" ([[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]]).

The experiment carries two distinct lessons in Peat's writing. First, it bears on **aging** as a phenomenon of impaired fluid renewal — a generalization of Alexis Carrel's earlier work on chicken cell cultures in which frequent medium changes also produced indefinite vitality. The cell does not have a programmed senescence; it has a need for the toxic by-products of its metabolism to be removed. Where renewal fails, aging happens. Where renewal is maintained, it does not.

Second, the same experiment bears on **cancer**. Peat described it in [[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix*]]: "When the organism can't remove a foreign object, the collagenous capsule that encloses it has a high probability of forming a cancer. This 'foreign body carcinogenesis' has been studied for many years." The same stagnant-fluid condition that produces rapid local aging also produces tumors. The Warburg-Peat picture of cancer as a respiratory-defect-plus-glycolysis state has its experimental correlate in Selye's rat tube: cells deprived of fresh oxygenated lymph for long enough revert to the primitive metabolism characteristic of both old age and cancer. Aging and cancer are, in this framework, the same defect on different timescales. Selye's tube experiment is one of the cleanest organism-scale demonstrations of the principle, and Peat cited it repeatedly across [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]], [[regeneration-and-the-anti-adaptogens|*Regeneration and the Anti-Adaptogens*]], and [[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix*]].

**Selye on stress and serotonin.** In a less-known 1965 experiment Selye showed that injection of [[serotonin]] into rats produced muscular dystrophy. Peat treated this as the first direct experimental demonstration of serotonin's catabolic action on muscle, predating the modern [[learned-helplessness|learned-helplessness]] literature by decades. "In 1965, Hans Selye showed that the injection of serotonin caused muscular dystrophy. Subsequent studies suggest that serotonin excess is involved in both muscular and nervous dystrophy or degeneration" ([[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|*Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging*]]). The mechanism Peat traced — serotonin shifts the cell out of mitochondrial respiration and into lactate-producing glycolysis, depleting ATP and inhibiting the energy required for protein synthesis — closed the loop between Selye's GAS framework and the modern bioenergetic critique of SSRIs. Serotonin is one of the chemical mediators by which Selye's exhaustion phase becomes muscular and neural dystrophy. "Serotonin reduces energy production in muscles; this is probably related to the muscular dystrophy it produced in Selye's experiments" ([[endotoxin-stress-depression|*Endotoxin, Stress, Depression*]]).

**Stress-induced bleeding (the thrombohemorrhagic syndrome) and the thymus.** Two of Selye's earliest and most replicated observations — that stress shrinks the thymus and that stress produces internal bleeding throughout the body — became, in Peat's hands, organizing facts for an entire pediatric and immunological framework. "Hans Selye showed that the thymus shrinks very early in the stress reaction" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). The thymus produces T cells; T cells underlie adaptive immunity; the chronic stress state that shrinks the thymus therefore produces the immunosuppression that mainstream medicine attributes to "aging" or "viral exhaustion" without recognizing the upstream cortisol-driven mechanism. Peat's hamster observation closes the loop: "In 1971 I found that none of the sexually mature hamsters in our lab had any detectable thymus glands during the winter, though they all had them in the summer" — a cyclic confirmation of Selye's principle in a non-stressed laboratory setting, mediated by the same winter-dark-cortisol axis Peat documented elsewhere.

The thrombohemorrhagic syndrome — Selye's observation that stress produces bleeding in lungs, adrenals, thymus, intestine, salivary and tear glands — Peat treated as one of the most clinically important and most neglected pieces of Selye's legacy. The bleeding after surgery, after burns, after strokes, after head injuries that mainstream medicine attributes to "DIC" or "stress ulcers" is the same systemic phenomenon Selye had characterized as a stereotyped feature of the stress response. The 1930s clinical use of gelatin to control such bleeding (oral, subcutaneous, intravenous) was a pharmacological match to Selye's mechanistic finding, and the disappearance of gelatin from the clinical armamentarium after the 1960s antibiotic-and-corticosteroid era represents, in Peat's view, the loss of a working clinical intervention to a paradigm that no longer recognized the underlying physiology.

## The estrogen-shock observation

One of Selye's earliest pharmacological observations was that an overdose of [[estrogen]] produces shock — the same physiological state of vascular collapse, capillary leak, and tissue hypoperfusion that other stressors elicit. "One of Hans Selye's earliest observations of the effect of an overdose of estrogen was that it causes shock" ([[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|*Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer*]]). This observation, combined with Selye's later work on progesterone as an anti-shock steroid, gave Peat the experimental anchor for his estrogen-versus-progesterone framework that runs through every page on women's health, [[learned-helplessness]], and degenerative disease.

Estrogen produces shock by mobilizing free fatty acids, increasing capillary permeability, suppressing thyroid function, and shifting the cellular redox toward glycolysis — the same end-state Selye called the alarm reaction. Progesterone reverses each of these effects: it lowers free fatty acids, tightens capillaries, supports thyroid function, and shifts the redox toward respiration. The shock/antishock pairing was not merely conceptual symmetry; it was experimentally documented by Selye himself in the 1940s. The fact that mainstream gynecological practice through the second half of the twentieth century gave women supplemental estrogen without progesterone — for contraception, for menopause, for IVF cycles — was, in Peat's reading, a sustained iatrogenic application of Selye's "shock without antishock" experiment to half the population.

Selye's broader principle that "puberty, because of hormonal changes, especially increased estrogen, can be seen as the first stage of a chronic stress, resembling diabetes, since elevated free fatty acids cause 'insulin resistance,' with slightly impaired oxidation of glucose" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]) extended the framework across the life course. The hormonal events that mainstream medicine treats as normal developmental transitions — puberty, menopause, the postpartum period — are, in Selye's and Peat's framework, episodes of chronic stress whose long-term consequences (the diabetes-like impairment of energy production, the cumulative tissue damage, the increased degenerative-disease risk) require active management with the antagonist hormones Selye had identified.

## Pavlov, lock-and-key, and the holistic frame

Peat treated Selye's intellectual lineage — the Central European systems tradition running from Pavlov through Cannon and Reich to Selye and Szent-Györgyi — as a directly contrasted alternative to the lock-and-key receptor-pharmacology paradigm that dominated American endocrinology after World War II. "When I began teaching endocrinology, some of my students didn't want to hear about anything except lock and key endocrinology, in which a hormone signals certain cells that have a suitable receptor for that hormone. But the studies of Hans Selye and Albert Szent-Györgyi made it clear that Pavlov's global, holistic approach to the organism in its environment was the soundest scientific basis for physiology, including endocrinology" ([[gelatin-stress-longevity|*Gelatin, Stress, Longevity*]]).

The stakes of this distinction were not academic. The lock-and-key model produced a generation of drugs each designed to occupy a specific receptor (the beta-blockers, the SSRIs, the H2-antagonists, the angiotensin-receptor blockers, the GLP-1 agonists) and to be evaluated on the basis of receptor occupancy and clinical endpoint without reference to the metabolic state of the receptor's owner. The Pavlovian-Selyean-Szent-Györgyian framework, by contrast, treated the cell's responsiveness to any signal as a function of its energetic and structural state — its mitochondrial respiratory capacity, its [[gilbert-ling|protein-water phase organization]], its substrate availability, its protective-steroid saturation. The same molecule that calmed an inflamed cell in one state would push an exhausted one further toward death.

Peat's clinical method — temperature, pulse, thyroid status, sugar tolerance, free-fatty-acid level, progesterone saturation, [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]] production — is the operational expression of this framework. The numbers it tracks are the substrate-state variables that determine which way a signal will tip the cell. Selye had taught Peat that the patient is not a receptor; the patient is a state.

## Counter-arguments

- **The "exhausted adrenals" doctrine and the broader integrative-medicine appropriation of Selye.** A large segment of alternative medicine — adrenal-fatigue diagnosis, salivary-cortisol panels, adaptogen supplementation — has built itself on a popularization of Selye's exhaustion-phase concept that Selye himself would not entirely have endorsed. Peat was emphatic that the adrenal cortex regenerates well and that what is misdiagnosed as "weak adrenals" is usually hypothyroidism, cholesterol or substrate deficiency, or PUFA-driven mitochondrial damage. "It's popular to talk about 'weak adrenals,' but the adrenal cortex regenerates very well... So I think the 'low adrenal' people are simply low thyroid, or deficient in cholesterol or nutrients" ([[cortisol|cortisol article]]). The exhaustion phase Selye described is real; the modern functional-medicine concept of "adrenal fatigue" is a misapplication of it.

- **The Sucker Punch / tobacco-industry funding controversy.** Beginning in the early 2010s, historians of medicine (notably Mark Petticrew and Kelley Lee, *American Journal of Public Health* 2011) documented that Selye's research program at the Université de Montréal was substantially funded by tobacco-industry payments in the 1960s and 1970s, that his published work was reviewed and edited by tobacco-industry consultants, and that his testimony in cigarette-litigation hearings — where he argued that stress, not smoking, was the primary cause of cardiovascular disease and certain cancers — was prepared collaboratively with tobacco-industry attorneys. The specific industry interest was in deploying Selye's nonspecific-stress framework as a *competing causal explanation* for the harms that epidemiology was assigning to smoking, and the funding correlated with Selye's late-career emphasis on stress-as-master-cause in his most popular publications. This is a serious historical finding that does not invalidate the underlying science — the GAS framework has held up across sixty years of independent research — but it does require honest reckoning with the political-economic context in which the broader popularization of Selye's framework occurred. The Peat corpus does not engage this controversy directly; the framework Peat built on was Selye's pre-1960 experimental work, not Selye's industry-aligned 1970s commentary, and the underlying mechanisms have been replicated in laboratories with no tobacco-industry funding. But the reader of this article should be aware that "stress" as a popular concept carries some tobacco-industry imprint, and that the proper Peat-style use of Selye's framework is the technical one (substrate-state physiology, hormone-pair antagonism, exhaustion as mitochondrial failure), not the popular one (stress is what kills you, lifestyle factors are the master variable). Selye himself signed his name to industry-friendly conclusions; the science he had done forty years earlier is independent of that fact.

- **The "non-specificity" claim has been contested.** Modern stress research, especially the work of Robert Sapolsky and Bruce McEwen, has emphasized that the response to different stressors is not as uniform as Selye claimed — there is real specificity at the neuroendocrine level (psychological versus physical stressors recruit different hypothalamic pathways), and the GAS framework's three-stage structure does not always fit the data. Peat's clinical use of the framework is essentially agnostic on this — he treats GAS as a useful organizing schematic rather than a literal three-stage program — but the methodological criticism is real and should be acknowledged.

- **Adrenal-cortical exhaustion as a histological claim.** Selye's original finding of hypertrophied-then-atrophied adrenal cortex has been partially walked back by subsequent histological studies, which show that under chronic stress the cortex hypertrophies and remains hypertrophied in most species rather than progressing to atrophy. The "exhaustion phase" may exist functionally (as Peat-Meerson described it in terms of mitochondrial damage) but not anatomically in the original sense Selye proposed. The endocrinological literature has largely abandoned the original three-stage histological model.

- **Polyvagal theory and the autonomic refinement.** Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory has argued that Selye's two-system (sympathetic-vs-parasympathetic) model of the autonomic nervous system is too coarse, and that the parasympathetic branch contains at least two functionally distinct sub-systems (ventral and dorsal vagal) whose disorders produce different clinical phenomenologies. The Curt-Richter / Peat reading of [[learned-helplessness|dorsal-vagal shutdown]] partially incorporates this refinement, but Selye's original framework did not.

- **The "stress" terminology problem.** As noted above, "stress" was a pre-existing engineering term whose meaning Selye inverted, and the resulting confusion has muddied both the scientific and the popular literature for eighty years. Walter Cannon, Selye's near-contemporary, preferred "fight or flight" and "homeostasis"; Wilhelm Reich preferred "armoring." Modern researchers sometimes use "allostatic load" (McEwen) for a more bounded technical concept. The cost of the term Selye chose is non-trivial.

- **Selye's metaphysical extensions.** *Stress without Distress* (1974) and Selye's late-career essays generalized the GAS framework into a moral and ethical program ("altruistic egoism") that reached well beyond what the physiology could support. Peat made no use of these popular extensions, and they are not part of the framework his clinical work depended on.

## Data gaps

- **The pediatric stress framework.** Selye's experimental program was almost entirely in adult rats. The pediatric extension — how prenatal and early-childhood stress maps onto adult GAS staging, what the thymus-shrinking observations mean for child immunology, whether the adult-onset exhaustion phase has a developmental analog — was not engaged systematically by Selye and is engaged only in fragments by Peat. The ACE (adverse childhood experiences) literature provides epidemiological signal here that has not been integrated with the Selye-Peat mechanistic framework.

- **Sex differences in GAS.** Selye worked mostly in male rats, and the role of the menstrual cycle and reproductive aging in modulating GAS staging — though Peat partially addressed it through the progesterone arm of the framework — was not systematically characterized by Selye and is incomplete in the Peat corpus.

- **Quantitative dose-response of progesterone in the protective steroid pool.** Peat consistently recommended progesterone as a primary anti-stress intervention but the formal pharmacokinetic and dose-response work that would establish where supplementation begins and stops being protective has not been done. The Katharina Dalton clinical literature provides some grounding for prenatal and PMS dosing; the broader stress-protection indication is empirical.

- **Long-term human follow-up of GAS interventions.** Most of the Selye-derived clinical interventions Peat advocated — thyroid supplementation, progesterone, pregnenolone, gelatin, sugar, salt, light exposure, [[PUFA]] avoidance — have not been studied in formal long-term randomized controlled trials. The case for them rests on mechanistic plausibility, clinical experience, and the underlying physiology Selye and his collaborators documented, rather than on the kinds of trial data that modern evidence-based medicine prefers.

- **The Soviet stress literature.** Meerson's bioenergetic extension of Selye, and the broader Soviet stress-research program of the 1960s through 1980s, remains poorly translated and poorly cited in the Anglophone literature. Whether Meerson's specific findings on mitochondrial damage and excitotoxicity replicate fully in modern laboratories is partly known and partly not.

- **Magnesium-calcium and the calciphylaxis story.** Selye's late-career *Calciphylaxis* (1962) developed an elaborate framework around stress-induced calcium-magnesium imbalance and tissue calcification that touches on osteoporosis, vascular calcification, kidney stone formation, and certain inflammatory disorders. Peat referenced this work occasionally but did not synthesize it systematically into his framework. The clinical implications of Selye's calciphylaxis findings for modern bone-health and cardiovascular practice have not been adequately worked out.

- **Selye's foreign-body carcinogenesis and modern implant medicine.** The rat-tube observation that long-term foreign-body capsules tend toward cancerous transformation has not been integrated into the modern medical-device safety literature in the way Selye's work might suggest. The relevance for breast implants, pacemaker leads, intrauterine devices, mesh implants, and the very large number of foreign objects modern medicine routinely places in human bodies is largely unexplored.

- **The tobacco-funding controversy and the science behind it.** A systematic re-evaluation of which of Selye's specific findings were and were not affected by his industry funding — separating the pre-1960 experimental program from the post-1960 popular-publication and litigation-testimony period — has not been carried out in the medical history literature in sufficient depth.
