# Broda Barnes

Broda Barnes was, in Ray Peat's estimation, one of the most important medical researchers of the twentieth century, and his work forms the clinical bedrock of Peat's approach to [[thyroid]] function. Barnes spent decades demonstrating that [[hypothyroidism]] is vastly underdiagnosed, that it causes not only the familiar symptoms of fatigue and weight gain but also [[heart-disease|heart disease]], [[cancer]], and susceptibility to [[infections]], and that the solution is remarkably simple: desiccated thyroid dosed to restore normal body [[temperature]]. His most extraordinary clinical claim, which Peat cited repeatedly across forty years of interviews, was that in over thirty years of practice, not a single one of his thyroid-supplemented patients died of heart disease. "Broda Barnes, a pioneer in thyroid therapy, found that low thyroid regions had high rates of cancer and heart disease," Peat told listeners on [[2019-03-19-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-health-of-the-human-body|2019 *One Radio Network* on the health of the human body]]. "He put his patients on thyroid supplements and in 30 years had no patient die of heart disease." The mainstream endocrine establishment responded not by testing Barnes's claims but by replacing his diagnostic method with a series of blood tests that Peat considered biologically meaningless.

## The Temperature Diagnostic

Barnes's most lasting contribution to clinical practice is the basal temperature test for thyroid function. The method is disarmingly simple: measure your temperature before getting out of bed in the morning. Barnes worked for most of his career in Fort Collins, Colorado, and found that a waking temperature near 98 degrees Fahrenheit, rising to 98.6 after breakfast, indicated adequate thyroid function. Anything significantly below that pattern suggested hypothyroidism, regardless of what blood tests might show.

Peat adopted this method early and spent decades refining it. In his [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|"TSH, Temperature, Pulse Rate, and Other Indicators in Hypothyroidism" (2007)]] newsletter, he explained both the value and the limitation of Barnes's approach. "Broda Barnes, who worked mostly in Fort Collins, Colorado, argued that the body temperature, measured before getting out of bed in the morning, was the best basis for diagnosing thyroid function," Peat wrote. Fort Collins, at high altitude with a cool climate, was an ideal environment for this test. The altitude itself supported thyroid function, with one study showing an 18% increase in [[T3]] at high altitude, and the cool dry air meant that a low metabolic rate would reliably produce a low temperature reading.

The trouble, Peat discovered, was that Barnes's protocol could miss hypothyroidism in warm, humid environments. "In Eugene during a hot and humid summer, I saw several obviously hypothyroid people whose temperature seemed perfectly normal, euthyroid by Barnes' standards," he wrote. "But I noticed that their pulse rates were, in several cases, very low." A person's body needs very little metabolic energy to maintain 98.6 degrees when the air temperature is in the nineties. Peat began asking patients whether they used electric blankets and ignoring their temperature data if they did. His conclusion was that "the combination of pulse rate and temperature is much better than either one alone," an extension of Barnes's method rather than a rejection of it.

In a [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on Hashimoto's and temperature]], Peat added further nuance. A caller reported following Barnes's exact protocol, placing a thermometer under the arm for ten minutes before rising, and averaging 96.4 degrees over eighteen days. Peat's response illuminated the complexity underneath the simple test. Hypothyroid people often have high [[adrenaline]], which cuts off circulation to the skin, fingers, and toes to preserve blood flow to the brain, heart, and lungs. Especially in overweight individuals, the skin and subcutaneous fat can take a couple of hours to reach core temperature because the person is thoroughly insulated. Checking the eardrum or mouth temperature alongside the armpit, while noticing whether the fingers, toes, and nose feel cold, gives a more complete picture. "Even a dead person will be warm in a hot room," Peat noted. "The best single test would be the Basal Metabolic Rate from the 1930s, measuring how much oxygen you breathe in and how much carbon dioxide you exhale. Temperature is just a rough approximation of how much fuel you're burning."

Beyond temperature itself, Peat endorsed several of the supplementary indicators that Barnes and his era of physicians used. High [[cholesterol]] often means low thyroid. [[Carotene]] accumulation in the skin suggests the liver is not converting carotene to vitamin A, a thyroid-dependent process. The speed of the Achilles tendon reflex correlates with thyroid status: quick relaxation corresponds to good function. As Peat explained on [[2014-06-02-its-rainmaking-time-it-s-rainmaking-time-energy-protective-materials|2014 *It's Rainmaking Time* on energy-protective materials]]: "Broda Barnes advocated morning temperature as a very important indicator. Other indicators include cholesterol, carotene levels, and the speed of muscle relaxation." The amount of water a person evaporates is another surprisingly sensitive measure. A person with good thyroid function should take in about two quarts more fluid per day than they lose in urine, representing normal evaporation. Many hypothyroid people evaporate less than a quart a day, meaning they are waterlogged.

## The PhD Research and the Pre-War Settlement

Barnes's scientific career began with a discovery that should have reshaped cardiology. As Peat detailed in his [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud|"Thyroid Therapies, Confusion and Fraud" (2007)]] newsletter, Barnes did his PhD research at the University of Chicago just a few years after [[otto-warburg|Otto Warburg]] in Germany had demonstrated the role of a "respiratory defect" in cancer. At the time Barnes was working, hypothyroidism was diagnosed on the basis of a low [[basal-metabolic-rate|basal metabolic rate]], meaning that the body required only a small amount of oxygen to sustain life. This deficiency of oxygen consumption involved the same enzyme system Warburg was studying in cancer cells. The connection between low cellular respiration, hypothyroidism, and cancer was visible to anyone who cared to look.

Barnes experimented on rabbits and found that when their thyroid glands were removed, they developed atherosclerosis, exactly as hypothyroid humans did. By the mid-1930s, Peat wrote, "it was generally known that hypothyroidism causes the cholesterol level in the blood to increase; hypercholesterolemia was a diagnostic sign of hypothyroidism. Administering a thyroid supplement, blood cholesterol came down to normal exactly as the basal metabolic rate came up to the normal rate." Peat's summary of this era is characteristically blunt: "The biology of atherosclerotic heart disease was basically solved before the second world war."

What happened next was not further investigation but suppression. The pre-war understanding of heart disease as a thyroid problem was gradually displaced by the cholesterol hypothesis, which treated high cholesterol as a cause rather than a symptom and prescribed dietary fat restriction rather than thyroid supplementation. Barnes's work was not refuted; it was simply rendered invisible by a shift in diagnostic methodology that occurred in the late 1940s.

## How the Diagnosis Rate Fell from 40% to 5%

The most consequential suppression of Barnes's findings came through the introduction of blood tests that replaced the clinical assessment of metabolic rate. Peat described this transformation on a [[2012-02-22-politics-and-science-politics-science-two-hour-fundraiser-ii|2012 *Politics and Science* episode]]. In Barnes's time, somewhere between 20% and 45% of the population benefited from taking some thyroid. The hormone had once been part of the ordinary meat supply until the agriculture department prohibited its sale. Barnes saw with his own eyes, across thousands of patients, that a large fraction of the population was hypothyroid by any meaningful clinical standard.

Then, around the same time that Synthroid (the synthetic T4 product) came on the market in the late 1940s, the Protein Bound Iodine (PBI) test was introduced. This test found that 95% of people had a fairly high level of iodine-bound protein in their blood, which was taken to mean that only 5% of the population needed thyroid supplementation. The problem, as Peat explained on [[2018-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-skin-cancer-part-2|2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* on skin cancer]], was that "in the 1940s, the drug industry introduced chemical diagnoses that were biologically meaningless but convinced everyone that 95% of the population is not hypothyroid." By the 1960s, new tests came along and researchers realized that the PBI test "had almost nothing to do with thyroid function." But the standard it created, that only 5% of the population is a candidate for thyroid supplementation, persisted. The basis for that standard had been proven irrelevant, yet the standard itself survived.

The eventual replacement was the [[TSH]] test, now considered the gold standard for thyroid diagnosis. Peat considered this an improvement only in the sense that it measured something real, but profoundly misleading in practice. "They eventually switched to TSH as the 'gold standard,'" he said, "but they neglect the fact that stress lowers your TSH. You can be under such intense stress with high [[cortisol]] that your TSH can be near zero while your thyroid gland is producing nothing." TSH is itself an inflammation promoter. It creates hypertension by causing inflammation directly in blood vessels. One of the effects of thyroid hormone on blood pressure is simply that it suppresses TSH. Many practitioners now argue the upper limit of TSH should not exceed 2.0, but even this revised threshold misses the fundamental problem: TSH measures the pituitary's opinion of thyroid status, not the tissues' actual metabolic state.

Barnes's temperature test, for all its limitations in warm climates, at least measured something that reflected the end result of thyroid action. The blood tests measured intermediary signals that could be normal while the patient remained profoundly hypothyroid.

## Heart Disease, Cancer, and Infection

Barnes's epidemiological work linked hypothyroidism to the three great killers, and Peat endorsed this linkage without reservation. In his [[thyroid-misconceptions|"Thyroid: Misconceptions" (1993)]] newsletter, Peat wrote that Barnes "clearly showed the protective action of thyroid against heart disease." The mechanism ran through multiple pathways. Hypothyroid patients compensate for their energy deficiency by secreting excess adrenaline. Their 24-hour urine metabolites of adrenaline sometimes reach 30 or 40 times the normal level, as Peat noted in his [[resistance-of-the-heart|"Resistance of the Heart" (1992)]] article. This chronic adrenaline excess drives the mobilization of [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] from storage, elevates cortisol, and produces the "Cushingoid" fat distribution, sudden balding, and hormone imbalances that are statistically associated with heart disease.

The diabetes connection was equally direct. Barnes's diabetic patients who received thyroid supplements avoided the circulatory complications of the disease. In diabetes, glucose cannot enter cells properly, and the body's response resembles that of [[hypoglycemia]], secreting adrenaline and mobilizing fats. Since a certain amount of intracellular glucose is needed before the liver can form T3, diabetics are unable to activate thyroid hormone, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that thyroid supplementation could break.

Barnes summarized his own epidemiological findings with an elegance that Peat admired and quoted often: if hypothyroid people do not die young from infectious diseases such as tuberculosis, they die a little later from cancer or heart disease. Peat echoed this in his [[oils-in-context|"Oils in Context" (2007)]] newsletter: "Broda Barnes' work clearly showed that hypothyroid populations are susceptible to infections, heart disease, and cancer." And in his [[stress-and-water|"Stress and Water" (2015)]] newsletter: Barnes "showed that tuberculosis, cancer, and atherosclerotic heart disease are endemic in hypothyroid regions." The geographic pattern, with high-altitude and iodine-rich areas showing lower rates of all three diseases, reinforced the thyroid connection.

Peat placed Barnes in the company of the twentieth century's most important thinkers about cellular energy and disease. In his [[immunity-hormones-and-yeast-infections|"Immunity, Hormones and Yeast Infections" (1985)]] newsletter, he listed "[[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Albert Szent-Gyorgyi]], Otto Warburg, Max Gerson, and Broda Barnes" as researchers who contributed supporting evidence for the importance of respiration in resistance to disease. Barnes belonged to a generation that understood disease as fundamentally an energy problem, and whose collective insight was buried by the rise of pharmaceutical-driven molecular medicine.

## The Liver Connection

Barnes was, in Peat's view, one of the first to grasp the critical role of the liver in thyroid physiology. The thyroid gland in the neck produces about 30% of the body's active hormone; the liver produces the remaining 70% by converting the inactive T4 into the active T3. This conversion requires glucose. Without adequate blood sugar, the liver stops making T3, metabolism drops, and temperature falls. The result is a vicious cycle: low thyroid function leads to poor liver function, which leads to lower T3 conversion, which deepens the hypothyroidism.

Barnes wrote a book about this relationship, *Hypoglycemia: It's Your Liver, Not Your Mind*, which Peat cited repeatedly. On [[2008-10-31-other-hope-for-health-hope-for-health-thyroid|2008 *Hope for Health* on thyroid]], Peat explained: "Broda Barnes wrote a very good book years ago called *Hypoglycemia: It's Your Liver, Not Your Mind*. He was one of the first to emphasize that most of the active thyroid, about 70%, is produced in your liver rather than in the thyroid gland." In a [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on diabetes]], Peat reiterated: "He pointed out that the liver provides three-quarters of our active thyroid hormone, and to do that it needs glucose. Without the active thyroid hormone, you tend to get hypoglycemia."

The insight extends beyond blood sugar. Barnes was also among the first to notice the liver-mediated antagonism between [[estrogen]] and thyroid. As Peat described in a [[2011-02-22-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-thyroid|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on the thyroid]], "Broda Barnes was one of the first to notice the importance of the liver in thyroid function." The Biskinds in the 1940s had shown how the liver regulates hormones: if the liver lacks thyroid, protein, or vitamins, estrogen accumulates unchecked. There is a direct antagonism in the liver between estrogen and thyroid, and Barnes's clinical experience with thyroid supplementation illuminated both sides of it.

## The Self-Experiment: Protein and Thyroid Dose

One of the more revealing stories about Barnes comes from his own experience with diet and thyroid dosing. For years, he had taken two grains of [[armour-thyroid|Armour Thyroid]] daily. Around age 60, he decided to increase the meat in his diet, adopting a high-protein approach. He found that he had to double his dose to four grains to maintain his normal functions.

Peat told this story on multiple occasions because it illustrated a principle central to his own dietary framework. On a [[2022-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-june-2022-kmud|2022 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]], Peat explained: "This shows that for an older person, what seems to be a good level of protein can actually be very powerfully suppressive to your thyroid function." The amino acids in muscle meat, particularly [[methionine]], [[cysteine]], and [[tryptophan]], inhibit thyroid function. [[Calcium]] in [[milk]] protects against this anti-thyroid effect, which is one reason Peat consistently recommended dairy protein over muscle meat. Barnes's personal experience with doubling his thyroid dose on a high-protein diet was, for Peat, a vivid confirmation of a mechanism he had studied extensively in the laboratory literature.

## Anemia and Temperature

Barnes connected [[anemia]] to hypothyroidism through one of the most elegantly simple observations in his work. As Peat relayed on [[2011-08-25-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-serotonin-and-endotoxin|2011 *EastWest Healing* on serotonin and endotoxin]]: "Broda Barnes believed that hypothyroidism was usually the cause of anemia. It's simply a matter of temperature. If your extremities are cold, the bones are too cold to make blood cells."

Peat provided the experimental evidence in a later interview. Barnes had mentioned an experiment in which rats, whose tailbones normally do not produce red blood cells, had their tails stitched into the abdomen to keep them at core body temperature. The tails quickly began producing red blood cells simply from the increase in temperature. The implication, which Peat found both charming and clinically important, was that "the safest way to treat anemia might be to wear long wool socks to warm the extremities." The deeper point was that conventional hematology, focused on iron supplementation and erythropoietin, was missing the most basic variable: cellular temperature determines the rate of blood cell production, and hypothyroidism lowers that temperature throughout the body.

## Practical Thyroid Dosing

Barnes's standard protocol, which Peat frequently endorsed, was to start patients on a well-standardized desiccated thyroid product and adjust the dose based on temperature response. His average maintenance dose was two grains (120 milligrams) of Armour Thyroid. Traditional thyroid doctors like Barnes usually started with 30 milligrams and worked up gradually.

Peat added an important caution regarding the starting phase. On [[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|2019 *One Radio Network* on vitamin D and thyroid]], he described seeing two patients who experienced heart pain on just 15 milligrams. The mechanism involves [[magnesium]]: thyroid hormone is needed for muscles to retain proper magnesium levels. If you suddenly increase thyroid when you have been deficient, the large leg muscles compete with the heart muscle for the limited magnesium supply, and the coronary arteries can constrict. "It's very important to make sure your diet includes plenty of magnesium when you're starting to use thyroid," Peat advised.

Peat was emphatic that Barnes's books should be read before any attempt at thyroid supplementation. "I think no one should supplement thyroid until they've read at least one of Broda Barnes' books," he said on [[#85-protein-restriction-lidocaine-for-hair-loss-brain-size-intelligence-symptom-recognition|*Generative Energy* episode 85 on protein restriction]]. This was not merely a recommendation to educate oneself. Barnes's books teach a way of thinking about the body that makes the supplementation intelligible. Without that context, a person adjusting their own thyroid dose is working blind.

## Mucopolysaccharides and Physical Diagnosis

Barnes also taught the physical examination of the skin as a diagnostic tool for hypothyroidism. When the metabolic rate falls, mucopolysaccharides, a class of gel-like substances, accumulate in the skin and subcutaneous tissues. This produces the puffy, waterlogged appearance that older physicians called myxedema. In a [[2020-12-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-december-2020-kmud|2020 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]], a caller asked Peat about this diagnostic approach, and Peat confirmed its validity: "Yes, there are physical tests to estimate the quality of the skin, the amount of water and jelly in it. Those mucopolysaccharides will go away very quickly when you get the right amount of thyroid and get your metabolic rate up."

Peat added that Barnes's books equip patients to deal with doctors fixated on TSH rather than meaningful metrics. "If you use Barnes' books, you'll know how to deal with doctors who are focused on TSH rather than meaningful metrics like metabolic rate, reflexes, and nerve conduction time." In a medical system that has largely abandoned the physical examination in favor of laboratory values, Barnes's insistence on looking at the patient remains a quiet act of clinical rebellion.

## Barnes as Personal Inspiration

Peat's own relationship to thyroid supplementation was shaped by Barnes, though in an unexpected way. As he told Danny Roddy on [[#43-body-temperature-inflammation-and-aging-copper-and-thyroid-mrna-vaccines-and-infertility|*Generative Energy* episode 43 on body temperature and aging]], Barnes's books appeared in the early 1970s, and Peat had been thinking about thyroid for years. But there was a problem: Peat had a much-above-normal metabolic rate. During a Basal Metabolic Rate test, he used up the oxygen before the test was finished, and the slope of the line suggested approximately three times the normal rate. He was aware of this for nine or ten years before finally convincing himself to try thyroid.

The result was striking. "When I did, within a few days, my rate of metabolism had come down to approximately normal." This seemingly paradoxical outcome, a person with triple the normal metabolic rate benefiting from thyroid supplementation, illustrates a principle Barnes would have recognized: the elevated metabolic rate was driven by stress hormones compensating for inadequate thyroid function at the cellular level. Adding thyroid allowed the cells to function properly, removing the need for the compensatory stress response, and the total metabolic rate normalized. Barnes's framework gave Peat the conceptual foundation to understand his own body's paradoxical response.
