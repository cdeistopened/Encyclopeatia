# Broda Barnes

Before the TSH test existed, doctors diagnosed [[hypothyroidism]] by measuring what the body actually did—how much oxygen it consumed, what temperature it maintained, how fast the heart beat at rest. Broda Barnes was the last prominent physician to insist that these functional measures mattered more than any blood chemistry. His 1976 book *Hypothyroidism: The Unsuspected Illness* argued that the epidemic of heart disease, infections, and metabolic dysfunction in modern populations was largely undiagnosed thyroid deficiency. Ray Peat considered Barnes's clinical legacy essential, even as he extended the framework well beyond where Barnes had taken it.

## The Temperature Method

Barnes's signature contribution was the basal temperature test: measuring axillary temperature for ten minutes immediately upon waking, before any activity. A consistent reading below 97.8°F suggested functional hypothyroidism regardless of what blood tests showed. As Barnes wrote, and as Peat quoted in his newsletter *Body Temperature, Inflammation, and Aging*, "As low temperature rises with thyroid treatment, the symptoms associated with hypothyroidism will disappear."

The method was elegant in its directness. Rather than inferring thyroid status from a pituitary hormone (TSH) that may or may not reflect what is happening in tissues, Barnes measured the metabolic output that [[thyroid]] hormone is supposed to produce. If the body runs cold, the thyroid system is failing at its fundamental task—regardless of what the numbers on a lab report say.

Peat explained the historical shift in a [[2018-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-skin-cancer-part-2|2018 episode]]: the drug industry introduced chemical diagnostics—first the protein-bound iodine (PBI) test, then the TSH test—that were "biologically meaningless" compared to direct metabolic assessment. By the old basal metabolic rate (BMR) standards, where oxygen consumption was measured directly using laboratory apparatus, roughly 30% of Americans were hypothyroid in 1940. Peat estimated that by current conditions, the figure is closer to 60%.

The TSH test narrowed the diagnostic window so severely that only the most extreme cases of thyroid failure were identified, leaving the vast middle range of functional hypothyroidism untreated. As Peat noted in a [[2019-10-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-brain-barriers|2019 episode]], oxygen consumption measured by BMR apparatus is "by far the best" diagnostic method—and by that standard, "ninety-five percent of the population might be hypothyroid."

## Barnes's Clinical Findings

Barnes treated thousands of patients with desiccated [[thyroid]] (Armour Thyroid) over decades, and his clinical records demonstrated outcomes that standard medicine has been unable to replicate with synthetic T4 alone. His [[diabetes]] patients who received thyroid supplementation avoided the circulatory complications—peripheral vascular disease, kidney damage, retinal deterioration—that are normally considered inevitable consequences of the condition. As Peat described in his newsletter *Thyroid Misconceptions*, Barnes showed that thyroid prevents the [[adrenaline]]-driven fatty acid mobilization that causes vascular damage in diabetics—the same [[Randle cycle]] mechanism Peat identified as central to metabolic disease.

Barnes's heart disease findings were equally striking. In an era when cardiovascular disease was the leading killer, Barnes's thyroid-treated patients had dramatically lower rates of heart attacks and atherosclerosis than the general population. He argued that hypothyroidism was the primary underlying cause of heart disease—a position that placed him far outside mainstream cardiology but that Peat considered well-supported by the physiological evidence.

## Barnes's Own Practice

Barnes personally took approximately two grains of Armour Thyroid daily. As Peat recounted in a [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-june-2022|discussion]], when Barnes switched to a high-protein diet around age sixty, he found he needed to double his thyroid dose to maintain his metabolic rate. This anecdote illustrated a principle Peat emphasized: thyroid needs are not static but change with diet, age, and metabolic demands. A high-protein diet increases the metabolic work required for digestion and amino acid processing, demanding more thyroid hormone to maintain body temperature and energy output.

## Peat's Relationship to Barnes

When Danny Roddy asked whether Barnes inspired Peat to first take thyroid, Peat clarified that Barnes's books appeared in the early 1970s, by which time Peat had already developed his own understanding of thyroid physiology. Because Peat already had an "above-normal metabolic rate," he had to learn other things—particularly about [[PUFA]], [[estrogen]], and [[progesterone]]—before thyroid supplementation became personally relevant.

The distinction matters. Barnes was primarily a clinician who observed and treated; Peat was a theorist who sought to explain *why* the treatments worked. Barnes knew that thyroid hormone prevented diabetic complications; Peat could explain the mechanism through fatty acid metabolism and the Randle cycle. Barnes measured temperature as a diagnostic proxy; Peat placed temperature within a comprehensive framework linking metabolic rate to [[stress]], [[aging]], and degenerative disease.

Peat honored Barnes's clinical courage—the willingness to treat based on functional evidence rather than arbitrary lab ranges—while building the theoretical structure that Barnes's work demanded but never fully provided.
