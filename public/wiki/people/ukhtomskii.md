# Aleksei Ukhtomskii

Aleksei Alekseevich Ukhtomskii (1875–1942) was, in Peat's reading, the Russian physiologist whose work supplied the missing organism-level concept the West never quite generated. American neurophysiology built upward from the reflex arc and stopped at the synapse; Soviet physiology built downward from the whole behaving organism, and Ukhtomskii's central construct — the *dominant* — named the level of organization at which behavior, attention, dream content, learning, and personality actually happen. A dominant is a *stable functional system* that has captured the organism's attentional and metabolic resources and is redirecting all incoming stimuli — relevant and irrelevant alike — toward its own ends. The hungry rat searching the maze has formed a dominant for food; the infatuated adolescent has formed one for the beloved; the scientist who can read a journal article while traffic noise fades to nothing has formed one for the problem. The dominant is the physiology of focus.

Peat cited Ukhtomskii across [[book-mind-and-tissue|*Mind and Tissue*]] (eleven distinct references, more than any single Soviet figure except Pavlov), in [[book-generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]], and through his 1995 [[adaptive-substance-creative-regeneration-mainstream-science-repression-and-creativity|*Adaptive Substance, Creative Regeneration*]]. Where [[hans-selye|Selye]] gave Peat the stress cascade, [[gilbert-ling|Ling]] the structured cytoplasm, [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Szent-Györgyi]] the electron-flow physics, and [[otto-warburg|Warburg]] the respiratory defect, Ukhtomskii gave him the integrating concept that joins those to ordinary psychological experience. Without the dominant, the chain from thyroid to image to behavior remains broken in the middle. With it, the chain closes: substrate determines the cell's response to stimuli, the cell's response determines which functional systems can stabilize, and the stabilized system *is* the behavior the organism shows.

## Biography

Ukhtomskii was born in 1875 in Yaroslavl province into a Russian aristocratic family of religious orientation. He was raised in his aunt's home in Rybinsk, where the Eastern Orthodox spiritual tradition and the patristic practice of *staretzhestvo* (eldership) became formative — sources he would later cite as the root of his concept of the *interlocutor*. He studied first at the Moscow Theological Academy, completing his thesis in 1898, and only then turned to natural science, entering St. Petersburg University in 1899 to work under N. E. Vvedenskii (Nikolai Vedensky), the founder of the Russian inhibition-physiology tradition whose work Peat returned to in his treatment of [[inhibition|protective inhibition]]. Ukhtomskii's 1911 doctoral dissertation, *On the Dependence of Motor Cortical Effects on Side Influences of the Central Nervous System*, was the founding paper of dominant theory. He succeeded Vedensky in the St. Petersburg (later Leningrad) State University physiology chair in 1922 and held it for the rest of his life.

Two unusual biographical facts shape how to read him. He remained a devout Orthodox Christian throughout his Soviet career — an extraordinary anomaly for a senior scientist under Stalin — and was reportedly tonsured as a monk; his preserved notebooks (posthumous as *Intuition of Conscience* and *The Worthy Interlocutor*) move freely between theological-ethical and scientific material. He survived the purges as a senior physiologist who was both politically careful and useful enough to the regime's "new man" project that his religion was tolerated. And he died of starvation in August 1942 during the Siege of Leningrad, having refused evacuation. The combination — devout Orthodoxy, senior Soviet position, death-in-place during the blockade — is exactly the cultural distinctiveness Peat returned to when he wrote that Ukhtomskii's "attitudes and concerns are representative of Russian culture in a general way. The deliberate use of science as a tool of human understanding as well as for practical ends, giving science a human shape, are some of these attitudes" ([[book-mind-and-tissue|*Mind and Tissue*]], Ch. 1).

## The Dominant Concept

The founding observation was simple. A spinal frog subjected to electrical stimulation of a motor cortical area that would normally produce flexion of the contralateral hindlimb would instead — if the animal had been carrying out some other behavior at the moment of stimulation — *intensify* that other behavior. A defecating frog, stimulated where its leg should have kicked, defecated more vigorously instead. The incoming stimulus had been *captured* by the ongoing functional system and rerouted into its support. Ukhtomskii generalized: at any moment, the organism contains one or several functional systems in elevated metabolic activity that exert a centripetal attraction on incoming excitations. They do not coexist with other neural processes; they *recruit* them.

The dominant has four defining properties. (1) **Elevated excitability** — the system is already partially activated, its cells in the high-readiness state of Vedensky's upper zone (see [[inhibition]]), primed to respond to weak stimulation that would not move a resting cell. (2) **Inertia** — the dominant persists once formed, continuing with diminishing intensity for seconds to hours depending on the cellular state, with a tendency to reassert itself when a partial version of its initial stimulus returns. (3) **Summation across irrelevant stimuli** — the dominant's deepest psychological feature: stimuli with no intrinsic relevance feed into and intensify it. A hungry organism is more startled by sudden noise; a sexually aroused organism is more responsive to incidental touch; an anxious organism reads ambiguous social cues as threats. The dominant captures and redirects. (4) **Inhibition of competing systems** — a formed dominant suppresses other potential dominants. This is the basis of selective attention: the dominant's metabolic and structural priority drains resources from competing systems, and the competing systems' inability to form coherent dominants is *what attention feels like from the inside*.

The dominant is not located in any single anatomical structure. It is a *configuration* spanning cortex, hypothalamus, autonomic centers, and skeletal muscles — wherever the elevated excitability propagates. This is why Peat treated it as the integrating organism-level concept synapse-and-circuit neuroscience cannot generate. The dominant is the answer to *what is doing the integrating* when an organism behaves as one creature rather than a federation of reflexes.

## How the Dominant Differs from "Attention" or "Focus"

The English-language vocabulary that comes closest — *attention*, *focus*, *concentration* — names only the visible surface of the underlying state. Three distinctions matter.

First, the dominant is a *whole-organism* state, not a localized brain process. Cognitive neuroscience treats attention as a frontal-parietal network's allocation of processing capacity. The dominant includes the cortical pattern but also the autonomic shift, the postural set, the metabolic readiness in target muscles, the digestive and respiratory adjustments. When the hungry rat's food dominant forms, salivary glands are recruited, stomach motility increases, heart rate adjusts, olfactory bulbs sensitize, skeletal musculature primes for locomotion. *All of this is the dominant.* Cortical attention is one symptom of an organism-wide state.

Second, the dominant is *constructive* rather than *selective*. Attention-as-spotlight implies a pre-existing perceptual field from which the system selects relevant elements. The dominant *creates* the perceptual field appropriate to itself: the lover does not "attend to" the beloved's face out of an array of equally valid alternatives; the beloved's face *is* the perceptual world for as long as the dominant holds. This is closer to Merleau-Ponty's figure-ground than to information-theoretic attention.

Third, the dominant has *temporal depth*. It carries its formation history — what stimuli formed it, what associative chains have been incorporated, what previous dominants it grew out of. Peat's quotation from Ukhtomskii returns to this: "Whatever an individual's dominants, such too is his integral image of the world; and whatever his integral image of the world — whatever his behavior — such is his happiness and misfortune." A person *is* the set of dominants he can form and the historical chain that constituted his formation.

The framework Peat builds on this is therefore not a technique-focused attention curriculum but a bioenergetic one: restore the substrate, and dominants form themselves where they should; degrade the substrate (hypothyroidism, [[pufa|PUFA]] loading, [[serotonin]] elevation, chronic stress), and the organism becomes incapable of forming any but the most primitive dominants — fear, escape, defense.

## Where Peat Used the Concept

The dominant appears at five load-bearing junctures across Peat's corpus.

**Memory formation.** Memory is the laying-down of a stable dominant whose later reactivation constitutes recall. Peat's [[memory]] page treats remembering not as the strengthening of a synaptic connection but as the establishment of a functional system whose elevated readiness persists across time. The dominant's *inertia* property is the property of remembering. A memory that can be recalled is a dominant reactivable by weak partial cues; a memory that has been lost is a dominant whose cells have lost their high-readiness state through metabolic depletion or structural damage.

**Dream content.** Dream content, in the Pavlovian framework Peat elaborated in *Mind and Tissue* Ch. 17, is the *reversal* of the previous day's focus-of-excitation into a focus-of-inhibition surrounded by excitation. The dominants formed in waking activity are the material the dream-process works on — reactivated, recombined, partially resolved in sleep. The dream's strange combinations are the available dominants drifting into and out of partial activation as the cortex performs its inhibitory housekeeping. Without a unit of analysis that has both content and metabolic state, the standard "REM-as-random-firing" model is the only available frame.

**Learning and the orienting reflex's aftermath.** The [[orienting-reflex]] is the whole-organism mobilization of attention toward novelty. What happens after orienting — whether the novel object is incorporated into a stable functional system or rejected as irrelevant — is, in Ukhtomskii's frame, the formation or non-formation of a new dominant. Learning *is* dominant formation. An organism whose substrate permits dominants to stabilize learns; an organism whose substrate is degraded orients but cannot consolidate, and the same novelty registers without changing the organism (the cognitive signature of chronic stress, hypothyroidism, and the late stages of [[stress|GAS exhaustion]]).

**Psychopathology.** The conditions American psychiatry treats as neurochemical imbalances — depression, anxiety, ADHD, the schizophrenias — appear in the Ukhtomskii frame as failures of dominant formation, dominant inertia, or dominant inhibition. The depressed patient cannot form positive dominants; her field is captured by negative ones. The ADHD child cannot inhibit competing incipient dominants. The schizophrenic in acute crisis has dominants forming and dissolving without integration. Each is a substrate failure expressing itself as a dominant-formation failure. Treatment is not primarily receptor pharmacology; it is restoration of the conditions under which dominants form and stabilize.

**The relational physiology of person-to-person contact.** Peat's [[effect-of-person]] chapter draws on Ukhtomskii's late concept of *the interlocutor* — the worthy receptive other in whose presence one is enabled to form a "dominant relative to the visage of another person" and thereby "enrich himself with impressions of the other's internal life, to get new impulses for growth, and to get new understanding of himself." In the Russian Orthodox-philosophical inheritance Ukhtomskii kept alive in Soviet science, this is the *spiritual* extension of dominant theory: the highest function of one person is to be the interlocutor through whose presence another person's dominants reorganize toward truth rather than toward self-assertion. The therapeutic frame in *Mind and Tissue* — the deliberate cultivation of warm in-person contact as a precondition for healing — is the interlocutor concept applied to clinical care.

## Lineage: Sechenov → Vedensky → Pavlov → Ukhtomskii → Anokhin

The Russian physiological lineage Peat treated as continuous, each generation incorporating its predecessors rather than replacing them. **Ivan Sechenov (1829–1905)** — "the father of Russian physiology" — established that the nervous system contains an active inhibitory mechanism; his frog's spinal reflex could be suppressed by intense sensory stimulation, and *Reflexes of the Brain* (1863) became the philosophical basis for treating mental life as scientifically tractable without flattening it. **Nikolai Vedensky (1852–1922)** — Sechenov's student, Ukhtomskii's teacher — distinguished inhibition from fatigue and identified the parabiotic cellular state that underpins Peat's [[inhibition|two-zones of inhibition]] framework. **Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936)**, working alongside this line, established the conditioned reflex as the basic unit of learning and insisted that the cerebral cortex participates in every reflex; his "what-is-it?" [[orienting-reflex|orienting reflex]] became the precondition for dominant formation. **Ukhtomskii** generalized these cellular-level findings into the organism-level concept of the dominant — what a reflex *becomes* when it persists, summates other excitations, and stabilizes as an organizing pattern. **Pyotr Anokhin (1898–1974)** then extended dominant theory into the *functional systems theory* that dominated Soviet physiology in the second half of the twentieth century. An Anokhinian functional system is essentially Ukhtomskii's dominant equipped with an *acceptor of action* (an anticipatory image against which feedback from action is compared). Peat cites Anokhin frequently in *Mind and Tissue* — especially the framing of the orienting reflex as having four properties (central integration, exclusion, adaptational effect, feedback) — and treats him as the carrier of the Ukhtomskii inheritance forward.

This is one cumulative tradition building upward from cellular state through reflex through dominant through functional system. The Anglo-American behaviorist and cognitive traditions, by contrast, have repeatedly *broken* with their predecessors (Skinner against Watson, cognitivism against behaviorism, neuroscience against cognitivism). Peat treated the Russian tradition's continuity as one of its principal scientific virtues.

## Influence on Soviet Psychology and the West

Within Soviet science, the dominant became one of the load-bearing concepts of mid-century theoretical physiology and the explicit point of departure for Anokhin's functional systems theory. The vocabulary running through Soviet neuropsychology of the 1950s–1980s — Bernstein on movement, Luria on frontal lobes, Sokolov on the orienting reflex, Leontyev on activity theory — carries the dominant inside it as the implicit organism-level unit on which the more specific frameworks rest.

In the West, the dominant's reception was minimal. It had no counterpart in the reflex arc, no place in the behaviorist program, no obvious mapping onto cognitive science's information-processing vocabulary, and no clinical-trial endpoint pharmaceutical research could pursue. Some isolated figures recognized the affinity — Kurt Goldstein's organismic biology, Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology, parts of the Gestalt tradition — and Peat noted as much in *Mind and Tissue* Ch. 1: "Some forms of Gestalt theory are practically identical to Ukhtomskii's theory, in emphasizing the ability of the organism to form new unities: Goldstein and Merleau-Ponty are in this group." But the integration never happened in mainstream American or English science. The conceptual gap is the same one that kept Szent-Györgyi's submolecular biology, Ling's structured cytoplasm, and Peat's bioenergetic medicine on the scientific margins: the dominant begins from a holistic substrate-and-organism unit that the West replaced with mechanism-and-circuit reductionism.

## Why Ukhtomskii Matters to the Peat Framework Today

Peat's framework joins a molecular-level account of energy metabolism (thyroid, glucose oxidation, [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]], structured water, the [[oxidative-metabolism|electron-transport chain]]) to a clinical account of how people behave and feel (depression, anxiety, learning, attention, relational presence). The gap between these levels is the gap most metabolic-medicine frameworks never close. A hormone is dosed in micrograms; a behavior is observed in life. *What sits in between* — what makes a particular metabolic state express itself as one personality structure rather than another, as the capacity for one kind of focus rather than another, as a particular character of dream and memory and social presence — is what the dominant addresses.

The dominant is the missing link, the level at which substrate becomes behavior. A metabolically replete cell in the upper zone of [[inhibition|protective inhibition]] can participate in stable, complex dominants; a depleted cell in the lower zone cannot. The hypothyroid patient's restricted dominant-formation capacity *is* her depression, her anhedonia, her cognitive narrowing. The patient on adequate thyroid, sugar, and progesterone whose dominants begin to form again across longer time-spans and broader subject matter *is* the patient recovering. Peat's clinical method — temperature, pulse, oxidation rate, free fatty acids, the protective steroid pool — tracks the substrate variables that determine which dominants the organism can form, and the dominants the organism actually forms are what the patient lives.

Without Ukhtomskii's concept (or a functional equivalent), Peat's framework would read as a metabolic-medicine prescription stack with no clear connection to its psychological claims. With it, the connection is direct: restore the substrate, and the organism's dominant-formation capacity restores itself; the patient's attention, memory, capacity for love, dream content, and relationship to work reorganize from the bottom up. This is why Peat returned to Ukhtomskii across decades. The dominant is the conceptual joint that makes the framework one thing rather than two adjacent things.

## Counter-arguments

- **Attention-as-circuit-process.** The dominant has no obvious mapping onto the dorsal- and ventral-attention-network architecture established by Corbetta and Shulman, or onto Dehaene's global-neuronal-workspace theory. These frameworks treat attention as the modulation of cortical activity by specific frontal-parietal networks acting through identifiable cholinergic, noradrenergic, and dopaminergic mechanisms. The Ukhtomskii-Peat reading does not contradict the circuit findings but treats them as one downstream description of an organism-wide state. Whether the circuit account is *sufficient* (as mainstream neuroscience tends to assume) or only *partial* (as the dominant framework implies) cannot be decided by current data.

- **The "dominant is mystical" critique.** The dominant's holistic character, combined with Ukhtomskii's religious orientation and the *interlocutor* concept, has made the framework readable to some Western philosophers of mind as a residue of pre-scientific spiritual psychology. The defense, which Peat implicitly adopted, is that the *empirical* core — the dominant's four properties, the cellular-state substrate, the relationship to inhibition and the orienting reflex — is independent of the spiritual extensions. One can use the dominant scientifically without committing to Ukhtomskii's metaphysics.

- **The general-systems-theory amendment.** What Ukhtomskii called the dominant is arguably what the mid-twentieth-century general-systems tradition (Bertalanffy, Wiener, Ashby) was independently discovering — a self-organizing dynamical attractor in a high-dimensional state space whose variables include cellular metabolic state, autonomic tone, hormonal levels, and cortical activation. Treating Ukhtomskii's framework as a domain-specific Russian instance of the broader systems-theoretic insight loses some clinical specificity but gains embedding in a broader tradition.

- **No integration with computational neuroscience.** No serious effort has been made to formalize the dominant in the vocabulary of predictive coding, free-energy minimization, or active inference. The conceptual affinities are obvious — a dominant is something like a high-prior generative model that captures incoming sensory evidence and biases action selection — but the work of translation has not been done.

## Data gaps

- **English-language scholarship on Ukhtomskii is thin.** A handful of articles in cross-cultural psychology and Soviet history journals, occasional translations of primary texts, and scattered chapter-references. There is no English-language biography of comparable depth to existing biographies of Pavlov or Vygotsky. The Russian corpus — *Collected Works*, the *Intuition of Conscience* and *Worthy Interlocutor* letters, the secondary religious-philosophical literature — is largely inaccessible to non-Russian-reading scholars.

- **The pre-1911 work.** Ukhtomskii's pre-doctoral writings are not systematically translated. Whether his theological training shaped the dominant concept in identifiable ways, and how the concept evolved between the 1911 founding paper and the 1923 *Dominanta* monograph, is largely unknown in the English literature.

- **The Stalinist-period professional history.** The exact conditions under which Ukhtomskii survived the purges as a religious senior scientist — what compromises he made, what protections he had — have not been thoroughly documented.

- **Comparison with parallel Western frameworks.** The detailed relationship between the dominant and Lashley's mass action, Goldstein's organismic concept, Merleau-Ponty's intentional arc, and contemporary global-workspace theories has not been worked out. A conceptual map of which Western frameworks are functionally equivalent, which are partial subsets, and which are genuinely incompatible would clarify what is recoverable from Soviet psychophysiology for current cognitive science.

- **Clinical operationalization.** The dominant has not been formally operationalized in a clinical-research instrument. The framework motivates specific predictions (substrate restoration should improve dominant-formation capacity; chronic stress should narrow the range of available dominants; specific metabolic interventions should shift dominant inertia) but the empirical work has not been done.

- **The Anokhin extension.** The *continuity* between Ukhtomskii's dominant and Anokhin's functional system — which features Anokhin retained, which he modified — has not been systematically analyzed in English.
