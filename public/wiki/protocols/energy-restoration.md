# Energy Restoration

Chronic fatigue, brain fog, and post-meal crashes are not failures of sleep, discipline, or mental health. They are the [[randle-cycle|Randle block]] in action. Your cells cannot use the fuel you are eating because [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] (especially PUFA) are occupying the mitochondrial machinery that should be burning glucose. The body is swimming in fuel and starving at the cellular level at the same time.

This is the defining metabolic picture of the modern fatigue epidemic. People eat, then feel tired. They sleep eight hours and wake exhausted. They pound coffee to get through the morning, crash at three, and catch a second wind at ten at night that keeps them from sleeping. Every symptom on that list has a specific physiological cause, and none of them respond durably to "more rest" or "stress management." They respond to breaking the Randle block, lowering circulating free fatty acids, and restoring thyroid-driven glucose oxidation.

The fix is not more caffeine. Caffeine used the wrong way deepens the problem by driving adrenaline on top of an already stressed system. The fix is not more sleep. Sleep quality depends on stable blood sugar and a working thyroid, so chasing sleep without fixing metabolism is chasing your tail. The fix is not willpower. Willpower cannot make a cell oxidize glucose when its mitochondria are blocked.

The fix is to feed the body correctly, lower the stress signal, clear the fatty acid load, and give the thyroid the substrates and conditions it needs to run metabolism at the right temperature. In practice this means sugar, salt, protein, saturated fat, frequent meals, targeted supplements, and in many cases [[hypothyroidism|thyroid]] support. The sequence matters. The first 48 hours of simple blood sugar stabilization will tell you most of what you need to know about how deep the problem goes. What follows is a conditional protocol: start at the top, see what responds, and go deeper only where you need to.

## Diagnosing the Problem

The energy crisis has a recognizable signature. Not every person has every symptom, but if several of the following track together, the problem is metabolic, not lifestyle.

1. **Morning fatigue despite adequate sleep.** Low blood sugar overnight forces cortisol and adrenaline to mobilize stored fuel. You wake up in a stress hangover, not rested.
2. **Post-meal crashes at 1-3 PM.** A blood sugar spike from a carb-heavy lunch triggers an oversized insulin response, then reactive hypoglycemia. The body rebounds with adrenaline, which feels like anxiety or brain fog.
3. **Brain fog.** Lactic acid accumulating in the brain from glycolytic metabolism. When mitochondria cannot complete glucose oxidation because of the Randle block, pyruvate backs up into lactate. The brain runs on incomplete fuel.
4. **Afternoon slump at 3-5 PM.** Cortisol is tapering on its normal diurnal curve, and an insufficient thyroid cannot bridge the gap. You hit a wall.
5. **A "second wind" at 10 PM.** This is not a good sign. It is a cortisol surge preparing the body to run on stress through the night because glycogen is depleted. It blocks sleep onset and sets up the morning fatigue on the next day.
6. **Cold hands and feet.** Peripheral vasoconstriction from adrenaline shunting blood to the core. A classic hypothyroid marker.
7. **Dependence on caffeine to function.** If you cannot start the day without coffee, you are running on adrenaline, not thyroid energy. Coffee in this state is a whip on a tired horse.
8. **Craving sweets late in the day but feeling worse after eating them.** Low blood sugar drives the craving; the Randle block prevents the sugar from being used efficiently.
9. **Wired-and-tired at night.** High cortisol, high adrenaline, low thyroid. The sympathetic nervous system is running the show because metabolism cannot.

If three or more of these match, the problem is metabolic. See [[hypothyroidism]] for the broader symptom picture and how it connects to thyroid insufficiency.

## The Immediate Interventions: First 48 Hours

Before going deeper, test whether the problem is simple blood sugar instability plus a modest Randle block. These interventions cost almost nothing and respond fast. If they work, you have your answer. If they do not, the problem has more layers.

1. **Orange juice plus salt within five minutes of waking.** Eight to twelve ounces of fresh or high-quality OJ with a generous pinch of salt. This floods the liver with glucose and fructose, refills glycogen, and shuts off the cortisol and adrenaline that have been running the show all night. See [[morning-routine]] for the full sequence.
2. **Eat a real breakfast within thirty minutes of waking.** Protein, carbohydrate, and saturated fat together. Eggs with fruit and cheese. Milk with honey and toast. A small steak with orange juice. Not a smoothie, not coffee and a bar, not a protein shake.
3. **Eat every two to three hours for 48 hours.** No going longer than three hours without food. This is not snacking; it is steady fueling. Small portions are fine. What you are breaking is the pattern of letting blood sugar fall until the stress hormones have to step in.
4. **A bedtime snack every night.** Salted ice cream, a glass of milk with honey, cheese and fruit, a small bowl of Jello. The goal is to keep the liver fed through the night so cortisol does not have to surge at 3 AM.
5. **One tablespoon of coconut oil in the morning coffee.** Thermogenic, raises the metabolic rate within an hour, and provides medium-chain triglycerides the body burns cleanly without touching the PUFA stores.
6. **Coffee with cream and sugar, after breakfast, not before.** Never black, never on an empty stomach. The cream and sugar prevent the adrenaline spike; the food buffers the caffeine.
7. **Salt liberally.** Energy crashes often track with low sodium. Salt food to taste, then add a little more. Sodium supports thyroid conversion and counters the aldosterone response that drives potassium loss.
8. **No fasting of any kind.** Not intermittent, not overnight beyond ten hours, not skipped meals. Fasting mobilizes free fatty acids and deepens the Randle block.
9. **No caffeine on an empty stomach.** This is the single most common mistake in fatigued people. It trains the body to run on adrenaline before any metabolic substrate is available.

If energy improves meaningfully within 48 hours, the problem was simple Randle block plus blood sugar instability. Continue with the full [[anti-stress-diet]] and expect further gains over weeks.

## If 48 Hours Did Not Work: Go Deeper

Three possible layers sit beneath a resistant case. Most chronic fatigue presentations have all three running at once.

1. **PUFA saturation.** Years of seed oils have loaded the tissues with polyunsaturated fats. These are slowly released into circulation, where they interfere with glucose oxidation continuously. Clearing tissue PUFA takes months to years of strict avoidance and saturated fat replacement. There is no shortcut. See [[free-fatty-acids]].
2. **Endotoxin load.** Gut bacteria producing lipopolysaccharide that crosses a compromised gut barrier after meals. Endotoxin activates estrogen, serotonin, and nitric oxide, all of which suppress metabolism. See [[endotoxin]].
3. **Hypothyroidism.** Often the base condition beneath the other two, and often missed because standard lab panels focus on TSH rather than free T3 and symptoms. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

Work the layers in parallel. Diet is the foundation; the other interventions sit on top of it.

## The Randle Block in Practice

The [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] is the single most practical concept in this whole framework, and understanding it changes how meals feel.

**What it feels like:** tired after eating when you should feel fueled; cold extremities when the room is warm; mental fog that worsens with higher-fat meals; muscle weakness during ordinary activity; stubborn weight that does not respond to caloric reduction; a sense that food is being "stored" rather than "used."

**How to break it:** sugar displaces free fatty acids from the mitochondrial machinery. Eating sugar actively lowers circulating FFAs by raising insulin, which suppresses lipolysis. Within hours of a sugar-forward meal, glucose oxidation can resume. The intuition feels backwards because most people have been told sugar causes energy crashes. Sugar by itself on an empty stomach does cause crashes. Sugar as part of a protein-and-saturated-fat meal breaks the Randle block and restores energy.

**Practical combinations that work:**
- Fruit with cream
- Orange juice with coconut oil and collagen
- Ice cream after a meal as dessert (not a snack alone)
- Warm milk with honey
- Potatoes with butter and salt
- Masa or white rice with cheese

**Combinations that deepen the block:**
- High-fat meals without carbs (bacon and eggs, no fruit, no juice)
- Fasting, which mobilizes free fatty acids straight into circulation
- "Healthy" fat-forward foods like avocado, almonds, walnuts, and olive oil in large amounts
- Salad and grilled chicken lunches with no starch or sugar
- Keto macros generally

A useful rule: every meal should contain carbohydrate, and every meal should contain protein, and most meals should contain saturated fat. No meal should consist of fat and protein alone.

## The Endotoxin Dimension

If meals make you tired in a specific way, not hungry-to-full, but sluggish, cloudy, flushed, or heavy, bacterial endotoxin is likely part of the picture. Endotoxin enters the bloodstream after meals in people with compromised gut barriers and activates estrogen, serotonin, and nitric oxide, all of which suppress metabolism and worsen the Randle block.

Interventions, in order of priority:

1. **Raw carrot salad daily.** One medium carrot, grated lengthwise, with a teaspoon of coconut oil, a splash of vinegar, and salt. The fiber binds endotoxin and estrogen in the gut. This is the highest-leverage intervention on the list.
2. **Reduce fermentable fiber.** Whole grains, beans, legumes, raw vegetables beyond the carrot, and resistant starch feed the bacteria producing the endotoxin in the first place. Cooked, peeled, and refined often beat raw and whole in Peat's framework.
3. **Cooked mushrooms once or twice a week.** Anti-endotoxin, but always cooked, never raw. White button mushrooms are fine.
4. **Bamboo shoots occasionally** for additional anti-endotoxin fiber.
5. **Address constipation directly.** If bowel movements are slow, endotoxin absorption climbs. Cascara sagrada at bedtime or magnesium citrate in the evening can restore daily transit. Coffee in the morning also helps.

Ray Peat's own notes on the topic live in [[endotoxin]].

## The Thyroid Dimension

If the diet interventions help but do not fully restore energy, measure morning temperature and pulse. See [[temperature-tracking]] for the method. The numbers to watch:

- **Waking temperature** should climb to 97.8-98.2°F after breakfast to 98.6°F by mid-morning.
- **Resting pulse** should sit between 75 and 85 beats per minute.

If waking temperature is under 97.5°F and pulse is under 75, the underlying issue is hypothyroidism. Diet alone will often not fully resolve it, especially in women, in people over 40, and in anyone with a history of long-term PUFA consumption or chronic stress. Follow [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for the full supplementation and monitoring protocol.

## The Caffeine Question

Most fatigued people rely on coffee, and most of them use it wrong. Peat's framework keeps coffee and changes how you use it.

1. **Coffee with cream and sugar, not black.** The cream provides saturated fat and calcium; the sugar prevents the adrenaline spike.
2. **Coffee after food, not before.** The stomach should have something in it before caffeine arrives.
3. **Coffee with a meal, not between meals.** This keeps the stimulant effect paired with substrate.
4. **Coffee is not a replacement for breakfast.** Food first, always.
5. **Coffee is not a hunger suppressant.** If you are hungry, eat.
6. **Coffee does not rescue a cortisol crash.** If you are crashing, eat sugar and salt. Coffee on a crashing system makes the next crash worse.

Used correctly, coffee is supportive. It stimulates progesterone, inhibits aromatase, provides niacin and magnesium, and supports bile flow. Used incorrectly, it is an adrenaline trap that locks in the fatigue pattern.

## The Exercise Question

Chronic fatigue plus hard training produces worse chronic fatigue. This is not controversial in Peat's framework and it is obvious in practice. If you are exhausted, reduce training volume and intensity before doing anything else.

What works:

1. **Walking**, ideally outside in sunlight.
2. **Short play-like movement**, five to ten minute bouts scattered through the day.
3. **Sauna or warm baths**, which support metabolism without producing lactate.
4. **Light resistance work**, kept short and well-fed.

What does not work:

1. **Fasted training.**
2. **Workouts before breakfast.**
3. **Long steady-state cardio.**
4. **Heavy lifting sessions on a fatigued nervous system.**
5. **HIIT repeated several times a week.**

If you train, do it in late morning or early afternoon when food is in the system, keep sessions short, and eat immediately afterward.

## Supplements Worth Considering

These sit on top of the diet, not underneath it. They will not rescue a broken foundation.

- **Thyroid glandular.** The most important intervention for many cases. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for the dosing protocol.
- **Vitamin B1 (thiamine).** Supports glucose oxidation and pyruvate dehydrogenase, the enzyme the Randle block most directly suppresses. 100-300 mg daily.
- **Niacinamide.** Slows lipolysis, reducing the free fatty acid load on mitochondria. 100 mg with meals, several times a day.
- **Magnesium.** Universal cofactor, especially if cramps, tension, or irritability are present. Magnesium glycinate or magnesium bicarbonate in water.
- **Aspirin.** Reduces stress-related FFA elevation, as Peat explicitly recommended. Start low, 80-325 mg with food, with awareness of the standard contraindications.
- **Coconut oil.** Thermogenic, breaks the PUFA-driven fatigue pattern, and provides clean calories. One to three tablespoons daily.
- **Gelatin.** Glycine supports relaxation, reduces the inflammatory load of muscle meat, and lowers stress hormone output. One to two tablespoons daily in coffee or warm liquid.

Not recommended:

- "Energy" supplements containing 5-HTP or tryptophan (drive serotonin, which Peat considered anti-metabolic).
- Creatine for fatigue (fine for muscle output but does not address the Randle block).
- Most adaptogens. Peat was consistently skeptical of the category.
- Mega-dose B complexes with synthetic forms.

## The Common Failure Modes

Most people who fail to restore energy fail in predictable ways.

1. **Eating "clean" but undereating.** Salads, fish, "lean protein," small portions. The body reads this as famine and keeps the stress hormones elevated.
2. **Using intermittent fasting to "fix" energy.** Fasting lowers insulin, mobilizes free fatty acids, and deepens the Randle block. It is the exact opposite of what a fatigued person needs.
3. **Going low-carb or keto.** Suppresses thyroid function within days to weeks. The initial "clarity" is adrenaline, not recovery.
4. **Cold plunges as a primary intervention.** Adrenergic stress, not anabolic restoration. Fine as a minor tool for already-healthy people; wrong tool for fatigue.
5. **Heavy morning workouts.** Layering cortisol on top of cortisol. Crushes recovery.
6. **Skipping breakfast.**
7. **Relying on caffeine alone** to carry the day.
8. **Adding supplements without fixing the diet.** The hierarchy is food first, then thyroid, then targeted supplements. Skipping steps does not work.
9. **Expecting immediate results from PUFA depletion.** Tissue PUFA clears over months to years, not days.
10. **Changing three variables at once.** Start with the 48-hour protocol, then add layers deliberately so you can tell what is working.

## Signs Restoration Is Working

You are looking for a constellation of changes, not a single marker.

1. **Morning:** waking before the alarm, hungry, warm hands and feet, no grogginess.
2. **Post-breakfast:** energy rising rather than crashing; no need to lie down.
3. **10-11 AM:** steady focus without a caffeine cycle.
4. **Post-lunch:** a slight natural dip but no crash; no urgent need for coffee or a nap.
5. **3-5 PM:** normal energy holding through the afternoon.
6. **Evening:** pleasantly tired at a reasonable hour, not wired.
7. **Sleep:** falling asleep within 15 minutes and sleeping through without 3 AM waking.
8. **Temperature climbing week over week** toward the 98.0-98.6°F range.
9. **Resting pulse climbing** toward 75-85.
10. **Mood stabilizing.** Less anxiety, less irritability, more flat-line baseline.

Progress is usually visible within 48 hours for blood sugar, within two to four weeks for thyroid support, and within months for the deeper PUFA and endotoxin layers. The first visible signs are warmer hands, waking without grogginess, and the disappearance of the 3 PM crash.

- **Pyruvate dehydrogenase** — the enzyme complex most directly suppressed by the Randle block; thiamine is its key cofactor
- **Lactic acid accumulation in the brain** — mechanism linking incomplete glucose oxidation to brain fog
- **Reactive hypoglycemia** — mechanism behind the 1-3 PM post-meal crash
- **Cortisol diurnal curve** — the normal taper whose gap the afternoon slump reveals
- **10 PM cortisol surge** — misread as "second wind," actually a stress response to overnight glycogen depletion
- **Aldosterone-driven potassium loss** — mechanism connecting low sodium intake to fatigue
- **Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)** — the specific endotoxin molecule crossing the gut barrier after meals
- **Aromatase inhibition by coffee** — mechanism for coffee's anti-estrogen effect in Peat's framework
- **Glycine from gelatin** — the specific amino acid responsible for gelatin's anti-stress effect
