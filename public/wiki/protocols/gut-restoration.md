# Gut Restoration

Gut restoration, in Ray Peat's framework, is not the project that the functional-medicine industry has built around the phrase. There is no probiotic flood, no fermented-food regimen, no expensive panel of stool tests, no months on glutamine powder. Restoration means three concrete things, in this order: shorten transit time so that putrefaction and fermentation cannot proceed; reduce the bacterial load and the [[endotoxin]] it produces; and rebuild the digestive capacity — stomach acid, bile, enzymes, intact mucosa — that hypothyroidism and chronic stress have eroded. The probiotic-and-prebiotic doctrine Peat encountered in his lifetime, with its emphasis on cultivating bacteria to produce short-chain fatty acids, he treated as a category error. "Acetic acid, butyric acid, and propionic acid all have their potential value, but they are also well known as toxins," he said in a [[2021-06-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-june-2021-kmud|2021 episode on SIBO]]. "You can get all your nutrients with things that are resistant to fermentation."

The anchor of the protocol is the inversion of mainstream gut advice. Where conventional treatment for SIBO, IBS, or "leaky gut" assumes the goal is to populate the bowel with the right microbes, Peat's goal was to make the small intestine as sterile as possible. "Something like 15% or 20% of the population who are very healthy have a sterile small intestine," he told Andrew Murray in the same episode. "The sicker a person is, the farther up in the small intestine you find bacteria living, closer and closer to the stomach." Healthy guts are not biodiverse jungles. They are quiet upper tracts where digestion finishes before microbes get a turn. Restoration moves the gut back toward that quiet.

What follows is the conditional protocol. Start at Phase 1 and move down only as the previous phase plateaus. Most people will not need to leave Phase 1. Severe SIBO, post-antibiotic dysbiosis, or chronic NSAID damage may require all four.

## Diagnosis: Recognizing the Pattern

Peat's gut framework does not depend on stool tests, breath tests, or microbiome sequencing. The signs are clinical and almost all show up together once you look for them.

- **Bloating that worsens through the day.** Gas distributed through the small intestine that makes "your whole belly resound like a watermelon," as Peat described in the 2021 episode. Tapping the abdomen reveals tympany rather than dullness.
- **Irregular bowel pattern — alternating constipation and looseness.** The hallmark of a chronically irritated bowel running on excess serotonin. "You get a chronic, moderate excess of serotonin that can cause periodic cyclic diarrhea alternating with constipation."
- **Brain fog within an hour of eating.** Endotoxin entering the bloodstream after a meal triggers the cascade of nitric oxide, serotonin, and inflammatory mediators that Peat traced from the gut to nearly every cognitive symptom. See [[endotoxin]].
- **Accumulating food sensitivities.** New foods become "intolerant" every year. This is not a primary allergy; it is the immune system reacting to the leakiness of the intestinal barrier, what Peat called "leaky cell substance, which involves leaky capillaries." The gluten reaction in particular he tied to estrogen stress, not the protein itself ([[2009-07-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-bowel-endotoxin|2009 bowel-endotoxin episode]]).
- **History of multiple antibiotic courses, especially in childhood.** Or chronic NSAID use other than aspirin. Both strip the mucosa and shift the microbiome upward into the small intestine.
- **Cold hands and feet, low morning temperature, slow gut.** The hypothyroid pattern. Stomach acid, bile, pancreatic enzymes, and peristalsis all depend on a working metabolic rate. "If your thyroid is very low, your stomach acid is going to be low. The muscles become weaker at the same time the secretions are weaker," Peat told Jodelle Fitzwater in the [[2019-09-10-jodellefit-jodellefit-how-to-fix-your-digestion-poop|2019 digestion interview]].
- **Stool that smells rotten and floats.** Peat's clinical heuristic was direct: "It should be basically the color and odor of a baby's. If it starts smelling rotten, usually you are feeling rotten."

If three or more of these track together, the gut is the bottleneck. Pure energy work or hormone work will hit a ceiling until the bowel is restored.

## Phase 1: Stabilization (Weeks 1–4)

The goal of Phase 1 is to lower the bacterial burden on the small intestine without doing anything aggressive. For most people, this phase alone is enough.

**Daily raw carrot salad.** The single most-cited intervention in Peat's gut work. One medium carrot, shredded lengthwise to maximize antiseptic surface area, dressed with coconut oil or olive oil, vinegar, and salt. The fiber is indigestible, which is the point — it cannot be fermented by bacteria, so it binds [[endotoxin]] and bile-borne estrogen and carries both out. Peat ate one every day for over twenty years. "Doing that every day kept my intestine healthy for 20 years." For the full mechanism and preparation details, see [[carrot-salad]] and [[endotoxin-reduction]].

**Bone broth or gelatin, one to three servings per day.** Long-simmered broth from joints, knuckles, and skin delivers glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline — the amino acids that rebuild the gut lining and counter the inflammatory tryptophan and cysteine loads of muscle meat. Peat's image of the leaky cell was instructive: "If you imagine a lump of gelatin soaked in oil, it will stay stable in the presence of water. But if you mix a little bit of lecithin with it, it will quickly start taking up water, and then the whole thing is on the road to dissolving and breaking up." The metaphor is also the substrate. See [[gelatin]] for dosing and amino-acid context.

**Well-cooked vegetables only. No raw cruciferous, no raw greens.** Raw lettuce, Peat said in a [[2020-11-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2020-kmud|2020 episode]], is "one of the most mucus-promoting inflammatory agents." Raw cruciferous vegetables and raw onions feed dysbiosis directly. The exception is the carrot, whose germicidal compounds are precisely what cooking destroys. Otherwise: peel, boil, or stew everything until it is soft. Bamboo shoots, mushrooms (well-cooked), zucchini, summer squash, and potato (without skin) are the safest base.

**White rice, ripe tropical fruit, and dairy as the bland baseline.** White rice has had the [[fiber|fermentable hull]] removed; ripe bananas, mangoes, and papayas absorb high in the small intestine; well-tolerated dairy (milk, cheese) provides calcium, complete protein, and sugars that are taken up before bacteria can touch them. "In very healthy people, the stomach and the whole small intestine are sterile," Peat said in the 2021 episode. "If you have normal digestive actions, sugars should be absorbed very high in the small intestine." Choose foods that cooperate with that pattern.

**Avoid the entire FODMAP-and-PUFA load.** All seed and vegetable oils, raw onion and garlic, beans and lentils, nuts, wheat, rye, and "healthy" whole grains. Peat agreed explicitly with the FODMAP exclusions in the SIBO conversation: "I think avoidance of many of them is very reasonable… all of those things have other harmful effects as well as the fermentable carbohydrates." He disagreed only on milk, which the FODMAP list flags incorrectly. PUFA does double damage — it ferments where it lingers and saponifies the cell membranes that are supposed to keep endotoxin out.

**Orange juice, well-strained, with meals.** Flavonoids without the irritation of raw greens. "Orange juice is a very wide-spectrum anti-inflammatory and energy-producing food."

Expected response: within two to three weeks, bloating drops, stool firms and stops smelling, brain fog after meals fades, and food sensitivities begin to recede. If Phase 1 produces this picture and holds it, do not escalate. The protocol is the maintenance.

## Phase 2: Targeted Intervention (Weeks 4–12)

If Phase 1 plateaus without resolving the picture — bloating persists, stool will not normalize, brain fog remains — the bacterial load needs an active reduction. Phase 2 is where Peat's framework departs sharply from natural-medicine orthodoxy, because the tools include real antibiotics and real binders, used briefly, at a low dose, and with a clear endpoint.

**Activated charcoal, 1–3 grams, two to three times per week, for acute insults.** Charcoal binds endotoxin, bile-borne estrogen, and circulating microbial fragments directly. The classical use case in Peat's writing is a flare after a known dietary infraction — restaurant seed oils, a fermented food experiment that backfired, a stretch of constipation. It is not a daily supplement. Take it apart from meals and medications by at least two hours, since it binds nutrients indiscriminately. See [[activated-charcoal]].

**A short course of an appropriate antibiotic, clinician-coordinated, for confirmed SIBO or chronic dysbiosis.** This is the most distinctive feature of Peat's gut work. He recommended the tetracycline family — tetracycline itself, minocycline, or doxycycline — and short courses of penicillin or erythromycin where appropriate. "Sometimes a third of the standard dose of penicillin, tetracycline, or erythromycin will work" for a fresh bowel infection ([[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|2016 allergy episode]]). The tetracyclines were preferred because they double as anti-inflammatory and anti-nitric-oxide agents: "both emodin (from cascara or Chinese rhubarb) and tetracycline (and related minocycline and doxycycline) are anti-inflammatory as well as antiseptic… Minocycline is being found to prevent or possibly improve dementia and Alzheimer's disease" ([[2014-12-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-you-are-what-you-eat-2014|2014 episode]]). Antibiotic-relieved fertility clinic patients — described in the [[2010-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endotoxins|2010 endotoxins episode]] — saw cortisol fall, estrogen fall, progesterone rise, and headaches and infertility resolve. The mechanism is endotoxin reduction, not microbiome curation.

**Methylene blue, 1–5 mg in the morning.** Anti-bacterial in the bowel, mitochondrially supportive at the cell. Peat treated it as analogous to the tetracyclines and to vitamin K — molecules in the same redox family that defend against nitric oxide. Low and consistent beats high and intermittent. See [[methylene-blue]].

**Cascara sagrada, low dose, intermittent.** When transit time is the limiting factor and dietary fiber is not enough. Cascara's emodin "has a structure similar to tetracycline" and "accelerates the movement through the intestine as well as reducing inflammation and maintaining oxidative energy production" ([[2014-12-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-you-are-what-you-eat-2014|2014 episode]]). Not a daily laxative — a metabolic adjunct. See [[cascara-sagrada]].

**Aspirin, low dose, with food.** Anti-bacterial in the bowel, anti-inflammatory systemically. "Aspirin sometimes is a big help to decrease bacterial activity," Peat said in the 2021 SIBO episode. It is the exception to the no-NSAID rule because, taken with food and adequate vitamin K, it does not strip the mucosa the way ibuprofen and naproxen do.

**Thyroid optimization, if hypothyroid.** The whole digestive cascade — stomach acid, bile flow, pancreatic enzymes, peristalsis — is downstream of metabolic rate. A morning temperature below 36.6°C / 97.8°F or a resting pulse below 75 makes Phase 1 and Phase 2 work harder than they should. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for the full protocol; the dosing details live in [[thyroid-dosing]]. Without addressing thyroid, gut work becomes Sisyphean.

Phase 2 is bounded in time. The antibiotic is a course, not a maintenance. The charcoal is for flares. Methylene blue and aspirin can become long-term low-dose tools. Most of the work is to clear a chronic burden so that Phase 3 has something to rebuild on.

## Phase 3: Digestive Capacity Rebuild (Weeks 12–24)

With the bacterial load lowered, the focus shifts to restoring the secretory and motor functions of the upper digestive tract. This is where most chronic-gut patients have been failing for years without knowing it, because the diagnostic culture treats heartburn and bloating as evidence of too much acid rather than too little.

**Gastric acid support, if low.** Stomach acid drops with age, with hypothyroidism, and with chronic stress. Without adequate hydrochloric acid, the upper small intestine cannot stay sterile, protein digestion stalls, and the bacterial migration that defines SIBO is locked in. Peat preferred to fix the underlying metabolism — "the ultimate source of the acidity is in oxidation and the production of carbon dioxide… It is essentially a respiratory process" — but a betaine HCl + pepsin capsule with the first bite of a protein meal, or two teaspoons of apple cider vinegar in water before eating, can bridge the gap while thyroid catches up.

**Bitter foods at the start of meals.** Bitter taste triggers the vagal reflex that primes acid and bile secretion. A small piece of dark chocolate, a few sips of bitter coffee, or a slice of grapefruit before the meal does work that no supplement does.

**Smaller, more frequent meals.** A stomach overloaded with volume secretes acid less effectively. Four to five smaller meals over the day, with protein at each, holds blood sugar steady and gives the upper tract a chance to actually finish digesting before the next round arrives.

**Slow chewing.** Mechanical breakdown and saliva enzymes do more work than people assume. Peat noted that saliva is "just as important as stomach acid" in the digestive cascade. Practically: chew everything until it stops being identifiable as the food it started as.

**Slow, deliberate reintroduction of mild raw foods.** Toward the end of Phase 3, ripe fruit eaten between meals, well-tolerated dairy in larger quantities, and occasional small servings of mild raw vegetables (cucumber, watermelon) test whether the upper tract has recovered. Raw cruciferous, raw onion, raw garlic, and any fermented food stay off.

Tracking transit time matters most in this phase. Eat a generous serving of beets or corn at one meal and time the appearance in the stool. Twelve to twenty-four hours is the target Peat cited from Burkitt: "the African on their typical diet has a 12-hour transit time, whereas the average American has a 72-hour transit time." If transit is still long, more carrot, more cooked mushroom fiber, possibly more cascara.

## Phase 4: Maintenance

Once the gut is restored, the cost of maintenance is small and the cost of letting it slide is high. The maintenance routine is the protocol minus the active interventions.

- **Carrot salad five to seven days per week.** Daily is ideal. Five days is the floor.
- **Bone broth or gelatin weekly.** A pot of broth on Sunday, used through the week in soups, sauces, and morning drinks. Gelatin in coffee or warm milk as a quick alternative.
- **PUFA below the threshold of harm.** Practically, that means cooking with butter, ghee, coconut oil, or beef tallow exclusively, and accepting that any restaurant meal carries a PUFA cost. Some carrot the next day handles the routine exposures.
- **Reintroduce nothing without testing.** Once a food has been excluded, it can come back, but one at a time, in small quantities, with a few days of monitoring. Sensitivities that resolved during the protocol often stay resolved if the underlying barrier holds.
- **Track transit time and stool quality monthly.** The earliest sign of slippage. Catch it before bloating and brain fog return.
- **Thyroid status checked twice a year.** Morning temperature, resting pulse, occasional labs. The gut tracks the metabolism.

## Tracking Signals

Use the same handful of signals to know whether the protocol is working. Do not chase new biomarkers.

- **Transit time:** 12–24 hours from eating to corresponding stool.
- **Stool form and odor:** Soft-formed, daily, no strong rotten smell. "The color and odor of a baby's."
- **Post-meal mental clarity:** No brain fog within an hour of eating. If it returns, the most recent meal is the suspect.
- **Mood stability:** Even keel through the afternoon. The serotonin-driven anxiety that tracks an irritated gut is one of the first symptoms to fade.
- **Food sensitivity ledger:** A list of foods that used to cause trouble. Most should be re-tolerable within three to six months. If new sensitivities accumulate rather than recede, the protocol is not deep enough.
- **Morning temperature and pulse:** 36.6–36.8°C / 97.8–98.2°F, pulse 75–85. If these slide down, gut symptoms will follow.

## Failure Modes

The protocol fails in predictable ways. Most failures come from importing assumptions from other gut frameworks.

- **Probiotic supplementation as the primary intervention.** Adding bacteria to a bowel already over-colonized is feeding the problem. Peat granted only one narrow exception — *Saccharomyces boulardii* and a small number of antibiotic-producing organisms — and treated even those as situational. "There are several bacteria and some fungus that produce antibiotics themselves. They are a combination of probiotic and antibiotic. They kill off harmful bacteria by the antibiotics they produce." The default position is fewer organisms in the small intestine, not more.
- **Aggressive cleanses or extended fasting.** Stripping everything starves the cells of the substrate they need to rebuild the barrier. Fasting also mobilizes free fatty acids, which deepens the [[free-fatty-acids|Randle block]] and the very metabolic suppression that opened the gut to colonization in the first place.
- **Raw-food enthusiasm during repair.** Raw vegetable matter is fiber the upper tract is not equipped to handle in a compromised state. The carrot is the exception, not the model.
- **"Healthy" high-FODMAP whole grains.** Oat bran, whole wheat, lentils, beans — the foods that the fiber-and-prebiotic doctrine recommends are the same foods that produce the toxic short-chain fatty acids and feed small-intestinal bacterial growth. Peat was unambiguous: "Oat bran breaks down into bowel carcinogens and estrogenic breakdown products."
- **Chronic low gastric acid left unaddressed.** Acid-suppressing medication (PPIs, H2 blockers) taken for what is actually low-acid reflux locks the gut into permanent dysbiosis. Many people on long-term PPIs will not recover until they taper off, with HCl support, under supervision.
- **Skipping the thyroid layer.** A hypothyroid gut is a slow gut. No amount of dietary effort moves it without metabolic rate.

## What to Avoid

- **Probiotic megadose products** (capsules with billions of CFU of mixed strains).
- **Fermented foods until late maintenance** — yogurt, kefir, kombucha, sauerkraut, kimchi. The lactic acid load alone is "extra work for your liver" and stimulates fibrosis ([[yogurt]]).
- **Raw cruciferous in dysbiosis** — raw broccoli, kale, cauliflower, cabbage in slaws and smoothies.
- **FODMAP-heavy whole grains and legumes** during Phases 1–3.
- **NSAIDs other than judicious low-dose aspirin.** Ibuprofen and naproxen strip the mucosa.
- **Long-term acid blockers** unless medically indispensable.
- **High-dose garlic and chili extracts marketed for SIBO.** "Those hot compounds can be harmful to your stomach and intestine if you eat large amounts. They do kill bacteria, but they can also kill your digestive system's epithelial cells."
- **Fish oil.** Unstable PUFA, the wrong tool entirely. See [[fish-oil]].

## When to Escalate

Phase 1 is safe to run without supervision. Phase 2 and beyond benefit from a clinician who understands the framework — or at minimum, is willing to prescribe a short course of a tetracycline-family antibiotic for confirmed SIBO. Escalate to medical evaluation if:

- **Blood in stool**, fresh red or dark. Not a Peat-protocol problem.
- **Unintentional weight loss** of more than 5% of body weight over a month or two.
- **Severe, persistent abdominal pain** — especially localized rather than diffuse.
- **Persistent diarrhea after four weeks of Phase 1.** May indicate a parasite, an infection, or a structural problem (celiac, IBD) that the protocol cannot address alone.
- **Fever with abdominal symptoms.**
- **Vomiting that prevents food intake** for more than 24 hours.

The Peat framework is designed for the chronic functional pattern that conventional medicine has nothing to offer. It is not a substitute for working up bleeding, weight loss, or fever.

## Counter-arguments

The functional-medicine and microbiome-research communities largely reject this framework. The dominant view is that beneficial bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids (especially butyrate) that nourish colonocytes, regulate immunity, and protect against disease; that dietary fiber is essential because it feeds these bacteria; that probiotic supplementation can correct dysbiosis; and that the goal is microbial diversity, not sterility. Peat read the same literature and drew the opposite conclusion: that the short-chain fatty acids are toxins at the doses observed, that fiber's value is mechanical (transit and binding), that probiotic supplementation does not stably colonize, and that the healthy small intestine is sterile in 15–20% of people for a reason.

Both sides accept the clinical reality of leaky-gut-like syndromes and the role of LPS in systemic disease. They disagree on the direction of the intervention. The strongest counter-evidence to Peat is the FMT (fecal microbiota transplant) literature in *C. difficile* and possibly IBS, where restoring a complex community appears to work. The strongest evidence for Peat is the consistent clinical improvement of women on short antibiotic courses described in the [[2010-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endotoxins|2010 endotoxins episode]], and the centuries-old observation Peat invoked from Metchnikoff and Bogomolets that long-intestined animals with long retention are short-lived.

## Data Gaps

- **No controlled trials of the full protocol.** Each component has supporting research; the integrated sequence is clinical practice, not RCT.
- **Methane-vs-hydrogen SIBO subtypes** are not directly addressed in Peat's writings. He treated methane as an anesthetic that drives constipation but did not differentiate tetracycline-vs-rifaximin choices for the two patterns.
- **The boundary between "use aspirin" and "use a course of antibiotics"** is clinician-judgment territory in Peat's framework rather than algorithmic.
- **Long-term outcomes of intermittent activated charcoal.** Plausibly fine; not formally studied at low doses.
- **Pediatric application.** Peat addressed adult patterns; pediatric gut restoration would adapt the framework but is not directly documented.
