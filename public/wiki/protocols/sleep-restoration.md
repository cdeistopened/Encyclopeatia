# Sleep Restoration

Mainstream sleep advice treats sleep as a restorative state and insomnia as a behavioral problem. Ray Peat inverted both assumptions. In his framework, sleep is not a state the body enters to recover. It is a defensive posture the body adopts to minimize the cumulative damage of darkness, which he treated as a metabolic insult rather than a neutral environmental fact. The body doesn't sleep to repair itself. It sleeps because darkness stresses cells, depletes glycogen, and triggers a cascade of protective hormones, and lying still with consciousness offline is the cheapest way to ride out that period. Sleep is rationing, not restoration.

This reframe matters because it relocates every sleep problem away from behavior and into metabolism. Insomnia is not a sleep disorder. It is a daytime metabolic disorder that happens to present at night. The canonical 3 AM wake-up is not a "sleep problem" any more than a hypoglycemic tremor at 10 AM is a "standing problem." It is a nocturnal cortisol event triggered by falling blood sugar, and the fix is nutritional, hormonal, and thermal rather than behavioral. Sleep hygiene advice about screens, bedtime routines, and black-out curtains addresses neither the cause nor the correct mechanism, and in several cases points the wrong direction.

This protocol is built from [[sleep|Peat's framework on sleep]] and lays out a conditional, sequenced path to restore deep, uninterrupted sleep. It assumes you are willing to eat before bed, willing to eat in the middle of the night if you wake, and willing to abandon the cold-dark-empty-room template. Each intervention targets a specific node in the nocturnal stress cascade described below. The sections are ordered: earlier interventions are higher leverage, and most people stop needing later ones once the foundation is in place.

## 1. The Nocturnal Cortisol Cascade

Waking between 1 and 4 AM is a predictable metabolic event. The sequence, compressed:

1. You fall asleep with a finite reserve of liver glycogen, most of which came from the last meal.
2. The brain and heart draw on that reserve continuously for six to eight hours.
3. After three to four hours, in most adults, glycogen runs low.
4. Blood sugar begins to fall.
5. The body responds with [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]] to mobilize gluconeogenic substrate (fat and muscle) and keep the brain fueled.
6. [[Cortisol]] and adrenaline are alerting hormones. They raise heart rate, raise body temperature, and break sleep.
7. You wake, often with a pounding heart, anxiety, or a sense of dread. It feels psychological. It is biochemical.
8. You lie there ruminating, which keeps adrenaline high, which delays sleep return.
9. The pattern repeats nightly. Over weeks, the liver's capacity to store glycogen is chronically low. The cascade fires earlier and harder. What started as an occasional bad night becomes chronic insomnia.

Peat's summary, via Sophie Lamb: insomnia is a daytime disorder presenting itself at night. The liver that could not stock up on glucose during the day cannot carry the brain through the night. Everything in this protocol aims, directly or indirectly, at refilling that reserve and blunting the cortisol response that replaces it when it runs out.

## 2. The Bedtime Snack — The Most Important Intervention

This is non-negotiable for anyone with sleep issues. Before adding supplements, changing your bedroom, or investigating hormones, start here. If you do nothing else from this document, do this.

The bedtime snack is a small, deliberate intake of sugar, salt, and some fat, taken 30 to 60 minutes before lying down. Its job is to top off liver glycogen, lower adrenaline at the moment of sleep onset, and buy you additional hours before the cortisol cascade fires. Peat's specific phrasing was that salt and sugar both lower adrenaline and both tend to raise body temperature, which is exactly what you want going into sleep.

Pick one of these:

1. **The Peat Classic.** 8 oz warm [[milk]] with a tablespoon of honey. The lactose and honey supply glucose and fructose. The fat in whole milk slows absorption. The calcium is calming. This is the default. If you have no idea where to start, start here.
2. **The Sugar-Forward.** A small bowl of ice cream. Peat's literal recommendation for insomnia more than once. The balance of fat, protein, and sugar makes it slow-releasing. For people who resist eating "real food" before bed, this is often the easiest sell.
3. **The Salt-Heavy.** 4 to 6 oz orange juice with a generous pinch of [[salt]]. Fast onset, good for people who cannot tolerate dairy. The salt blunts the adrenaline curve more than the juice alone.
4. **The Gelatin Version.** A small cup of bone broth or warm water with a scoop of gelatin, a teaspoon of honey, and a pinch of salt. The glycine from gelatin is calming in its own right. Useful if you wake digesting a heavier snack.
5. **The Cheese Version.** A small portion of hard cheese with a piece of fruit. Calcium plus tryptophan plus sugar. Good for people who find milk too bloating.

Take the snack 30 to 60 minutes before bed, not right as you lie down. You want some absorption before sleep onset but not a full stomach working against you. The sugar maintains liver glycogen overnight. The salt suppresses adrenaline and supports blood volume. The fat slows the glucose curve so it does not spike and crash.

If you try only one thing and wait a week, try this, and wait a week.

## 3. Why NOT Melatonin

[[Melatonin]] is the most widely sold sleep aid in American health food stores and one of the substances Peat opposed most consistently over four decades. His position was that melatonin is not a sleep hormone at all. It is a stress hormone, produced in the pineal gland in response to darkness and in response to any condition that raises adrenaline. The fact that it rises at night does not make it sleep-promoting any more than cortisol rising at night makes cortisol sleep-promoting. Both are signatures of nocturnal stress.

The specific harms Peat attributed to chronic melatonin elevation or supplementation:

1. Suppression of [[thyroid]] function
2. Suppression of [[progesterone]]
3. Stimulation of [[estrogen]]
4. Shrinkage of the thymus, which damages immunity
5. Shrinkage of the sex organs
6. A sluggish, dulled quality to the brain rather than genuine rest

If you are considering melatonin, the real problem is not a melatonin deficiency. The real problem is almost certainly cortisol driven by falling blood sugar, addressed by sections 2 and 5 of this protocol. Melatonin may make you feel sleepy in the same way a low-grade stressor can make you feel tired, but it does not produce the deep, high-metabolism sleep that restores glycogen and rebuilds tissue. Drop it and address the cortisol cascade directly.

## 4. The 3 AM Wake-Up Protocol

If you wake in the night, do not lie there and try to "get back to sleep." Lying in bed with rising cortisol teaches your nervous system to associate bed with alertness and wastes an hour during which you could have short-circuited the cascade in ten minutes.

The protocol when you wake:

1. Get up. Go to the kitchen.
2. Have one of the following:
   - 4 to 6 oz of orange juice with a pinch of salt
   - 4 to 6 oz of warm milk with a teaspoon of honey
   - A teaspoon of honey with a pinch of salt dissolved in a small glass of warm water
3. Drink it. Do not force down a full meal.
4. Go back to bed.
5. Sleep usually returns within 15 to 30 minutes, because cortisol drops when glucose rises.

This is not bad sleep hygiene. It is the only effective response to a cortisol-driven wake. Peat described knowing people who had nightmares every hour through the night and who lengthened the gap between nightmares simply by getting up and drinking juice or milk. Within a week, the liver would rebuild enough capacity to carry them through the whole night without a wake.

If you wake at the same time every night for weeks, Peat recommended a preemptive variation: set an alarm for about 30 minutes before your usual wake time, have a small carb snack, and go back to sleep. This often breaks the pattern within a week by re-teaching the liver to hold glycogen for longer.

## 5. Evening Food Strategy

The bedtime snack is necessary but not sufficient. Dinner composition sets up whether you go into the night fueled or fasted.

1. Include some protein, but not too much. Roughly 20 to 30 grams. A whole fish, a modest portion of meat, cottage cheese, or eggs. Protein alone at dinner drives a cortisol surge through the night because the body metabolizes amino acids into glucose via gluconeogenesis, the same stress pathway you are trying to avoid. A large steak without carbs is a recipe for 3 AM waking.
2. Include saturated fat. Butter, cream, coconut oil, ghee. Fat slows gastric emptying and smooths the glucose curve.
3. Include a carb. Potato, fruit, milk, honey, well-cooked squash, masa, white rice. Something the body can store as glycogen. This is where the liver refills.
4. Salt liberally. Peat consistently recommended salting to taste rather than restricting, because salt lowers cortisol and adrenaline and protects blood volume overnight.
5. Eat dinner 2 to 3 hours before bed, then the bedtime snack closer to bed. A large meal in the last hour before sleep competes with sleep onset because digestion is active, and a large fatty meal immediately before lying down can produce reflux.

Avoid the following at dinner:

1. Protein alone without carbs. The classic "just a piece of chicken and some greens" dinner is one of the most reliable ways to engineer a cortisol surge at 2 AM.
2. Starchy vegetables and beans in large quantity if you are prone to endotoxin-driven insomnia. These ferment in the gut and raise serotonin and histamine overnight.
3. Raw cruciferous vegetables in large volumes. They can ferment and irritate.

A simple template: a small portion of protein, a generous serving of fruit or potato with butter and salt, a glass of milk or a small dessert with the bedtime snack.

## 6. The Light Question — Peat's Counterintuitive Position

Conventional sleep advice is to make the bedroom pitch black. Peat was not pro-total-darkness. He wrote consistently that bright incandescent light in the evening was protective, that red and infrared wavelengths quenched the free radicals that accumulate during darkness, and that a dim amber or red lamp through the night was preferable to absolute black.

Reasons, in his framework:

1. Total darkness triggers [[melatonin]] production, which Peat treated as a stress signal.
2. A pitch-black room plus a cold room drives adrenaline, not relaxation.
3. Red incandescent light at night is mitochondrially supportive. It quenches excited electrons in tissue and does not disrupt sleep in the way blue light does.
4. Even a few seconds of bright incandescent exposure during the day can interrupt the free radical chain reactions that build up through the night.

Practical implementation:

1. Use incandescent bulbs in the bedroom, not LEDs or fluorescents. Amber or red-tinted is best.
2. Keep a dim red or amber lamp on through the night, or leave a small incandescent night light in the hallway. Resist the urge to blackout the room with tape over the smoke detector.
3. Avoid blue light from screens in the last hour or two before bed. This is the one place where Peat and conventional advice converge, but for a different reason: it is not "screen time" per se, it is the wavelength and the metabolic cost of elevated stimulation at the wrong time.
4. In the morning, get sunlight on your skin and eyes within an hour of waking. This resets temperature and thyroid signaling and breaks the nocturnal stress cycle for the day.

If you have been religiously blacking out your bedroom and still waking at 3 AM, loosen it. Let some ambient light in. The problem is not that your room is too bright.

## 7. Temperature for Sleep

The mainstream advice is to sleep in a 65-degree room. Peat disagreed for most people. Temperature drops naturally through the night, and every drop in temperature magnifies the stress response. Cold feet are one of the most reliable predictors of difficulty falling asleep and staying asleep.

His position:

1. A moderately warm room is better than a cold room for most people. Warmth supports metabolism and allows the deep slow-wave sleep that actually restores glycogen.
2. Cold feet will wake you or prevent sleep onset. Wool socks solve this faster than any supplement.
3. Use a down comforter or wool blanket, not thin sheets. You want to sleep under something that holds heat.
4. The "cold room" advice you read in sleep magazines triggers adrenaline in the average hypothyroid American and makes insomnia worse, not better.
5. A hot bath 30 to 60 minutes before bed raises core temperature, then as it falls it can ease sleep onset. Peat said warming the feet alone had the same effect as thyroid in improving sleep quality.

The exception: if you are genuinely running hot, for instance a febrile illness or a rare hyperthyroid state, cooler is fine. For everyone else, the rule is warm feet, warm room, heavy covers.

## 8. Progesterone for Sleep, Especially for Women

[[Progesterone]] is the most potent natural sleep aid in Peat's pharmacopeia, and it is particularly important for women. Its calming action comes through two pathways. It is converted to allopregnanolone, which acts on the GABA receptor the same way benzodiazepines do, but without the addiction and tolerance. And it raises the temperature set point, which supports the metabolic state in which deep sleep occurs.

Women in the luteal phase of a normal cycle sleep better than in the follicular phase because progesterone is high. Women around menopause lose that benefit, and insomnia is one of the most common menopausal symptoms for this reason. Women with estrogen dominance at any age tend to sleep poorly because estrogen opposes progesterone, lowers temperature, and suppresses GABA.

Practical use:

1. Topical natural progesterone (Progest-E or similar Vitamin E based preparation) applied at bedtime. A drop or two inside the lip or on the gums. Titrate up slowly.
2. For women with severe menopausal insomnia, doses can be higher; consult a practitioner who understands Peat's framework.
3. For men, small amounts of progesterone can help in specific high-cortisol or high-estrogen states, but it is not a routine sleep aid for men the way it is for women.
4. Do not confuse this with synthetic progestins, which are a different class of compound and do not have the same effect.

If you are a woman with years of sleep issues that got worse around 40, progesterone is often the single highest-leverage intervention after the bedtime snack.

## 9. Supplement Support

Conservative list, in rough order of usefulness, all secondary to food and thermal strategy:

1. **Magnesium glycinate**, 200 to 400 mg at bedtime. Cofactor for hundreds of enzymes, mildly calming. Peat generally approved of magnesium supplementation because most people are deficient. Glycinate is well tolerated and adds a small glycine load.
2. **[[Glycine]]**, 3 to 5 grams at bedtime. Inhibitory neurotransmitter, anti-cortisol, calming. Can be taken as a powder in warm water or as a few tablespoons of gelatin dissolved in broth.
3. **Vitamin B6**, as P5P, 25 to 50 mg. Supports dopamine synthesis and helps balance serotonin. Useful for PMS-related insomnia.
4. **Progesterone** for women as above.
5. **Niacinamide**, 100 to 500 mg at bedtime. Slows nocturnal lipolysis, which means less PUFA mobilization during the hours when glycogen is low. Useful for people who have been on high-PUFA diets historically.
6. **Gelatin at dinner**, a tablespoon or two stirred into broth or a sauce. Supplies glycine and balances the tryptophan load of muscle meats.
7. **Aspirin**, used cautiously. Peat noted it can help with sleep but sometimes its first effect is stimulating, so try it earlier in the day before using it at night. If you use aspirin regularly, get some vitamin K from liver or leafy greens.

Not recommended:

1. **Melatonin supplements.** See section 3.
2. **5-HTP.** Pushes serotonin, which Peat associated with torpor rather than genuine deep sleep. Avoid.
3. **Tryptophan supplements.** Same reason.
4. **Valerian.** Mild but Peat was neutral on it and noted a risk of habituation.
5. **Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs (Ambien, Lunesta).** Not Peat's territory, but chronic use produces dependence, tolerance, and fragmented non-restorative sleep.

## 10. The Over-Exercise Trap

Chronic heavy training raises cortisol and depletes glycogen in ways that directly oppose the goals of this protocol. If you are training hard six days a week and sleeping poorly, reducing training volume is cheaper, faster, and more effective than any supplement.

Light movement, walking, carrying, easy yard work, short sessions with weights at lower intensity, all support sleep. Long endurance sessions, heavy lifting to failure, and fasted training all tax the same glycogen system that runs out at 3 AM. If you are stuck, pull training back for two weeks and see if sleep returns before you add anything else.

## 11. Signs Restoration Is Working

You know the protocol is taking hold when:

1. You fall asleep within 15 minutes of lying down.
2. You are not waking between 1 and 4 AM.
3. You wake at roughly the same time each morning without an alarm.
4. You feel warm, not cold, on waking.
5. Your dreams are vivid and generally pleasant, a sign of adequate REM sleep on a fueled brain.
6. Your morning oral temperature is above 97.8°F, ideally rising toward 98.2 to 98.6 within an hour of eating breakfast.
7. Your energy is stable from mid-morning through the afternoon, without the 3 PM crash that signals cortisol compensation.
8. You do not need caffeine to function, though you may enjoy it.

If most of these are true, you are done adjusting and just need to hold the pattern. If none of these are true after eight weeks, go back to section 2 and check whether you are actually doing the bedtime snack every night without exception.

## 12. The Chronic Insomnia Pathway

For someone with years of disrupted sleep, recovery takes weeks, not days. The liver has to relearn how to stock glycogen. The cortisol axis has to recalibrate. Expect the following rough timeline:

1. **Week 1 to 2.** Bedtime snack every night. Warm feet. Track wake times in a simple log. Expect no change yet or a small improvement.
2. **Week 2 to 4.** Add the evening routine: dinner with carbs and salt 2 to 3 hours before bed, warm milk with honey 30 to 60 minutes before bed. Begin the 3 AM wake protocol if you still wake. Expect fewer wakes and faster sleep onset.
3. **Week 4 to 6.** If you are still waking, add magnesium glycinate and glycine at bedtime. Verify the room is warm enough and your feet are warm. Expect most of the remaining wakes to fade.
4. **Week 6 to 8.** If you are still waking, women add topical progesterone. Men add niacinamide. Revisit dinner composition: are you eating enough carbs with dinner, or are you still defaulting to protein-and-salad?
5. **Week 8 and beyond.** If you are still waking, the limiting factor is almost certainly thyroid. Hypothyroid people cannot store glycogen well enough to sleep through the night no matter how much sugar they eat before bed. This is the point at which sleep restoration turns into hypothyroid recovery, which is a longer project with its own protocol. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

The order matters. Thyroid is the ultimate answer for many chronic insomniacs, but starting there without the bedtime snack is like turning up a furnace in a house with all the windows open. Fix the food and warmth first. Add thyroid last, when everything else has been ruled out.
