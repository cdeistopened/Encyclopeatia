# Dietary Fats Ranked

Ray Peat's framework for dietary fat is not "eat more fat" and not "eat less fat." It is a framework about which fats your mitochondria can burn without being damaged. The axis he cared about was saturated versus polyunsaturated, not animal versus plant, and not total fat intake. A diet built around stable saturated fats leaves thyroid hormone free to reach its receptors, keeps the stress-hormone vicious circle from starting, and allows the body to oxidize glucose cleanly. A diet built around [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] and the other polyunsaturated fats does the opposite: it displaces thyroid from transthyretin, feeds the inflammatory prostaglandin cascade, poisons mitochondria, and locks the body into the [[randle-cycle|Randle block]] where stored fat crowds out sugar oxidation.

What follows is a ranking of common dietary fats against this framework, drawn entirely from the fat entity articles in this vault. S-tier fats are the ones Peat actively recommended and ate himself. F-tier fats are the ones he said to eliminate. The middle tiers describe the gradient in between. Where Peat was explicit, the ranking follows him directly. Where the corpus is thinner, the ranking errs conservative.

## The Endogenous Exception: Mead Acid

There is a fat Peat ranked above all dietary fats, and the body makes it itself. [[mead-acid|Mead acid]] is a 20-carbon omega-9 fatty acid (5,8,11-eicosatrienoic acid) that the body synthesizes from oleic acid when dietary linoleic acid is absent or very low. Mainstream biochemistry treats the presence of Mead acid in blood as a clinical alarm, labeling it "essential fatty acid deficiency." Peat argued that this labeling inverts the biology. The delta-5 and delta-6 desaturases that produce Mead acid are suppressed by dietary PUFA; their activity is the normal state, and their suppression is the real deficiency.

Mead acid derivatives are anti-inflammatory without being immunosuppressive. Where arachidonic acid produces prostaglandins that amplify inflammation into scar tissue, keloids, and tumors, Mead acid derivatives keep the inflammatory signal proportional to the injury. Some of them act on the cannabinoid receptor. All of them resist oxidation in a way that omega-3 and omega-6 fats cannot. Human newborns produce Mead acid even when their mothers eat fish and grains, which Peat read as evidence that the placenta filters PUFA and the fetus builds its brain out of the endogenous omega-9 series.

The practical implication of Mead acid is that the goal of dietary fat management is not to eat a specific "good fat." The goal is to lower dietary PUFA below the threshold (roughly 0.6% of calories) at which the body's own desaturases wake up and produce the protective fat Peat considered the real physiological standard.

## The Cooking Hierarchy

Heat compounds the difference between these fats. A stable fat heated to frying temperature stays intact; an unstable fat generates peroxidation products long before its smoke point. The usable ranking for cooking is:

- **High heat (frying, searing, roasting):** coconut oil, beef tallow, lamb fat, cocoa butter, ghee, palm kernel oil. Anything ruminant-saturated or tropical-saturated.
- **Medium heat (sauteing, baking):** butter (watch for the milk solids browning), ghee for higher temperatures.
- **No heat / finishing only:** olive oil, in trace amounts, for flavor.
- **Never heat:** any oil containing meaningful PUFA. This includes flaxseed, fish oil, and all seed oils, even if a label calls them "high-heat safe." The label is wrong.
- **Never eat:** anything fried at a restaurant. Almost all commercial deep-frying since 1990 uses soybean, canola, or a blend. Peat's "I fry the bacon to remove some of the fat, and then refry it in coconut oil, to remove most of the PUFA" is the opposite of what happens in a commercial kitchen.

## The Saturated-vs-PUFA Distinction

This is the axis Peat actually cared about. The mainstream debate about whether saturated fat "raises cholesterol" or whether butter is "back" misses the point. The framework is about what happens inside a mitochondrion when a given fatty acid reaches it.

Saturated fats burn cleanly. They enter the mitochondrion, get oxidized to CO2 and water, and produce ATP without leaving peroxidation products in the membrane. Short and medium-chain saturated fats (coconut, butter's butyric acid) burn especially fast and behave more like sugar than like stored fat, which is why they can be simultaneously pro-thyroid and pro-glycolytic. Long-chain saturated fats (stearic, palmitic) are slower but equally stable. None of them feed the arachidonic acid cascade. None of them displace thyroxine from [[transthyretin]]. None of them generate lipofuscin.

Polyunsaturated fats do all of these things. [[linoleic-acid|Linoleic acid]] blocks thyroid at four separate points: gland secretion, blood transport, T4-to-T3 conversion, and tissue reception. Its conversion to arachidonic acid feeds the prostaglandin cascade Peat read as the engine of chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and metastatic cancer. Its peroxidation products (4-hydroxynonenal, malondialdehyde, acrolein) damage mitochondrial enzymes and generate the protein crosslinks mainstream science misattributes to glucose. It drives the stress-hormone vicious circle Peat described in "Fats, Functions and Malfunctions," where PUFA in the free fatty acid pool stimulates cortisol and adrenaline, which release more PUFA. And it locks in the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] block, where circulating free fatty acids outcompete glucose for oxidation and leave the body unable to burn sugar even when sugar is present.

This is why the ranking is what it is. A fat is good for you if it supports oxidative metabolism without contributing to any of these processes. A fat is bad for you if it contributes to them. Total fat intake matters less than composition. A diet with 35% of calories from butter and coconut oil is safer than a diet with 20% of calories from olive oil and canola, because composition determines what ends up in the mitochondrion and what ends up stored in adipose tissue for the next four years.
