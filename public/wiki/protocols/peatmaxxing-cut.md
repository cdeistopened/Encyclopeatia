# The Peatmaxxing Cut

This protocol assumes you've read [[anti-stress-diet]]. The weight loss variant doesn't replace the baseline; it dials specific levers within it. Every food, supplement, and rule below is already present in the anti-stress framework. What changes is the fat content of the milk, the presence of a small daily dose of coconut oil, the steadiness of the carrot, and the instrument you measure progress with.

## The Thesis in Peat's Own Words

The inversion at the center of this document is a single sentence from one of Peat's emails on weight loss: "My recommendation is to eat to increase the metabolic rate (usually temperature and heart rate), rather than any particular foods." That is the whole program. Raise the metabolic rate. Fat loss follows. The scale does not. The inverse of the usual advice is the point, and it is not a rhetorical trick. Peat spent decades watching calorie-restricted women crash their thyroids, atrophy their muscles, and gain water back the instant they stopped starving. He concluded that the lever mainstream dietetics pulls on is the wrong lever.

The right one is heat. You want to run warmer, not eat less. You want your temperature to rise after breakfast, your pulse to settle in the mid-seventies to mid-eighties, your hands and feet to stay warm all day, and your stored polyunsaturated fats to slowly leave your tissues across a horizon measured in years, not weeks. When that happens, body composition reorganizes around the metabolic state you've restored. Muscle returns because protein is no longer being wasted as fuel under cortisol. Water leaves the tissues because stress hormones are no longer retaining it. Fat oxidation picks up because free fatty acids and thyroid-suppressing linoleic acid are no longer flooding the bloodstream. The shape that results, lean and warm and capable, is what a properly-fueled body looks like. It is a byproduct of the metabolism, not a goal pursued independently of it.

Measure the right things and the protocol becomes obvious. Measure the wrong things and you'll throw it away inside of a month. Temperature and waist circumference are the instruments. The bathroom scale is noise.

## The Mechanism: Why Undereating Fails

The Peat corpus contains a ruined cohort of women who tried the mainstream approach and arrived at his door with broken metabolisms. In [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|a 2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on weight gain]], he described the pattern with unusual bluntness. The textbook standard that everyone loses weight under 1,700 calories a day, he said, came from a 1920s study on healthy people. When researchers tested women who claimed they were gaining weight on 1,000 calories and locked them in a closed ward, what they found was that "some could maintain their weight on 700 calories a day, especially women who had dieted a lot."

Those women were not lying about their intake. They were running metabolically cold. Peat laid out the cascade: "Several adaptations happen due to chronic, poorly balanced dieting. The thyroid slows down, and the muscle tissues atrophy from the stress. If you think of the stress metabolism as being very similar to the diabetic metabolism, basically you shift over to burning fat rather than sugar." The body banks on its own tissue for fuel and defends fat stores as a safety margin against perceived starvation. The scale obliges with a drop for a few weeks. The metabolism quietly collapses behind it.

The atrophy ran visibly through his practice. "Many of the women I saw for weight loss diets had muscles the size of a finger," he told listeners on the same show. When they started eating a proper diet, the first thing that happened was pounds of water loss as the tissue came out of the stress condition and shed retained fluid. Then, "for about a month, they would start putting on weight as their muscles began growing." The muscle regrowth is the phase that destroys most people psychologically. Scale weight rises for weeks. The reader who is trained to worship the scale quits, goes back to restriction, and finishes the cycle a little smaller in muscle mass and a little more hypometabolic than when they started.

The fasting failure mode is a faster version of the same physiology. In a [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 show on sugar]], Peat described what happens after glycogen runs out. "Cortisol begins breaking down your tissues, turning protein into fat and carbohydrate for energy. Under that extreme condition, when you've been out of glycogen long enough to run on cortisol for about a day, your thymus will be dissolved just in a few hours." The thymus goes first because it dissolves fast under stress. Skin atrophies next. Then skeletal muscle begins its long slow conversion into sugar for the brain. "People who fast for a week or two will usually lose more muscle weight than fat because of the very rapid conversion of protein into fuel under the influence of cortisol." This is the opposite of the physiology you want for body recomposition. It is also, across the emails and transcripts, the single most consistent thing Peat warned people away from. Link to [[cortisol]], [[thyroid]], and [[hypothyroidism]] for the full mechanism.

## The Two Levers That Actually Work

Everything in this protocol reduces to two levers. The first is the metabolic rate itself, raised by food, salt, warmth, thyroid support where needed, and the restoration of glycogen storage in the liver. The second is the stored polyunsaturated fat in your own tissues, which Peat identified as the slowest and most decisive weight loss variable. In his weight loss email he wrote: "It's the stored PUFA, released by stress or hunger, that slow metabolism. Niacinamide helps to lower free fatty acids, and good nutrition will allow the liver to slowly detoxify the PUFA, if it isn't being flooded with large amounts of them. A small amount of coconut oil with each meal will increase the ability to oxidize fat, by momentarily stopping the antithyroid effect of the PUFA."

The first lever responds in weeks. You feel it as warmth returning to the hands and feet, sleep deepening, morning energy steadying. The second lever responds in years. Peat wrote, in the same email, that "when the polyunsaturated fats in the diet are reduced, the amount of them stored in the tissues decreases for about four years, making it progressively easier to keep the metabolic rate up, and stress hormones down." Most readers want a result in ninety days. Understand going in that ninety days gets you the first lever pulled. The second lever is a horizon you walk toward for four years while the first one pays you daily in energy and warmth.

## The Weight Loss Plate

The literal prescription Peat gave for weight loss is short enough to put on an index card. From his weight loss email exchange: "I have heard from several people that they think I recommend drinking whole milk, which I don't, because the amount of fat in whole milk is very likely to be fattening when a person is using it to get the needed protein and calcium. When a person wants to lose excess fat, limiting the diet to low fat milk, eggs, orange juice, and a daily carrot or two, will provide the essential nutrients without excess calories."

That is the weight loss plate in one sentence. Low fat milk, eggs, orange juice, a daily carrot. Everything else in this protocol is either a support lever or a clarification of how those four foods should be used.

**Low-fat milk.** Peat himself averaged a gallon of 2% or 3% milk a day through his adult life, and his baseline anti-stress diet is written around whole or two-percent milk. For weight loss specifically, he reversed that advice. The logic is calorie arithmetic without calorie obsession. On a quart of 1% milk you get roughly 400 calories, 32 grams of high-quality protein, and about 1,200 mg of calcium, with minimal fat contributing to the total. Two quarts supplies the protein floor, the calcium floor, and enough milk sugar to keep cortisol quiet, for under 800 calories. Sarah Johanneson Murray, speaking on the [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 weight gain episode]], made the arithmetic explicit: "If you use 1% milk, that's only 400 calories per quart. Orange juice has chemicals that are anti-estrogenic, and milk calcium is very important as an anti-inflammatory and anti-stress agent. People who eat the same number of calories without milk are much more likely to be fat." The calcium matters as much as the protein. Peat returned repeatedly to the calcium-to-phosphate ratio as a weight loss variable on its own: "The ratio of calcium to phosphate is very important; that's why milk and cheese are so valuable for weight loss, or for preventing weight gain." Target two quarts of 1% or skim milk a day. Three is fine if appetite and metabolism support it.

**Eggs.** One or two a day is the weight loss target. Sarah's cap in the [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 food episode]] was blunt: "You can't eat more than two eggs a day because they contain some PUFA." Peat himself wrote in his diet emails that "2 or 3 eggs would be o.k., though in the longer run it's good to eat liver about twice a month, limiting the daily eggs to one or two." Cook them in butter, keep the yolks soft. The yolk is where the choline, the vitamin A, and the real nutrient density live. Pair every egg meal with orange juice. Peat wrote, "I find that I need almost a pint of orange juice to balance one egg." The pairing is not aesthetic; it is to buffer the cortisol surge that follows protein in the absence of carbohydrate.

**Orange juice.** A quart a day is the working target, more if you can drink it and keep your temperature up. Peat's baseline in the [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 food episode]] was "as much as is available; if it's good, at least a pint, preferably more." In his diet-general email he noted: "I have often had a gallon of orange juice in a day, with 100 grams of other sugar, and didn't see any problem, even while being sedentary." The fructose component of sucrose, he added, "helps to increase the metabolic rate." Orange juice carries potassium, magnesium, the anti-estrogenic flavonoids Sarah mentioned, and sugar in a form the liver can convert straight into glycogen. It is the engine of the cut. Cutting it because it is "sugar" is the fastest way to sabotage the protocol. Fresh is best. Not-from-concentrate in a glass bottle is acceptable. Frozen concentrate is acceptable if the fresh options are poor.

**Raw carrot.** One or two a day. Eat the carrot raw, scraped or grated or chewed whole, with a little vinegar and olive oil if desired, with salt. This is not a filler vegetable. It is an endotoxin and estrogen sponge. In his diet email Peat wrote: "A daily raw carrot often helps to balance progesterone, cortisol, and estrogen, by improving intestine-liver functions." In his weight loss email he was more direct: "Yes, I know people who have lost weight just by eating a raw carrot every day, reducing endotoxin stress." Read that sentence twice. People have lost weight on the carrot alone, because the carrot's fiber binds bacterial endotoxin in the small intestine and the weakly-bound estrogens moving through enterohepatic circulation, and because endotoxin and estrogen are two of the main stress loads depressing your thyroid. The carrot is the cheapest intervention in the entire protocol and one of the highest leverage.

Those four foods form the core. Around them, gelatinous soup, cheese for calcium, an occasional shellfish meal for trace minerals, small amounts of fruit beyond the orange juice, and well-cooked potato or masa for variety are all consistent with the baseline anti-stress diet. What you are avoiding on the cut is the excess fat of whole milk, ice cream and cream, the large protein meals that drive cortisol, and the endurance exercise that burns protein for fuel. You are not avoiding sugar. You are not restricting starch categorically. You are not fasting. You are eating enough to stay warm, with the fat calories pulled back to open a small and sustainable deficit while the metabolic rate does the real work.

## Coconut Oil as the Specific Weight Loss Lever

Peat devoted more attention to [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]] than to any other single fat, and the mechanism for its weight loss effect is explicit across the corpus. Short and medium-chain saturated fatty acids enter the mitochondria without requiring the carnitine transport system. They are oxidized fast. In the hour or two after a tablespoon of coconut oil, they occupy the oxidative machinery and displace the long-chain polyunsaturated fats that would otherwise circulate and suppress thyroid function. The metabolic rate climbs for an hour or so and then returns to baseline. Taken with each meal, coconut oil supplies what amounts to intermittent thyroid support across the day.

The dosing across Peat's own sources spreads across three tiers, and the protocol should name all three honestly. In the [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 weight gain episode]], Sarah Johanneson Murray told the story of a hypometabolic woman burning only 700 calories a day and asked whether her standard 3-tablespoon recommendation applied. Peat answered: "No, more like a small teaspoon three times a day." This is the hypometabolic tier. If your morning temperature is below 97°F and you gain weight easily on small intakes, start with a teaspoon of coconut oil at each meal and nothing more. Adding more at this stage can drive lipolysis of your own stored PUFA faster than the liver can clear it, and you will feel worse for several days.

The middle tier is Peat's own n=1. In his weight loss email he wrote: "When I tried adding about a tablespoon of coconut oil once a day I lost about two pounds a week, for several weeks, without eating less." One tablespoon a day, no other changes, two pounds a week. That is the experiment he reported on himself. It is a reasonable target if your metabolism is already reasonably intact, meaning your morning temperature sits near 97.5° or higher and you warm up promptly after breakfast.

The upper tier is the standard Sarah recommended for clients with normal metabolism in the same 2013 episode: three tablespoons a day, typically one with each meal. This is the dose most practitioners in Peat's orbit default to, and it is the dose the weight loss animal literature supports. If you tolerate the first two tiers well for several weeks and your temperature is climbing, three tablespoons divided across meals is the working target.

Pick a tier based on where you are starting, not where you want to be. Move up a tier when your temperature and pulse say you can. Never start at three tablespoons if you've been hypometabolic for years; the short-term burden on the liver as PUFA mobilize from stored tissue is real, and you will feel the breathlessness, lightheadedness, or transient weight gain that corresponds to free fatty acids in the bloodstream. Take coconut oil with food, not on an empty stomach. Hydrogenated, deodorized, refined versions are what Peat preferred; he warned in his diet emails that unrefined coconut oil is allergenic because of the residual proteins. The goal is the fatty acids, not the whole-food experience.

## Gelatin and the Cortisol Suppression Rule

Every protein meal you eat needs carbohydrate with it. This is not a preference. It is the difference between a meal that supports thyroid and a meal that spikes cortisol and burns your own tissue for energy. The mechanism was something Peat repeated across a dozen sources. In his diet-general email he wrote: "Eating protein by itself can cause a big surge of cortisol. Preceding the protein with some carbohydrate makes the protein go farther, otherwise under the influence of cortisol a lot of protein is used just for energy." In his protein email he added: "Food proteins stimulate insulin secretion, and to prevent hypoglycemia cortisol is increased. The food proteins (along with tissue proteins) can be used for energy under the influence of cortisol." A big protein meal, he said in the [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|sugar episode]], "will cause a huge surge of cortisol."

The practical rule is to precede any protein meal by fifteen or twenty minutes with fruit, fruit juice, or milk. "I think it's good to precede a big protein meal by maybe 15 or 20 minutes with fruits so that you get your liver stocked with some glycogen," Peat said in the same episode. "That way, you don't respond so violently when you eat pure protein." Orange juice before eggs. Milk with the cheese plate. A glass of juice before the oysters. The glycogen pre-load gives the liver something to release when insulin drops after the protein meal, and that forestalls the cortisol surge.

[[gelatin|Gelatin]] is the second half of the cortisol rule. Muscle meats are heavy in tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine, all of which suppress thyroid function and drive the inflammation-serotonin axis. Gelatin, the cooked form of collagen, contains none of the tryptophan, very little methionine and cysteine, and about 35% [[glycine]]. In his article on gelatin and stress longevity, Peat wrote that "a generous supply of glycine/gelatin, against a balanced background of amino acids, has a great variety of antistress actions. Glycine is recognized as an inhibitory neurotransmitter, and promotes natural sleep." His practical form recommendation was broths, not powder: "I occasionally use some powdered gelatin for things like making marshmallows, but usually I get my gelatin from soup, such as ox-tails, lamb shanks, or chicken backs and wings." In the [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 food episode]] he described his own daily pattern: "Almost every day, some kind of gelatinous soup, like kale soup or chicken soup with all the fat skimmed off."

Peat never specified a gelatin gram target. The research brief flags this explicitly as a gap. The rule is structural, not numeric: enough gelatin in the day to balance the muscle meat you ate that day. Treat it as a ratio and a presence, not a weight. If you ate two eggs and a piece of fish, you want a bowl of ox-tail or chicken-back broth in the day somewhere. Skim the fat off the top if you are tracking fat intake for the cut.

## Salt, the Unsuspected Lever

Low-salt dieting is the hidden failure mode of most weight loss attempts. People cut sodium because the mainstream told them to, their adrenaline climbs, their sleep collapses, their morning temperature drops, and they blame the sugar or the milk instead of the thing they took out. Peat's position on salt is explicit and runs counter to almost every nutrition source the reader has ever encountered.

From his newsletter on salt, energy, metabolic rate, and longevity: "Salt restriction, according to a review of about 100 studies, lowers the blood pressure a few points. But that generally doesn't relate to better health. In one study (3000 people, 4 years), there was a clear increase in mortality in the individuals who ate less salt. An extra few grams of salt per day was associated with a 36% reduction in coronary events." And: "The increase of adrenalin caused by salt restriction has many harmful effects, including insomnia. Many old people have noticed that a low sodium diet disturbs their sleep, and that eating their usual amount of salt restores their ability to sleep." And the synthesis: "A lack of sodium slows metabolism, lowers carbon dioxide production, and creates inflammation, stress and degeneration. Rephrasing it, sodium stimulates energy metabolism, increases carbon dioxide production, and protects against inflammation and other maladaptive stress reactions."

In the [[55-bioenergetic-nutrition-basics|*Generative Energy* episode on nutrition basics]], Georgi Dinkov asked Peat directly whether anything less than five grams of elemental sodium a day chronically activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. Peat's answer was: "Oh, for sure. That tiny amount brings up your anxiety and inflammation. So salt is definitely anxiolytic and anti-inflammatory. A population in Mongolia, for example, eats an average of about 30 grams of salt per day and doesn't have hypertension." Five grams of elemental sodium is roughly thirteen grams of salt, or a bit over two teaspoons. That is the floor, not the ceiling. Mongolia's thirty grams a day without hypertension is Peat's way of signaling that the upper range is very wide.

Salt food to taste, and let the appetite run. If you crave salt, the craving is information. Pecorino, feta, a handful of good chicharrones, anchovies, a well-salted broth: the craving will find its form. The salt lowers adrenaline, lowers cortisol, raises carbon dioxide production, and keeps the metabolic machinery warm. Without enough of it, the rest of the protocol is working against an invisible headwind. Link to [[salt]] for the full treatment.

## Supplements During the Cut

Peat's position on supplements was cautious by default. In his supplements email he wrote that "because of contaminants in supplements I seldom recommend the oral use of any of them, except aspirin." And: "Thyroid and aspirin are among the safest, and the most likely to be valuable indefinitely." The supplements below are the ones that earn their place in a weight loss protocol from his own quotes. Everything else should wait until the food, salt, and coconut oil levers are pulled for at least three weeks.

**[[aspirin|Aspirin]].** Works on two of the protocol's main bottlenecks at once. It reduces stress-related free fatty acid release from tissue, and it inhibits aromatase, which converts testosterone to estrogen in adipose tissue. In the weight loss email, Peat wrote: "Aspirin is another thing that reduces the stress-related increase of free fatty acids, stimulating metabolism." The research brief flags explicitly that these source emails do not contain a specific milligram dose. Peat discussed aspirin at ranges from a few hundred milligrams up to several grams a day in other contexts. Cross-reference the [[aspirin]] article for the dose range appropriate to your situation, and dissolve it in warm water before drinking if possible, as Peat did, to remove most of the binders.

**Niacinamide.** One hundred milligrams. This is the dose he named in the supplements email ("100 mg of niacinamide can reduce some stress symptoms") and the function he named in the weight loss email ("Niacinamide helps to lower free fatty acids"). Its role during a cut is direct: as you mobilize stored fat, free fatty acids rise in the bloodstream, and the niacinamide lowers the rate of lipolysis enough to prevent the flood from overwhelming the liver. It also blocks some of the PUFA-driven stress signaling that comes with fat mobilization. A hundred milligrams a day is a small, safe dose Peat used himself. Do not confuse niacinamide with niacin; niacin causes flushing and is a different compound with different effects.

**[[vitamin-e|Vitamin E]].** Acts as the chain-breaker that prevents mobilized PUFA from oxidizing into the damaging peroxidation products while they leave your tissues. The supplements email lists vitamin D3, vitamin K, and selenium deficiencies as extremely widespread and safe to address. Vitamin E fits the same category in his weight loss writing. The research brief flags that a specific daily dose is not pinned down in these source files; cross-reference the [[vitamin-e]] article for a number.

**[[vitamin-k|Vitamin K]].** Directs circulating calcium out of soft tissue and into bone. Peat listed it as widely deficient in the supplements email. A one to five milligram range appears in one of the transcripts with ambiguous context; take the reference article as the source of truth on the dose.

**Thyroid glandular.** This is the supplement Peat was most direct about in a weight loss context. From the weight loss email: "Taking a thyroid supplement is reasonable until the ratio of saturated fats to PUFA is about 2 to 1." Read that carefully. He is saying the thyroid supplement is a bridge, not a lifetime commitment, and the bridge ends when the stored fat composition of your body has shifted enough toward saturation that endogenous thyroid signaling is no longer being suppressed by circulating PUFA. That is the four-year horizon again. In his thyroid email he described a starting dose as "5 mcg of T3 twice a day, while watching for changes in your pulse rate, temperature, and ability to sleep. Half a grain of Armour, or about 30 mcg of T4 and 7.5 mcg of T3, is traditionally a common starting dose; it should be taken with a meal, so that it absorbs slowly." Do not start thyroid until the diet has been in place for at least three weeks. The food alone will raise the metabolic rate in most readers, and adding thyroid on top of rising endogenous function can overshoot.

**[[progesterone|Progesterone]].** For women, briefly: Peat used progesterone widely for estrogen dominance, and estrogen dominance is a common driver of stubborn fat retention, particularly in the hips, thighs, and lower abdomen. See the progesterone article for the protocol; it is outside the scope of this document.

## The Four-Year Horizon

Everything above pulls the first lever. The second lever is slower and more decisive, and you should know about it now so you do not abandon the protocol in month three when the scale hasn't moved the way a crash diet would have moved it. Peat wrote in the weight loss email: "When the polyunsaturated fats in the diet are reduced, the amount of them stored in the tissues decreases for about four years, making it progressively easier to keep the metabolic rate up, and stress hormones down."

The stored [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] in your adipose tissue is the slowest clock in the body. It has a half-life of roughly two years. If you have been eating seed oil, [[fish-oil]], or large amounts of PUFA-heavy meats and nuts for decades, it is in your fat cells, your cell membranes, your liver, your brain, and your skin. When stress or hunger mobilizes that fat, the PUFA comes out with it and recirculates, acting on thyroid, suppressing oxidative metabolism, and forming [[age-pigment|age pigment]] as it oxidizes. Fast weight loss in a PUFA-loaded person is not a clean win. It is the mobilization of an inflammatory lipid pool into the bloodstream.

The slow lever is the reason Peat framed the weight loss horizon in years rather than months. It is also the reason the protocol can feel frustratingly modest in the first ninety days to someone who has been on a seed-oil diet since childhood. The first three months mostly restore water balance, repair muscle, and raise the metabolic rate. The fat loss that people associate with Peat's approach, the slow steady leaning out with no crash and no rebound, is the four-year turnover playing out in real time as the dietary ratio shifts. The coconut oil accelerates this. The aspirin and niacinamide buffer the mobilization. The vitamin E protects against the peroxidation products during the transit. But the horizon itself is fixed by physiology. Understand that going in and the protocol becomes a direction, not a deadline.

## Exercise: Low Volume, High Intensity, Never Fasted

Peat's exercise advice for weight loss is short, concrete, and directly opposed to almost every mainstream fat loss program. From his exercise email: "I think periods of intense muscular exertion should be limited to 20 or 30 seconds, followed by rest periods. Otherwise, T3 falls and the stress signals rise." And: "Concentric resistance training has an anabolic effect on the whole body. Sprinting is probably o.k. Endurance exercise is the worst." And, for the concrete prescription: "Ten pound dumbbells, lifted quickly for 30 to 60 seconds, for example, is usually good for increasing the anabolic and protective hormones."

The two things that make exercise harmful, he added, are "activity that keeps the lactic acid high chronically, and eccentric exercise, in which muscles are stretched while contracting, as in running downhill." Concentric movement, the muscle shortening under load, is preferred. Squats up from a chair, dumbbell curls, kettlebell swings, brief hill sprints of ten or fifteen seconds. The goal is to signal the muscle to grow and to raise metabolic rate briefly, not to burn calories through volume.

Endurance running is the worst thing a person on a cut can do, in Peat's framing, and he gave the reason in the 2013 weight gain episode: "There was a study using radioactive testosterone. They compared runners to weightlifters who had developed huge skeletal muscles. Using isotopes, they imaged the heart, lungs, and brain. The runners' tissues had atrophied; the lean long-distance runners had lean, flabby, very thin hearts." The endurance runner looks lean because their tissue is catabolized, not because their metabolism is high. It is a cortisol-driven lean, not a thyroid-driven lean.

Never train fasted. Eat something with sugar and salt fifteen to thirty minutes before lifting or walking. Walking is fine in any volume provided you are warm and fed. Sauna is fine and arguably useful for the warmth itself, provided you replace the fluid and salt afterward. A single set of ten to thirty squats twice a day, a few sets of light dumbbells, a walk after dinner. That is the entire exercise protocol.

## Measurement: Waist, Not Scale

The instrument you measure with determines the behavior you get. Use the wrong instrument and you will quit. Peat was explicit about this in the 2013 weight gain episode. Asked how he tracked progress with his weight loss clients, he answered: "We would have people measure their waists, thighs, and hips, and each week they would get smaller even as they gained weight."

This is the single most important sentence in the protocol for adherence. Scale weight during the first month of the cut will go down (water loss), then up (muscle regrowth), then slowly down again (fat loss) while circumferences continue to shrink across the whole arc. If you weigh yourself daily and panic at the muscle-regrowth phase, you will walk away from the protocol just as it starts working. If you measure your waist, thighs, and hips weekly with a tape measure, you will see the real signal: the body is reorganizing from the outside in.

The temperature and pulse targets are the second instrument. From Peat in the Hashimoto's, temperature, and pulse episode: "People have their chronic adaptation and can stay well at those numbers, but you have the greatest ability to resist stress and recuperate if your temperature and pulse are a little above average." The working target is 98.6°F oral temperature after breakfast and a resting pulse in the 75 to 85 range. Measure temperature and pulse on waking, then again mid-morning after breakfast, then again in the mid-afternoon. The pattern matters more than any single reading. "When the temperature goes up and pulse goes down, that's what breakfast should do," Peat said in the same episode. That is the signal that the carbohydrate is replacing adrenaline as the engine of your metabolic rate. A cold morning and a hot afternoon is fine. A cold morning that stays cold after breakfast is a signal that the glycogen is not going in fast enough.

From his weight loss email: "There are several convenient indicators of the metabolic rate: the daily temperature cycle and pulse rate (the temperature should rise after breakfast), the amount of water lost by evaporation, and the speed of relaxation of muscles (Achilles reflex relaxation)." Track temperature and pulse. Track waist. Ignore the scale for at least the first month, and use it only as a rough check thereafter.

## Signs the Protocol Is Working

Read these in order. The early signals precede any visible change in the mirror.

Morning temperature rising toward 98.6°F across weeks. Resting pulse moving into the mid-seventies to mid-eighties. Hands and feet staying warm through the day instead of going cold after meals or in the late afternoon. Sleep deepening. Dreams returning or becoming more vivid, which is a sign of thyroid function recovering. Morning erections returning in men. Cycles becoming regular in women, with less premenstrual distress. The craving for the right foods, especially salt, sugar, and occasionally liver or shellfish. The craving for ice water at room temperature rather than for cold liquids that chill the gut. Waist circumference shrinking before scale weight moves. More energy during the cut, not less. A calmer baseline mood, less reactivity to stress, less need for stimulants to get through the morning.

The scale pattern across the first six weeks will follow a specific shape that is worth naming in advance. Week one, pounds of water loss as the stress tissue releases fluid. Weeks two through five, possible scale weight gain as muscle regrows under cortisol-suppressed conditions. Weeks six onward, steady fat loss in the half-pound-to-one-pound-per-week range, with continued inches off the waist. Peat saw this arc in hundreds of women in his practice and described it in the 2013 episode almost exactly as above. The person who panics in weeks two through five and reverts to restriction will never reach week six.

## Common Failure Modes

Under-salting. The single most common way to crash this protocol is to keep the sodium low because mainstream advice drilled it in. Adrenaline climbs, sleep collapses, temperature drops, and the reader blames the sugar.

Cutting sugar because carbs are "bad for weight loss." The same reader who cuts sugar will burn muscle for gluconeogenesis within days, drive cortisol up, and crash the metabolic rate. Orange juice is the engine of the cut, not its saboteur. Leave it in.

Fasting to "test it" or for a perceived reset. In the 2013 weight gain episode Peat described fasting as a cortisol-driven dissolution of the thymus within hours and of the skin and muscle within days. Intermittent fasting, even the mild 16:8 version, is running the protocol backward for most hypometabolic readers.

Eating protein alone. A breakfast of eggs or cottage cheese without orange juice or fruit beforehand produces the cortisol surge Peat warned about repeatedly. Every protein meal gets carbohydrate before or with it.

Treating olive oil as a "healthy fat." Peat was neutral-to-negative on olive oil as a regular cooking fat because of its oleic acid content and its vulnerability to oxidation. It is not in the weight loss plate. Butter and coconut oil are the weight loss fats.

Taking fish oil for "recovery" or "inflammation." This is the opposite of what Peat recommended across the entire corpus. [[fish-oil]] is among the most concentrated forms of stored PUFA you can eat, and it is exactly the load you are trying to deplete. Stop it.

Training fasted. The fasted workout uses cortisol for fuel, catabolizes muscle, and drives up lipolysis of stored PUFA. Eat before you move.

Expecting linear scale loss. The scale will move non-linearly: water out, muscle in, then fat out, while waist circumference shrinks the whole time. Believe the tape measure, not the scale.

Impatience. Real PUFA depletion is a four-year horizon. The first three months are the setup. Reasoning about the results of the first three months as if they were the total result of the protocol is the reason people abandon Peat's framework for something faster that promises what it cannot deliver.

## If You Hit a Plateau

Plateaus in this protocol are usually recomposition phases. Muscle is rebuilding while fat is leaving, and the scale is a flat line for weeks. Temperature and waist are the real markers. If both are trending the right direction (warmer, smaller), the plateau is not a plateau; it is the body reorganizing under the hood and the scale is the wrong instrument to see it with.

If temperature is stalling below 97.8°F in the morning and you have been on the protocol for four weeks or more, do not add restriction. Add coconut oil (one tier up), add salt, or add a starting dose of thyroid glandular if the diet alone is not moving the rate. If temperature is rising but waist is stuck, give it another two weeks before changing anything; body composition lags the metabolic rate by a month or more. If sleep is getting worse, you have under-salted or under-sugared the day. The bedtime snack of milk with salty crackers or a small bowl of fruit is not optional in this protocol; it is the mechanism that keeps cortisol from climbing through the night and ruining the next day's temperature.

The wrong response to any plateau is to cut calories. The right response is almost always to raise the metabolic rate further by adding the next support lever.
