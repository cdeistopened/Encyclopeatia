# Carbohydrate Sources Ranked

Ray Peat ranked carbohydrates almost exactly opposite to the mainstream nutrition consensus. Where the standard dietary pyramid puts whole grains at the base and refined sugar at the apex of things to avoid, Peat flipped the order. Fruit and sucrose sat at the top of his recommendations. Whole wheat, oats, and brown rice sat at the bottom. This is not a contrarian stunt. It follows directly from how he read the physiology.

The axis Peat cared about was not blood sugar response or caloric density. It was four things at once. First, whether the carbohydrate supports [[thyroid]] function rather than suppressing it. Second, whether its digestion generates [[endotoxin]] in the gut. Third, whether the fructose content supports liver glycogen and aerobic metabolism. And fourth, whether the fiber matrix, if present, feeds bacteria or passes through inertly. Measured against those four axes, ripe fruit and pure sucrose win on every count. Whole grains lose on every count.

The misalignment with mainstream advice is sharpest around the [[glycemic-index|glycemic index]]. Peat pointed out, repeatedly and with some exasperation, that "orange juice and sucrose have a lower glycemic index than starch or whole wheat or white bread." The fear of sugar has driven two generations of health-conscious eaters toward starches that spike insulin harder than the sugar they were trying to avoid. The biochemistry is not hidden. It is in every glycemic index table. People simply refuse to look.

Peat's summary sentence, the one he returned to in interview after interview, was this: "Sugar is my current understanding of what is most protective." He did not mean sugar as a vague category of sweetness. He meant sucrose, the fifty-fifty glucose-fructose molecule, or its equivalents in ripe fruit, honey, and milk. The rest of the ranking below follows from that foundation.

See also [[fructose]], [[sucrose]], [[randle-cycle]], [[anti-stress-diet]].

## The Ranking

The tiers below are Peat's framework translated into a practical hierarchy. S-tier is what he actively recommended as the backbone of the daily diet. F-tier is what he told people to eliminate.

### S-Tier — The Foundational Carbs

| Source | Peat's Take | Why |
|---|---|---|
| [[oranges\|Orange juice]] | Daily staple. Multiple glasses across the day. | Vitamin C, potassium, magnesium, bioflavonoids with aromatase-inhibiting activity, quick-absorbing fructose and glucose, and no fiber burden. The closest thing to a perfect carbohydrate Peat identified. |
| Ripe tropical fruit | Mango, papaya, pineapple, ripe banana. Eat freely. | High sugar, high potassium, high vitamin C, digestible fiber matrix, mature enzymes that finish their own starch conversion. Tropical fruit evolved to be eaten. It is the least adversarial food on the planet. |
| [[sucrose\|White sugar]] | Pure, protective, the reference standard. | Exactly fifty percent fructose, fifty percent glucose. No contaminants, no antinutrients, no fermentable fiber. Peat used table sugar deliberately to correct low blood sugar and to spare protein from the cortisol cycle. |
| Honey | Quick energy, pre-digested sugars, good at bedtime. | Roughly the same glucose-fructose mix as sucrose with trace minerals and enzymes. Peat recommended a spoonful with salt before bed to prevent the 3am adrenaline spike that wakes people. |
| [[milk\|Milk]] (lactose) | Two quarts a day is the target. | Lactose plus the complete calcium-phosphorus-protein-fat matrix. The calcium floor alone (roughly 2000mg from two quarts) is why Peat considered any diet below that intake nutritionally incomplete. |
| Ice cream | Literal Peat recommendation, not a joke. | Sugar, milk, and saturated fat in a single bite. Peat named it repeatedly as a near-ideal food. The combination hits the sugar target, the calcium floor, and the saturated fat requirement in one spoon. |

### A-Tier — Recommended But Situational

| Source | Peat's Take | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sub-tropical citrus | Oranges, tangerines, grapefruit. Daily. | Slightly less sugar density than tropical fruit but same vitamin C and bioflavonoid profile. Grapefruit adds naringenin, which Peat noted has anti-estrogenic activity. |
| Maple syrup | Acceptable. Avoid commercial blends cut with corn syrup. | Pure sucrose plus trace minerals. Functionally interchangeable with honey for cooking and sweetening. |
| Pulp-free fruit juice | Fine, often preferable to whole fruit. | Peat warned that fruit pulp can contain [[pufa|polyunsaturated fats]] from the seeds and skins. Straining removes them. Pulp-free juice is not a compromise; it is often the cleaner choice. |
| Pomegranate juice | Recommended for the antioxidant and anti-estrogen content. | The ellagic acid and punicalagins hit some of the same targets as the citrus bioflavonoids. |
| Watermelon, cherries | Seasonal staples. | Watermelon is the most efficient fructose-plus-water delivery system. Cherries carry anti-inflammatory anthocyanins. |
| Guava | High bioflavonoids, high vitamin C. | When available, a good rotation fruit. Functionally similar to citrus in the micronutrient profile. |

### B-Tier — Acceptable But Secondary

| Source | Peat's Take | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Apples, pears, grapes | Fine. Not the first choice. | Cooler-climate fruits. Lower sugar density than tropical fruit, lower vitamin C than citrus, but free of the antinutrient problems that kill grains. Rotate in when better options are not available. |
| Berries | Moderate sugar, moderate antioxidant benefit. | Blueberries and raspberries are acceptable. The seedy matrix is the main reason Peat did not elevate them higher. Strained berry juice is better. |
| Dried fruit | Concentrated sugar. Limit if iron-loaded. | Raisins, dried figs, dried apricots. The sugar is fine. The concern is iron concentration, which Peat flagged as a cumulative stressor for adults who cannot shed iron. |
| Dates, figs | Concentrated sugar, good minerals. | Useful for athletes or anyone with genuinely high carbohydrate demands. Not a daily staple for sedentary people. |

### C-Tier — Acceptable With Preparation, Never Staples

| Source | Peat's Take | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Well-cooked peeled potato | Backup carbohydrate source. Peel it, cook it fully, serve with butter and salt. | Peat wrote in his general-diet emails: "Well cooked potatoes, with butter or cream, are a very good way to get carbohydrate." The peel carries the problematic compounds. Full cooking collapses the starch matrix so it digests high in the small intestine rather than fermenting in the colon. |
| Masa | Nixtamalized corn. Traditional preparation works. | The lime treatment neutralizes phytate and unlocks niacin. Tortillas made from real masa are acceptable. Industrial corn products are not. |
| White rice | Lesser evil of the grain category. | Polished white rice is the least bad grain because the PUFA-containing germ is gone. Parboiled rice is worse (the parboiling drives nutrients into the endosperm but also concentrates other compounds). Standard polished white rice is the version Peat would tolerate. |
| Traditional sourdough | Long fermentation reduces antinutrients. | Genuine sourdough, fermented for many hours, breaks down phytate and some of the gluten. Acceptable as an occasional vehicle for butter and cheese. Not a staple. |

### D-Tier — Not Actively Recommended

| Source | Peat's Take | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pasta | Refined wheat in a different shape. | No nutritional upside. Spikes blood sugar harder than table sugar. Feeds the same gut bacteria that convert wheat starch to [[endotoxin]]. If you are going to eat refined carbohydrate, eat sucrose. |
| Plain bread | Even sourdough is marginal. | Commercial bread is a delivery system for refined wheat and seed oils. Pass. |
| Green bananas | Ripe is fine. Green is starchy and problematic. | Unripe banana is mostly resistant starch, which ferments in the colon. Ripe banana, with the brown spots, has converted most of that starch to sucrose and fructose and becomes S-tier. Timing matters. |
| Quinoa | Pseudo-grain, goitrogenic saponins. | The saponin coating on quinoa interferes with [[thyroid]] function and irritates the gut. Rinsing helps but does not eliminate the issue. Peat saw no reason to include it. |

### F-Tier — Peat Said To Avoid

| Source | Peat's Take | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Whole wheat and whole grains | Avoid. | Higher [[pufa\|PUFA]] content from the intact germ, higher phytate, higher lectin load, higher glycemic response than sucrose, and they feed the gram-negative bacteria in the gut that produce [[endotoxin]]. The health halo around whole grains is one of the most thoroughly wrong pieces of conventional nutrition advice. |
| Oats | Avoid. Goitrogens, phytates, higher PUFA than rice. | Oats combine several of the worst properties of grains in one food. They interfere with thyroid, bind minerals, and carry enough polyunsaturated fat to go rancid on the shelf. The breakfast of champions is a metabolic liability. |
| Raw or undercooked starches | Ferment into endotoxin. | Undercooked potato, raw oats in overnight soaks, and the whole "resistant starch" supplement category. The premise (feeding the gut bacteria is good) is exactly backwards in Peat's framework. He wanted the gut starved of fermentable substrate, not fed. |
| Brown rice | Worse than white. | The bran is the problem. It contains PUFA, phytic acid, and a fiber matrix that resists complete digestion and ferments in the colon. White rice is a refinement that removes the worst part of the grain. |
| Legumes as a carb source | Avoid. | Beans, lentils, and chickpeas carry estrogenic isoflavones, goitrogenic compounds, lectins, phytates, and non-trivial PUFA. Peat treated them as one of the clearest "no" categories in the plant kingdom. |
| High fructose corn syrup | Worth a precise note. | The fructose itself is fine. Peat did not lump HFCS into the same bucket as seed oils. The real problems with HFCS are the contexts it shows up in (industrial soda paired with vegetable oil snacks) and the industrial processing residues, not the fructose molecule. If you pulled HFCS out of a Coke and put it into a glass of milk, it would be a reasonable calorie source. In its native environment it is not. |

## The Glycemic Index Myth

This deserves its own section because it is the most common place where well-intentioned eaters go wrong.

Conventional advice: avoid sugar because it spikes blood sugar. Eat whole wheat bread because it has a lower glycemic response. This is simply false. The glycemic index tables show it plainly. Sucrose is around 65. Whole wheat bread is around 74. White bread is around 75. Boiled white potato is around 82. Cornflakes are around 92.

Orange juice is around 50. Lower than almost every starch.

Peat's point was that the dietary establishment had been telling people to avoid a moderate-glycemic food (sucrose) in favor of higher-glycemic foods (starches and grain breads) while claiming the swap was for blood sugar control. The claim is self-contradicting on its own published data. The reason most people believe it anyway is that the word "sugar" triggers a stronger moral reaction than the word "whole wheat," regardless of what the numbers say.

The deeper reason sucrose behaves well has to do with the fructose half of the molecule. Fructose does not require insulin to be taken up by the liver. It refills liver glycogen directly, which is exactly what the body needs between meals to avoid the cortisol-adrenaline cascade that starchy meals trigger three hours later. Pure glucose from a starch meal, on the other hand, is fully insulin-dependent, gets stored quickly, and leaves the liver empty again within a few hours. The blood sugar crash that follows is what drives people to eat more starch on the same schedule. Sucrose breaks that loop. Starch perpetuates it.

This is the single most important thing to internalize from the Peat framework on carbohydrates. If you remember nothing else: **the glycemic index of sucrose is lower than the glycemic index of whole wheat, and the fructose half of sucrose refills liver glycogen in a way that starch cannot.**

## The Raw Carrot Exception

Not a carbohydrate source in any meaningful sense (the digestible sugar content is trivial), but the daily raw carrot deserves a callout in any document about Peat's food framework. He recommended a medium raw carrot, grated or sliced lengthwise, eaten with a little coconut oil and vinegar, every day.

The point is not the sugar. It is the fiber. Carrot fiber is one of the few plant fibers that is not fermentable by gut bacteria. It is stiff enough to physically sweep the small intestine, and it binds bacterial endotoxin and excess estrogen for excretion. Most "fiber is good for you" advice is about fermentable fiber, which is exactly what Peat did not want in the gut. The carrot is the exception. It is a mechanical scrub brush, not a bacterial food source.

A daily raw carrot is an anti-[[endotoxin]], anti-[[xenoestrogens|xenoestrogen]] tool. It belongs next to the orange juice and the milk in the daily [[anti-stress-diet]] template, not in the "vegetables" category of a generic meal plan.

## Carb Targets

Peat's numbers on total carbohydrate intake are higher than almost any other framework, including most "high-carb" diets. The anti-stress diet template lands somewhere in the range of 180 to 350 grams per day for typical adults, with the upper end for anyone with high metabolic demand.

For athletes and hard-working people, the target moves higher still. Peat wrote: "Carbohydrate can run to a pound a day or more for high metabolic rates." A pound is roughly 450 grams. The direct quote he gave in interviews, when asked about the 100-gram low-carb diets that were popular during his later years, was blunt. "I've experimented on people with a 100-gram carbohydrate diet, and I don't think that was even close to a good health level. I think it's probably around that range of 200 or 300 that's minimum."

Two hundred to three hundred grams was the minimum. Not the target. The minimum. Three glasses of orange juice, two quarts of milk, a piece of fruit with each meal, and a spoon of sugar in the coffee will get a sedentary adult to that floor without effort. Active people need more on top of that.

The failure mode Peat saw most often was protein without carbohydrate. A chicken breast alone triggers cortisol. A chicken breast after a glass of orange juice is a normal meal. The rule on the [[anti-stress-diet]] is that protein always gets a carbohydrate preload, and the carbohydrate comes from the S-tier of this ranking first. Starch substitutions are a fallback, not a foundation.
