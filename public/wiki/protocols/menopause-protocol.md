# Menopause Protocol

The mechanism is in [[menopause]] — progesterone collapse in continuing estrogen production, hypothalamic excitotoxic exhaustion, the four-year tissue-estrogen window, the WHI confirmation. This page is the action layer. What to do in perimenopause that you do not do in early postmenopause. What you stop doing at five years out. The dose ranges Peat actually quoted in newsletters and emails, the sequencing rules, and the tactical pieces — sugar at bedtime, vitamin K2 with magnesium, methylene blue at the cognition end — that mainstream HRT misses because it is fighting a different war.

What this protocol is not: it is not "natural HRT." It is not bioidentical-estradiol therapy. Peat's life's work pointed in the opposite direction of estrogen replacement — even bioidentical, even started in the early window, even balanced against natural progesterone. Progesterone is the missing hormone. Thyroid is the missing metabolic floor. PUFA is the lipid background the rest of the field has to clear. Estrogen is the load.

The protocol is phase-aware because the substrate changes across the decade. A 47-year-old with shortening luteal phases needs cyclical progesterone overlaid on a still-functioning ovary. A 53-year-old two years past the last menstrual period needs continuous progesterone, full T3 restoration, and aggressive PUFA-clearance. A 65-year-old eight years out needs less progesterone, more pregnenolone, and the bone-density and cognition pieces moved to the front.

## Phase 1: Perimenopause (Cycle-Aware, Roughly Ages 40–52)

Perimenopause begins when the luteal phase shortens, not when periods stop. The clinically useful definition is the appearance of anovulatory cycles, mid-cycle spotting, lengthening or shortening intercycle intervals, and the first symptoms of unopposed estrogen — heavier or clottier menses, breast tenderness lasting more than three days, PMS spreading earlier into the cycle, water-weight swings, migraines tracking the cycle. The mistake is to wait for the last menstrual period. The intervention window is years earlier.

**Cyclical progesterone, days 14 through 28.** This is the canonical Peat regimen. From [[progesterone-summaries-progesterone-supplementation-dosage-of-progesterone|*Progesterone Summaries*]]: "The cyclic use of progesterone, two weeks on, two weeks off, will often keep the normal menstrual cycle going. Three to four drops, providing ten or twelve milligrams of progesterone, is typical for a day, but some women prefer to repeat that amount." The Progest-E formulation delivers approximately 3 mg per drop in a vitamin E oil; three drops on the gums or inner lip delivers 9–12 mg, absorbed through oral mucosa, bypassing the liver. The dose can be repeated once or twice across the day if symptoms persist. Start at the lower end. Increase only if symptoms continue. Progesterone is reasonably dose-flexible — Peat noted physiological production averages 10–20 mg daily during the luteal phase and that "if estrogen is extremely high, even large supplements of progesterone will have no clear effect" — but the right first dose is the one that controls the immediate symptom and gets dropped on the days you do not need it.

**T3 trial at the first sign of low temperature.** Perimenopausal women whose morning oral temperature reads below 97.8°F or whose resting pulse sits below 75 bpm should not wait for menopause to address thyroid. Restoring temperature and pulse is the substrate on which progesterone supplementation works, because PUFA-loaded tissues hold more estrogen and respond less to a given dose of progesterone. The full thyroid protocol is at [[hypothyroid-recovery]]; the perimenopausal-specific note is that a low-dose T3 trial (5 mcg twice daily with food, building toward three doses) often dramatically shortens the response time of the progesterone intervention. From the [[progesterone-summaries-progesterone-supplementation-dosage-of-progesterone|*Progesterone Summaries*]] email: "If a person is hypothyroid, the tissues are likely to contain more estrogen, making them less sensitive to progesterone."

**Anti-PUFA, hard.** The PUFA load that drives the menopausal field is built across decades; the depletion clock is years long. See [[pufa-depletion]] for the systemic version. The perimenopausal-specific implication is that every meal still containing seed oil, supplemental fish oil, or "heart-healthy" mayonnaise extends the protocol's failure window. Replace cooking fats with butter, coconut oil, beef tallow, or ghee. The aspirin-as-aromatase-inhibitor piece (81–325 mg daily with vitamin K1 to offset prostaglandin and gut bleeding risk) compensates for tissue-PUFA prostaglandin signaling while the lipid pool turns over.

**Salt and sugar to spec.** Frequent eating with sucrose-rich fruits, milk, gelatin, honey, white rice or potatoes with butter, and unrestricted salt-to-taste. Perimenopausal women are especially vulnerable to the cortisol-adrenaline-CRH cascade that drives both vasomotor symptoms and bone loss; falling blood sugar provokes it more readily than at any other point in adult life. A carbohydrate snack at 3 p.m. and a second one before bed often does more for sleep and mood than any supplement.

**Avoid in this phase.** Bioidentical estradiol or compounded estrogen creams, even at "physiological" doses. SSRIs prescribed for early PMS-mood symptoms; serotonin is the flush precursor (see [[serotonin]]). Iron supplements unless a documented deficiency exists — perimenopausal women already retain too much iron because of irregular menstrual clearance. Soy, flax, and beer for the estrogen-mimic reasons covered in [[estrogen-detox]].

## Phase 2: Early Postmenopause (LMP to Roughly 5 Years Out, Ages 52–57)

The last menstrual period changes the regimen because cyclical bleeding no longer marks the calendar. The four-year window described in [[menopause]] — when ovarian, adrenal, and adipose estrogen production continues at near-premenopausal levels without monthly progesterone clearance — is the period of maximum tissue-estrogen exposure across a woman's lifetime. The protocol intensifies.

**Progesterone, continuous or three-on / one-off.** From [[progesterone-summaries-progesterone-supplementation-dosage-of-progesterone|*Progesterone Summaries*]]: "Some women continue the cyclic use of progesterone after menopause, because the pituitary gland and brain may continue to cycle long after menstruation has stopped... But some women prefer to use progesterone without interruption after the menopause, for its protective antistress effects. Slender people usually find that two or three drops are enough, but this amount may be repeated once or twice as needed to relieve symptoms." Two cadences work clinically: continuous low-dose daily (2–3 drops, ~6–9 mg, repeated as needed) or a 3-weeks-on / 1-week-off cycling pattern to maintain pituitary cyclicity. Heavier women, women with persistent hot flashes, or women with active perimenopausal symptoms carry over often need higher doses — Peat himself referenced 30–100 mg for moderate symptoms and noted the Prior and Hitchcock 2012 trial used 300 mg oral progesterone for effective hot-flash suppression with no rebound. Always titrate from the bottom up.

**Form matters.** From [[the-progesterone-deceptions|*The Progesterone Deceptions*]]: "The idea of dissolving progesterone, a fat soluble steroid hormone, in vitamin E which is then incorporated into chylomicrons absorbed via the lymphatics, and thus avoids the liver on the so called first pass is so simple it is amazing that the pharmaceutical companies have not jumped on it." Progest-E is the canonical vehicle. Oral micronized progesterone (Prometrium) is second-best — sedating from first-pass conversion to allopregnanolone, useful for insomnia-dominant cases. Transdermal cream lacks the vitamin-E carrier and absorbs less reliably; if used, rotate sites to prevent local fat-depot saturation.

**T3 maintenance, not just trial.** Most early-postmenopausal women on the full protocol settle into 5–15 mcg of T3 (Cytomel or compounded) two to three times daily, often paired with 1–2 grains of desiccated thyroid (NDT) at breakfast. The target is morning temperature 98.0–98.2°F, afternoon temperature 98.6°F, resting pulse 75–85 bpm. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for the full diagnostic and dosing apparatus and the warning that T4-alone therapy frequently makes hypothyroid postmenopausal women worse because estrogen-impaired livers cannot efficiently convert T4 to T3.

**Vitamin K2, vitamin D, magnesium — the bone trio.** Bone loss accelerates immediately when progesterone fails because cortisol then dominates the diurnal hormonal field. The dedicated bone protocol is later in this page; in the early postmenopausal years the daily floor is vitamin K2 (MK-4, 5–15 mg, or MK-7, 100–200 mcg), vitamin D3 (2,000–5,000 IU daily, dosed to keep serum 25(OH)D in the 50–70 ng/mL range), and magnesium glycinate or chloride (300–600 mg elemental nightly). Calcium comes from food — two quarts of milk, cheese, gelatin broth — not from supplements, which raise serum calcium acutely and provoke arterial calcification without proportional bone benefit.

**Pregnenolone, low dose.** Pregnenolone (50–200 mg daily, oral, with breakfast and a small amount of fat) acts as a precursor pool for adrenal progesterone and supports cortisol buffering. Not a replacement for exogenous progesterone — conversion is too slow — but it improves the substrate.

**Daily raw carrot, aspirin, coffee.** The estrogen-detox baseline (see [[estrogen-detox]]) continues without modification. Raw carrot is the gut-side clearance pathway for conjugated estrogen. Aspirin (81–325 mg with food and vitamin K1) inhibits aromatase, suppresses prostaglandin E, and chelates iron — from the August 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode referenced in [[menopause]]: "Aspirin and caffeine are also useful. Aspirin blocks aromatase, and caffeine stimulates progesterone production." Coffee (two to four cups daily with cream and sugar, never black) supports liver glucuronidation.

## Phase 3: Five-Plus Years Postmenopause (Roughly Ages 58 and Up)

By five years out, the tissue-estrogen window has largely closed if the protocol has been running. Endogenous production from adipose, adrenal, and brain aromatase continues but the per-day load is lower and the cumulative damage to the hypothalamic LH/FSH-regulating nerves has stabilized. The regimen reduces in the progesterone direction and shifts toward the late-aging adjuncts.

**Progesterone reduce — but do not stop.** From the email correspondence Peat sent across the late 1990s and 2000s, the general guidance was that older postmenopausal women on a strong dietary and thyroid baseline can often drop to 1–3 mg of progesterone (1 drop of Progest-E) daily or alternate-daily for maintenance. The brain and pituitary continue to cycle independent of ovarian function and continue to benefit from periodic progesterone exposure. The exception is severe symptom flare during a stressor (illness, surgery, bereavement, sleep disruption) — short-term return to early-postmenopausal dosing handles the transient surge of estrogen and cortisol that the stressor produces.

**Pregnenolone takes the front role.** Pregnenolone (200–400 mg daily, divided across two doses) becomes more central in the 5+ year window because the steroid pool that supplies progesterone, DHEA, and the protective neurosteroids has thinned. Pregnenolone in this dose range improves memory, mood stability, and stress tolerance — particularly relevant given the cognition-protection priority that increases with each year past 60.

**DHEA cautiously.** DHEA (5–25 mg daily, oral, started low) is the adrenal androgen Peat treated with judgment. The peripheral aromatase conversion to estrogen in adipose tissue makes it a poor choice for women with significant body fat or persistent estrogen-dominance markers. In thinner women with good thyroid function it improves bone density, libido, and mood — but only in this physiological dose range. The hundreds-of-milligrams doses that circulated in 2000s anti-aging clinics produce the same syndrome the protocol is meant to fix.

**Bone protection becomes the front-line priority.** The cortisol-PTH-prolactin cascade that drives osteoporosis is most aggressive in the 5+ year window because cumulative tissue damage to bone-formation machinery compounds with each year. Vitamin K2, magnesium, vitamin D, weight-bearing movement, and adequate protein (100+ g/day) move from "supplements" to "non-negotiable daily program."

**Watch for the cognition signal.** Peri- and early-postmenopausal brain fog often resolves on the progesterone-thyroid baseline. Persistent or worsening cognition in the 5+ year window is the dementia-precursor signal, and the intervention apparatus is at [[dementia-protocol]] (when written) or [[dementia]] in the interim. Low-dose methylene blue (0.5–4 mg daily, sublingual, pharmaceutical grade only), T3 maintained at the upper end of the physiological range, and PUFA depletion are the load-bearing interventions.

## Hot Flash Tactics

The mechanism (hypoglycemia → CRH → cortisol/adrenaline → estrogen-sensitized vasodilation) is in [[menopause]]. The tactical pieces:

**Sugar at bedtime.** A glass of orange juice with a teaspoon of honey, a bowl of ice cream, a small piece of fruit with cheese, or a glass of milk with sugar before sleep. From the [[2012-08-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hot-flashes-night-sweats-the-relationship-to-stress-aging-pm|August 2012 hot-flashes episode]] (cited in [[menopause]]): "In the last few years, studies have shown that menopausal hot flashes can be prevented with sugar. A big dose of carbohydrate at bedtime works better than estrogen." Night sweats are the 3 a.m. blood-sugar drop made vasomotor. Feed the liver before sleep and the cascade attenuates.

**Salt liberally.** A pinch of salt with the bedtime sugar dose. Salt restriction raises aldosterone and adrenaline, both of which sensitize the vasomotor reflex. Salt-to-taste at every meal, sodium intake unrestricted unless a specific cardiac contraindication applies.

**Progesterone topical-sublingual at flash onset.** Three to four drops of Progest-E on the gums or inner lip at the first vasomotor sign aborts roughly two-thirds of flashes within 5–10 minutes. The vitamin E carrier allows trans-mucosal absorption fast enough to reach blood levels before the vasodilation cascade completes. Useful especially at night and during the daytime hot-flush window (typically 2–4 p.m., coinciding with the cortisol-rebound from morning).

**Environmental.** A cool bedroom (62–66°F), light cotton or wool sleepwear (not synthetic which traps), a small fan at the bedside, and a glass of cold water on the nightstand. Caffeine before bed is contraindicated for the obvious reasons; caffeine across the day is fine and supportive.

**What does not work and why.** SSRIs reduce flash frequency 30–60% in trials and are mechanistically the precursor of the symptom (serotonin is the flush amine). Estradiol relieves flashes by lowering metabolism enough that the autonomic system cannot mount the flush — symptomatic suppression at the cost of the long-term damage the [[menopause]] page details. Black cohosh has weak phytoestrogenic activity and adds to the very load the protocol is meant to clear.

## Bone Density Protocol

The bone-loss reframe in [[menopause]] — cortisol-PTH-prolactin-thyroid, not estrogen deficiency — translates into a protocol that looks nothing like the bisphosphonate-and-calcium-supplement standard. The full mechanism page is [[osteoporosis]].

**Vitamin K2 (MK-4 or MK-7).** The matrix-gla protein and osteocalcin carboxylation cofactor that directs calcium into bone rather than soft tissue. MK-4 at 5–15 mg daily, taken with fat for absorption, is the form Peat referenced most directly. MK-7 (100–200 mcg daily) has a longer half-life and may be preferable for compliance.

**Magnesium glycinate, malate, or chloride.** 300–600 mg elemental magnesium nightly, before bed, taken with the bedtime carbohydrate snack. Magnesium deficiency drives PTH upward; PTH is the osteoclast-activating signal that causes most postmenopausal bone resorption. From the [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|*Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis* newsletter]]: "I think rational therapy for osteoporosis would include thyroid, vitamin A, bright light, and either pregnenolone or a mixture of progesterone and DHEA, with a diet rich in protein and all the salts: calcium, magnesium, sodium, and potassium. A bias toward magnesium, rather than calcium, would protect against many conditions that occur in aging and stress."

**Weight-bearing movement.** Walking 30–60 minutes daily, modest resistance training two to three times weekly, gardening, carrying groceries, climbing stairs. The mechanism is mechanotransduction at the osteocyte; bone formation requires mechanical load. Endurance cardio (long runs, hours of cycling) is counterproductive — chronic cortisol elevation drives the catabolic side of the bone equation harder than the mechanical loading helps it.

**Adequate calcium from food, not supplements.** Two quarts of milk daily provides ~2,000 mg of calcium plus the protein and lactose context. Cheese and gelatin broth supplement. Calcium-carbonate or calcium-citrate tablets raise serum calcium acutely without bone benefit and contribute to arterial calcification; food-source calcium does not.

**Sugar and salt, again.** Same rationale as the hot-flash tactics. Cortisol is the osteoclast-activating hormone of the diurnal pattern; cortisol rises with falling blood sugar and with salt restriction.

**Vitamin D3 (2,000–5,000 IU/day) plus sunlight when available.** Target serum 25(OH)D 50–70 ng/mL. Below 30 is associated with accelerated bone loss; above 90 carries hypercalcemia risk in this combined-supplement context.

**Avoid bisphosphonates and denosumab.** Both class drugs suppress osteoclast activity, which produces the appearance of preserved bone density on DXA without restoring bone quality. The bone preserved is older bone, no longer remodeled; fracture rates in long-term users (>5 years) include atypical femur fractures and osteonecrosis of the jaw. The mechanism for the protocol is restoration of normal bone turnover under progesterone-thyroid-K2 conditions, not suppression of the cleanup phase.

## Cognition Protection

The cognition piece links to [[dementia-protocol]] (planned) and to the mechanism page [[dementia]]. The same estrogen-as-excitotoxin pathway that drives postmenopausal brain fog continues, if unaddressed, into Alzheimer-pattern decline. Women develop Alzheimer's at roughly twice the rate of men; cumulative-estrogen exposure is Peat's load-bearing explanation.

**T3 maintained at the upper physiological range.** Cellular oxidative metabolism is the substrate the brain needs against amyloid accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and glutamate excitotoxicity. Underdosed thyroid produces the cognition signal first.

**Anti-PUFA continues across the late decades.** The brain is roughly 60% fat by dry weight; the peroxidation-susceptible fraction is the PUFA. Tissue turnover takes years and the protocol becomes lifelong dietary practice.

**Low-dose methylene blue.** Pharmaceutical-grade methylene blue at 0.5–4 mg daily (sublingual, divided) supports mitochondrial electron transport. Higher doses (>10 mg) interact with serotonergic medications.

**Progesterone for sleep architecture.** Oral micronized progesterone at bedtime (100–200 mg) supports slow-wave sleep, during which glymphatic clearance of amyloid and other cerebral metabolic waste accelerates.

**Avoid estradiol-based HRT after age 60.** The WHI Memory Study showed doubled dementia risk in women initiating estrogen-plus-progestin after age 65. The Newhouse 2010 trial cited in [[hot-flashes-energy-and-aging|*Hot flashes, energy, and aging*]] found "E2-treated compared with placebo-treated participants exhibited significant worsening of cognitive performance on tasks measuring attentional performance and psychomotor speed."

## Sleep, Mood, Libido

**Sleep.** Bedtime carbohydrate + salt + magnesium is the floor. Oral micronized progesterone (Prometrium 100–200 mg) at bedtime is the most reliable single intervention. Avoid melatonin — Peat treated it as a darkness-and-stress signal whose chronic supplementation suppresses thyroid.

**Mood.** Progesterone is anxiolytic via allopregnanolone-GABA. T3 supports dopamine and serotonin-clearance. Avoid SSRIs, 5-HTP, and tryptophan loading — serotonin is upregulated in the metabolic field this protocol is meant to fix. Adequate sugar and frequent eating prevent the cortisol-adrenaline crashes that look like depression but are hypoglycemic.

**Libido.** Returns when the broader metabolic recovery is in place — adequate thyroid, low estrogen, restored progesterone, low cortisol, low prolactin. Coffee and aspirin lower prolactin. Pregnenolone and modest DHEA improve sexual function in the late window. Vaginal atrophy responds to topical Progest-E (a drop or two applied directly two to three times weekly); severe atrophy sometimes warrants compounded estriol cream as a middle-ground option without the systemic risks of estradiol.

## Counter-arguments

**The early-window argument for HRT.** Post-WHI subgroup reanalyses, plus KEEPS and ELITE, argue that estradiol started within 5 years of the last menstrual period may be net-protective for cardiovascular and cognitive outcomes. NAMS and the Endocrine Society endorse short-term HRT for symptomatic women in early menopause as reasonable medicine. The Peat framework predicts cumulative-exposure damage independent of timing and treats the early-window claim as not yet falsified by long enough trials. Some women, especially those with severe vasomotor symptoms unresponsive to the progesterone-thyroid baseline within 6–12 weeks, will pragmatically choose short-term low-dose transdermal estradiol as a bridge. The protocol is not a religion; the framework would treat that choice as "metabolic suppression of symptoms at probable long-term cost" but acknowledge the short-term clinical reality.

**Bisphosphonate fracture-prevention data.** Alendronate and zoledronic acid show roughly 50% vertebral-fracture reduction and 25–40% hip-fracture reduction in randomized trials. The framework predicts these benefits come from suppressing the osteoclast cleanup phase, producing the appearance of preserved density without restoring quality; long-term (>5 year) fracture data and atypical-femur-fracture incidence support the concern. But the short-term fracture-prevention signal is real and matters for women already deep into osteoporosis at first diagnosis.

**SSRIs do reduce hot flash frequency.** Paroxetine 7.5 mg and venlafaxine 75 mg both reduce flash frequency 30–60% in trials. The framework predicts they work via the same metabolic-suppression mechanism as estrogen — lowering serotonin clearance lowers metabolic responsiveness — but the clinical effect is there. Women who cannot tolerate the time the metabolic protocol takes (typically 4–12 weeks for full flash resolution) sometimes use them as a bridge.

**Progesterone dose-response is not well-characterized.** Peat's recommended range spans 30-fold (10 to 300 mg). The natural-progesterone-in-tocopherol delivery system has not been studied with formal pharmacokinetic precision. Some women report breast tenderness, sedation, or breakthrough bleeding at doses that produce no effect in others. The protocol relies heavily on titration based on symptoms rather than serum levels.

**The mainstream cardiology consensus on HRT.** Current American Heart Association guidance treats HRT as cardiovascular-neutral or net-negative for primary prevention. The framework agrees with the conclusion (estrogen is not protective) but disagrees with the mechanism (cardiology attributes the WHI-era harm to clot/stroke risk; the framework adds long-term carcinogenic, cognitive, and metabolic effects). Practitioners working in conventional cardiology will mostly converge on "don't use HRT for cardiovascular protection" without crossing into the Peat framework's full reorientation.

**Bioidentical vs Premarin.** The post-WHI rebranding of compounded estradiol-plus-natural-progesterone as "bioidentical" argues the WHI harm was specific to conjugated equine estrogens and medroxyprogesterone acetate. The framework agrees natural progesterone is substantially safer than medroxyprogesterone — Peat made this distinction repeatedly — but treats compounded estradiol as the same drug under a different label. The WHI's breast-cancer signal came predominantly from the estrogen-plus-medroxyprogesterone arm; the estrogen-alone arm in hysterectomized women showed a paradoxical reduction over 7+ years. The framework reads the medroxyprogesterone signal directly but does not fully accommodate the estrogen-alone paradox.

## Data gaps

**Optimal progesterone dosing across phases.** The 30-fold dose range is empirical, not pharmacokinetically modeled. Whether 10 mg daily plus titration on symptom flares matches 100 mg continuous in long-term bone, cognition, and cardiovascular outcomes has never been formally tested.

**Surgical menopause protocol.** Bilateral oophorectomy produces immediate complete progesterone collapse without the four-year transition. Peat treated this as a more severe variant requiring more aggressive progesterone and thyroid, but the specific dosing differential is not laid out in the corpus.

**Comparative bioavailability of progesterone formulations.** Progest-E (vitamin E oral), Prometrium (oral micronized in oil capsules), transdermal cream, vaginal suppositories, and compounded sublingual all have different absorption profiles. The serum-level data are thin and the symptom-based clinical equivalences are estimated.

**Thyroid-progesterone responsiveness threshold.** What TSH, free T3, or temperature level predicts what progesterone response is not in the corpus. Clinically, women below 97.6°F morning temperature tend to need higher progesterone doses to achieve the same symptom control, but the quantitative relationship is anecdotal.

**Long-term outcome data for the full protocol.** No cohort on the complete menopausal program (progesterone + T3 + PUFA depletion + bone trio + late-stage pregnenolone) has been followed across 10–20 years for cardiovascular, oncologic, cognitive, or fracture endpoints against either no intervention or standard HRT.

**Methylene blue dosing for cognition protection.** The 0.5–4 mg range is at the low end of the published literature, which mostly studies higher doses for acute applications. The chronic-low-dose preventive regimen is not formally trialed.

**Environmental estrogen contribution.** BPA, phthalates, atrazine, parabens, and other xenoestrogens add to the lifetime burden. Quantitative contribution to the menopausal field has not been measured against the dietary and pharmacologic estrogen load.

**Vaginal atrophy management.** The protocol addresses the systemic field but does not have a fully developed local-tissue regimen for severe atrophy. Topical progesterone helps; compounded estriol works but reintroduces some systemic estrogen load; hyaluronic acid suppositories are an inert option some women combine with the protocol.

**Genetic variation in aromatase activity.** CYP19A1 polymorphisms produce 3–5× variation in aromatase activity between individuals. Women in the high-aromatase tail need more aggressive estrogen-suppression interventions; the protocol does not currently screen for or stratify on this.

**The role of weight loss during the protocol.** Adipose tissue is the major site of postmenopausal aromatization. Weight loss reduces estrogen production but also acutely releases stored PUFA from fat cells, which adds to the lipid peroxidation load. The optimal pacing of weight loss during the menopausal transition is underdeveloped in the corpus.
