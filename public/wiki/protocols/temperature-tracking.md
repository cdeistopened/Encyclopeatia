# Temperature and Pulse Tracking

Temperature and pulse are the real thyroid test. Every blood panel the modern endocrinologist orders measures something other than what you actually want to know, which is whether your cells are producing enough heat and moving enough blood to sustain an energetic, stress-free life. The thermometer and the second hand on a watch measure exactly that. [[broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]] proved this in the 1940s through the 1970s with thousands of patients whose recovery from [[hypothyroidism]] could be predicted entirely from morning body temperature, and Ray Peat spent forty years refining the method by adding pulse rate, post-meal readings, and attention to the daily rhythm of metabolic output.

The reason this matters is that the blood tests that replaced Barnes's method were engineered, not discovered. Peat documented how the introduction of the Protein Bound Iodine test in the late 1940s collapsed the diagnosed rate of hypothyroidism from roughly 40 percent of the population to about 5 percent overnight, not because anyone had proven the old diagnostic wrong but because a new test produced a more convenient number. That convenient number survived even after the PBI test itself was abandoned, and it migrated into the [[tsh|TSH]] reference ranges still in use today. See [[tsh]] for the fuller history of how a calibration error became a clinical standard.

What temperature and pulse measure, by contrast, is the cell's actual use of [[thyroid]] hormone. A thermometer under the tongue on waking is not a proxy for metabolism; it is metabolism, expressed as heat. A pulse at the wrist is not a proxy for cardiac output; it is cardiac output, expressed as beats. Peat's position, repeated in every interview where the subject came up, was that any blood test can disagree with the tissues and the tissues are always right.

This article gives you the targets, the measurement protocol, the interpretation matrix, the confounders, and the decision rules for when to act. Track for two weeks before drawing conclusions. Everything depends on the pattern, not any single reading.

## The Target Numbers

These are the numbers Peat returned to across decades of interviews and newsletters. They are drawn from the research brief assembled for [[core-diet-protocol.research]] (Section 14) and from the [[hypothyroidism]] article. Memorize them. Vagueness in a measurement protocol is useless.

**Morning oral temperature on waking**
- Typical low-hypothyroid range: 97.0 to 97.8 degrees Fahrenheit
- Borderline: 97.8 to 98.2 degrees Fahrenheit
- Target: rising toward 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit over the course of treatment

**Post-breakfast temperature, 30 to 60 minutes after eating**
- Should rise above the morning baseline
- Target: 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit or slightly above

**Afternoon temperature, 3 to 4 PM**
- This is the peak of the daily thyroid rhythm
- Target: 98.6 to 99.0 degrees Fahrenheit
- Below 98.4 in the afternoon is a strong hypothyroid signal even if morning readings look acceptable

**Resting pulse rate, seated and relaxed**
- Peat target: 75 to 85 beats per minute
- Below 75 is NOT fitness. It is metabolic suppression.
- Peat cited studies of elderly pacemaker patients whose cognitive function, memory, and mood improved measurably when their paced rate was raised from around 70 to around 85. The brain wants more blood than the modern cardiology textbook thinks it does.
- Athletic bradycardia at 55 to 60 is hibernation physiology, not health. Endurance athletes often have the thyroid profile of a cold-adapted rodent.

**Post-meal pulse rate**
- Should rise 5 to 10 beats per minute after a balanced meal containing carbohydrate
- A rising pulse after food is a sign that the liver converted some glucose into [[T3]] and the heart responded
- A falling pulse after food is a stress signal (see the interpretation matrix)

**Body surface temperature**
- Hands and feet should feel warm to the touch
- Cold extremities are the signature of high [[adrenaline]] compensating for low thyroid
- The nose and fingertips are the most sensitive indicators because they are the first places the body cuts circulation under stress

**Other physical signs Peat endorsed as adjuncts**
- High [[cholesterol]] above roughly 200 without a high-PUFA diet suggests hypothyroidism
- Carotene accumulation in the skin (yellowish palms) means the liver is not converting carotene to vitamin A
- Puffy skin, waterlogged face, slow tendon reflexes, and thinning outer eyebrows all belong to the Barnes physical exam

## The Measurement Protocol

A digital oral thermometer accurate to 0.1 degrees Fahrenheit costs under twenty dollars. Buy one. The cheap ones with 0.2 degree accuracy are not adequate for tracking the small changes that matter here.

1. **Morning baseline.** On waking, before drinking water, eating, speaking more than necessary, or getting out of bed, place the thermometer under the tongue and hold it until it beeps. Record the number. Then count your pulse at the radial artery (thumb side of the wrist) for a full 60 seconds, seated or still reclining. Record both.
2. **Post-breakfast reading.** Eat a full breakfast that includes carbohydrate, protein, and some fat (orange juice, milk, eggs, and a piece of fruit works). Wait 30 to 60 minutes. Take temperature and pulse again in the same position each day.
3. **Afternoon reading.** Between 3 and 4 PM, take a third temperature and pulse. This is the daily peak and the most reliable single reading if you only take one.
4. **Evening optional reading.** Some people add a reading after dinner to watch for late-day crashes, particularly if they have insomnia or night sweats.
5. **Recording.** Use a spreadsheet or a paper notebook. Columns: date, AM temp, AM pulse, post-breakfast temp, post-breakfast pulse, PM temp, PM pulse, notes on sleep quality, mood, energy, bowel movements, menstrual phase (for women), ambient temperature, and anything unusual (alcohol the night before, travel, illness, exercise).
6. **Duration.** Track for a minimum of 14 days before drawing any conclusions. Two weeks smooths out the random daily variation and exposes the pattern. One or two bad readings mean almost nothing. Fourteen days of flat low readings mean you are hypothyroid.

## Reading the Pattern

Peat taught that the relationship between morning and post-breakfast readings is the most diagnostic single piece of information you can get from the method. The matrix below is the core of the interpretation.

**High morning temperature that falls after breakfast**
This is high adrenaline masking hypothyroidism. Your sleeping stress hormones were keeping the temperature up, and the food lowered those stress hormones while revealing your underlying low metabolic rate. The food did not cause the problem; it exposed the problem. The response is not to skip breakfast. The response is to add more carbohydrate, salt, and calcium to the breakfast so that the liver has the raw materials to convert [[T4]] to T3 and keep the temperature up without cortisol. Orange juice, milk, a ripe banana, a couple of eggs with salt, and coffee is a standard Peat breakfast for exactly this reason.

**Low morning temperature that falls further after breakfast**
This is Peat's worst-case combination, which he called "stress plus hypothyroidism." The baseline is already low, stress hormones have been exhausted overnight, and the food is deepening the drop rather than providing a lift. This is a person who needs the full [[anti-stress-diet]] immediately: ripe fruit, orange juice, milk, cheese, gelatin, coconut oil, and salt, eaten in small frequent portions throughout the day. If the pattern does not shift in two weeks on a proper diet, thyroid supplementation (glandular or T3, never T4 alone) is probably necessary.

**Low morning temperature that rises after breakfast**
This is the most hopeful pattern. The baseline is low, but the post-meal rise proves that the thyroid system can still respond when given fuel. The diet is working. Continue. Track whether the morning baseline itself rises over the coming weeks as the cumulative metabolic debt is repaid.

**Normal or high morning temperature that rises further after breakfast**
This is good metabolic function. No action needed. Enjoy breakfast.

**Pulse that rises after a carbohydrate meal**
Healthy. The liver made T3, the T3 found its way to the heart, and the heart responded. This is the feedback loop working.

**Pulse that falls after a carbohydrate meal**
Stress response. Cortisol was compensating for low thyroid, the food triggered an insulin response, the insulin drove the cortisol down, and the pulse dropped with it. Same diagnosis as the falling-temperature pattern. Treat with food, not fasting.

**Pulse that stays high (above 90 at rest) with low temperature**
Classic adrenaline compensation. The body is burning fuel fast enough to keep the heart racing but not fast enough to produce heat. This pattern is common in anxious, underweight, insomniac people. The treatment is more food, more calcium, more salt, and time.

## Why Blood Tests Lie

Peat's case against TSH as a diagnostic tool is documented in detail in [[tsh]]. The short version: TSH is the pituitary's opinion of the thyroid, not the tissue's actual metabolic state. Stress lowers TSH directly. A person under enough cortisol stress can have a TSH near zero while producing almost no thyroid hormone at all. The pituitary is silenced by the same cortisol that is silencing the thyroid, so the feedback signal disappears, and the test reads "normal."

T4 blood tests measure the inactive storage form. Most T4 never becomes active in the cells that need it; it gets converted to T3 in the liver if the liver has enough glucose, protein, and [[selenium]] to run the conversion enzymes. A person with low liver function can have high T4 and low effective thyroid activity simultaneously.

T3 blood tests measure the active hormone but T3 has a half-life of about a day and fluctuates with every meal, every stress, and every hour of the circadian cycle. A snapshot T3 tells you about that moment, not about the average tissue saturation.

Peat's summary, paraphrased from multiple interviews: the temperature and pulse are much more reliable than any blood test because they measure what the cells are actually doing with thyroid hormone. The blood test measures the messenger. The thermometer measures the message received.

This is not a license to ignore blood tests entirely. Cholesterol is genuinely useful (it rises in hypothyroidism because the liver cannot process it without T3). Free T3 in the upper quartile combined with a TSH below 2.0 is at least consistent with good function. But if the blood tests say euthyroid and the temperature says 97.2 at 3 PM, the temperature is right.

## Confounders

Several things affect temperature and pulse readings in ways that have nothing to do with thyroid status. Ignore the method on days when any of these apply, or make a note and interpret accordingly.

1. **Ambient temperature.** Do not track in a freezing bedroom or during a 95-degree summer afternoon. The body adjusts its surface temperature to the environment, and the measurement becomes noise. Peat specifically noted that Barnes's Fort Collins patients were ideal subjects because the cool dry climate forced the body to make its own heat. In a hot humid Eugene summer, Peat saw obviously hypothyroid people with "normal" temperatures because the air was doing the work.
2. **Illness and fever.** Obviously disregard readings during acute infection. Fever is a stress response, not a metabolic signal.
3. **Menstrual cycle.** Women run about 0.5 degrees Fahrenheit higher in the luteal phase (post-ovulation) due to [[progesterone]]. Track the two phases separately. The follicular phase is the more reliable hypothyroid indicator.
4. **Pregnancy.** Body temperature is elevated throughout pregnancy. Use pulse and symptoms instead.
5. **Alcohol.** A nightcap raises temperature briefly and suppresses it overnight, often producing a deceptively low morning reading.
6. **Fasting.** Skipping dinner or skipping breakfast drops temperature reliably. This is the negative marker the whole protocol is designed to avoid. If you are intermittent fasting and wondering why your morning temperature is low, you have your answer.
7. **Over-exercise.** Cortisol from a hard workout persists for hours. A 6 AM workout can suppress the afternoon reading the same day and the morning reading the following day.
8. **Electric blankets, heated mattress pads, and hot bedrooms.** These mask low morning temperature by keeping the skin warm while the core is still running cool. Peat specifically asked patients whether they used electric blankets and threw out their morning data if they did. A cool bedroom with adequate wool blankets is the Peat-compliant sleep environment.
9. **Thyroid supplementation timing.** A T3-containing product taken shortly before a reading will artificially raise that reading. Take the reading before the dose, not after.
10. **Cold drinks or gum within 15 minutes of an oral reading.** Obvious but easy to forget.

## The Achilles Reflex

The fastest physical indicator of thyroid status is the Achilles tendon reflex, and it requires no equipment. Sit on the edge of a counter or table so your foot hangs freely. Have someone tap the Achilles tendon firmly with a pencil or reflex hammer. In a healthy, euthyroid person, the foot swings down sharply and returns to neutral quickly, sometimes with a small overshoot in the other direction. In a hypothyroid person, the foot swings down and then settles back slowly, like a door-closer with worn fluid. The slowness of relaxation, not the strength of the kick, is the hypothyroid signature.

Barnes used this as a quick office screen. You can do it on yourself by kneeling on a chair with the foot hanging off the edge and tapping your own Achilles tendon. It is free, fast, and surprisingly sensitive.

## When to Act

Use the following decision rules after you have two weeks of tracking data.

**First two weeks:** observe only. Do not change anything except to correct obvious problems (stop fasting, add salt, warm the bedroom). You need a clean baseline.

**Morning temperature stable below 98.0 degrees Fahrenheit for two or more weeks with no dietary problems:** diet alone is not enough. The cumulative metabolic debt is too deep. Consider a [[thyroid-glandular]] product or [[cynomel|T3]] under guidance. Start low (Peat cautioned that even 15 milligrams of [[armour-thyroid|Armour]] has caused heart pain in magnesium-deficient patients; see [[magnesium]]).

**Temperature rising toward 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit week over week:** the diet is working. Continue without changes. Resist the urge to add supplements. The body is doing the work.

**Temperature dropping after a specific dietary change:** whatever you added is counterproductive for you. Roll it back. Common culprits include adding too much starch, switching to low-fat dairy, introducing muscle meat (the [[tryptophan]], [[cysteine]], and [[methionine]] content suppresses thyroid), excessive coconut oil without enough carbohydrate, or adding caffeine without enough food.

**Resting pulse below 70 with cool extremities:** metabolism is suppressed. Warm up with coconut oil, sugar, salt, and a hot drink. If the pulse does not climb within an hour, the issue is chronic, not acute.

**Resting pulse above 90 with low temperature:** adrenaline compensation. The fix is not beta-blockers. The fix is more food more often, more calcium (milk, cheese), more salt, more carbohydrate at each meal, and stress reduction. [[progesterone]] can help women in this state. [[aspirin]] can help everyone.

**Pulse falling after every meal:** food is triggering stress. Work backwards: reduce the meal size, increase the frequency, add salt and sugar to each small meal, and check for hidden [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] in the diet.

## The Feedback Loop

For anyone working through the [[anti-stress-diet]], the [[peatmaxxing-cut]], or the broader [[core-diet-protocol]], temperature and pulse replace the bathroom scale as the primary feedback instrument. The scale lags metabolic changes by weeks. Water weight obscures fat changes for months. Body composition tools are expensive and inaccurate at home.

Temperature, by contrast, moves within days of any real dietary change. Eat a proper breakfast instead of skipping and the post-breakfast reading shifts that morning. Add two quarts of orange juice over a week and the afternoon peak typically rises by 0.3 to 0.5 degrees. Cut out a hidden source of PUFA and the warming effect is noticeable within ten days.

This is the lead indicator. Track it, respond to it, and trust it over any number on the scale or any lab value on a printout. The body will tell you whether you are heading toward higher energy or deeper hibernation, and it will tell you in the language it has always spoken: heat.
