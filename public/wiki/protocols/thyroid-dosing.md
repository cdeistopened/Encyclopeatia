# Thyroid Dosing

The standard American thyroid prescription is 50 to 100 micrograms of synthetic T4 (Synthroid or levothyroxine), taken once daily on an empty stomach, dosed against a TSH target inside a lab range that runs from 0.3 to 4.5 microunits per milliliter. A large fraction of patients on this protocol still feel hypothyroid. They are cold, tired, constipated, depressed, losing hair, gaining weight, sleeping badly, and unable to lose it back. Their TSH is "normal." Their endocrinologist is satisfied. The complaint they bring is rejected as anxiety, perimenopause, deconditioning, or aging. Ray Peat spent fifty years documenting why this protocol fails for so many people, and what to do instead: combination T4-plus-T3 from desiccated glandular preparations, divided microgram doses taken with food across the day, titration against waking and afternoon temperature and pulse rather than against a pituitary hormone, and a sequencing rule that puts food and cortisol stabilization before the first thyroid tablet. **This page documents Peat's recommendations. Thyroid hormone is prescription medication in most jurisdictions, dose mistakes can precipitate cardiac arrhythmias or adrenal crisis, and any actual change to a person's regimen should be coordinated with a prescriber who is willing to look at temperature and pulse alongside the bloodwork.**

For background on the underlying biology, see [[thyroid]] (the substance), [[hypothyroidism]] (the condition), [[tsh]] (why the standard blood test misleads), and [[metabolic-rate]] (the variable the dose is actually steering). This article focuses on the dose decisions themselves.

## Diagnosis First: Who Actually Needs Thyroid Replacement

Peat's diagnostic standard was the pre-1945 clinical method: oxygen consumption, basal body temperature, resting pulse, the Achilles tendon relaxation rate, and the syndrome catalogued in McGavack's *The Thyroid* and Broda Barnes's *Hypothyroidism: The Unsuspected Illness*. He treated the post-war blood tests as supplementary information at best and as actively misleading at worst (see [[tsh]] for the full argument, and [[metabolic-rate]] for the diagnostic methodology in detail).

The minimum signal that prompts consideration of a therapeutic trial:

- **Morning oral temperature below 97.8°F** taken under the tongue before getting out of bed, after at least three weeks of consistent measurement.
- **Afternoon temperature failing to reach 98.6°F** between roughly 2 and 4 PM, which is the daily peak of thyroid rhythm.
- **Resting pulse below 75 beats per minute** measured at the radial artery for a full sixty seconds, in the absence of athletic training. (Peat cited studies in which the best-functioning high schoolers had average resting rates around 85, and elderly pacemaker patients showed measurable cognitive improvement when their paced rate was raised from 70 to 85.)
- **Symptom cluster:** cold hands and feet, dry skin, hair loss along the outer eyebrows, constipation, morning fatigue requiring caffeine to function, brain fog, low libido, irregular menses or PMS, falling cholesterol metabolism (elevated total cholesterol while protective steroids fall), water retention with low urine evaporation.

A patient whose pulse and temperature do not respond to three weeks of the [[anti-stress-diet]] (milk, orange juice, coconut oil, gelatin, weekly liver, eggs, raw carrot, no PUFA, no fasting, bedtime snack — full version in [[hypothyroid-recovery]]) is a candidate for the therapeutic trial. A patient whose temperature normalizes on diet alone does not need thyroid medication. The order matters because exogenous thyroid raises metabolic demand and a person who has not first fed their liver will often crash on the first tablet.

The therapeutic trial itself completes the diagnosis. "If your symptoms improve as your temperature, pulse, and food intake normalize, the diagnosis is confirmed and the treatment is already working," is how the framework was summarized in [[hypothyroid-recovery]]. A blood test that contradicts a clinical recovery is the blood test that needs reinterpretation, not the recovery.

## The Available Preparations

There are four product categories Peat discussed across his correspondence and interviews. The choice among them is partly clinical and partly logistical (what is actually obtainable, what is actually potent in a given lot).

### 1. Desiccated natural thyroid (NDT)

Brand names: Armour Thyroid, Nature-Throid, WP Thyroid, NP Thyroid, Thyroid-S (Thailand), Erfa (Canada), historical Westhroid. Made from porcine thyroid gland, dried, ground, standardized by USP iodine content. **One grain (65 mg) contains approximately 38 mcg T4 and 9 mcg T3** in the natural 4:1 ratio that the healthy human gland secretes, plus trace T2, T1, and (in pre-1990s formulations) calcitonin. Available by prescription in the US, over the counter in some countries.

Peat called this his preferred preparation for most patients, especially women, in his 1993 ["Thyroid: Misconceptions" newsletter](#) ([[thyroid-misconceptions]]): "Every batch is biologically standardized, and studies have shown it to be reliably within 1% of the labeled potency. The best known brand of the supposedly chemically precise levothyroxine, however, was for a long time 30% below the labeled potency." The pharmaceutical narrative that Armour was sloppy and Synthroid was precise was factually inverted.

The reformulation history matters. Pre-1990 Armour contained calcitonin and a fuller range of trace components. In the 1990s, the manufacturer "began having thyrocalcitonin extracted from the pig thyroid powder to sell separately as a new drug," Peat wrote in his email correspondence ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]), leaving "stearic acid as the only ingredient the current product might have in common with traditional Armour thyroid, USP." Asked directly for an NDT recommendation later in life, he answered: "I haven't seen anything that compares well with the original Armour" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The modern products carry the name but not the composition. Patients who did well on Armour in the 1980s and reported that it "stopped working" in the 2000s were not imagining it.

### 2. T3-only (Cytomel, Cynomel, liothyronine)

Synthetic liothyronine sodium. Standard tablet sizes: 5 mcg, 25 mcg, 50 mcg. Peat repeatedly named Cytomel and the Mexican equivalent Cynomel as the only T3 products he trusted: "Cytomel and Cynomel, so far, have been very well made, and there isn't any other T3 product that I trust" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). He flagged that Cytomel's formulation changed at some point in the 2010s and that he was "not sure of its present potency" — characteristic skepticism toward any unmonitored reformulation.

T3 acts within minutes. Half-life is roughly 24 hours, much shorter than T4's 14-day half-life. This makes it the right tool when a fast effect is needed (acute hypothyroid crisis, myxedema coma, insomnia, migraine, post-heart-attack metabolic support) and the wrong tool as a sole steady-state therapy because each dose must be small and the day must be punctuated with multiple doses to match the body's natural production rate of about 4 mcg of T3 per hour.

### 3. T4-only (Synthroid, levothyroxine)

The American medical default. Available as 25, 50, 75, 88, 100, 112, 125, 137, 150, 175, 200 mcg tablets. Peat considered it inadequate for a large fraction of patients because it depends entirely on the liver's ability to convert T4 to T3, and the livers of hypothyroid women (impaired by estrogen, PUFA, and the metabolic state itself) are precisely the livers where that conversion fails. In the [[2008-10-31-other-hope-for-health-hope-for-health-thyroid|2008 Hope for Health interview]], Peat told the case of a woman whose dose had been escalated to 400 mcg of thyroxine in pursuit of a TSH target. She went into myxedema coma and recovered within hours of receiving intravenous T3. T4-only therapy can make a poorly converting patient *more* hypothyroid by suppressing the TSH that drives the remaining gland output.

### 4. Combination products and compounded thyroid

Cynoplus (Mexico): T4 plus T3 in fixed ratio. Thyrolar (US, intermittent availability): T4 plus T3 (liotrix). Compounded T4/T3 prepared by a pharmacy: ratios and release profiles customizable. Peat used Cynoplus and Cynomel routinely in his own life and recommended them when desiccated formulations were unavailable or unreliable: "I use Cynomel and Cynoplus mostly, but they come in only one size, so I cut the tablets into about ten parts" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

Compounded thyroid is the answer in jurisdictions where NDT is hard to source and synthetic combinations are unavailable. The drawback is variable potency from compounding pharmacies. "I think it's fairly customary for thyroid supplements to be compounded by crooks, morons, and pharmacists, so it's good to be very watchful when trying out any specific product," Peat warned in his email correspondence ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The validation method, again, is temperature and pulse over two weeks, not the lot certificate.

## Peat's Recommended Starting Protocols

Three patterns recur across the correspondence. Which one applies depends on cortisol reserves, severity of hypothyroidism, prior medication history, and individual tolerance.

### Protocol A: Very low Cytomel pulses (for high-cortisol, fragile, or acute cases)

The starting point when stress hormones are high, when the patient has been on T4-only and is decompensating, when the patient is elderly or post-cardiac event, or when prior thyroid trials produced palpitations and anxiety.

**Dose:** 1 to 2 micrograms of Cytomel at a time, every 1 to 3 hours, with food, while awake. Peat wrote: "Thyroid is the only thing that safely lowers cholesterol, but when your stress hormones are very high, you shouldn't take more than about one microgram of Cytomel at a time, and should accompany it with things like milk and orange juice" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). In the same source: "For some people, 15 mg of Armour was enough, and for some 1 mcg of Cytomel was an effective dose."

A 25 mcg Cytomel tablet, cut into sixths or eighths, yields the 3-to-4 mcg pieces Peat repeatedly described as the upper end of a single physiological dose. For 1-to-2 mcg pieces, the tablets have to be quartered and then halved again, or a compounded liquid is needed.

The post-heart-attack case Peat described illustrates the principle: "A man in the hospital right after a heart attack started taking one mcg per hour; the doctors had said that at the rate his enzymes were rising they would be expected to keep rising for another day, but they started decreasing exactly when he started the small doses, and they had decreased the next day when he left the hospital, without symptoms. T3, sugar, and aspirin are the most heart-protective things" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). For an 84-year-old grandmother with dementia, he suggested "even smaller amounts at a time might be better" than 3 mcg per hour.

The principle is that the body produces approximately 4 mcg of T3 per hour under normal conditions. Anything below that is physiological. Anything substantially above it presents the liver with a bolus that triggers detoxification enzymes and the adrenal medulla with a metabolic signal that triggers adrenaline.

### Protocol B: Combination Armour at half-grain divided doses (the standard adult starting point)

The default for most adult women and many men, when cortisol reserves are intact and the patient has been on a Peat-style diet for at least three weeks.

**Dose:** Half a grain (32.5 mg) of desiccated thyroid daily, split into two or three portions with meals. "Half a grain of Armour, or about 30 mcg of T4 and 7.5 mcg of T3, is traditionally a common starting dose; it should be taken with a meal, so that it absorbs slowly. Taking a very small amount at bedtime usually helps with insomnia" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).

A practical split: a quarter-grain (15 mg) at breakfast, a quarter-grain at lunch. If the bedtime snack pattern from [[hypothyroid-recovery]] is in place, an additional sliver of tablet at bedtime can support sleep without raising overnight stress hormones — Peat noted that "a dose at bedtime typically makes it possible to go to sleep quickly; it should be supported by things like orange juice, cheese, and milk."

For the Mexican equivalent: "Cynoplus (www.farmaciadelnino.com has a good price) is cheaper than Armour, and an eighth to a fourth of a tablet would be a reasonable amount to start with" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). One Cynoplus tablet contains 120 mcg T4 + 30 mcg T3, so a quarter tablet is 30 mcg T4 + 7.5 mcg T3 — exactly the half-grain Armour equivalent.

### Protocol C: Full hypothyroid recovery at 1.5 to 2 grains divided, plus cofactors

The maintenance target for patients with established hypothyroidism, especially in winter or at higher latitudes, after the starting dose has been incrementally raised over weeks while watching temperature and pulse.

**Dose:** 1.5 to 2 grains (roughly 100 to 130 mg) of desiccated thyroid daily, divided into three or four portions with meals and at bedtime. This is the steady-state Peat cited as Broda Barnes's *average* dose across thirty years of practice: "His average dose was 120 milligrams (2 grains) of Armour Thyroid" ([[2019-03-19-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-health-of-the-human-body|One Radio Network 2019-03-19]]). Barnes "would increase the dose while watching the patient's temperature. If the temperature didn't rise to normal (98.6°F after breakfast), he looked for interferences."

For T3 equivalence: "25 mcg of T3 has approximately the activity of a grain (65 mg) of thyroid gland" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). So 2 grains equals roughly 50 mcg of T3 activity, of which half is provided as ready T3 and half as T4 to be converted by the liver. In synthetic combination terms: "As long as it's divided so that you don't get a big dose of T3 all at once it should be o.k. to take a total of 25 mcg T3 and 100 of T4. That would be similar to the traditional 2 grain dose of Armour thyroid. A healthy person should produce the equivalent of about four grains per day, so with 2 grains of supplement, or the equivalent, there isn't a risk of over-dosing."

The fact that the healthy gland produces about 4 grains per day means that 2 grains of supplementation, on top of whatever residual gland output remains, is rarely physiologically excessive. The risk at this dose level is not the integrated 24-hour load. It is the size of the individual T3 spike from a single tablet, which is why the dose is divided. A 1-grain Armour tablet taken all at once produces a much larger transient T3 elevation than two half-grain doses six hours apart.

Cofactors at this stage are non-negotiable: at least 2,000 mg of calcium (from two quarts of milk), 100 grams of protein, adequate vitamin A (from weekly liver), selenium (from weekly shellfish or one Brazil nut per day), magnesium (from milk and orange juice), and elimination of PUFA. The full cofactor stack is documented in [[hypothyroid-recovery]] Stage 3.

## How to Titrate

The titration loop is the practical heart of Peat's framework. A patient who knows how to titrate properly can run their own dose adjustments between appointments. A patient who follows TSH alone is at the mercy of a lab range engineered to certify continued illness as normal.

### Daily measurements

1. **Waking oral temperature**, before drinking, eating, or getting out of bed. Under the tongue until the thermometer beeps.
2. **Waking pulse**, sixty seconds at the radial artery.
3. **Mid-afternoon temperature** between 2 and 4 PM.
4. **Mid-afternoon pulse**.
5. **Notes:** mood, energy on waking, sleep depth, bowel function, cold hands/feet, menstrual phase if applicable.

The target Peat cited repeatedly:

- **Waking temperature** trending toward 98.0°F. Below 97.8°F is a signal of low metabolism (or, in chronically stressed patients, of stress-masking that lifts as the cortisol pattern resolves).
- **Afternoon temperature** at 98.6°F or slightly higher. This is the daily peak.
- **Resting pulse** at 75 to 85 beats per minute. Athletic bradycardia below 60 is, in Peat's framework, hibernation physiology and not health.
- **The temperature should rise after a meal**, not fall. A post-meal drop reveals a low metabolic rate that had been propped up by stress hormones until food relieved them: "If you see a person's temperature and pulse rate fall after a good meal, that reveals a low metabolic rate that was being held up by emergency stimulation from adrenaline and cortisol" ([[thyroid|hypothyroidism vault article]]).

### The titration rules

1. **Stay on a starting dose for at least 7 to 14 days before changing it.** T4 has a 14-day half-life and the cumulative effect of any combination tablet takes weeks to plateau. Peat: "It's important to remember that it's cumulative, and the effect of any daily dose increases with time, and is affected by many things, so it's important to keep a chart [of temperature and pulse], watching for changes during a period of about two weeks" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]).
2. **Increase by the smallest available increment** (a quarter-grain of Armour, 5 mcg of Cytomel, an eighth of a Cynoplus tablet) **only if morning temperature is still below 97.8 or resting pulse is still below 70** after two weeks.
3. **Decrease the dose** if any of the following appear: anxiety, heart palpitations, insomnia where there was none, tremor, hot flashes in a non-perimenopausal patient, resting pulse above 95 to 100 (in the absence of fever or stimulant use), or persistent waking temperature above 99.0°F.
4. **Adjust by season.** "The need for thyroid increases greatly during the winter in high latitudes, for example when I needed half a grain in the summer, I had to increase it to two grains during the winter" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The summer requirement can be a quarter of the winter requirement. A patient who maintains a winter dose into summer can develop low-grade overshoot symptoms.
5. **Never increase the dose just because the TSH "is still elevated."** Peat's target for TSH on therapy was *below 1.0, ideally close to zero* ([[tsh]]), but the temperature and pulse govern the dose. A TSH of 0.3 with afternoon temperature of 98.4°F and resting pulse of 78 is a successfully treated patient. A TSH of 2.0 with afternoon temperature of 97.4°F and pulse of 64 is an undertreated patient regardless of where the TSH sits inside the normal range.
6. **Never follow TSH alone.** This is the single most damaging mistake in mainstream practice. TSH is suppressed by cortisol, adrenaline, dopamine, somatostatin, caffeine, fever, aging, and infection — all independently of thyroid sufficiency. The full mechanism is in [[tsh]]. A "normal" TSH in a stressed patient confirms stress, not thyroid adequacy.

### The Achilles reflex check

A bedside maneuver Peat cited as a fast functional readout: "Checking the relaxation rate of the Achilles reflex is a quick way to check the effect of the thyroid on your nerves and muscles; the relaxation should be instantaneous, loose and floppy" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). A sluggish, slow-returning Achilles reflex (the "hung-up" reflex) is a classic sign of hypothyroidism. The reflex normalizes within days of adequate dosing, before some of the temperature and pulse changes catch up.

### What a good response looks like, in order

Week 1: warmer hands and feet after meals. Week 2: morning temperature up 0.3 to 0.5°F. Week 3: vivid dreams, easier morning waking without coffee. Week 4: libido returning, more regular cycles. Week 6: resting pulse up by 5 to 10 bpm. Week 8: hair thickening at the eyebrows and temples, dry skin softening. Week 12: morning temperature consistently 98.0–98.6°F, the original symptom cluster substantially resolved.

A patient who is not seeing the week 1 or week 2 markers should audit the diet (hidden PUFA, inadequate sugar, missed bedtime snack) before raising the dose. The most common failure mode is increasing thyroid against a broken diet, which produces palpitations rather than recovery.

## The Cortisol Pre-Condition

This is the single most important sequencing rule and the one most often violated. Thyroid hormone raises metabolic demand. The body responds to elevated metabolic demand by mobilizing fuel. If the liver has glycogen reserves, that mobilization is calm. If the liver is depleted, the mobilization is stress-mediated: cortisol breaks down muscle and skin to make glucose, adrenaline pulls glucose out of the residual glycogen and constricts skin vessels to redirect blood to the brain and heart. The patient experiences this as anxiety, palpitations, insomnia, and the sense that the thyroid "isn't working."

The mistake is concluding that the dose is wrong. The actual mistake is that the dose has been added on top of a chronic-stress state.

The signal that cortisol is the limiting factor:

- The patient wakes routinely between 1 and 4 AM with a pounding heart.
- Morning temperature is paradoxically *high* (sometimes 98.4°F) while afternoon temperature is *low* (97.6°F). The morning is artificially elevated by overnight stress hormones; the true metabolic rate is what shows up after a good meal.
- The patient describes a wired-and-tired pattern: exhausted but unable to nap, brain fog with adrenaline overlay.
- Salt cravings are intense and salt makes the patient feel calmer rather than thirstier (a sign of high aldosterone driven by low cortisol or by sustained adrenaline).
- The patient is underweight or has lost weight in the last six to twelve months without trying.

In these cases, Peat consistently recommended **starting with the lowest possible Cytomel pulses (Protocol A above), supported by frequent meals**, before considering any combination dose. "Thyroid is the only thing that safely lowers cholesterol, but when your stress hormones are very high, you shouldn't take more than about one microgram of Cytomel at a time, and should accompany it with things like milk and orange juice" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The reasoning: 1 to 2 mcg of T3 is below the per-hour physiological production rate, the milk and juice prevent the blood sugar drop that would otherwise trigger adrenaline, and the slow ramp gives the adrenal cortex time to recalibrate cortisol output downward as the chronic-stress signal abates.

If the patient is on T4-only Synthroid and has developed cortisol-mediated symptoms, the answer is *not* to push the Synthroid higher. The T4 is failing to convert. Peat's recommendation in this scenario, drawn from the email correspondence and the *Hope for Health* myxedema-coma case, was to **add a small amount of T3 (5 mcg or less, with food) and reduce or hold the T4 dose**, while watching for the temperature and pulse changes that confirm conversion has been restored.

See [[adrenaline]] for the broader stress physiology and [[cortisol]] for why endogenous cortisol can be both protective (against acute hypoglycemia) and tissue-destructive (when chronically elevated).

Progesterone deserves a separate note here. "If a person has an enlarged thyroid gland, progesterone promotes secretion and unloading of the stored 'colloid,' and can bring on a temporary hyperthyroid state. This is a corrective process, and in itself isn't harmful. A thyroid supplement should be used to shrink the goiter before progesterone is given" ([[progesterone-summaries-progesterone-supplementation-dosage-of-progesterone|Progesterone Summaries newsletter]]). In women with goiter, the sequencing flips: thyroid first (small doses), then progesterone, to prevent a transient flood of stored hormone.

## Failure Modes

The reliable ways for a thyroid trial to go wrong and what each failure mode actually means.

### Overshoot: palpitations, anxiety, insomnia, tremor

The patient feels worse on the new dose than before. Pulse climbs to 95+ at rest. Sleep breaks up. Hands shake. Heat intolerance appears. **This is the dose being too high *for the current cortisol state*, not necessarily too high for the eventual maintenance target.** The fix is to reduce the dose to the prior level (or to zero for several days if symptoms are severe), feed more frequently with salty and sugary food to bring stress hormones down, and resume titration from a lower starting point.

Peat: "When I used only Cytomel, any little stress would make me suddenly hypothyroid, and my heart would stop several times in a minute; when I started using some thyroid, USP, that contained both T4 and T3 it stopped happening" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). T3-only is fragile under stress because there is no T4 buffer to convert into. Patients who overshoot on Cytomel-only sometimes stabilize on a combination preparation at lower total T3 activity.

A specific case from the [[2014-12-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-you-are-what-you-eat-2014|2014 Ask the Herb Doctor episode]]: a caller had been escalated to 50 mcg T3 daily plus T4 and developed palpitations. Peat's first move was to check albumin, blood sugar, and electrolytes — confirming that the body had the substrate to handle the metabolic demand the thyroid was generating — rather than reaching for a beta-blocker or simply abandoning the dose.

### Undershoot: still cold, still sluggish, no symptom change

Two weeks into a starting dose and nothing has moved. Morning temperature still 96.8°F. Pulse still 62. Energy unchanged. **This is either an inadequate dose or an inactive product.** Increase by one increment and watch another two weeks. If a second increment also produces no movement, suspect the product. Peat's drug-store survey is the warning: "In the 1970s, someone bought 10 products sold in US drugstores as 'thyroid, USP,' and found (by immuno-assay) that only 2 of them contained any thyroid glandular tissue. After I had bought a kilo of 'thyroid USP' that was 99% gelatin, people at USP told me that anything which contains a certain amount of iodine meets their standard" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). A reformulated or compromised lot is a real possibility and a switch to a different preparation is the validation step.

### T4-only adequacy failure (the Synthroid trap)

The patient has been on Synthroid for years. TSH is 1.5. Free T4 is mid-range. Patient still has every hypothyroid symptom. **This is failed conversion.** The liver is not making enough T3 from the T4 being supplied. Causes overlap with the rest of Peat's framework: estrogen excess (which impairs liver conversion enzymes), PUFA accumulation (which blocks the deiodinase enzymes that strip iodine off T4), nutritional deficiency in selenium or vitamin A, chronic stress shifting deiodinase activity toward reverse T3 instead of T3, gut dysbiosis (the intestinal flora participate in deiodination), and low glucose intake (the conversion requires glucose).

The diagnostic move is to add a small T3 dose (5 mcg with breakfast) and watch the response over two weeks. A patient who feels dramatically better on the addition was a conversion-failure patient all along. A patient who feels no different was either adequately converting or has a separate problem (low cortisol, low ferritin, sleep apnea, depression masquerading as fatigue). The selective T3 add-back is the diagnostic.

### Conversion failure from intestinal dysbiosis

A subtler version of the conversion failure. The patient has done the diet, eliminated PUFA, taken thyroid for months, and the temperature is still 0.5°F below target. The intestinal flora are participating in the T4-to-T3 conversion and an overgrown small intestine can interfere. Peat's recommendation in his email correspondence: "If you were deficient in selenium, the correcting effect would be quick, but if there was a problem with intestinal flora, that would have to be taken care of before conversion was good. Other nutritional deficiencies could be involved. Daily raw carrot, weekly seafood and liver, enough sunlight and vitamin D, a good ratio of calcium to phosphate, are often helpful" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). The raw carrot binds endotoxin (see [[endotoxin]]), the calcium suppresses parathyroid hormone, and the seafood provides selenium for the deiodinase enzymes.

### Estrogen excess blocking the response

In women, the most common hidden variable. Estrogen blocks thyroid at the gland (inhibiting the proteolytic enzymes that release stored hormone), in the bloodstream (increasing thyroid-binding globulin so less free hormone is available), and in the liver (suppressing the conversion enzymes). A perimenopausal woman or a woman on hormonal contraception can require larger thyroid doses without symptom resolution because the estrogen is metabolically antagonizing every tablet. The fix is the broader estrogen-detoxification approach in [[estrogen-detox]] plus the raw carrot, weekly liver, adequate progesterone if indicated, and patience for tissue PUFA to clear (the four-year body).

### Fluoride inactivation

Peat's warning, stated bluntly in his email correspondence: "In areas with fluoridated water, taking a tablet with water can inactivate it" ([[thyroid|email exchanges on thyroid]]). Elsewhere he said that "a glass of fluoridated water is enough to knock out a day's portion of the active thyroid hormone." A patient taking thyroid with municipal tap water in a fluoridated city may be dosing themselves with a thyroid-fluoride complex that is functionally inactive. Use filtered water or take the tablet with milk or juice.

## When NOT to Start Thyroid

The contraindications and the situations in which a therapeutic trial should be deferred or run only under medical supervision.

1. **Acute infection or febrile illness.** The body's stress hormones are already elevated. Thyroid added on top can precipitate arrhythmias. Wait until the illness resolves and reassess.
2. **Severe dehydration or recent vomiting/diarrhea.** Electrolyte derangement plus thyroid is a recipe for cardiac instability. Rehydrate, restore potassium and salt intake, then revisit.
3. **Very low body temperature without diagnostic clarity** (e.g., a body temperature of 95°F that might be hypothermia, severe myxedema, sepsis, or hypoadrenalism). This is an emergency-department situation, not a self-titration situation.
4. **Pregnancy without obstetric coordination.** Thyroid hormone crosses the placenta and the developing fetal brain has specific T3 requirements. Maternal hypothyroidism during pregnancy is associated with measurably lower IQ in offspring, so under-treatment is a real harm — but dose adjustments during pregnancy should be made with the obstetrician monitoring free T4 and TSH against pregnancy-specific reference ranges. Peat's framework supports treating pregnant women with low temperatures and low pulses; the supervision requirement is about getting the dose right rather than about whether to dose at all.
5. **Active cardiac arrhythmia or recent myocardial infarction.** Most cardiac contexts benefit from thyroid (Barnes's case series found heart disease prevented by adequate replacement, and Peat's post-heart-attack case used 1 mcg/hour Cytomel as protective), but the dose escalation should be cardiologist-monitored. Atrial fibrillation can be precipitated by overshoot in a vulnerable patient.
6. **Untreated severe adrenal insufficiency (Addison's).** Thyroid raises metabolic demand on cortisol synthesis. A patient with destroyed adrenal cortex cannot meet that demand and can crash into an adrenal crisis. Cortisol replacement comes first, thyroid second.
7. **Active uncontrolled hyperthyroidism or thyrotoxicosis.** Self-evidently. Confirm the diagnosis (often Graves' or toxic nodule rather than the goiter-unloading transient Peat described in women starting progesterone).
8. **Known severe iodine deficiency.** Treat the deficiency with food sources (seafood, iodized salt) for some weeks before adding thyroid. Adding thyroid to an iodine-starved gland can sometimes precipitate goiter formation rather than resolve it.

## Counter-arguments

Where mainstream endocrinology and Peat diverge, and the substance of the disagreement.

**"TSH is the gold standard."** The American Thyroid Association and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists treat TSH as the primary marker of thyroid status, with free T4 as a secondary check and T3 testing reserved for specific contexts. The mainstream position is that intact pituitary feedback is the most sensitive integrated readout of thyroid sufficiency at the tissue level. The Peat counter, documented in [[tsh]], is that the TSH reference range was calibrated against a politically-determined 5% prevalence figure from the Protein Bound Iodine era, that TSH is suppressed by stress hormones independently of thyroid status, that the pulsatile and diurnal pattern means a single morning draw catches the hormone at its 24-hour low, and that "over a period of several years I never saw a person whose TSH was over 2 microunits per milliliter who was comfortably healthy" ([[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|2007 newsletter]]). The empirical question is whether patients dosed against TSH feel better than patients dosed against temperature and pulse. The mainstream answer is implicit (continued patient complaints in TSH-normal patients are reframed as separate conditions). Peat's answer is that they don't.

**"T4-only is adequate for the great majority of patients."** Multiple meta-analyses (e.g., the 2006 Cochrane review on T4 vs T4+T3) have failed to show statistically significant symptom benefit from adding T3 in pooled trials. The mainstream conclusion is that T4 alone is sufficient and that patient complaints on T4 reflect non-thyroid issues. The Peat counter is that the meta-analyses pool conversion-adequate patients (who get no marginal benefit from added T3 because their livers were doing the conversion) with conversion-impaired patients (who benefit dramatically), washing out the signal. The selective T3 add-back as a diagnostic — does this patient feel different on 5 mcg of T3 — separates the two populations and is the clinical move the pooled trials cannot do.

**"Desiccated thyroid is unstable, imprecise, and obsolete."** The mainstream view, repeated in endocrinology textbooks for decades, is that NDT's potency varies lot-to-lot and that synthetic levothyroxine is chemically precise. Peat's counter, citing USP testing, is that the opposite is closer to the historical truth: NDT was biologically standardized batch-by-batch on mice and stayed within 1% of label, while synthetic T4 ran 30% under label for years. The contemporary American situation (post-2009 reformulations, Acella recalls, lot variability in foreign NDT) has weakened Peat's claim about modern Armour specifically, but the underlying principle — that lot variability is a real-world problem with any thyroid product and that titration against temperature and pulse is the only safe response — survives.

**"Wilson's syndrome is pseudoscience."** Denis Wilson promoted T3-only therapy in the 1990s on the theory that reverse T3 dominance was a free-standing syndrome curable by transient high-dose T3. The American Thyroid Association published a position statement rejecting Wilson's syndrome as a recognized diagnosis, citing risks of T3-induced thyrotoxicosis and the lack of controlled trial evidence. Peat's framework overlaps with Wilson's in acknowledging that stress shifts T4 toward reverse T3 (the mechanism is documented in [[hypothyroidism]] and in the Kabadi-Premachandra paper Peat cited), but Peat did not endorse high-dose T3 cycling. His T3 doses were small and chronic, not large and pulsed. The ATA's rejection of Wilson's protocol is not a rejection of the underlying physiology of reverse T3, only of Wilson's specific therapeutic approach to it. Peat's framework here is closer to a moderate position than to either pole.

**"Subclinical hypothyroidism doesn't need treatment."** The mainstream practice in patients with TSH between 4 and 10 with normal free T4 is to recheck in six months and treat only if it rises or "clinical" symptoms develop. The Peat counter is that "subclinical" already produces measurable inflammation (elevated hsCRP), measurable insulin resistance (fasting hyperinsulinemia), measurable arterial damage, and ongoing accumulation of estrogen, cortisol, and PUFA-driven downstream damage. The full argument is in the [[hypothyroidism]] article's section on the subclinical trap. The empirical question is whether the wait-and-watch policy produces better outcomes than treating the patient against temperature and pulse, and the mainstream answer relies on the same TSH-target endpoint that the Peat framework rejects as circular.

## Data Gaps

What the corpus does not settle and what would need new data to resolve.

- **Modern Armour potency relative to the pre-1990 product.** Peat's "I haven't seen anything that compares well with the original Armour" is a clinical impression, not a published assay comparison. A blinded immunoassay study comparing pre-1990 lots (if any verified samples survive in research collections) with current Armour, NP Thyroid, Nature-Throid, and Thyroid-S would settle the empirical question.
- **NP Thyroid and Acella reliability post-2020 recall.** The 2020 sub-potency recall (87–113% of label) is exactly the failure mode Peat warned about, but the post-recall product has not been independently re-validated in any source cited in the vault.
- **Compounded thyroid quality variance.** Peat's "crooks, morons, and pharmacists" warning is anecdotal. Systematic potency surveys of US compounding pharmacies in the 2020s would be useful.
- **The selective T3 add-back as a diagnostic.** No randomized trial has tested the protocol of "T4 patients with normal TSH and persistent symptoms receive 5 mcg of T3 added with breakfast; responders are reclassified as conversion failures and treated long-term with combination therapy." The closest published evidence is the 1999 Bunevicius trial and its replications, which used fixed T3 substitution rather than diagnostic add-back. The diagnostic protocol Peat used in correspondence has not been formally trialed.
- **Pregnancy dosing details.** Peat consistently said the framework supports treating low-temperature pregnant women, but the dose-by-trimester adjustments, the interaction with pregnancy-induced thyroid-binding globulin rises, and the optimal monitoring frequency are not laid out in his correspondence at the level of detail an obstetrician would want.
- **Pediatric dosing.** The corpus has very little on infant and child thyroid dosing in the Peat framework. Pediatric hypothyroidism (congenital and acquired) is mostly managed under specialist supervision and the Peat material does not extensively cover it.
- **Genetic deiodinase variants.** The DIO2 polymorphism that affects T4-to-T3 conversion in some patients is a 21st-century clinical-genetics finding that postdates much of Peat's writing. The framework predicts that DIO2 variant carriers would be the population for whom T4-only fails and combination therapy helps, but the specific genetic literature is not synthesized in the corpus.
