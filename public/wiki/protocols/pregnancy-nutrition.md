# Pregnancy Nutrition

This is the eating protocol for the forty weeks after conception, plus postpartum. The fertility prerequisite — six months of preconception correction for both partners — lives at [[fertility]]; do not skip it. This page assumes the pregnancy has begun and gives the trimester-by-trimester plate, supplement hierarchy, morning-sickness response, and the inversion at the center of the framework: that the standard "watch your salt, watch your weight, take a fish-oil prenatal" prescription is, in Peat's reading, the cause of toxemia rather than its prevention.

The lineage is Tom Brewer through Peat. Brewer proved that "toxemia of late pregnancy" — preeclampsia and eclampsia — is a malnutrition syndrome cured by protein, salt, and calories, not a "genetic" placental disease controlled with diuretics and bed rest. Peat extended Brewer's plate with thyroid, progesterone, and PUFA exclusion. Together they describe a nutrition closer to what a 1955 obstetrician would have advised — more salt, more protein, more food — than to the ACOG handouts in the clinic.

## When to Use This Protocol

Run this as the **default daily template throughout pregnancy** if any are true: pregnancy confirmed and you want a plate before the first OB visit; previous preeclampsia, eclampsia, or HELLP; previous miscarriage; hypothyroidism, heavy periods, low body temperature, or estrogen-dominance going in; twin or multiple pregnancy; edema or rising BP already showing in the second or third trimester; standing "high-risk" label for advanced maternal age, prior gestational diabetes, BMI, or "unexplained" miscarriage.

**Route to obstetric care immediately, not to this page, if:** active vaginal bleeding past the first trimester; severe headache with visual disturbance and rising BP; decreased fetal movement in the third trimester; suspected placental abruption; ruptured membranes; any acute abdominal pain. This protocol is daily nutrition; acute obstetric emergencies are the emergency room.

## Phase 1: Pre-conception → First Trimester (Weeks 1–13)

The first trimester is built on three premises: the embryo is most vulnerable now; the corpus luteum makes the progesterone that sustains the pregnancy through week 10, then the placenta takes over; and morning sickness is a hypoglycemia-plus-salt-deficiency signal, not a fetal-protection signal. Most miscarriages happen in this window, and most of what causes them is upstream of the embryo — the maternal metabolic state at conception.

### Staple plate

Run [[core-diet-protocol]] as the base: two to three quarts of milk daily, a quart of OJ, salt to taste plus a breakfast extra, coffee with cream and sugar, two real meals plus a bedtime snack, two to four eggs daily, liver four to eight ounces weekly, oysters weekly, daily raw carrot or cooked greens. No vegetable oils, fish oil, soy, plant milks, fasting, "low-fat," "low-salt."

The pregnancy plate runs heavier on protein than the Core Diet floor: 80–100 g first trimester, 100–120 g second, 120–150 g third (singleton); 150–175 g for multiples. Two quarts of milk gives 65 g; the rest from eggs, cheese, beef, gelatin, oysters, liver. Brewer specified 80 g/day as floor; the [[eclampsia-in-the-real-organism-a-paradigm-of-general-distress-applicable-in-infants-adults-etc|eclampsia newsletter]] makes the case for going further at risk: "the toxemic women who ate 260 g of protein daily had a 7% increase in osmotic pressure, and a group who ate 20 g had a decline of 9%."

### First-trimester adjustments

**Salt at the bedside.** A glass of salted orange juice or salted water within five minutes of waking, before standing up. The overnight drop in blood volume plus the fetus's overnight glucose draw produce the cortisol-and-adrenaline surge that presents as nausea. Pre-empt it.

**Continue cyclic [[progesterone]] through week 12.** If the [[fertility]] protocol included luteal-phase progesterone, do not taper at the missed period. The corpus luteum makes progesterone through week 10; the placenta takes over at 10–12 weeks; tapering during the handoff is the most common iatrogenic miscarriage cause. From Peat's [[progesterone|email exchanges]]: "Progesterone production depends on good thyroid function, and as it declines cortisol and other stress hormones increase." Dose: 20–40 mg/day on gums through week 12; threatened miscarriage 100–200 mg/day under physician supervision.

**Hold thyroid in range.** Morning oral temperature 97.8–98.6°F, resting pulse 75–85 bpm. Pregnancy increases thyroid demand 30–50%; if temperature drops in the first trimester, the dose needs to go up, not down. From the [[pregnancy|email exchange]]: "A background of hypothyroidism, even when it's compensated by high production of the stress hormones so that the classical symptoms aren't present, is a major factor in reproductive problems." Small frequent T3 doses (1 mcg every 2–4 hours) titrated to temperature.

**Calories: 150–300 extra/day from week 6.** Most women in the first trimester undereat because of nausea. The fetus is small, but the maternal liver is doing heavy lifting — blood volume expansion, albumin synthesis, hormone clearance. Calorie restriction drives [[cortisol]] and stress-hormone elevation. If anything is cut, cut fat (highest caloric density) before protein or carbohydrate. Never cut salt.

### What to stop at the positive test

Fish oil capsules (oxidized PUFA). Standard prenatal vitamins with seed-oil-carried vitamin E and synthetic folic acid — switch to mixed-tocopherol vitamin E and natural folate from liver and well-cooked greens. DHA supplements "for fetal brain development" (see counter-arguments). Alcohol in any volume. Smoking, vaping, recreational cannabis. Acetaminophen — use [[aspirin]] at low dose (81 mg) if needed, per Peat's [[pregnancy|email exchange]]: "Aspirin has a good record of safety in pregnancy, paracetamol doesn't. It became the most commonly used analgesic, displacing aspirin, because of advertising, not science."

## Phase 2: Second Trimester (Weeks 14–27)

The placenta has taken over hormone production. Morning sickness usually resolves by week 14–16. Energy returns. The fetus is growing fast — about half the total weight gain happens in the second trimester. This is the window to load the supplement hierarchy and bank the maternal stores the third trimester will draw on.

### Protein: 100–120 g/day, singleton; 150 g+ multiples

Two to three quarts of milk daily gives calcium plus 65–100 g protein. Add 4 oz of beef or 2–3 eggs at lunch, small low-mercury fish weekly (sardines, scallops, shrimp; avoid tuna, swordfish, king mackerel), liver weekly. Add 10–20 g of gelatin daily from bone broth or powder to balance the tryptophan/methionine load and damp [[serotonin]] and [[cortisol]]. From the [[2011-12-16 water-retention transcript]]: "The calcium has its direct effect on blood pressure, and the sugar of milk has a diuretic-like action. So the calcium and sugar add to the quality of the protein."

### Salt: 6–10 g daily, more if craving

Salt your food liberally. Salt your eggs. Salt the orange juice. Sip salted water in the afternoon. The ACOG handout says 2.3 g/day sodium; the Brewer-Peat number is closer to 4–6 g sodium (two to three teaspoons of salt). From [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity"]]: "Tom Brewer... emphasized the importance of prenatal nutrition... adequate protein (especially milk), calories, and salt... sodium, in association with serum albumin, is essential for maintaining blood volume. Without adequate sodium, the serum albumin is unable to keep water from leaving the blood and entering the tissues. The tissues swell as the volume of blood is reduced."

The paradox — eat more salt, retain less water — resolves once you see gestational edema as hypovolemic edema. Low blood volume drops kidney perfusion; the kidney releases renin; aldosterone retains sodium and water in the tissues rather than the blood; the cycle compounds. Adequate dietary salt holds blood volume up, suppresses renin, and reduces tissue edema.

### Calcium, carbohydrate, vitamin K

Calcium 2,000 mg/day from food: two quarts of milk gives 1,800 mg; cheese, yogurt, and well-cooked greens carry the rest. Powdered eggshells (¼ teaspoon = 750 mg calcium carbonate) is the fallback for dairy intolerance.

The fetus runs on glucose. Orange juice through the day, fruit between meals, milk with each meal. Hypoglycemia drives cortisol, cortisol drives free fatty acids, free fatty acids drive the [[randle-cycle|Randle block]] in the placenta. Well-cooked chard, kale, collards, mustard greens, beet tops daily for K1; K2 in grass-fed dairy and egg yolks. K2 (MK-4) at 100–200 mcg/day from a supplement from the second trimester on; per the [[pregnancy|email exchange]], vitamin K is on Peat's "no need to avoid in pregnancy" list.

## Phase 3: Third Trimester (Weeks 28–40)

The third trimester is where preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, and gestational diabetes show up if they are going to. It is also the window where most American obstetric advice goes wrong: low-salt for edema, glucose tolerance test for gestational diabetes, weight-restriction handouts, and prophylactic induction at 39 weeks. The Brewer-Peat third trimester is the opposite plate.

### Protein, calories, salt

Protein: 120–150 g/day, the bare floor; 175–200 g/day for twins per Brewer. 150 g is realistic with three quarts of milk, four eggs, 6 oz of beef or fish, an ounce of cheese, and 20 g of gelatin.

Calories: 450 extra/day from week 28, three real meals plus snacks plus a bedtime snack. The pre-sleep snack is non-negotiable — nocturnal hypoglycemia is the standard trigger for the morning BP spike. Milk with honey, vanilla ice cream, or salted OJ with cheese.

Salt: 8–12 g daily, tracked by craving. From [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity"]]: "Many young women periodically crave salt and sugar, especially around ovulation and premenstrually, when estrogen is high. Physiologically, this is similar to the food cravings of pregnancy." Estrogen rises through the third trimester; salt craving rises with it. Override the cultural rule, not the body.

### Daily edema and BP monitoring

A $20 home BP cuff, morning and evening, lying then sitting. Trend, not single readings — a morning BP rising over a week from 110/70 to 125/80 is a signal; a single 130/85 in the OB office is noise. Action thresholds match mainstream practice (140/90 sustained, 160/110 acute), but the response is more food, more protein, more salt, plus magnesium — not antihypertensive drugs and bed rest.

### Thyroid in late pregnancy

Pregnancy is the maximum thyroid demand of a woman's life. From the [[pregnancy|email exchange]]: "Progesterone production depends on good thyroid function, and as it declines cortisol and other stress hormones increase." If morning temperature drops below 97.5°F or resting pulse below 70 bpm in the third trimester, increase the dose under physician supervision. T3 crosses the placenta minimally; maternal T3 supports the maternal liver and damps the cortisol elevation that compromises the fetus. For magnesium and B-complex, see Supplement Hierarchy below; the [[eclampsia-in-the-real-organism-a-paradigm-of-general-distress-applicable-in-infants-adults-etc|eclampsia newsletter]] specifies "magnesium in an effective form" as the standard eclampsia drug and notes "B vitamin deficiencies alone are enough to cause the liver's underactivity, and to cause estrogen dominance."

## Phase 4: Postpartum

The first six weeks postpartum continue pregnancy's metabolic demand plus lactation. Hold the third-trimester plate through six weeks minimum, six months if breastfeeding.

**Lactation.** Breastfeeding costs ~500 kcal and 20 g protein/day. Cut food and the body cuts lactation, then thyroid, then delays the period. Hold the third-trimester plate; weight loss happens gradually over six to twelve months without restriction.

**Progesterone.** Estrogen falls catastrophically after delivery; progesterone falls with it. Katharina Dalton's protocol: 100–400 mg/day progesterone for the first 8–12 weeks postpartum in women with prior postpartum depression. Peat extended this with the observation that "postpartum psychosis" sometimes reflects micro-strokes from the prothrombotic late-pregnancy state.

**Thyroid.** Postpartum thyroiditis affects 5–10% of women and is often missed because symptoms (fatigue, weight retention, depression, hair loss) overlap with "new motherhood." Check temperature and pulse weekly through six months postpartum.

**Iron and B12.** Liver weekly for both, plus oysters, eggs, beef. Iron supplements only on lab confirmation — supplemental iron in iron-replete women drives [[lipid-peroxidation]].

## The Brewer-Peat Lineage

Tom Brewer was the obstetrician who, between roughly 1950 and 1980, demonstrated repeatedly that "toxemia of pregnancy" is a maternal malnutrition syndrome. His book *What Every Pregnant Woman Should Know* (with Gail Brewer, 1977) is cited in Peat's [[the-edema-of-stress|"Edema of Stress"]] newsletter. The mechanism: pregnancy requires nearly doubling blood volume by the third trimester; volume expansion requires sodium plus serum albumin plus thyroid; the standard American diet plus "watch your salt" advice prevents that; the underperfused kidney releases renin; renin drives angiotensin-aldosterone; aldosterone retains sodium in tissues rather than blood, producing edema; the placenta is underperfused; the fetus is hypoxic. The treatment is the inverse of standard practice: more salt, more protein, more calories, thyroid, magnesium.

Peat's contribution, layered on Brewer: thyroid is upstream of all of it. From the [[eclampsia-in-the-real-organism-a-paradigm-of-general-distress-applicable-in-infants-adults-etc|eclampsia newsletter]]: "Anything that causes a thyroid deficiency will make the problem worse. Thyroid therapy alone has had spectacular success in treating and preventing eclampsia... Broda Barnes, who prescribed thyroid as needed, delivered more than 2,000 babies and never had a case of pre-eclampsia, though statistically 100 would have been expected." Brewer solving the problem with protein and salt, Barnes solving the same problem with thyroid — two convergent routes that the modern obstetric consensus has unlearned.

## Preeclampsia / Eclampsia Inversion

The inversion at the center of the protocol: low-salt, low-calorie, weight-restriction pregnancy dieting is the cause of toxemia, not its prevention. From the [[2011-12-16 water-retention transcript]]: "It was clear to some physicians (and to most veterinarians) that salt restriction, especially combined with salt-losing diuresis, was very harmful during pregnancy, but that combination became standard medical practice for many years, damaging millions of babies."

The mechanistic chain from the [[eclampsia-in-the-real-organism-a-paradigm-of-general-distress-applicable-in-infants-adults-etc|eclampsia newsletter]]: "a diet deficient in protein, impairing liver function, producing inability to store glycogen, to inactivate estrogen and insulin, and to activate thyroid. Low protein and high estrogen cause increased tendency of the blood to clot. High estrogen destroys the liver's ability to produce albumin. Low thyroid causes sodium to be lost. The loss of sodium albuminate causes tissue edema, while the blood volume is decreased."

Warning signs that warrant escalation: visual disturbance — flashing lights, blind spots, scotomata (the newsletter's etymology, "shining out," is precisely this); persistent right-upper-quadrant pain (HELLP); sudden severe headache with rising BP; sudden rapid edema; proteinuria with rising BP. Acute home response while transporting: strongly salted juice plus magnesium glycinate plus the pre-existing thyroid dose. The hospital handles IV magnesium sulfate, labetalol or hydralazine, delivery if at term. The home protocol's job is to prevent the call in the first place.

## Morning Sickness

Most morning sickness is hypoglycemia plus salt deficiency plus a B6 shortfall plus the high estrogen of early pregnancy. From the [[eclampsia-in-the-real-organism-a-paradigm-of-general-distress-applicable-in-infants-adults-etc|eclampsia newsletter]]: "salty carbohydrate (soda crackers, typically) is the standard remedy for morning sickness... X-ray studies have demonstrated that there are spasms of the small intestine (near the bile duct) associated with estrogen-induced nausea." From the [[pregnancy|email exchange]]: "Salt is often the most important thing for pregnancy nausea. Two quarts of milk daily, cheese, eggs, and orange juice, but with anything salty, even sips of salty water first thing in the morning, should stop it. Low thyroid function, with a low ratio of progesterone to estrogen, causes the kidneys to be unable to retain salt efficiently."

Bedside sequence: salted water before standing; salted OJ, milk with honey, or soda crackers within five minutes; real breakfast (eggs, toast, more juice) within thirty minutes; B6 50–100 mg/day if persistent; ginger is palliative only. Hyperemesis gravidarum — vomiting that prevents fluid retention and produces ketonuria — is the emergency endpoint and warrants hospital IV fluids, pyridoxine, magnesium, thiamine, with salted broth and milk as oral tolerance returns.

## Supplement Hierarchy

In order of priority — use this list when the budget is finite or the woman is overwhelmed by the prenatal-vitamin aisle:

1. **Vitamin K** — K1 from greens daily; K2 (MK-4) 100–200 mcg from second trimester on.
2. **Vitamin D3** — 2,000–5,000 IU/day; target serum 50–60 ng/mL by mid-pregnancy.
3. **Magnesium glycinate or chloride** — 200–400 mg/day; topical magnesium oil if oral loosens stool.
4. **B-complex** — balanced daily; extra B6 50–100 mg for first-trimester nausea.
5. **Mixed-tocopherol vitamin E** — 100–400 IU/day; not synthetic dl-alpha.
6. **Progesterone** (transdermal/buccal) — 20–40 mg/day through week 12 prophylactic; 100–200 mg/day for threatened miscarriage or prior preeclampsia, supervised.
7. **T3 thyroid** — for documented hypothyroidism, titrated to morning temperature and pulse.
8. **Aspirin 81 mg/day** — for prior preeclampsia, antiphospholipid antibodies, or recurrent miscarriage. Stop at week 36; coordinate with provider.

The standard prenatal stack — folic acid, iron, fish oil — is *not* on this list. Folate is better from liver and greens; iron should follow lab confirmation; DHA is discussed in counter-arguments.

## Counter-arguments

### ACOG low-salt and sodium-restriction guidance

ACOG, the AHA, and most clinical handouts continue to recommend 2.3 g/day sodium (one teaspoon of salt) in pregnancy, with stricter limits for gestational hypertension or preeclampsia. The Brewer-Peat claim — that this is causal in the toxemia it is meant to prevent — is a minority view in modern obstetrics. From [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|"Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity"]]: "Salt restriction, according to a review of about 100 studies, lowers the blood pressure a few points. But that generally doesn't relate to better health." The clinic generalizes from "small BP drop on low-sodium diet" to "low sodium prevents preeclampsia," which does not follow.

The honest counter to the counter: well-designed randomized trials of high-salt vs low-salt pregnancy dieting do not exist. Brewer's evidence is large clinical cohorts; the mainstream evidence is population epidemiology. The mechanistic argument — that pregnancy is a hypovolemic state, that sodium plus albumin is required for blood volume expansion, that aldosterone elevation is the mediator of edema rather than its solution — is consistent with the corroborating clinical evidence from Brewer and Barnes.

### Low-mercury fish and DHA-during-pregnancy

The standard prenatal-vitamin DHA recommendation rests on observational studies linking maternal cold-water fish intake to infant cognitive measures. The Brewer-Peat objection: fish oil is by composition oxidation-prone PUFA. From [[oils-in-context|"Oils in Context"]] the oil researcher who ate the high-fish-PUFA diet had "blood lipid peroxides reached a level 50 times higher than normal, and although MDA is teratogenic, he said he wasn't worried about fathering deformed children, because his sperm count had gone to zero." Maternal lipid peroxide level affects the developing fetus. The cognitive-outcome studies behind the DHA claim show modest and inconsistent effects, against the background of substantial mercury and persistent-organic-pollutant exposure from the same fish. The Brewer-Peat plate prioritizes egg yolks (preformed DHA in a saturated-fat matrix), liver, and shellfish in moderation over fish-oil capsules. From [[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|"Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress, and the Hormones"]] Peat grouped "decreased sperm counts, premature births and birth defects" with the postwar shift from saturated animal fats to industrial seed oils. This is a substantive disagreement with mainstream obstetric nutrition; the data is genuinely contested.

### Alcohol-during-pregnancy

Mainstream consensus is no safe level. Some European cohort studies suggest light occasional drinking does not produce measurable harm. Peat's position in the [[alcohol|email exchange]]: "small amounts of alcohol can have some good antioxidant effects, but beer, wine, and dark whiskey, etc., contain enough estrogen to be harmful." His estrogen-during-pregnancy concern applies with extra force. He cites Randall et al. 1991 in the eclampsia-newsletter references — aspirin "dose-dependently reduces alcohol-induced birth defects" in mice, meaning alcohol is dose-dependently producing them. Skip the wine for forty weeks.

### Gestational diabetes screening and the glucose tolerance test

ACOG recommends universal screening at 24–28 weeks with a 50 g glucose challenge, followed if positive by a 75 g or 100 g OGTT. A positive diagnosis triggers a low-carbohydrate handout, often with insulin or metformin if glucose remains elevated.

The Brewer-Peat objection: the glucose challenge is the most stressful nutritional challenge offered in pregnancy — a fasted, isolated bolus of refined glucose with no protein, fat, or salt. Pregnancy normally produces insulin resistance, raised free fatty acids, and reduced hepatic glycogen, all of which sensitize the woman to a glucose challenge. The test diagnoses the maternal metabolic state more than it diagnoses fetal risk. Magnesium deficiency is a documented contributor; the [[diabetes-scleroderma-oils-and-hormones|"Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones"]] references include Bardicef et al. 1995, "Extracellular and intracellular magnesium depletion in pregnancy and gestational diabetes." If the diagnosis is made, the low-carbohydrate dietary response is exactly wrong: it raises free fatty acids, drives the [[randle-cycle|Randle block]], and compounds insulin resistance. The Peat-corrected response is the same plate as the rest of pregnancy plus extra protein, magnesium, and a hard look at thyroid and PUFA status.

### Weight gain restriction

Mainstream guidance recommends 25–35 lb weight gain in a normal-BMI singleton pregnancy. Brewer and Peat both regarded weight-gain restriction as a proximal cause of much preeclampsia. From the [[eclampsia-in-the-real-organism-a-paradigm-of-general-distress-applicable-in-infants-adults-etc|eclampsia newsletter]]: "The attempt to restrict weight gain in pregnancy has expanded the problem of eclampsia beyond its association with poverty, into the more affluent classes." Eat to appetite plus a margin. Track temperature, pulse, BP, and urine — not the scale.

## Data gaps

**Protein dose for twins and multiples.** Brewer recommended 150 g/day for twins by clinical experience. No randomized data.

**Magnesium prophylactic dose.** The hospital uses IV magnesium sulfate for acute eclampsia; the oral prophylactic dose has no consensus number. 200–400 mg/day is by extrapolation.

**Thyroid dosing across pregnancy.** Total T4 demand rises 30–50% but the optimum dose-by-trimester curve for women on T3 is undocumented. Titrate by morning temperature and resting pulse — not TSH, which is unreliable in pregnancy.

**Long-term outcomes of the Peat-corrected plate.** Brewer and Barnes left thousands-of-babies clinical cohorts; no large prospective trial exists. The Katharina Dalton IQ data comes from a different population (severe-PMS women treated with high-dose progesterone). Whether the 130-IQ finding generalizes to women on a corrected diet without exogenous progesterone is unknown.

**Aspirin dose and timing.** Low-dose aspirin in selected high-risk groups (prior preeclampsia, antiphospholipid, autoimmune) is supported; ranges 60–150 mg/day, start preconception to week 12, stop at week 36.

**Postpartum progesterone dose.** Dalton used 100–400 mg/day. The lower end is well-tolerated; the upper end has no controlled trial.

**Caffeine in pregnancy.** Peat regarded coffee as protective; ACOG limits to 200 mg/day. No good evidence either way at 200–400 mg; above 600 mg, a plausible miscarriage signal in some studies.

**Cannabis, hemp, CBD in pregnancy.** Not addressed in Peat's corpus. Default to abstinence.
