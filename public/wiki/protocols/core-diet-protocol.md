# Core Diet Protocol

This is the plate. The longer mechanistic argument lives at [[anti-stress-diet]]. The weight-loss variant lives at [[peatmaxxing-cut]]. The acute energy-crisis sequence lives at [[energy-restoration]]. This document is the action layer underneath all of them: what to buy, what to eat, in what order, and what to throw out.

The protocol assumes you already accept three premises: that [[oxidative-metabolism]] is the protective state and the fat-burning stress state is the disease state; that the dietary [[pufa]] load is the slow background driver of nearly every chronic complaint; and that body temperature, resting pulse, and warmth in the hands and feet are the real instruments. If those premises are not yet settled in your mind, read [[anti-stress-diet]] first. If they are, what follows is everything you need to start eating differently tomorrow.

## When to Use This Protocol

Run this as the **default daily template** if any of the following are true:

- You are trying to raise a chronically low body temperature or recover from [[hypothyroidism]].
- You have spent years on low-carb, keto, paleo, plant-based, or calorie-restricted diets and want to come off them without crashing.
- You are recovering from a stressful season — surgery, divorce, postpartum, training overload, chronic illness, undereating — and need a metabolic baseline.
- You want a non-rigid eating pattern that does not require timed fasting, tracked macros, or calorie counting.
- You are healthy and want to keep being healthy on the same food Peat ate for sixty years.

Skip this protocol and route elsewhere if: you have advanced kidney disease (modify protein and salt with your physician); you have a true dairy allergy (substitute with hard cheese, eggs, gelatin, and shellfish — see "Adaptations" below); or you are in a clearly acute condition that needs the energy-crisis sequence at [[energy-restoration]] first.

## Phase 1: The Four Staples

Everything else is optional. These four are not.

### Staple 1 — Milk: two quarts a day, minimum

Two quarts a day is the floor. Three is normal. A gallon is Peat's lifetime average. Two quarts gives you roughly 2,000 mg of calcium, 65 g of high-quality protein, and 96 g of lactose at a calcium-to-phosphate ratio that no other food matches.

**What to buy.** Whole organic milk if you are at maintenance or trying to gain warmth. 1% or skim if you are using this as the weight-loss plate (see [[peatmaxxing-cut]]). Ultra-pasteurized if that is what is available — pasteurization is fine, the dose is the lever. Avoid plant milks: almond, oat, soy, rice, and coconut milk are not substitutes. Soy milk is actively harmful through its [[aromatase]]-activating isoflavones.

**How to drink it.** Cold from a glass, warm with honey and a pinch of salt before bed, in coffee (see Staple 4), poured over fruit, blended with frozen berries for the closest thing to a Peat-shake. Two quarts works out to roughly a quart at breakfast or through the morning, and another quart through the afternoon and evening.

**Lactose intolerance is usually a soft signal.** Real lactose intolerance is rare; most people who think they have it actually have a small-intestine bacterial overgrowth that recovers once endotoxin load drops (see Staple 3, salt, and the daily carrot below). Start with 1% milk, salt it lightly, and drink it slowly with food for the first two weeks. Hard aged cheese (parmigiano reggiano, cheddar, gouda) contains essentially zero lactose and can carry the calcium load for the first month if needed.

### Staple 2 — Orange juice: one quart a day

A quart is the working target. A pint is the minimum if good juice is scarce. A half-gallon is fine in summer. This is the most distinctive and most-attacked recommendation in the entire framework, and the one most likely to be cut by people who keep "everything except the sugar" and then wonder why the diet stopped working.

**What you are getting.** Roughly 100 g of sugar (mostly sucrose, some fructose, some glucose) in a form the liver converts straight to glycogen. About 2 g of potassium. Magnesium and trace minerals. Vitamin C surrounded by the [[bioflavonoids]] (naringenin, hesperidin) that keep it from oxidizing into a pro-oxidant in the gut. Naringenin is also an [[aromatase]] inhibitor — orange juice is the only common food in the American supermarket that suppresses estrogen synthesis through a direct enzyme block.

**What to buy.** Fresh-squeezed if possible. Not-from-concentrate in a glass or carton if not. Frozen concentrate is acceptable. Avoid orange "drinks," orange "beverages," orange "cocktails," and anything with added preservatives that taste metallic. The only ingredient should be orange juice.

**How to drink it.** Eight to twelve ounces with a pinch of salt within five minutes of waking. The rest spread across the day, especially fifteen minutes before any protein meal (see "The Preload Rule" below) and as part of the bedtime snack. If a full quart causes loose stools at first, start at a pint and ramp up over a week as the gut adapts.

**If oranges are out of season or expensive.** Other ripe tropical fruit (mango, papaya, pineapple, watermelon, lychee, sapota) covers the same nutritional slot, plus added sucrose in water or milk with a squeeze of lemon. Apple juice and grape juice are inferior substitutes — they lack the bioflavonoids and the anti-estrogenic activity. They are acceptable as variety, not as the daily quart.

### Staple 3 — Salt: to taste, with a floor of about 13 g (≈ 2.5 tsp) a day

Most people coming to this protocol are under-salted. Decades of public health messaging have trained Americans to fear salt the way Soviet citizens were trained to fear capitalism — as a cultural taboo, not a physiological reality. The taboo is wrong. In the [[55-bioenergetic-nutrition-basics|Generative Energy nutrition basics episode]], Peat endorsed Georgi Dinkov's claim that anything less than five grams of elemental sodium daily (≈ 13 g salt) chronically activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system: "Oh, for sure. That tiny amount brings up your anxiety and inflammation. So salt is definitely anxiolytic and anti-inflammatory." He added that a population in Mongolia eats roughly 30 g of salt a day and does not have hypertension.

**What to buy.** Plain refined table salt is fine. Real Salt, Redmond, Celtic, Himalayan — all fine, the trace minerals do not move the needle compared to getting them from food. Iodized table salt is acceptable but unnecessary if you eat shellfish weekly. Avoid the low-sodium "potassium chloride" salt substitutes; they raise potassium in a way that food does not and are unpleasant.

**How to use it.** Salt every meal to taste. Then add a little more. Keep a salt cellar on the table. Salt the orange juice. Salt the milk. Salt the cheese (Peat noted that he sometimes salted gouda and emmental). Salt the bone broth heavily. Salty cheeses — pecorino, feta, ricotta salata — count toward the daily intake and are good vehicles for a salt appetite.

**The two markers that you are getting enough salt.** Your sleep deepens. Your morning anxiety quiets. If neither has moved after two weeks at perceived "lots of salt," double the amount actually leaving the shaker. Most people undershoot.

### Staple 4 — Coffee with cream and sugar

Black coffee on an empty stomach is the single most common mistake in the American breakfast. It runs the body on adrenaline before any metabolic substrate is available. The cream supplies saturated fat. The sugar suppresses the adrenaline release. The coffee itself stimulates metabolism, supports liver function, and lowers serotonin.

**What to buy.** Whatever quality coffee you like. Whole-milk cream or half-and-half — not "coffee whitener," which is industrial seed oil. White sugar, brown sugar, or honey. Avoid stevia, monk fruit, aspartame, and sucralose; they do nothing useful and may interfere with sugar's adrenaline-suppressing effect.

**How to drink it.** After breakfast, not before. Two to four cups across the day, last cup before three in the afternoon if caffeine disrupts your sleep. If you must drink coffee earlier than breakfast, take it with milk, cream, and sugar — the equivalent of a *cafe con leche*, which Peat mentioned in his [[diet-general|general-diet correspondence]] as a regular feature of his eating pattern.

## Phase 2: The Daily Plate Architecture

The four staples deliver the macronutrient floor. Around them sits a deliberate **two-meal architecture** that mirrors what Peat actually ate. Two real meals — breakfast and dinner — anchored by frequent small additions through the day. This is not "intermittent fasting." It is the opposite. The point is that no gap between food is ever long enough to trigger a cortisol release.

### Grazing vs. two-meal — which to run

**Run grazing (every 2–3 hours, 4–6 feedings a day) if:**

- Morning temperature is below 97.8°F.
- Resting pulse is below 70.
- Hands and feet are cold most of the day.
- You wake at 3 a.m. and cannot fall back asleep.
- You crash hard mid-afternoon.

This is the hypothyroid rescue pattern. "Small meals help to increase the metabolic rate, single big meals increase fat storage," Peat wrote in his [[diet-general|general-diet correspondence]]. Run it until temperature and pulse normalize — usually two to six weeks. Then transition.

**Run two real meals plus snacks if:**

- Morning temperature is above 98°F and rises after breakfast.
- Resting pulse is in the mid-seventies or higher.
- Sleep is unbroken.
- You wake hungry and feel warm through the day.

"When the thyroid and liver are working, 2, 3, or 4 meals are good. For me, 2 meals and some snacks are most convenient," Peat wrote in the same correspondence. This is the long-term steady state.

### The breakfast template (run every day)

1. **On waking, before anything else:** 8–12 oz orange juice with a pinch of salt. Within five minutes. The point is to refill liver glycogen before the cortisol-adrenaline morning surge gets a chance to mobilize fatty acids.
2. **Within thirty minutes:** the breakfast itself. One or two eggs fried in butter (yolks soft), the rest of your morning orange juice, a glass of milk, and coffee with cream and sugar *after* the food.
3. **Optional add-ons:** a slice of well-cooked potato with butter, a small piece of aged cheese, a small bowl of berries.

**The feedback signal that breakfast worked.** Your pulse goes *down* and your temperature goes *up* in the hour after eating. "That's good. That is the carbohydrate lowering the adrenaline. When the temperature goes up and pulse goes down, that's what breakfast should do," Peat said in the Hashimoto's, temperature, and pulse episode. If your pulse rises instead, you ate too much protein without enough carbohydrate. Add another half-pint of orange juice next time and a smaller egg.

### The midday template (light, not heavy)

A small protein-and-fruit meal works better than a large protein meal. Examples:

- A small portion of grilled white fish or shrimp with rice, butter, and a salted tomato.
- A quart of milk and a piece of cheese with fruit.
- Leftover gelatinous broth with a small piece of well-cooked potato and a soft-cooked egg.
- A bowl of berries with cream and a wedge of cheese.

Heavy meat-and-starch lunches are the most common midday failure. They drive a cortisol surge in the early afternoon and the 3 p.m. crash that follows. Lighter midday + heavier evening is the durable pattern.

### The afternoon snacks (only if you need them)

The grazing pattern fills these slots. The two-meal pattern leaves them mostly empty. If you start to feel hungry, cold, or shaky between meals, eat something — that hunger is a cortisol warning, not a virtue.

Good afternoon snacks: an orange and a glass of milk; a square of [[chocolate]] (the real kind, with cocoa butter not vegetable oil) and a coffee; a cup of warm milk with honey; a small bowl of ice cream; a piece of fruit and a slice of cheese.

### The evening template

The evening is where saturated fat and sugar weighted heavier than during the day. "It's better to take your protein during the day, sugar and fat in the evening," Peat wrote in his [[protein|email exchange on protein]]. The logic is circadian — cortisol drops in the evening, and a heavier fat-and-sugar meal supports the overnight sleep state.

A representative dinner:

- A bowl of gelatinous bone broth (ox-tail, lamb shank, chicken backs) seasoned heavily with salt. Add well-cooked greens (kale, chard) for vitamin K and minerals.
- A modest portion of beef, lamb, or white fish.
- A baked potato with butter.
- A glass of milk or a small glass of orange juice.
- A small dessert: ice cream, custard, milk with honey, or a piece of dark chocolate.

### The bedtime snack (non-negotiable in recovery)

If you wake between 2 and 4 a.m., your liver ran out of glycogen and stress hormones took over. The fix is a bedtime snack. Peat described the pattern in the [[2015-02-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-uses-of-urea|2015 urea episode]]: "Having a carbohydrate meal late in the afternoon or before bed — like a glass of orange juice or milk with honey — helps. Sometimes the dose of sugar puts you to sleep for only an hour or two, so it's good to have another glass ready for when you wake up. Salty snacks at bedtime, like milk with salty tortilla chips or pork rinds, help stabilize blood sugar and energy production."

Standard bedtime snack options, all roughly equivalent:

- A glass of milk with a tablespoon of honey and a pinch of salt.
- A bowl of vanilla ice cream with a sprinkle of salt.
- A glass of orange juice and a small piece of cheese.
- A cup of warm bone broth.
- A small piece of cooked fruit with cream.

If you wake at 3 a.m. anyway, eat. Keep a glass of milk-with-honey on the nightstand. This is not weakness — it is the protocol working. Within two weeks of consistent bedtime snacks, the 3 a.m. wake-up usually disappears.

### The preload rule

Never a protein meal without sugar in front of it. "Eating protein by itself can cause a big surge of cortisol," Peat wrote. "Preceding the protein with some carbohydrate makes the protein go farther, otherwise under the influence of cortisol a lot of protein is used just for energy." In the [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 sugar episode]] he was more specific: "I think it's good to precede a big protein meal by maybe 15 or 20 minutes with fruits so that you get your liver stocked with some glycogen."

Practical rule: a glass of orange juice or a piece of fruit goes down fifteen to twenty minutes *before* a steak. Not after. Not with. Before.

## Phase 3: Optimization Layers

Layer these in once Phase 1 and Phase 2 are running cleanly for two to three weeks. Adding them too early just adds complexity without payoff.

### Optimization 1 — Gelatinous broth, daily

Replace some of your muscle-meat protein with gelatin from home-simmered bone broth. The reason is amino-acid balance: muscle meat is heavy in tryptophan, methionine, and cysteine, all of which suppress thyroid and feed [[serotonin]]. Gelatin is heavy in glycine, an inhibitory neurotransmitter that promotes sleep and counters the pro-inflammatory amino acids in steak.

**The form Peat preferred.** Home-extracted from ox-tail, lamb shank, or chicken backs and wings. Simmer for 6–24 hours with a splash of vinegar to extract minerals. Skim the fat. Salt heavily. "Almost every day, some kind of gelatinous soup, like kale soup or chicken soup with all the fat skimmed off," he described in the [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 food episode]].

**Powdered gelatin as backup.** Acceptable when broth is impractical. Great Lakes (red can) or Vital Proteins are the two most reliable brands. 1–2 tablespoons stirred into warm milk with honey, or bloomed in cold water and added to soup, gives roughly 10–20 g of gelatin protein. Peat used powdered gelatin himself, but mostly "for things like making marshmallows" rather than as a primary source.

**Glycine as a third option.** 1–3 g of pure glycine powder dissolved in water before bed is the cleanest sleep aid in the Peat framework. Cheaper than melatonin, no morning grogginess.

### Optimization 2 — Liver, four to eight ounces a week

Liver is the densest source of vitamin A, copper, and B vitamins in the food supply. Peat ate it weekly to twice-monthly: "in the longer run it's good to eat liver about twice a month, limiting the daily eggs to one or two." In the [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 food episode]] he described his own pattern: "Typically about once a week, some oysters or liver."

**How to eat it.** Beef liver sautéed in butter with onions, calf liver pâté, chicken liver mousse. Frozen-and-cubed liver swallowed like a pill is acceptable for people who hate the taste but want the nutrients.

**Why not more.** "Too much liver can inhibit thyroid function," Peat noted by email, "so the amount depends on the individual. I think there are other reasons, but the high A content is the main one. A high ratio of phosphate to calcium contributes." Once a week is sufficient. More than twice a week can begin to suppress thyroid through vitamin A overload.

### Optimization 3 — Oysters and shellfish, once a week

Shellfish covers the trace-mineral gap that dairy and muscle meat leave open. "Shellfish provide trace minerals that are often lacking from other foods," Peat wrote. "Mercury content is high in the big (old) fish, but not in the small shellfish or small fish such as cod and sole."

Six oysters a week, plus occasional shrimp, crab, scallops, or small white fish (cod, sole, sardines), covers zinc, copper, selenium, and iodine for most people without measurable mercury exposure. Avoid large predatory fish — tuna, swordfish, mackerel — as routine items.

### Optimization 4 — Coconut oil, tiered to metabolism

The dose depends on where your metabolism is starting from. Use this as a tier ladder rather than a fixed prescription.

- **Tier 1 (hypometabolic, morning temp below 97.5°F):** 1 teaspoon × 3 a day. Peat's recommendation for someone burning only 700 calories a day.
- **Tier 2 (recovering, morning temp 97.5–98.2°F):** 1 tablespoon a day. Peat's own n=1 weight-loss dose. "When I tried adding about a tablespoon of coconut oil once a day I lost about two pounds a week, for several weeks, without eating less."
- **Tier 3 (healthy metabolism, morning temp 98.2°F+):** Up to 3 tablespoons a day. Sarah Murray's baseline for someone with a working thyroid.

**What to buy.** Refined, deodorized coconut oil. Peat preferred refined over virgin: "I advise against eating the solid parts of coconut, as a regular part of the diet, and recommend the deodorized refined oil, because so many people are allergic to the proteins (and starches) of coconut." The flavor of virgin coconut oil is also overpowering in food applications.

**How to use it.** Melted into coffee, stirred into hot cereal, sautéing potatoes, drizzled over a baked sweet potato, swallowed by the teaspoon if necessary. Going from zero to three tablespoons in one day causes loose stools — ramp over two weeks.

### Optimization 5 — Eggs, capped at two a day

Eggs are good food at one to two a day and a problem at four or five. The cap is about PUFA: yolks contain 1–2 g of polyunsaturated fat each, and people eating six or eight a day are taking in 12 g of PUFA they did not need to take in. Limit to one or two, fry in butter, eat with orange juice.

### Optimization 6 — A daily raw carrot

A medium raw carrot eaten daily binds [[endotoxin]] and unconjugated estrogen in the gut and carries them out. "A daily raw carrot often helps to balance progesterone, cortisol, and estrogen, by improving intestine-liver functions," Peat wrote. In his [[weight-loss|weight-loss correspondence]] he was blunter: "I know people who have lost weight just by eating a raw carrot every day, reducing endotoxin stress." See [[carrot-salad]] for the canonical preparation — grated lengthwise, with a teaspoon of coconut oil, a splash of vinegar, and salt.

This is the cheapest intervention in the entire framework. Pennies a day. Worth doing on day one.

### Optimization 7 — Aged cheese for calcium and salt

Parmigiano reggiano, gouda, cheddar, gruyere, and emmental concentrate calcium, provide saturated fat in a tolerated form, and (when salted hard) carry the daily salt appetite. 1–3 oz a day as a snack with fruit, after dinner, or melted on potatoes. Avoid processed cheese products (Velveeta, American slices, "cheese food") — these contain seed oils and emulsifiers.

### Optimization 8 — Ice cream as food and medicine

Ice cream sits in a special category: food and sleep aid at the same time. "It has a good balance because it has fat, protein, and sugar. It can help a lot of people go to sleep," Peat said in the [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 weight gain episode]]. A small bowl as dessert, or in the middle of a wakeful night, is one of the simplest applications of the whole framework.

**What to buy.** Real ice cream made from cream, milk, sugar, and eggs. Häagen-Dazs vanilla is the cleanest mass-market option. Avoid anything with vegetable oil, mono- and diglycerides, "natural flavors" beyond vanilla, carrageenan, or gum stabilizers if you can.

## Hard Exclusions

These are not "moderate occasionally" foods. They are off the plate.

### Polyunsaturated oils (the big one)

Soybean, corn, canola, safflower, sunflower, cottonseed, grapeseed, peanut, rice bran, sesame as a cooking oil. See [[pufa]] and [[seed-oils]] for the mechanism. The protocol fails if these are still on the plate. Read every label. "Vegetable oil" in any ingredients list is a seed oil. Most restaurant food is cooked in them — eat at home by default; for the long-horizon version of this exclusion, see [[pufa-depletion]].

### Fish oil and omega-3 supplements

Same molecule, same problem, more concentrated. No fish oil, no cod liver oil, no krill oil, no algae oil, no flaxseed oil. The "anti-inflammatory" claim is a confusion of acute prostaglandin suppression with chronic mitochondrial damage.

### Soy as a staple

Soy protein powder, soy milk, tofu, tempeh, edamame in volume. The genistein in soy activates [[aromatase]] and raises estrogen production — the exact opposite of what orange juice's naringenin does. Small amounts of traditionally fermented soy (a teaspoon of soy sauce, occasional miso) are acceptable. Soy as a primary protein source is not.

### Raw cruciferous vegetables in volume

Raw kale, raw broccoli, raw cauliflower, raw cabbage — daily smoothies and salads — contain goitrogens that suppress thyroid. Well-cooked is fine. A daily kale smoothie is not. Cook them; don't blend them.

### Beans, lentils, and legumes as staples

Phytates, lectins, and fermentable fiber that feeds endotoxin-producing bacteria. Occasional small portions, well-soaked and well-cooked, are tolerable. A bean-based diet is not.

### Whole grains as staples

Whole wheat, oats, brown rice, barley, rye. The phytates bind minerals; the fiber feeds endotoxin-producing bacteria; the gluten in wheat is a problem for a meaningful percentage of people. Well-cooked white rice and masa (lime-treated corn) are acceptable starches. Bread and pasta are not.

### Fasting in any form

Intermittent fasting, 16:8 windows, OMAD, water fasts, fasted training, skipped meals for any reason. The mechanism is in the [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 sugar episode]]: "Cortisol begins breaking down your tissues, turning protein into fat and carbohydrate for energy. Under that extreme condition, when you've been out of glycogen long enough to run on cortisol for about a day, your thymus will be dissolved just in a few hours." See [[intermittent-fasting]] for the full mechanism. Even pre-blood-draw fasts are best broken with orange juice and milk the moment the needle comes out.

### Low-fat and low-salt dieting

Both raise stress hormones for the same reason: depriving the body of a substrate it needs forces the [[adrenaline]]-cortisol axis to compensate. "The increase of adrenalin caused by salt restriction has many harmful effects, including insomnia," Peat wrote in his [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|newsletter on salt]]. Low-fat dairy is acceptable for weight loss specifically (see [[peatmaxxing-cut]]); a low-fat *diet* generally is a stress regimen.

### Plant milks, "alternative" sweeteners, industrial seed-oil snacks

Almond milk, oat milk, soy milk, rice milk, coconut milk drinks. Stevia, monk fruit, aspartame, sucralose, erythritol. Crackers, chips, granola bars, protein bars, "healthy" packaged snacks, anything in the supermarket middle aisles. All carry seed oil and offer no metabolic upside.

## Common Pitfalls and Troubleshooting

**Under-salting.** The single most common failure. People come from a culture that treats salt as poison and salt their food at one-third of what their appetite would request. Diagnostic: morning anxiety, broken sleep, cold extremities, a vague sense that the diet "is not working." Fix: salt every meal until food tastes salty. Then add a little more.

**Cutting the sugar.** The second most common failure. People accept the milk, eggs, and dairy fat but balk at "drinking that much juice." Without the sugar, free fatty acids stay elevated, the [[randle-cycle|Randle block]] persists, and the diet collapses back into a high-protein regimen with high cortisol. Fix: a full quart of orange juice a day, non-negotiable, for at least four weeks. Then assess.

**Whole milk causing weight gain at maintenance.** If you are at a healthy weight and eating three quarts of whole milk, ice cream, and butter, you may simply be at a caloric surplus. Drop milk to 2% or 1% — Peat himself drank 2% for decades. The calcium and protein are unchanged.

**3 a.m. wakeups that won't stop.** Add the bedtime snack. If you already have one, make it larger and add salt. If still no improvement after two weeks, the underlying issue is hypothyroidism — see [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

**Loose stools or bloating in the first two weeks.** Real but transient. Most often from too much milk too fast, too much fiber from raw vegetables, or too much coconut oil too fast. Ramp the milk over a week, drop the raw vegetables (except the daily carrot), and start coconut oil at a teaspoon, not a tablespoon.

**Temperature drops after a few weeks on the diet.** You are eating too little. Almost without exception. Add more food — more orange juice, more milk, more starch, more cheese — and re-measure in a week.

**The protocol is "boring."** That is partly the point — Peat ate roughly the same dozen foods every day for sixty years. If the variety problem is real and stable, add: lychees, mangoes, papayas, watermelon, white fish, scallops, shrimp, lamb, well-cooked beets, the occasional sweet potato, dark chocolate. The vocabulary inside the protocol is wider than it looks at first.

**Friends and family pushback.** Expect it. The recommendations look extreme by mainstream nutritional standards in 2026. The shorter version of the protocol that survives small talk: "I'm eating a kind of traditional dairy-and-fruit diet, like the populations that don't get the diseases we get." Most conversations end there.

## Counter-arguments

### Mainstream nutrition science

The current consensus position from the American Heart Association, USDA dietary guidelines, and most major academic departments contradicts almost every element of this protocol. Saturated fat is to be reduced (the protocol increases it). Sugar is to be minimized (the protocol prescribes 200+ g a day). Polyunsaturated vegetable oils are recommended for "heart health" (the protocol bans them). Plant-based proteins are favored over animal (the protocol is animal-centric). Whole grains are praised (the protocol relegates them to occasional use). Low-fat dairy is preferred (the protocol mostly accepts that but for different reasons).

The mainstream evidence base rests on epidemiological associations that have not held up cleanly under reanalysis (the Seven Countries Study selection bias, the questionable extrapolation from the Lyon Diet Heart Study, the failure of the Women's Health Initiative to show low-fat-diet benefit, the recent meta-analyses showing no association between saturated fat and cardiovascular outcomes). The Randle mechanism, the [[lipid-peroxidation]] cascade from PUFA, and the metabolic-rate-driven framework Peat used were always available — they were simply outside the consensus.

A reader convinced by the current mainstream position will reject this protocol on prior grounds. The honest counterclaim is that the mainstream position is a hypothesis that was treated as fact for fifty years and is now slowly being walked back by the field that promoted it. The protocol here represents one of several minority traditions (Weston Price, Broda Barnes, the Latin American dairy-and-fruit pattern) that the mainstream conflated with "fringe" but that have outperformed in long-running clinical and traditional populations.

### Plant-based (vegan) framework

A vegan reader will object that the protocol is animal-centric: milk, eggs, gelatin, liver, shellfish are all unacceptable. The two main counterarguments from that camp are environmental (animal agriculture's resource footprint) and ethical (animal welfare). Neither of those is a medical claim, and neither is addressed here.

The medical counterargument from the plant-based side is usually that animal protein is associated with various chronic diseases in observational studies (T. Colin Campbell's *China Study*, the EPIC-Oxford cohort, the various Adventist cohorts). The associations are real but consistently confounded by the healthy-user effect: people who choose plant-based diets in observational cohorts also smoke less, exercise more, drink less alcohol, and have higher socioeconomic status. Intervention trials of plant-based diets generally show small short-term improvements in lipid markers but do not show the mortality benefits the observational literature suggests.

The harder problem for the vegan position in the Peat framework is the inability to deliver adequate protein, calcium, vitamin A, vitamin K2, B12, taurine, carnitine, creatine, choline, glycine, and bioavailable iron without supplementation that essentially reproduces the animal-food nutrient profile. The Peat protocol is dense in those nutrients by design. Soy- and grain-based plant diets are dense in PUFA, phytates, and goitrogens by design.

### Ketogenic / low-carb framework

The closest direct opponent. Keto and Peat agree on PUFA exclusion, on the importance of saturated fat, on insulin sensitivity as a marker, and on the value of whole foods over processed. They diverge on the central lever. Keto restricts carbohydrate; Peat prescribes 200+ g a day of it.

The keto counterargument is that low-carb diets show better short-term weight loss in head-to-head trials, that high-carb diets drive insulin resistance, and that the body adapts to "fat as fuel" through ketosis without negative consequence. The Peat counter is that ketosis is functionally indistinguishable from the stress-fasting state Randle described — free fatty acids dominate, glucose oxidation is blocked, cortisol is elevated to maintain glycogen, and the metabolic rate slowly declines. The empirical signal: long-running ketogenic dieters frequently develop symptoms of low thyroid function (cold intolerance, hair thinning, low libido, sleep disruption, slow recovery), and many cycle off keto for those reasons. Short-term improvements in lipid markers do not necessarily translate to long-term metabolic health.

Where this divergence matters most: a reader with reactive hypoglycemia or established Type 2 diabetes may need a graduated transition from keto rather than a direct switch. See [[blood-sugar]] for the mechanism and [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for the entry sequence.

### Paleo / ancestral framework

The friendliest opponent. Paleo and Peat agree on eliminating seed oils, on preferring traditional fats, on shellfish and liver, on minimal processed food. They diverge mainly on dairy (paleo excludes; Peat centers it) and on sugar (paleo restricts; Peat embraces it from fruit). They also diverge on grain (paleo excludes all; Peat allows well-cooked white rice and masa as backup carbohydrates).

The paleo counterargument on dairy is that humans only began consuming dairy in the last 10,000 years and that lactose intolerance in much of the global population reflects an evolutionary mismatch. Peat would have answered that the populations that did adopt dairy (European, Maasai, North African pastoralists) show some of the best longevity and metabolic profiles in the literature, and that the practical relief dairy provides (calcium, B vitamins, balanced amino acids, low PUFA, lactose as a clean carbohydrate) is real regardless of evolutionary timeline.

The paleo counter on sugar — that fruit was seasonal, small, and far less sweet than modern varieties — is partially fair. Peat's response would be that ripe modern fruit and orange juice are still preferable to the alternatives most modern people reach for, and that the metabolic effect of sugar on the liver does not turn negative until [[fructose]] arrives without micronutrients (i.e., as high-fructose corn syrup in a soda).

A reader who has been thriving on a strict paleo diet will probably find the Peat protocol an easy addition rather than a contradiction. Most of the changes are at the margins: add dairy back, add orange juice, add more salt, eat less muscle meat and more gelatin.

### Mediterranean / Blue Zones

Often presented as a third path between Peat and keto. The Mediterranean pattern shares Peat's olive oil tolerance (oleic acid is mostly fine), dairy (especially aged cheese and yogurt), modest fish, and moderate wine. It departs on the heavy use of nuts, legumes, and whole grains, all of which are PUFA- and phytate-heavy by Peat's standards. The Blue Zones literature is also methodologically weak — recent reanalyses by Saul Newman and others have found large statistical artifacts in the "supercentenarian clusters" that drove the original claims.

A reader currently running a Mediterranean diet will find the easiest transition: replace the olive oil with butter and coconut oil, swap the whole-grain bread for a baked potato, swap the lentils for a glass of milk, keep the fish, keep the cheese.

## Data gaps

**Aspirin dosing.** Peat clearly endorsed [[aspirin]] as one of the safest tools for reducing free fatty acids and inflammation, but the verbatim research brief did not surface a specific daily mg target. Cross-reference with `sources/articles/aspirin-*.md` and the aspirin wiki page before prescribing a number. Most practitioners in the Peat orbit use 325 mg once or twice daily; higher therapeutic doses (1–3 g) appear in some of his writing on cancer and chronic inflammation.

**Vitamin E and vitamin K target doses.** Both are referenced as widely deficient and as supporting tools for PUFA depletion. Specific gram or IU targets did not surface in the primary sources. Topical vitamin D in winter is documented; topical vs. oral E and K are not specified in the brief.

**Specific gelatin gram target.** Peat never published one. The protocol gives a ratio-based recommendation (enough gelatin to balance the tryptophan and cysteine of the day's muscle meat) and points to bone broth as the preferred form. Practitioners commonly target 10–20 g of powdered gelatin if home-cooked broth is unavailable, but this is convention rather than direct Peat prescription.

**Long-term outcomes data.** The protocol relies primarily on Peat's clinical observations, his readings of mid-20th-century physiology, and the empirical track record of his correspondents. There is no large randomized trial of "the Peat diet" against any control. The research the protocol depends on is mechanism-driven (Randle, lipid peroxidation, thyroid physiology) rather than outcomes-driven (mortality endpoints, all-cause hospitalization). A serious reader should treat the framework as a well-reasoned hypothesis with substantial mechanistic support and modest direct outcome evidence — not as proven clinical practice.

**Caffeine dosing in this protocol.** Coffee is endorsed; a specific mg ceiling is not documented in the brief. Most readers tolerate 200–400 mg a day (2–4 cups) without disruption, but individual variation is wide.

**Population subgroups.** Peat gave principles, not subgroup tables. Specific protocols for pregnancy, lactation, children, elite athletes, postmenopausal women on HRT, and people with chronic kidney disease are all underdetermined in the primary sources. Adapt with caution.
