# The Peatmaxxing Morning Routine

The first thirty minutes of your day set the hormonal tone for the next sixteen hours. You wake into a metabolic emergency that is entirely normal and entirely fixable. [[cortisol]] has been climbing all night, peaking right around the moment you open your eyes. Your liver glycogen has been depleted by the overnight fast. Free fatty acids are mobilized from storage and circulating at their highest daily levels, carrying the polyunsaturated fats stored in your tissues with them. Adrenaline is elevated. Blood sugar is at its lowest stable point of the day. This is not illness. This is the body doing what it evolved to do when food was scarce: cannibalize tissue to maintain blood glucose until food arrives.

The entire question of the morning is whether food arrives quickly enough to shut the emergency down, or whether you let it run for another two, four, or eight hours and call that discipline.

Ray Peat was blunt about this. The popular advice to drink black coffee on an empty stomach, skip breakfast in service of "intermittent fasting," or take a cold shower before eating is, in his framework, the single most destructive daily habit in mainstream health culture. Every one of those interventions extends the cortisol state that morning food is supposed to interrupt. You are not "fat-adapting" or "giving your digestion a rest." You are running a cortisol drip, and cortisol cannibalizes muscle, thymus, skin, and brain to make glucose.

The Peatmaxxing morning routine is the opposite of all of this. It is a rapid, deliberate interruption of the cortisol cascade using food, starting within five minutes of waking. By the sixty-minute mark you should have consumed orange juice with salt, coffee with cream and sugar, a real breakfast with protein and carbohydrate, and any morning supplements. Then and only then are you allowed to think about the day. This protocol gives you the exact sequence. See also [[anti-stress-diet]] and [[sleep]].

## Why Morning Cortisol Is the Enemy

During sleep your liver burns through its glycogen stores to keep blood glucose stable. By four or five in the morning, the tank is low. The body responds the way it responds to any fuel shortage: adrenaline mobilizes fatty acids from adipose tissue, and cortisol breaks down muscle and other tissue protein into amino acids that the liver converts to glucose. This is gluconeogenesis, and it is the definition of a catabolic state.

Two things make the morning cortisol state particularly damaging. First, the fatty acids being mobilized include the [[polyunsaturated-fats]] stored in your tissues. Circulating [[free-fatty-acids]], especially PUFA, block glucose oxidation at the cellular level through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]. Even after you eat, the cells cannot fully switch back to burning sugar until those fatty acids clear. Second, cortisol does not fall on its own schedule. It falls when the body receives unambiguous evidence that the fast is over. That evidence is food. Specifically, carbohydrate. Protein alone does not do it, because protein without carbohydrate triggers its own insulin-mediated hypoglycemic dip and another cortisol pulse.

The correct intervention is to eat quickly. The wrong intervention is to drink water, take a cold shower, exercise, meditate, journal, or check email. All of those either do nothing about cortisol or actively raise it further.

## The Opening Move: OJ + Salt Within Five Minutes of Waking

This is the most important sentence in the protocol.

**Before teeth, before shower, before coffee, before the phone, before any decision or conversation: eight to twelve ounces of orange juice with a pinch of salt.**

Keep it on the kitchen counter. Keep it in a pitcher next to the bed if you have to. The friction between waking and drinking needs to be as close to zero as you can engineer it. The reasons this one move matters more than any other morning habit:

1. [[oranges|Orange juice]] replenishes liver glycogen faster than any other food. Fructose bypasses the insulin-regulated glucose pathway and goes directly to the liver, where it refills glycogen stores in minutes. This is the single fastest way to tell the body the overnight fast is over.
2. The potassium in orange juice reverses the overnight sodium-potassium imbalance that builds during sleep.
3. The [[salt]] lowers the cortisol and aldosterone axis directly. A low-sodium state raises both, and the overnight sodium losses have already tilted you in that direction. A pinch of salt in the juice repairs it.
4. The [[bioflavonoids]] in orange juice, particularly naringenin, inhibit aromatase. For men, this matters for morning testosterone. For women, it matters for the estrogen-to-progesterone balance that drives the whole cycle.
5. Together, these effects signal "stress is over" to the brain within five minutes of waking. Ray Peat was explicit about the priority: "you need orange juice first to get your thyroid going."

If you take nothing else from this protocol, take this. Orange juice with salt, within five minutes, every morning, before anything else.

## Within Thirty Minutes: Real Breakfast

The operating rule is simple. **Never leave the house without eating a real breakfast.** Not a smoothie. Not a bar. Not coffee and vibes. A sit-down meal with protein, carbohydrate, saturated fat, and salt.

Acceptable options, all of which Peat endorsed in one form or another:

- **The Peat Classic.** Two eggs cooked in coconut oil or butter, a tall glass of whole milk, a second glass of orange juice, coffee with cream and sugar.
- **The Quick Version.** Two tablespoons of [[gelatin]] dissolved in hot water with honey and a splash of milk, plus coffee with cream. Ready in three minutes.
- **The Sweet Version.** Full-fat Greek yogurt with honey and berries, coffee with cream and sugar.
- **The Traditional Version.** Oatmeal with butter, milk, honey, and berries. Oats are a B-tier carb in the Peat framework because of their starch content and moderate PUFA, but they are acceptable in the morning when the glucose window is widest.
- **The Low-Appetite Version.** A glass of milk, a ripe banana, a teaspoon of honey, coffee with cream and sugar. This is the minimum viable breakfast when you wake up without hunger.

The non-negotiables across all versions are the same. Protein from eggs, dairy, or gelatin. Carbohydrate from fruit, juice, or honey. Saturated fat from butter, coconut oil, or cream. Salt to taste. Miss any one of those and the meal has a hole in it that cortisol will fill by mid-morning.

## Coffee With Cream and Sugar, Not Black

Coffee deserves its own section because it is the single most commonly mismanaged item in the morning. Black coffee on an empty stomach is a cortisol amplifier. The caffeine accelerates cellular glucose consumption, the stomach has no sugar to replace what gets burned, blood glucose drops, adrenaline and cortisol climb to compensate. The jittery, anxious feeling people attribute to "caffeine sensitivity" is almost always a blood sugar crash dressed up as a drug reaction.

[[coffee|Coffee]] with cream and sugar, taken with or after food, is a completely different substance pharmacologically. The cream provides saturated fat and calcium. The sugar provides the glucose that the caffeine accelerates consumption of. The coffee itself provides magnesium, niacin, and caffeine, which in this context is thermogenic, anti-serotonin, progesterone-elevating, aromatase-inhibiting, and protective against a long list of cancers and liver diseases.

Peat's own guidance on this was specific. When women told him coffee made them shaky, he told them to try two ounces of coffee with cream or milk alongside a meal. It almost always worked. The drug reaction was never the drug. It was the missing meal.

If you are used to black coffee, the correction is to add whole milk or cream, add a real spoonful of sugar (or honey), and drink it after you have already had the orange juice and some food. That is coffee as Peat drank it, five or more cups a day, for decades.

## What Not to Do in the Morning

The failure modes, in rough order of how common they are:

1. **Intermittent fasting.** Extends the overnight cortisol state by eight to fourteen hours. No metabolic benefit and significant metabolic harm in the Peat framework. See [[intermittent-fasting]].
2. **Black coffee first thing.** Amplifies cortisol without food to counteract it. Discussed above.
3. **Cold showers and cold plunges.** Adrenergic stressor on top of an already-peaked adrenaline state. Peat was opposed to deliberate cold exposure, period. The body needs to get warmer in the morning, not colder.
4. **Heavy exercise before eating.** Cortisol on cortisol. Catabolic. Any training done fasted comes at the price of the tissue the body consumes to fuel it.
5. **Checking the phone before eating.** Adds psychological stress to a state that is already stressed biochemically. The email is not more urgent than your thyroid.
6. **Skipping breakfast to "save calories."** The cortisol that replaces the food catabolizes muscle and connective tissue at roughly the same calorie value. You do not save anything. You pay in protein.
7. **Bulletproof coffee alone.** Butter and MCT oil blended into coffee, no actual food. Fat does not suppress cortisol the way glucose does. The fat in the cup is not a substitute for the sugar that tells the body the fast is over.
8. **Fruit juice alone, no protein.** Better than nothing, but incomplete. The sugar hits without the amino acids to balance it, and you crash by mid-morning. Juice is the opener, not the meal.

## The 3 AM Wake-Up Protocol

If you wake up at three in the morning and cannot fall back asleep, you are in a cortisol surge from low overnight blood sugar. Lying there stewing in cortisol is the worst possible response. Get up and have a small snack. Any of these will work:

- Four to six ounces of orange juice with a pinch of salt.
- A glass of milk with a teaspoon of honey.
- A teaspoon of honey with a pinch of salt directly.

Then go back to bed. You will usually fall asleep within fifteen minutes and sleep through until morning. This is the same principle as the bedtime snack, applied in the middle of the night. Peat used this on himself. In his 1991 newsletter "Solving Some of the Problems of Aging," he described getting in the habit of drinking milk or orange juice in the night to raise his blood sugar enough to lower his adrenaline and cortisol so he could return to sleep. See [[sleep]] for the full picture.

## Morning Light, Without the Cold

Get outside for ten minutes after breakfast. Sunlight on the face and skin resets the circadian rhythm and supports the shift from nighttime cortisol dominance to daytime thyroid dominance. The order matters. Light exposure on an empty stomach is itself a cortisol amplifier. Orange juice and food come first, then the walk. Not the other way around.

Do not combine the light exposure with a cold plunge. Do not go stand on wet grass in shorts in February and call it grounding. The morning goal is to get warmer, calmer, and better fueled, not to introduce another stressor and then congratulate yourself for tolerating it.

## Morning Supplements If You Use Them

Most of the commonly recommended Peat supplements belong at breakfast, not before it. Taking them before food is a fast way to create nausea, reflux, or a wasted dose.

- **Aspirin.** With breakfast, never on an empty stomach. The food protects the stomach lining.
- **Vitamin E.** With breakfast, because it is fat soluble and needs the saturated fat in the meal to absorb.
- **Thyroid glandular.** A small dose with breakfast if you are using one, split through the day.
- **Coconut oil.** The first tablespoon of the day goes into the coffee, onto the eggs, or stirred into oatmeal.

See [[aspirin]], [[vitamin-e]], [[thyroid]], and [[coconut-oil]].

## The Sixty-Minute Rule

By the sixty-minute mark after waking, you should have consumed:

1. Eight to twelve ounces of orange juice with salt (within five minutes).
2. Coffee with cream and sugar (with or after food).
3. A real breakfast with protein, carbohydrate, saturated fat, and salt (within thirty minutes).
4. Morning supplements if you use them (with the meal).
5. Ten minutes of outdoor light (after the meal).

If you are not fully fed by sixty minutes, the day is already in cortisol territory. Everything after that is damage control. The mid-morning crash, the ten o'clock cravings, the afternoon fog, the evening anxiety, the broken sleep that same night: most of it traces back to a morning that was not interrupted properly.

## The Signs It Is Working

You know the protocol is landing when:

- You wake up hungry, not nauseated or anxious.
- Your morning body temperature rises by roughly half a degree Fahrenheit after the orange juice and breakfast.
- Your pulse climbs slightly after eating, rather than dropping.
- You feel warm, not cold, by thirty minutes after the meal.
- You are focused and calm by the end of the first coffee, not jittery.
- You do not crash between ten and eleven in the morning. A mid-morning energy drop is a near-certain sign the breakfast was incomplete, usually missing either the carbohydrate or the salt.

The morning routine is the hinge of the day. Fix the first sixty minutes and most of the other bioenergetic problems become tractable. Leave them broken and nothing downstream will fully work.
