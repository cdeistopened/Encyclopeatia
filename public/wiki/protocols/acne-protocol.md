# Acne Protocol

Acne is the skin's report card on the rest of the body. Peat traced his own way into the bioenergetic framework through acne and dandruff in his twenties — "it was acne and dandruff that led me into studying the steroids and thyroid, and in the process I found that they were related to constipation and food sensitivity" ([[acne|acne email]]). What looks like a localized skin problem is the convergence of [[vitamin-a]] insufficiency relative to need, [[thyroid]] hypofunction, [[estrogen]] excess (in women) or [[cortisol]]-driven androgen flare (in men), prostaglandin inflammation downstream of [[pufa]], gut irritation that breaches the intestine-skin axis, and a sebum chemistry that supports bacterial overgrowth. Treating the lesion without treating the system gets you a maintenance schedule for life.

The dermatology default — benzoyl peroxide, isotretinoin (Accutane), spironolactone, long-term doxycycline — works by suppressing oil production, killing bacteria, or shutting down androgens. Each one trades acne for a different cost: barrier disruption and accelerated skin aging from peroxides, mood and liver risk from isotretinoin, contraceptive estrogen exposure routed through "hormonal acne" prescribing. The Peat alternative is to ask what the skin is signaling about thyroid, vitamin A, PUFA load, and estrogen exposure, then move those levers first. Topicals and short antibiotic courses earn their place as adjuncts, not as the protocol.

Sequencing matters. The first six to eight weeks usually include a mild initial flare as gut and skin rebalance — this is not failure, it's the system finishing what was already in motion. Months two through six are where lesion count drops, oil production normalizes, and post-inflammatory pigmentation begins to fade. Maintenance from month six forward is a minimal effective stack — vitamin A from liver weekly, PUFA avoided, thyroid supported, sleep protected.

## Diagnosis

Acne is not one condition. Six patterns dominate, and the lever weighting changes by pattern.

**Mild comedonal.** Clogged pores, blackheads, whiteheads, little inflammation. Vitamin A and thyroid carry the most weight here — Peat: "One function of vitamin A is to increase progesterone in the skin... Another function is to differentiate the skin cells, reducing keratin plugging of the glands" ([[acne|acne email]]). PUFA reduction is the diet lever. Topicals are minimal.

**Moderate inflammatory.** Red papules and pustules along with comedones. Prostaglandin inflammation is the dominant driver. PUFA reduction, daily aspirin, topical aspirin solution, and zinc all matter. Tetracycline-class agents work here partly through their anti-inflammatory action, not just antibacterial — Peat: "the tetracycline type is usually taken internally, for their antiinflammatory effect" ([[acne|acne email]]).

**Severe cystic.** Deep, painful nodules, scarring risk. The systemic lever still leads, but short-course oral antibiotics and clinician involvement enter the protocol. The window for scar prevention is narrow.

**Hormonal-cyclical (women).** Jawline and chin flares timed to luteal phase, premenstrual week, or post-ovulation. [[estrogen]]-progesterone imbalance is the lead driver; [[progest-e|Progest-E]] cycle-coordinated and [[estrogen-detox]] work the most. The pattern often coexists with [[pms]] symptoms.

**Adult female pattern.** Persistent acne beyond age 25, often jawline-dominant, often with thinning hair or cycle irregularity. Estrogen dominance is usually deep, frequently entangled with hormonal birth control, hypothyroidism, or [[pcos]] biology. This pattern needs the full estrogen-suppression stack, not just topical work.

**Male androgenic.** T-zone-dominant, often associated with stress flares, sleep deficit, or sustained cortisol elevation. Zinc, sleep, and stress reduction lead. The dermatology reflex is to suppress androgens; the Peat reflex is to ask why cortisol and adrenal androgens are running high — usually thyroid and food.

The protocol below is the spine; each pattern emphasizes different sections.

## The Daily Core

Every pattern starts here.

**[[anti-stress-diet]] baseline.** Acne diets that work have one thing in common: they don't starve. Skin needs glucose for differentiation and steroid synthesis; under fasting or low-carb conditions, cortisol and free fatty acids rise, prostaglandin inflammation worsens, and acne flares before it improves. The frame is two quarts of milk a day for calcium and tryptophan-buffered protein, a quart of orange juice for potassium and naringenin's anti-aromatase effect, ripe sweet fruit, well-cooked low-fiber starches, gelatinous meats, salt to taste, and sugar in clean carriers (orange juice, ripe fruit, milk) rather than donuts or fried pastries that pair sugar with PUFA.

**Daily aspirin, 81–162 mg with food.** Aspirin is the systemic anti-prostaglandin lever — see [[aspirin]] for the full case. Peat used aspirin both internally and topically for acne inflammation. Take with [[vitamin-k|vitamin K2]] (1–5 mg) to offset the platelet effect on collagen quality.

**[[vitamin-a]] 10,000–30,000 IU daily, primarily from food.** This is the most important nutrient for acne in Peat's framework. From the [[acne|acne email]]: "Vitamin A affects the differentiation of skin cells, the production of steroids, and resistance to infection and inflammation. The great increase in formation of the sex steroids at puberty increases the need for vitamin A, and makes its regulatory actions more important." Peat himself, during a high-metabolic phase in bright light, ran 100,000 IU/day to prevent acne and ingrown whiskers. Most adults need 10,000–30,000 IU daily during a flare; food-based is preferred. Sources: liver (3 oz beef liver once or twice weekly delivers ~30,000–40,000 IU per serving), egg yolks, butter, cream, cheese. Small supplemental retinol (Nutrisorb-A drops were Peat's named topical) bridges. Vitamin A must be balanced with [[thyroid]] — excess vitamin A without thyroid suppresses thyroid function. Vitamin E (50–100 IU/day) protects vitamin A from oxidation.

**[[zinc]] 15–30 mg daily, with food.** Zinc is essential for skin immunity, sebum chemistry, and 5-alpha reductase modulation. From the [[acne|acne email]]: "Both vitamin A and zinc are essential in the right amount for good skin health, but too much of either can disturb the immune function." Food zinc (oysters, beef, lamb) is better tolerated than supplements; if supplementing, pick zinc picolinate or zinc carnosine and stay under 30 mg unless cystic. Oysters once weekly cover most adults.

**[[vitamin-d]] 2,000–5,000 IU daily + [[vitamin-k|K2]] 1–5 mg.** Vitamin D supports skin immune function and steroidogenesis. Most adults are deficient; aim for serum 25(OH)D of 50–80 ng/mL.

**[[magnesium]] 200–400 mg daily.** Magnesium glycinate or magnesium bicarbonate water. Cortisol-reducing and thyroid-supporting.

**[[pufa-depletion]] — non-negotiable.** Eliminate seed oils (soybean, corn, canola, sunflower, safflower, cottonseed, grapeseed, rice bran), fish oil supplements, daily nut consumption, and fried-food restaurant exposure. The dietary substrate for inflammatory prostaglandins is PUFA. Circulating free fatty acids drop within two weeks; tissue stores take roughly four years to deplete substantially — see [[pufa-depletion]]. Cooking fats: butter, ghee, coconut oil, beef tallow.

**Minimize chocolate and nuts.** Both deliver PUFA and iron in problematic ratios; chocolate adds caffeine-paired-with-fat and theobromine that perturbs steroid metabolism. Occasional small amounts are fine; daily intake is not.

## Adult Female Pattern Emphasis

Acne persisting into the late twenties, thirties, or forties in women is almost always estrogen-dominant, often thyroid-suppressed, and frequently complicated by hormonal birth control. The full [[estrogen-detox]] protocol applies. Specific additions for the acne case:

**[[progest-e|Progest-E]] topical, 10–30 mg cycle-coordinated.** In a regularly cycling woman, days 14–28 of the cycle. In an irregular cycle or perimenopause, three weeks on / one week off. Application can be to the affected jawline and chin areas; transdermal absorption is real. Peat on the contraceptive-acne connection from the [[acne|acne email]]: "Estrogen causes the oil glands to atrophy, so the skin doesn't support bacterial growth so well." The contraceptive estrogen "fix" for acne trades a skin problem for systemic estrogen dominance — progesterone gets the same downstream calm without the cost.

**Thyroid optimization.** Morning oral temperature below 98.0°F, afternoon below 98.6°F, resting pulse below 75 — these are the lab-cheap indicators that thyroid is part of the acne picture. Cyronine (T3) or Cynoplus (T4/T3) per the [[thyroid-dosing]] guide. From the [[acne|acne email]]: "Thyroid's important functions for the skin are the production of steroids and preventing their imbalance, and maintaining the immune function and production of sebum." Acne and dandruff were Peat's own clinical signal for needing more thyroid.

**Eliminate hormonal birth control where possible.** The reflexive prescription of combined oral contraceptives for acne deepens estrogen dominance, suppresses endogenous progesterone production, and creates a rebound flare on discontinuation. Non-hormonal alternatives exist (copper IUD has its own issues — see [[copper]] — barrier methods, fertility awareness). The post-pill acne flare is real and routes back through this same protocol; six months is the typical timeline.

**Reduce raw vegetable burden.** Indigestible plant fibers feed gut endotoxin, which routes through the liver and elevates estrogen reabsorption. The well-cooked carrot salad (raw, daily) is an exception — its specific fibers bind estrogen for elimination without feeding fermentation.

## Male Androgenic Pattern

The dermatology reflex is spironolactone or finasteride; Peat's reflex is to investigate the upstream conditions producing the androgen-flare pattern.

**Zinc 30–50 mg daily, food-prioritized.** Higher end of the daily-core range. Oysters twice weekly if tolerated.

**Sleep priority.** Cortisol-driven adrenal androgen overflow is the most common driver of male adult acne; broken sleep keeps cortisol elevated, suppresses thyroid, and elevates inflammatory tone. See [[sleep-restoration]]. Eat-before-bed (a small starch-plus-salt-plus-fat snack) prevents the 3 AM cortisol surge that wakes most men with stress-pattern acne.

**Saw palmetto skepticism.** Saw palmetto and other "natural" 5-alpha reductase inhibitors share the mechanism of finasteride at a milder dose. The mechanism itself is the wrong end of the stick — see [[hair-loss-protocol]] for the longer argument. DHT is not the problem; the inflammatory milieu in which DHT acts is the problem. Treat the milieu.

**Stress reduction is concrete, not vague.** Cortisol comes down when blood sugar stays up (frequent meals, no skipping breakfast, no caffeine on an empty stomach), when thyroid is adequate, when sleep is protected, and when [[pregnenolone]] (50–100 mg/day) is available as raw material for the steroidogenic cascade. "Stress reduction" without these specifics is theater.

## Severe Cystic Pattern

Deep nodules, scarring risk, and pain push the protocol into clinician-coordinated territory.

**Tetracycline or doxycycline short-course.** 50–100 mg daily for 2–3 months, coordinated with a physician. The rationale, from the [[acne|acne email]]: tetracyclines are taken internally "for their antiinflammatory effect." They are simultaneously antibacterial against *Cutibacterium acnes*, anti-inflammatory at sub-antimicrobial doses, and PUFA-microflora-reducing in the gut. The same email: "Changing the diet while using an antibiotic can make the effect permanent." This is the critical pairing — the antibiotic creates the window; the diet and vitamin A close it. Antibiotic monotherapy without diet change yields rebound when the course ends. Doxycycline 50 mg daily is the common low-dose anti-inflammatory schedule; minocycline carries higher pigmentation and autoimmune risks. Probiotic timing (apart from the antibiotic dose) and a few months of post-course gut rebuilding (gelatin, well-cooked starches, fruits) are part of the workflow.

**Topical aspirin solution.** Dissolve a 325 mg aspirin tablet in 1–2 tablespoons of water; apply with a cotton round to active inflammatory lesions twice daily. Peat: "A solution of aspirin in water on the skin helps with the inflammation, and is mildly germicidal" ([[acne|acne email]]). Higher concentration: dissolve aspirin in vodka or witch hazel for better skin penetration. The salicylate is the active.

**Topical tetracycline cream or neomycin lotion.** Peat's named easy topical: "I think the easiest thing would be an over-the-counter neomycin lotion" ([[acne|acne email]]). Compounded tetracycline cream from a pharmacy is the prescribed version. Apply nightly to active areas during the acute phase.

**10% sulfur soap or sulfur slurry.** Precipitated or sublimed sulfur mixed into a slurry with water and patted onto affected areas. Peat: "10% sulfur soap leaves an antiseptic residue on the skin that can prevent infection. I think it would be good to try the sulfur soap first" ([[acne|acne email]]). Adding dissolved aspirin to a sulfur slurry compounds the antiseptic-plus-anti-inflammatory effect. The match-smell is normal; a strong hydrogen-sulfide stink indicates contamination.

## Topical Care

The default skincare aisle works against the protocol. The Peat skincare frame is minimalist and barrier-protective.

**Gentle cleansing.** Avoid daily benzoyl peroxide — Peat: "I suspect that it will age the skin" ([[acne|acne email]]). Avoid harsh foaming cleansers that strip sebum; the rebound oil production that follows is part of the acne loop. A mild soap or non-foaming cleanser once or twice daily is plenty. 10% sulfur soap on flares is the targeted antimicrobial.

**Retinoids — caution on chronic use.** Peat was skeptical of long-term prescription retinoid topicals; they thin the stratum corneum, disrupt barrier function, and create photo-sensitivity. Acute use during a flare is reasonable; daily-for-years use trades acne for accelerated photo-aging. Whole-food vitamin A from liver does the same differentiation work systemically with the offsetting steroid and thyroid support.

**Coconut oil with small vitamin E.** As a barrier moisturizer, coconut oil (mostly saturated, lauric acid antimicrobial) is one of the few "natural" oils that doesn't deliver PUFA to the skin. A few drops of tocopherol mixed in protects against oxidation. Avoid argan, rosehip, almond, jojoba and most "facial oils" — they're PUFA-heavy.

**Salt water rinses.** A mild saline rinse (½ tsp salt to 1 cup warm water) is gentle, antimicrobial, and barrier-friendly. Useful for back acne in shower.

**Sun exposure.** See [[morning-sunlight]]. Bright light stimulates steroidogenesis in skin and clears mild acne — Peat: "Bright light stimulates the production of steroids, and consumes vitamin A very quickly, but when the balance is right, the acne clears up in just a day or two" ([[vitamin-a]] research file). Twenty to thirty minutes of unprotected morning or late-afternoon sun on the affected area; avoid midday burn. Sunscreen daily-everywhere is part of the photo-aging-PUFA-oxidation problem, not the solution.

**Minimize harsh actives.** Glycolic acid, salicylic acid peels, retinol-plus-acid stacks, daily exfoliation — all disrupt the barrier. Acne improves on these for two months, then rebounds harder when the barrier collapse catches up.

## The Accutane Question

Isotretinoin (Accutane, Roaccutane) is the dermatology nuclear option: 5–10 months of high-dose synthetic 13-cis-retinoic acid that shrinks sebaceous glands, often permanently. The remission rate is real (60–80% lasting clearance). The cost profile is real too. Peat's skepticism, distilled:

**The hepatotoxic and lipid effects are vitamin A overdose effects.** Synthetic retinoids share the toxicity mechanism of high-dose retinyl palmitate: they suppress thyroid (see [[thyroid]] interactions with vitamin A), elevate triglycerides, and oxidize easily without sufficient [[vitamin-e]]. The whole-food alternative — liver weekly, milk daily, vitamin E 50–100 IU — delivers vitamin A in the matrix the liver evolved to handle.

**Mood and depression risk.** The post-marketing signal of isotretinoin-associated depression and suicidality is contested in the literature, but the mechanism is plausible: retinoic acid affects hippocampal neurogenesis and serotonin tone. Peat's broader framework on [[serotonin]] inflammation reinforces caution about anything that perturbs the serotonin axis chronically.

**Long-term hair, skin, and joint effects.** Persistent dryness, joint pain, accelerated photoaging, telogen effluvium, and sebum-gland suppression that doesn't fully recover are documented in long-term followup cohorts.

**The Peat alternative.** Liver once or twice weekly (3–4 oz beef or chicken liver). Whole egg yolks daily. Butter and cream from grass-fed sources. Thyroid optimization. Vitamin E to prevent vitamin A oxidation. The clearance is slower (6 months vs. 4) and the discipline is harder (diet adherence vs. a pill), but the systemic profile is repair-oriented rather than suppression-oriented.

**Reserved for clinician-required cases.** Severe scarring trajectory in adolescents, profound psychosocial impairment, failure of the full systemic protocol after 6 months — these are the cases where isotretinoin earns its place. The decision belongs to patient and dermatologist; this protocol's frame is that the question should be asked, not assumed.

## Dietary Triggers

The acne-diet literature has converged on several drivers; the Peat frame reads each one through metabolic and inflammatory mechanisms rather than glycemic-index reductionism.

**Excess PUFA in any carrier.** Restaurant fryer oil, salad dressings, mayonnaise, "heart-healthy" margarines, daily nut consumption. The mechanism is downstream inflammatory prostaglandin synthesis from arachidonic-acid precursors.

**Chocolate and nuts.** Both PUFA-loaded; chocolate adds caffeine-paired-with-fat and theobromine that perturbs sleep architecture and steroid metabolism. Cacao nibs and dark chocolate are not the exception they're marketed as.

**Low-quality dairy from BST-treated cows.** Recombinant bovine somatotropin (rBST/rBGH) elevates IGF-1 in milk and shifts the milk hormonal profile. Conventional skim milk from BST-treated herds is the dairy-acne signal in epidemiology. Whole milk from non-BST herds (most US organic milk, all European milk, all A2-only producers) is the form Peat consistently endorsed. Two quarts daily of clean milk is calcium-rich, tryptophan-buffered, and reduces parathyroid hormone — none of which drives acne.

**Sugar in PUFA carrier.** Donuts, pastries, fried sweets, ice cream with seed-oil emulsifiers — sugar paired with PUFA produces the spike-plus-inflammation combination that gets blamed on sugar alone. Sugar in a clean carrier (orange juice with salt, ripe fruit, milk, honey-sweetened gelatin) is different physiologically.

**Excess raw cruciferous vegetables.** Goitrogenic load suppresses thyroid in susceptible people; without thyroid, vitamin A can't do its skin work. The well-cooked carrot salad is the exception.

**Coffee on an empty stomach.** Routine cortisol-elevating habit. Coffee with cream and sugar, after food, is the Peat formulation.

## Phase 1: Weeks 1–8 — Core Stack

Execute the daily core in full. Topical work matches the pattern (sulfur soap or aspirin solution for moderate-inflammatory; tetracycline coordination begins this phase for severe cystic). Expect a mild flare in week one — gut and skin rebalance, dormant comedones surface and clear. Do not abandon the protocol in week one; this is the most common failure point.

By week four: oil production should noticeably decrease; new comedone formation slows; redness on existing lesions begins to fade. By week eight: lesion count down 40–60% from baseline in mild and moderate cases; cystic cases on antibiotic coordination should show new-cyst suppression.

Track morning temperature and resting pulse weekly. If temperature is still below 98.0°F at week four, thyroid dosing needs attention — see [[thyroid-dosing]].

## Phase 2: Months 2–6 — Track and Adjust

Adjust vitamin A toward the lower end of the range (10,000–15,000 IU) if calluses on palms or soles begin to yellow with carotene — Peat's sign that tissue saturation is high. Maintain zinc at 15–30 mg. Aspirin daily continues. PUFA avoidance becomes habit, not effort.

Women: track flare timing against cycle phase; if luteal-phase pattern persists, increase [[progest-e|Progest-E]] from 10 mg to 20–30 mg in the affected week. Reevaluate estrogen exposure — birth control, soy, conventional skim milk, body fat percentage.

Cystic cases: complete the tetracycline course; do not extend beyond 3 months without clinician input. Begin gut rebuilding (gelatin daily, well-cooked starches, ripe fruits, a couple of weeks of activated charcoal for endotoxin if bowel function lags). Topical work tapers as new-lesion formation drops.

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (the brown marks that persist after lesions clear) responds to time, vitamin A (cellular turnover), morning sun (steroidogenesis), and avoidance of fresh damage. The 6-month horizon for visible fading is realistic; year-long timeline for full clearance in darker skin types.

## Phase 3: Maintenance — Minimal Effective Stack

Once clear:

- **Liver once or twice weekly.** The food source of vitamin A in a thyroid-compatible matrix.
- **Whole eggs daily, milk two quarts daily, orange juice one quart daily.** The dietary backbone.
- **PUFA avoidance continues — for life.** Acne is the early warning signal of inflammatory tone the rest of the body shares. The skin is loudest about it; the heart, the brain, and the liver are quieter.
- **Zinc from food (oysters weekly, beef regularly).** Supplements unnecessary in maintenance.
- **Aspirin daily at low dose** if cardiovascular or inflammatory case warrants; optional if acne was the only signal.
- **Sun, sleep, salt to taste.** Cheap, foundational.
- **Topical interventions retire.** A simple cleanser, occasional sulfur soap for emerging flares, coconut oil as moisturizer.

Most maintenance failures trace to one of three: PUFA crept back in (restaurant meals, "just one nut handful"), thyroid dosing dropped under stress, or estrogen exposure resumed (new birth control, new soy habit, new low-fat conventional dairy).

## Tracking Signals

**Lesion count weekly.** Simple count of active inflammatory lesions (papules, pustules, nodules) on face. Photograph weekly under consistent lighting.

**Severity.** Inflammatory grade — mild redness, deep painful nodules, drainage. Cystic-flare frequency is the cleanest single signal.

**Post-inflammatory pigmentation healing.** The fade rate of brown marks left by previous lesions. Slow fade indicates persistent inflammation upstream; rapid fade means the protocol is working.

**Oil production.** T-zone shine by mid-afternoon. Excess oil at month two and beyond means PUFA load or vitamin A is still off; chronic dryness means topical work is too aggressive or vitamin A is too high.

**Morning temperature and resting pulse.** The thyroid surrogate. Temperature climbing toward 98.0°F by month two is part of the signal that the systemic work is landing.

**Mood and sleep.** Stress-driven acne is bidirectional — cortisol drives acne and acne drives cortisol. Improved sleep depth and mood often precede skin clearing by two to four weeks.

**Menstrual cycle regularity (women).** Cycle length normalizing toward 28 days, mid-cycle ovulation pain (mittelschmerz) returning, less PMS — these are downstream signals that the estrogen-progesterone work is moving.

## Failure Modes

**Over-cleansing and topical aggression.** Twice-daily peroxide, daily glycolic acid, retinol stacking. Two months of apparent improvement followed by barrier collapse, rebound oil production, and a worse acne baseline than starting. The skin's barrier is the immune system's first line; stripping it is not "deep cleaning."

**Spot-treating without addressing systemic.** Treating individual lesions with topicals while the diet, thyroid, and estrogen drivers remain untouched. Results plateau by week four; the lesion location may shift but the rate of new lesions doesn't drop.

**Ignoring gut and estrogen drivers in adult women.** The dermatology default for adult female acne is spironolactone or low-dose oral contraceptive. Both move a downstream lever without addressing why estrogen is high to begin with. The gut-liver-estrogen axis is the upstream picture — see [[estrogen-detox]].

**Diet half-measures.** Eliminating obvious junk while continuing daily nuts, weekly chocolate, regular restaurant fried foods. The PUFA exposure is the rate-limiting step in inflammatory acne; partial compliance produces partial results that look like the protocol failing rather than the diet not being executed.

**Vitamin A without thyroid.** Pushing vitamin A to 30,000+ IU without [[thyroid]] support creates the antithyroid effect Peat warned about — fatigue, cold extremities, dry skin, paradoxical persistence of acne. Vitamin A and thyroid are paired levers; running one without the other produces predictable failure.

**Quitting the antibiotic course at month two without diet change.** The acne returns within four to eight weeks of stopping. The diet change is what makes the antibiotic effect lasting — Peat: "Changing the diet while using an antibiotic can make the effect permanent" ([[acne|acne email]]).

**Treating teenage acne as inevitable.** Puberty raises vitamin A and thyroid demand — see the [[acne|acne email]]: "The great increase in formation of the sex steroids at puberty increases the need for vitamin A." The fact that most teenagers have some acne does not mean nothing can be done; it means the demand curve is steeper. The protocol works in adolescents with parental compliance on the diet side.

## When to Escalate

**Severe scarring trajectory.** Deep nodules, ice-pick scars forming, visible textural change before the protocol has had its 6-month window. Dermatologist involvement for short-course oral antibiotic, possibly isotretinoin, against the cost calculus above.

**Suicidal ideation flare.** Acne severity correlates with depression in observational data, and the relationship is bidirectional. If mood deteriorates significantly during a flare or during isotretinoin treatment, this is a clinical priority, not a "wait it out" situation.

**Persistent severe cystic.** No improvement after 3 months of full protocol including antibiotic coordination. Dermatologist consultation for second-line options; reassessment for underlying [[pcos]], [[hypothyroidism]], or insulin-resistance contribution.

**Sudden adult-onset acne in a previously clear adult.** Especially if accompanied by hirsutism, cycle disruption, or weight redistribution — workup for [[pcos]], adrenal hyperplasia, or hormonal medication side effects.

**Failure of progesterone in clear hormonal-cyclical pattern.** If 3 cycles of [[progest-e|Progest-E]] at adequate dose don't change the luteal-phase flare, deeper estrogen-detoxification investigation is warranted (gut function, liver detoxification capacity, body fat as estrogen reservoir).
