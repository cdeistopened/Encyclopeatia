# Vegetables Ranked

The biggest inversion in Ray Peat's framework is this: vegetables are not the center of a healthy diet. Mainstream nutrition treats "eat your vegetables" as the one piece of advice too obvious to question, the floor beneath every other debate about fat, protein, and carbohydrates. Peat questioned it. When he listed the foods he considered genuinely safe, he named milk, cheese, eggs, low-starch fruit, meat, fish, shellfish, gelatin, coconut oil, butter, honey, and orange juice. Vegetables appeared as an afterthought, hedged with warnings, and only a handful of them were recommended at all. Most of what the produce aisle calls vegetables are goitrogenic, estrogenic, oxalate-loaded, or endotoxin-producing. Some are all four.

The few Peat actively endorsed were highly specific and served precise therapeutic functions. The raw carrot, eaten daily as a shredded salad, sterilizes the intestine and lowers estrogen. Well-cooked white button mushrooms combine antimicrobial action with aromatase inhibition. Boiled bamboo shoots provide antiseptic fiber that survives cooking. Cooked greens, consumed sparingly, can yield magnesium when the cooking water does the extraction. Nothing else on the produce shelf belongs in the daily rotation of a pro-metabolic diet, and a great deal of it belongs nowhere near it.

The deeper problem underneath the rankings below is the fiber dogma. Mainstream nutrition treats "fiber" as a single monolithic good, prescribing 25 to 35 grams per day without distinguishing between the kinds. Peat drew the distinction Lawrence the dietitian cannot: fermentable fiber feeds the gram-negative bacteria whose [[endotoxin]] leaks through the intestinal wall, overwhelms the liver, and drives the cascade of estrogen, serotonin, cortisol, and inflammation at the center of his disease model. Most plant fiber is fermentable. The carrot is the hero exception because its fiber uniquely resists bacterial digestion, which is why it binds estrogen and endotoxin in the gut without feeding the organisms that produce them. Understanding that distinction is the difference between a produce aisle that protects you and one that slowly poisons you. See [[endotoxin]], [[carrot-salad]], [[xenoestrogens]].

## The Raw Carrot: The Exception That Proves the Rule

Before the ranking table, the raw carrot deserves its own callout, because it is the only vegetable Peat prescribed as a daily staple and because understanding why reframes everything else below it.

One medium raw carrot per day, shredded lengthwise to maximize surface area, tossed with coconut oil or olive oil, a splash of vinegar, and salt. Peat ate this every day for over twenty years and credited it with keeping his intestine healthy across that span. Women who adopted the practice measured their hormone panels before and after and saw circulating estrogen drop within two or three days, with progesterone and thyroid hormone rising to fill the space. Infertile women who took an antibiotic showed the same shift. The mechanism is identical in both cases: clean the intestine, interrupt the enterohepatic recycling of estrogen, lower the endotoxin burden on the liver, and the endocrine system returns to a fertile pattern on its own.

What makes the raw carrot work is something no other common vegetable provides. Its fiber is uniquely non-fermentable. Almost no microorganism can multiply on raw carrot tissue, which is why carrots sit firm in a refrigerator while lettuce wilts into slime. The same germicidal defenses the carrot uses to survive in bacterial soil carry through the entire digestive tract, suppressing the bacteria that produce endotoxin while physically binding bile-excreted estrogen before it can be deconjugated and reabsorbed. Cooking destroys this property. A cooked carrot becomes bacterial food like any other starch, which is why Peat was specific that the carrot had to be raw to do its work.

This is the carrot salad protocol, and it is the single most important vegetable recommendation in the entire Peat framework. See [[carrot-salad]] for the full preparation and the research history behind it.

## The Cooking Rule

Almost every vegetable worth eating needs cooking to be tolerable. Cooking deactivates goitrogens, breaks down some oxalates, denatures lectins, and partially degrades phytic acid. The raw-food movement of the twentieth century inverted this. Traditional diets across the world ate vegetables cooked, fermented, dried, or not at all. The pattern of large daily raw salads as the centerpiece of a meal is a recent invention with no historical precedent in healthy populations.

The exceptions are narrow. The raw carrot must be raw because cooking destroys its uniquely non-fermentable fiber and turns it into bacterial food. Ripe tomato and cucumber are low enough in antinutrients that raw consumption is harmless if small. Everything else in the green and cruciferous categories benefits from heat, and the boiling-and-draining protocol Peat described for greens is more effective than steaming or sauteing because the water-soluble antinutrients leach into the discarded liquid.

The corollary is that cooking method matters as much as cooking at all. Boiling kale and pouring off the water extracts magnesium while discarding goitrogens, oxalates, tannins, and [[pufa]]. Sauteing the same kale traps all of those compounds in the leaf and in the pan oil. Steaming falls between the two. When Peat spoke of greens as "good magnesium supplements," he meant the broth, not the leaves.

## The Fiber Question

The mainstream dogma that "fiber is good for you" is one of the positions Peat inverted most completely. His argument was not that fiber is universally bad but that the word "fiber" is doing dangerous work by conflating two opposite categories.

Fermentable fiber, the kind in beans, bran, oat flakes, chicory root, and most raw leafy greens, is bacterial food. It arrives in the lower intestine intact, provides substrate for gram-negative bacterial overgrowth, and drives the production of endotoxin, serotonin, histamine, and other inflammatory mediators. The industry markets these fibers under friendly names (prebiotic, resistant starch, inulin) and treats the gas they produce as evidence of "healthy gut flora." In Peat's framework the gas is evidence of a fermenting bowel, and the downstream consequences run through hormonal disruption, mood disorders, and degenerative disease.

Non-fermentable fiber is the opposite. Raw carrot fiber, boiled bamboo shoot fiber, and the fibrous material in well-cooked mushrooms all resist bacterial digestion, pass through the intestine intact, and bind bile acids, estrogen, and endotoxin along the way. They function as gentle mechanical laxatives and as binding agents, not as bacterial fuel. This is why Peat could simultaneously warn against high-fiber diets and recommend a daily raw carrot. The two recommendations only contradict each other if "fiber" is treated as one thing.

Psyllium, carrageenan, guar gum, pectins, and most of the fiber additives in modern processed food fall on the fermentable side. Peat specifically named several of these as intestinal carcinogens in his writing on endotoxin. The health halo around fiber supplements is a case study in how an undifferentiated category can turn a genuine nutritional distinction into a marketing slogan, and the slogan moves product in both directions regardless of which fiber the customer needs. See [[endotoxin]].
