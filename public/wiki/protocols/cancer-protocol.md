# Cancer Protocol

This is the action layer for the framework laid out on [[cancer]]. The mechanism — cancer as the local terminal expression of an organism-wide respiratory and hormonal failure, driven by PUFA-cardiolipin substitution, estrogen, iron, endotoxin, and stiffened collagen — lives upstream. The companion articles on the hormonal axis ([[estrogen-and-cancer]]) and the case against standard oncology ([[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]) carry the political-history and treatment-critique loads. What follows is the protocol the Peat-frame patient actually runs, in three phases, with the dose ranges Peat used in correspondence and the explicit decision tree for when conventional treatment is the right call.

**This is not a cancer cure and it is not a substitute for oncology.** The Peat framework is adjunctive, preventive, and recurrence-reducing; it is not a replacement for surgical resection of solid tumors, for the treatment of acute leukemias and aggressive lymphomas, or for emergencies where the natural history without intervention is rapid death. Everything below is intended to be coordinated with an oncologist. The protocol's value lies in the three lanes mainstream oncology serves poorly: prevention in a high-risk patient, metabolic optimization alongside conventional treatment, and recurrence reduction in the years after a remission. Nothing here is individual medical advice. The choices about screening, biopsy, surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy are the patient's to make with a clinician who knows the case.

## Diagnosis prerequisites: which protocol are you running

Before reading further, name the situation. The phases below are not interchangeable. A patient running the Phase 1 prevention protocol against a known high-grade tumor is under-treating. A patient on the Phase 2 active-cancer protocol who has no diagnosis is over-treating.

1. **No diagnosis, elevated risk.** Family history (BRCA carrier, first-degree relatives with the same cancer, Lynch syndrome), known carcinogen exposure (smoker, occupational, atomic-veteran cohort), long-term HRT user, elevated breast density on screening, elevated PSA without confirmed cancer, polycystic-ovary-syndrome with anovulation. Run Phase 1.
2. **Acknowledged cancer, in active treatment.** Biopsy-confirmed solid tumor, currently in surgery / radiation / chemotherapy / immunotherapy / endocrine-therapy phase under oncology supervision. The protocol runs *alongside* conventional treatment, not instead of it, with the patient's oncologist informed of every supplement and hormone. Run Phase 2.
3. **Cancer survivor, off active treatment.** Completed surgery / radiation / chemotherapy / endocrine therapy; in surveillance phase; clinically free of disease but at recurrence risk. The dormancy literature ([[cancer]]) is the relevant frame. Run Phase 3.
4. **Acknowledged cancer, declining or delaying conventional treatment.** Early-stage low-grade prostate cancer on watchful waiting, low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ, indolent lymphoma, advanced disease where conventional treatment has failed or been refused. This is the highest-stakes situation in the protocol. Read the alongside-vs-instead-of decision tree section before proceeding. Run Phase 2 with the explicit acknowledgment that the protocol is what you are relying on.

The cancer type matters. Estrogen-dependent cancers (breast, endometrial, ovarian, much of prostate) are the cleanest fit for the framework; colorectal and hepatic also fit well; the leukemias, lymphomas, pediatric cancers, and rare aggressive cancers (small-cell lung, sarcomas, certain brain cancers) are the framework's weak spots. The cancer-type subsections below cover breast, prostate, and ovarian in detail.

## Phase 1 — Pre-cancer prevention and family-history patients

The highest-leverage phase. Every intervention is a [[core-diet-protocol|core-diet]] / [[pufa-depletion|PUFA-depletion]] / [[endotoxin-reduction|gut-decontamination]] intervention applied with cancer-specific priorities. The aim is to keep the metabolic field — respiration, hormonal balance, gut barrier, antioxidant status — well outside the range in which dormant cells break dormancy.

**Diet.** Run the full [[core-diet-protocol]]. The cancer-relevant priorities, briefly: eliminate seed and vegetable oils absolutely (corn, soy, safflower, sunflower, canola, grapeseed, peanut, fish oil) — the [[pufa]] load is the structural carcinogen and Peat's 1927 Bernstein-Elias citation ("rats eating a fat free diet had almost no spontaneous cancer," [[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy* (2013)]]) is the foundational dietary observation. Replace cooking fats with butter, ghee, beef tallow, and coconut oil. Two quarts of low-fat milk daily for the calcium floor (suppresses parathyroid hormone, which is pro-aromatase and pro-fibrotic). A quart of orange juice daily for the potassium, magnesium, naringenin, and hesperidin (mild aromatase inhibition). Salt to appetite. Daily gelatin (broth, gelatin tea, or unflavored gelatin in juice — 10 to 20 g/day) to shift the protein mix toward glycine and away from tryptophan and methionine. A daily raw carrot for the gut and estrogen-clearance benefit (see the cascara / emodin / bowel-toxin section below). No restaurant food where seed-oil exposure is uncontrolled. No carbohydrate restriction. No prolonged fasting.

**PUFA depletion.** This is a multi-year project. PUFA stored in adipose tissue has a half-life of two to four years; structural PUFA in cardiolipin turns over more slowly still. Aggressive PUFA mobilization (rapid weight loss, prolonged exercise without adequate carbohydrate, ketogenic dieting) liberates stored PUFA into circulation where it can be peroxidized faster than the tissue can replace it. The depletion strategy in this phase is slow substitution, not mobilization. Detail at [[pufa-depletion]].

**Thyroid baseline.** Run [[temperature-tracking]] and morning pulse measurement. Targets: morning oral temperature trending toward 98.0–98.6 °F, resting pulse 75–85, TSH below 1.5 (ideally close to 1.0), free T3 in the upper third of the reference range, reverse T3 low. If labs and symptoms point to subclinical hypothyroidism, the entry is the [[hypothyroid-recovery]] protocol: six weeks of dietary correction first, then desiccated thyroid at one-quarter to one-half grain (15–30 mg) with the largest meal, titrated by 15 mg every one to two weeks. Most prevention candidates land at one to two grains per day at steady state. Peat's standing recommendation is desiccated NDT (Armour, NP Thyroid, Nature-Throid) or a T4/T3 combination over T4 alone — T4-only therapy depends on hepatic conversion that the same estrogen-PUFA-iron picture impairs. See [[thyroid-dosing]].

**Aspirin.** 325 to 650 mg daily with food, taken in solution if gastric irritation is an issue (dissolved in water or seltzer, ideally with a small amount of milk or food). The colon-cancer-aspirin literature gives mainstream epidemiology's strongest single endorsement of the framework: daily aspirin reduces colorectal-cancer incidence by roughly half in long-running cohorts. Peat's case in [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|*Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer* (2006)]] is broader than the antithrombotic effect — aspirin inhibits aromatase and the prostaglandin cascade, suppresses free-fatty-acid release, stabilizes mitochondria, and antagonizes the lactate-HIF feedback. Co-supplement [[vitamin-k|vitamin K2]] (MK-4 1 mg/day or MK-7 100 µg/day) to offset chronic coagulation effects. Start at the low end and titrate up; stop and reassess if there is GI bleeding, bruising, or melena.

**Vitamin E.** 100 to 400 IU per day with a fat-containing meal. Mixed-tocopherol preparation, not synthetic dl-alpha-tocopherol alone. Vitamin E protects stored PUFA from peroxidation during the slow tissue turnover; Peat described it in [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|*Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer*]] as having been "defined as a brain-protective, pregnancy protective, male fertility protective, antithrombotic, antiestrogenic agent" before the estrogen industry repositioned it as a generic "antioxidant." It also suppresses COX-2, synergizing with aspirin and opposing estrogen.

**Niacinamide.** 100 to 500 mg, two to three times per day with meals. Use the amide form, not niacin (which flushes and at high doses is hepatotoxic). The mechanism is free-fatty-acid suppression — niacinamide inhibits lipolysis from adipose tissue, lowering the PUFA load on the circulation, which matters most when tissue PUFA reserves are being slowly depleted ([[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]]: "Niacinamide inhibits lipolysis"). It also restores NAD+ supply to the respiratory chain.

**Vitamin D.** 4,000 to 5,000 IU per day in the morning with a fat-containing meal in winter, sunlight in summer. Target serum 25-OH-D 50–80 ng/mL. Vitamin D dampens the inflammatory cascade endotoxin drives, supports local steroidogenesis, and is independently associated with lower cancer incidence in cohort studies.

**Progesterone in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.** 10 to 30 mg/day topical or sublingual ([[progest-e]] or equivalent vitamin-E-carried bioidentical) is the prevention-phase dose. Cycle by calendar in still-cycling women (luteal phase, days 14–28); continuous in postmenopausal women. The case for progesterone-as-prevention is in [[estrogen-and-cancer]]; the dose range is the same as the [[menopause-protocol]]. Synthetic progestins (medroxyprogesterone in particular) are the wrong molecule and Peat consistently distinguished them from bioidentical progesterone.

**Iron control.** Adult men donate blood every three to four months from age forty. Premenopausal women generally do not need iron-restriction interventions beyond avoiding fortified cereals and iron-supplement multivitamins. Target ferritin 30–70 ng/mL; ferritin above 100 is a warning sign and above 200 is a red flag. The iron-mortality literature, repeatedly cited by Peat, gives this intervention some of the strongest evidence in the protocol.

**Light, sleep, warmth.** Morning sunlight to the eyes (not artificial blue light). Red and infrared light to the body if available (an incandescent bulb or a dedicated red-light panel). Warmth — particularly to the feet and abdomen. Adequate sleep with the room cool and the body warm. The cytochrome-oxidase support these provide is part of the metabolic restoration; the cortisol- and serotonin-suppression is part of the estrogen-and-stress-axis correction.

**What to avoid in prevention.** Estrogen replacement (the WHI literature is decisive — see [[estrogen-and-cancer]]). Statins (the Mendelian-randomization case applies in selected high-risk patients but the routine statin in a metabolically healthy person depletes [[coenzyme-q10|CoQ10]] and mevalonate-pathway substrates the framework relies on; see [[heart-disease-protocol]] for the statin renegotiation conversation). Long courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics without restoring the gut. SSRIs (serotonergic, pro-fibrotic, pro-cancer in the framework's reading). Iron-fortified foods. Soy in volume (estrogenic). Routine mammography in dense-breasted premenopausal women (the over-diagnosis and radiation-exposure case is in [[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]; this is a contested recommendation and the patient should hear both sides). Fish oil supplementation (concentrated PUFA, failed in major trials).

## Phase 2 — Active-cancer adjunct

A patient with biopsy-confirmed cancer in active treatment runs the prevention protocol intensified, with cancer-specific additions, and coordinated with the oncology team. The aim is to *support the metabolic field around the conventional treatment* — improve tolerance, reduce treatment-related toxicity, suppress the lactate-prostaglandin loop the cancer field depends on, and reduce the recurrence risk. The framework does not treat conventional treatment as the enemy here; it treats the iatrogenic excess as the enemy.

**Diet during chemotherapy or radiation.** Sugar and protein up, not down. The "starve the tumor" instinct — ketogenic diets, prolonged fasting, calorie restriction during chemotherapy — is one of the most consequential errors a patient can make in this framework. Peat cited the Sauer-Dauchy and Schlappack literature ([[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]) showing that glucose deprivation and 2-deoxyglucose actively promote metastasis via matrix-metalloproteinase activation and stress-hormone elevation. "Adequate sugar and protein during chemotherapy" is the headline. Target 2,500–3,000+ kcal/day with sugar liberal (orange juice, ripe fruit, honey, milk, ice cream as needed), gelatin-rich protein 80–120 g/day, salt to appetite. Do not lose weight involuntarily. The cancer-cachexia state — weight loss, muscle wasting, weakness — is its own mortality driver and is partly driven by lactate, free fatty acids, and stress hormones the protocol aims to suppress. See [[core-diet-protocol]].

**Thyroid in active cancer.** If hypothyroidism is documented or the symptom-temperature-pulse picture supports it, replace under cardiology and oncology supervision. The case is direct: oxidative respiration is the framework's central anti-cancer goal, and thyroid hormone is the master metabolic regulator. The cautious entry (NDT 15–30 mg or T3 4 µg) titrated weekly is the same as Phase 1. The cancer-specific addition is that *aspirin can amplify T3 effects* — Peat noted in [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|*Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer*]] that "by activating T3, aspirin can sometimes increase the temperature and pulse rate." Track temperature and pulse daily through the dose-titration phase.

**Progesterone in active cancer.** 100 to 300 mg/day. This is the dose range Peat used in correspondence with cancer patients and the range supported by the in-vitro and observational literature ([[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone* (2007)]]). Delivery: sublingual or buccal Progest-E (vitamin-E-carried bioidentical progesterone, ~33 mg per drop in standard formulations) divided through the day; topical application directly to the breast in breast cancer is an option Peat discussed and the Plu-Bureau topical-progesterone breast-cancer data (50% risk reduction in topical-progesterone users vs nonusers, [[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone*]]) supports. The upper end (200–300 mg/day) is for active disease with high tumor burden; the lower end (100 mg/day) is for stable post-surgical disease. Drowsiness is the practical dose-limiting symptom in most patients, not toxicity. Coordinate with oncology — synthetic progestins (medroxyprogesterone, the molecule that drives the FDA's cancer warnings) are *not* bioidentical progesterone, and the protocol must use the bioidentical molecule. The hormonal-axis detail is in [[estrogen-and-cancer]].

**Aspirin in active cancer.** 300 to 1,500 mg/day, divided. Peat's case for the upper dose ranges is in [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|*Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer*]]: aspirin inactivates COX-1 and COX-2 (suppressing the prostaglandin cascade that activates aromatase and HIF), inhibits free-fatty-acid release, antagonizes the lactate-HIF feedback, and at higher doses has direct anti-tumor effects in vitro. "When people with cancer ask for my recommendations, they usually think I'm joking when I tell them to use aspirin... Several years ago, a woman whose doctors said it would be impossible to operate on her extremely painful 'inflammatory breast cancer' had overnight complete relief of the pain and swelling from taking a few aspirins" ([[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|*Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer*]]). Co-supplement vitamin K2 throughout. Dissolve aspirin in water with food to minimize gastric irritation; the stomach tolerates higher doses over time. If anticoagulation is already prescribed, the dose decision is the oncologist's. Stop at the first sign of GI bleeding, melena, easy bruising, or unexplained hematuria.

**Niacinamide.** 500 to 1,500 mg/day in three to four divided doses with meals. The active-cancer dose range is higher than prevention because the free-fatty-acid suppression matters more when stored PUFA is being mobilized by treatment-related stress. The amide form, not niacin. Liver labs should be checked every three to six months at doses above 1 g/day; hepatic toxicity is dose-dependent and reversible on discontinuation.

**Methylene blue.** Microdoses: 0.5 to 4 mg/day, taken with food, well diluted in water (the pharmaceutical-grade USP form, not the laboratory dye). The mechanism is electron-cycling — methylene blue accepts electrons from NADH and donates them to cytochrome c, partially bypassing the cytochrome-oxidase block that the cardiolipin-PUFA substitution causes. The detail is in [[methylene-blue]] and [[methylene-blue-protocol]]. **Contraindications:** methylene blue is a monoamine oxidase inhibitor. It cannot be combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, or MAOIs without serotonin-syndrome risk. The patient must be off serotonergic drugs for at least two weeks (six weeks for fluoxetine) before starting methylene blue. This is non-negotiable.

**DCA (dichloroacetate), where available.** DCA activates pyruvate dehydrogenase ([[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]]), shifting metabolism from glycolysis toward complete oxidation — a direct counter to the Warburg shift. The University of Alberta clinical trials in the 2000s established a reasonable safety profile and modest efficacy in glioblastoma and some other cancers; the regulatory environment in the US is hostile (off-label only, supply chain unreliable), the Canadian situation is more permissive. Typical dose 10–25 mg/kg/day divided; peripheral neuropathy is the dose-limiting side effect and is dose-dependent and reversible. Co-supplement thiamine 100 mg/day to mitigate neuropathy. DCA is a clinician-supervised intervention, not a self-administered supplement.

**Vitamin K2.** 1 mg/day MK-4 or 100 µg/day MK-7. Mechanism: aspirin-coagulation offset, vascular-calcification reduction, direct anti-cancer effects observed in hepatocellular carcinoma cohorts (the Japanese MK-4 hepatoma data is the strongest single human trial). The K2 also synergizes with vitamin D for calcium handling.

**Vitamin E.** 200 to 600 IU per day with a fat-containing meal. The active-cancer dose is higher than prevention because PUFA mobilization is higher. Mixed-tocopherol preparation.

**Pregnenolone and DHEA.** Pregnenolone 30 to 100 mg/day, divided. DHEA 5 to 25 mg/day in older men, 5 to 10 mg/day in women (start low; androgenic side effects in women cap the dose). Mechanism: upstream steroidogenic substrate that supports endogenous progesterone synthesis and antagonizes the cortisol-and-aldosterone axis the cancer field depends on. Pregnenolone is broadly safe; DHEA in older men requires aromatase monitoring (the conversion of androgen to estrogen in adipose tissue is what the protocol is trying to suppress) — co-administer aspirin and keep PUFA low.

**Caffeine.** Two to four cups of coffee daily with milk and sugar. Mechanism: serotonin antagonism, adenosine antagonism, anti-estrogenic effects, support of T4-to-T3 conversion, mild aromatase inhibition. From [[caffeine-a-vitamin-like-nutrient|*Caffeine: A Vitamin-Like Nutrient*]]: caffeine is one of the most consistently protective dietary constituents in cancer-incidence cohorts. The cancer-industry combination of caffeine with paclitaxel ([[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]]) is an oblique acknowledgment of the synergy.

**Bag breathing.** Two to five minutes, three to five times per day. Detail in the CO₂ section below.

**What to avoid during active treatment.** Opiate analgesia where possible (opiates raise prolactin, activate endorphin signaling that antagonizes progesterone, and are pro-tumor in the framework — see [[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone*]] on the naltrexone literature). SSRIs and serotonergic antidepressants. Iron supplementation (very common in chemotherapy-associated anemia; coordinate with oncology to use copper-supportive interventions and address the underlying erythropoietic stress instead). Long courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics without restoring the gut. Calorie restriction or fasting protocols. Ketogenic diets. PUFA-rich "anti-inflammatory" diets (the omega-3 marketing).

## Phase 3 — Post-treatment recovery and recurrence reduction

The lane in which the framework's contribution is most distinctive. The dormancy literature is the relevant frame: 36% of post-mastectomy women carry measurable circulating tumor cells seven to twenty-two years after surgery ([[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix*]]), held in dormancy by tissue conditions that do not support proliferation. Recurrence prevention is largely a field-management problem and the field-management interventions are the same ones used in prevention, applied with more discipline and sustained for years.

**Diet.** The full [[core-diet-protocol]], maintained indefinitely. No PUFA. No restaurant cooking oils. No iron-fortified foods. No soy in volume. Two quarts of milk, a quart of orange juice, daily gelatin, daily raw carrot, salt to taste. The diet is permanent, not a temporary intervention.

**Thyroid maintenance.** If thyroid replacement was started in Phase 1 or 2, continue at the established maintenance dose, tracking temperature and pulse quarterly. If labs were never abnormal but symptoms have re-emerged after chemotherapy or surgery, reassess — chemotherapy and especially radiation impair the thyroid axis, often subclinically.

**Progesterone maintenance.** 30 to 100 mg/day, continuously, for women with breast / ovarian / endometrial cancer history. The lower end if the patient is asymptomatic and tolerating well; the upper end if there is any sign of recurrence risk (rising tumor markers, dense breasts on imaging, return of PMS-like symptoms in a postmenopausal woman). For men with prostate cancer history: 10–30 mg/day, sublingual or topical, as part of the anti-estrogen package (see prostate subsection).

**Aspirin maintenance.** 325 to 650 mg/day, indefinitely, with vitamin K2 co-supplementation. The post-treatment dose is the prevention dose, not the active-cancer dose. The aspirin-and-colorectal-cancer-recurrence literature is the strongest single human-trial endorsement of this intervention.

**Niacinamide maintenance.** 250 to 750 mg/day in divided doses. Liver labs annually.

**Vitamin K2, vitamin E, vitamin D** at prevention doses, maintained.

**Iron donation** for men every three to four months from age forty, indefinitely. Premenopausal women generally do not need additional iron reduction.

**Tumor-marker and imaging surveillance** on the oncology team's schedule. The patient is not in the protocol to avoid surveillance; the framework augments surveillance, it does not replace it. Rising markers or imaging changes are reasons to escalate the protocol intensity (move toward Phase 2 doses, recheck thyroid and progesterone status, reassess gut and liver) and to reopen the conversation with the oncologist about further intervention.

**What to avoid in survivorship.** HRT in any form (the breast-cancer-survivor HRT data are the strongest contraindication in the literature). SSRIs (commonly prescribed for menopausal symptoms in breast-cancer survivors who can't take HRT — the framework's case against SSRIs is sharpest in this population). PUFA-rich diets marketed as anti-inflammatory. Routine repeat radiation imaging where MRI or ultrasound would suffice. Tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors: this is a contested decision that overlaps the alongside-vs-instead-of tree — the framework's reading is that the metabolic-restoration protocol does the same work by different mechanisms and with less iatrogenic burden, but the patient with high-risk biology coming off active treatment is the case where the conventional adjuvant therapy has the strongest evidence base.

## Cancer-type-specific subsections

### Breast cancer

The most heavily documented cancer in Peat's writing and the cleanest fit for the framework. Specific additions to the phase protocols:

**Topical progesterone to the breast** in addition to systemic. The Plu-Bureau French data ([[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone*]]) showed a 50% relative risk reduction in topical-progesterone users for benign breast disease. The mechanism is local — progesterone reaches the breast tissue at higher concentration than systemic dosing achieves. 50–100 mg topical to the affected breast or both breasts, daily, in addition to systemic.

**Cycle-phase timing of surgery.** If surgery is unavoidable (lumpectomy, mastectomy, biopsy), schedule it in the luteal phase (progesterone-high, estrogen-low — roughly days 14–28 of the menstrual cycle, or weeks 2–4 of a 4-week cycle). The cycle-phase mastectomy outcome data — identical surgery, different hormonal milieu, dramatically different long-term survival — is the cleanest single piece of evidence in Peat's reading of the breast-cancer literature ([[breast-cancer|*Breast Cancer* (2006)]], [[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix*]]). Mainstream surgical oncology has declined to investigate this, but a patient or surgeon can act on it without controversy: schedule in the luteal phase where possible.

**No HRT after diagnosis or in survivorship.** The WHI literature is decisive. The hot-flash and menopausal-symptom management runs through progesterone and the metabolic-restoration protocol — see [[menopause-protocol]].

**Mammography decisions** are the patient's. The over-diagnosis and radiation-exposure case is in [[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]. Ultrasound and MRI are radiation-free alternatives.

**Tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors** — the framework's position: the metabolic-restoration protocol (progesterone, aspirin, low PUFA, thyroid, gelatin) does the same anti-estrogen work by different routes. The patient with a high-risk biology (BRCA-positive, high-grade, triple-negative, large tumor burden) is the case where conventional adjuvant therapy has the strongest case. The patient with a low-grade, hormone-receptor-positive, slowly-progressing tumor is the case where the framework most strongly favors the metabolic protocol alone.

### Prostate cancer

The framework's most counter-intuitive cancer-type-specific protocol because it inverts the mainstream androgen-deprivation model. Peat's writing is in [[prostate-cancer|*Prostate Cancer* (2013)]] and the [[estrogen-and-cancer]] sub-article. The headline is that in the older man the relevant hormone is *estrogen, not testosterone*: aromatase activity in adipose tissue rises with age and BMI, testosterone declines, and the estrogen-progesterone-DHEA balance shifts in a pro-tumor direction. The prostate, like the breast in older women, becomes a site of fibrosis, collagen cross-linking, and estrogen-driven proliferation.

**No estrogen.** Synthetic estrogens were the mainstream prostate-cancer treatment in the 1950s and 60s and Peat dismantled the literature in [[prostate-cancer|*Prostate Cancer*]]: estrogen does not prolong survival in prostate cancer, and the trials that endorsed it were "research" of the questionnaire-mailed-to-physicians variety. Avoid estrogenic exposures (soy in volume, xenoestrogens, BPA, environmental estrogens). The patient on androgen-deprivation therapy (chemical castration with leuprolide or similar) is in the position the framework most aggressively contests; coordinate with urology to reassess the AD regimen if PSA is stable and tumor burden is low.

**Vitamin A.** Retinyl palmitate or retinyl acetate, 25,000 to 100,000 IU per day (higher end for short courses with liver monitoring; lower end for chronic). Mechanism: vitamin A antagonizes estrogen at the level of epithelial proliferation — "the antagonism between estrogen and vitamin A in controlling epithelial proliferation is clear wherever it has been tested; vitamin A restrains epithelial proliferation. Wherever estrogen is a factor in the development of abnormal tissue, vitamin A supplementation would seem beneficial" ([[prostate-cancer|*Prostate Cancer*]]). The prostate is one of the most estrogen-and-vitamin-A-sensitive epithelial tissues. Adequate vitamin A is the prostate-specific addition to the protocol; liver weekly (4–6 oz) is a reasonable food-first source for patients reluctant to supplement.

**Supplementary androgens, not anti-androgens.** Testosterone replacement in the older man with documented hypogonadism is the framework's reading — provided aromatase suppression is in place (aspirin, low PUFA, vitamin E, weight management). The patient with high BMI converting administered testosterone to estrogen in adipose tissue is the failure mode; address the adiposity first. DHEA 10–25 mg/day, pregnenolone 30–100 mg/day are upstream substrates.

**Naloxone or naltrexone.** Peat cited the literature ([[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone*]]) on low-dose naltrexone for prostate and breast cancer. The mechanism is endorphin antagonism — endogenous endorphins antagonize progesterone and activate POMC-related stress signaling that promotes tumor growth. LDN 1.5–4.5 mg at bedtime is the clinical-experience dose range, prescribed off-label by clinicians familiar with the protocol.

**Bromocriptine, if pituitary hyperfunction is documented.** Elevated prolactin in the older man is pro-prostate-tumor; bromocriptine 1.25–2.5 mg/day suppresses prolactin. Coordinate with endocrinology for prolactin testing first. The bromocriptine literature is the basis for Peat's mention in [[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|*Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone*]] that "dopaminergic lisuride and bromocriptine are sometimes useful in cancer treatment."

**Watchful waiting** is the framework-defensible position in low-grade slow-progressing prostate cancer. "When cancer specialists were asked what they would do if diagnosed with prostate cancer, most of them said they would do nothing" ([[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix*]]). The protocol is the framework-active form of watchful waiting.

### Ovarian cancer

The third of the female cancer triad and the cleanest case for the progesterone protocol. Peat's writing is in [[a-physiological-approach-to-ovarian-cancer|*A Physiological Approach to Ovarian Cancer* (2010)]].

**Progesterone at the high end.** 200 to 300 mg/day in active disease; 100 to 200 mg/day in maintenance. The in-vitro and observational case for progesterone in ovarian cancer is the strongest of the three female cancers; Peat noted that "the reports of positive responses in patients treated with progesterone or synthetic progestins are more interesting" than the in-vitro data alone, "since the most logical approach involves normalization of the pituitary gonadotrophins" ([[a-physiological-approach-to-ovarian-cancer|*A Physiological Approach to Ovarian Cancer*]]).

**Gonadotropin suppression.** The post-menopausal rise in FSH and LH is one of the gonadotrophin-driven cancer-stimulation mechanisms in Peat's reading. Progesterone at the high end suppresses both. Naloxone or naltrexone (1.5–4.5 mg/day) can augment the suppression in resistant cases.

**PUFA elimination is non-negotiable.** The estrogen-promoting effect of essential-fatty-acid supplementation in the ovarian context ([[a-physiological-approach-to-ovarian-cancer|*A Physiological Approach to Ovarian Cancer*]]) makes the dietary fat correction particularly load-bearing in this cancer type.

**Vitamin D and vitamin A** both relevant; aspirin and the rest of the core stack as in Phase 2.

**Surgical considerations.** Like breast surgery, ovarian surgery in the luteal phase is preferred where the schedule and the surgeon allow.

## CO₂ elevation interventions

Carbon dioxide is the framework's anti-Warburg anion: it lowers intracellular pH, inhibits lactic acid formation, opposes the histamine-serotonin-estrogen activation of carbonic anhydrase, and stabilizes mitochondrial membranes against the excitotoxic insult that PUFA peroxidation delivers. Peat returned to CO₂ across decades and the protocol-relevant interventions are simple.

**Bag breathing.** Breathe in and out of a paper bag (lunch-bag size, not a plastic bag — never plastic) for two to five minutes at a time, three to five times per day. Or simply hold the breath comfortably after exhalation, for 10–30 seconds, repeated 5–10 times. The aim is to raise arterial CO₂ enough to be felt as warming of the hands and a small reduction in anxiety. From [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]] on the cardiovascular application: "Breathe into a paper bag for a minute or two at a time, to increase the carbon dioxide content of the blood. This has a vasodilating effect, reducing the force required to circulate the blood, and reduces anxiety." The cancer-relevant effect is the lactate-CO₂ inversion and the carbonic-anhydrase inhibition.

**Altitude exposure.** Sustained residence at 5,000–8,000 ft elevation raises baseline CO₂ and red blood cell mass; the epidemiology of cancer incidence at high altitude is consistently lower than at sea level. Not a practical intervention for most patients, but worth mentioning for those who can spend extended periods at altitude (a month or more) — vacation, residency, sabbatical.

**Dietary baking soda for hyperosmotic fluids.** Sodium bicarbonate 1/4 to 1/2 teaspoon (~1.25–2.5 g) in water, one to three times per day, between meals. The mechanism Peat described in [[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]] is not the popular "alkaline body" misreading but specifically the hyperosmotic-fluid intervention: small volumes of dense hypertonic salts (bicarbonate, glucose, sodium) acutely raise extracellular osmolality, dehydrate edematous tumor tissue, and counter the intracellular alkalinization the Warburg cells produce. The intervention is most useful in advanced disease where tumor-related edema and intracellular alkalosis are clinically evident. Stop if there is gastric irritation; co-administer with food or with magnesium if there is reflux.

**Acetazolamide,** prescription, where indicated. Acetazolamide is a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that raises blood CO₂ and is the pharmaceutical version of the bag-breathing intervention. Peat noted in [[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]] that "existing carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, such as acetazolamide, will inhibit those enzymes, without harming other tissues." Typical dose 125–250 mg twice daily; this is a clinician-supervised intervention and is most often used in altitude medicine, glaucoma, and idiopathic intracranial hypertension. The cancer-specific use is off-label.

## The cascara / emodin / bowel-toxin protocol

The most idiosyncratic Peat-frame recommendation and the one with the least mainstream overlap. The mechanism stack is three-layered: regular bowel motility prevents endotoxin reabsorption from the colon; the anthraquinone emodin (concentrated in cascara) is independently anti-tumor (HIF suppression, COX-2 inhibition, MMP-13 inhibition — see [[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]] citing the Ha et al. 2011 work); and the bowel-toxin-cancer link is a load-bearing piece of Peat's broader argument ([[endotoxin-reduction]]).

**Daily raw carrot.** Medium carrot, scraped or julienned (not finely ground — Peat was specific that "just chewing a carrot is best, any saturated fat around the same time is o.k. What doesn't work very well is to grind the carrot very fine"). With vinegar, salt, and coconut or olive oil. Daily, non-negotiable. The non-fermentable fiber binds conjugated estrogen in bile, sweeps small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and reduces endotoxin reabsorption.

**Cascara sagrada, properly aged, intermittent.** 100–500 mg of aged cascara bark or a low-dose tincture, taken once or twice per week or as needed for bowel motility — never daily long-term in healthy users, more frequently in active-cancer patients with documented constipation or endotoxin signs. Cascara must be sun-dried and aged for at least one year; fresh cascara contains harsh glycosides that cause cramping and irritation ([[cascara-sagrada]]). The "stimulant laxative damages the colon" mainstream concern is the lipofuscin-from-chronic-constipation pattern Peat dismantled; properly aged cascara at moderate intermittent doses is well-tolerated. The emodin content is the cancer-specific benefit beyond the motility effect: emodin "attenuated the expression of cyclooxygenase 2 (COX-2), VEGF, hypoxia inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α), MMP-1 and MMP-13 at mRNA level in IL-1 and LPS-treated synoviocytes under hypoxia" — exactly the cancer-field-modulating signature aspirin and progesterone produce by different routes ([[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]]).

**Activated charcoal, episodic.** 1–3 g once or twice weekly, away from meals and medications by at least two hours. Use for acute endotoxin episodes (bloating, slow-transit week, dietary slip with inflammatory rebound) — not daily. Charcoal does bind some vitamins and medications, which is why Peat cited Frolkis as using it "only intermittently." See [[endotoxin-reduction]].

**Judicious antibiotics.** Short courses of poorly-absorbed antibiotics (rifaximin, neomycin) or low-dose minocycline / doxycycline have a role in patients with documented small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth or with endotoxin-driven inflammatory flares not controlled by the dietary protocol. This is a clinician-supervised intervention; coordinate with a gastroenterologist or integrative-medicine physician. Restore the gut with gelatin, sugar, milk, and the raw carrot during and after.

## The "alongside vs instead of" decision tree

The most clinically actionable Peat-frame content anywhere — and the most consequential to get wrong. The headline: in most cancers, in most stages, the framework runs *alongside* conventional treatment, not instead of it. The cases where the framework supports declining or delaying conventional treatment are narrower than the framework's most enthusiastic readers sometimes suggest, and the cases where the framework supports declining treatment are also the cases where the patient is most exposed to the consequences if the framework is wrong.

**Run alongside conventional treatment when:**

- The cancer is aggressive (high-grade, fast-growing, triple-negative, large tumor burden, evidence of metastasis, rapid PSA rise, symptomatic).
- The natural history without treatment is rapid death (acute leukemias, aggressive lymphomas, small-cell lung, glioblastoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma).
- Conventional treatment has high-evidence efficacy in the specific cancer (testicular cancer with platinum, childhood ALL, HER2-positive breast cancer with trastuzumab, CML with imatinib).
- Surgical resection is the standard of care for a solid tumor and the tumor is operable.
- The patient is younger (under 50–55) with decades of life expectancy where recurrence over time is the dominant risk.
- The patient prefers conventional treatment after informed discussion.

**Consider delaying or declining conventional treatment, with explicit clinician coordination, when:**

- The cancer is indolent (low-grade prostate cancer with low PSA, low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ, certain follicular lymphomas, papillary thyroid microcarcinoma).
- The patient is older (75+) where the natural history of the cancer is competing with other causes of mortality and the iatrogenic burden of treatment is high.
- The mainstream evidence base for treatment efficacy is weak (recurrence-only-aromatase-inhibitor in a low-risk hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer, prophylactic chemotherapy in a stage-I cancer with low recurrence risk).
- The treatment proposed has high iatrogenic burden with marginal benefit (the "I just want to be aggressive" oncologist proposing chemotherapy where the survival benefit is two months).
- The patient has experienced a previous course of conventional treatment and is at high risk of cumulative toxicity (re-irradiation, multiple chemotherapy lines).
- Watchful-waiting is the documented standard of care for the diagnosis (low-grade prostate cancer, low-grade chronic lymphocytic leukemia, smoldering myeloma).

**Refuse the iatrogenic excess regardless of the alongside-vs-instead-of decision:**

- Routine repeat biopsy of known stable disease.
- Routine repeat imaging at intervals where MRI or ultrasound would substitute for CT or repeated mammography.
- Opiate analgesia where non-opiate (aspirin, magnesium, regional blocks) substitution is feasible.
- HRT in any form in survivors of breast / ovarian / endometrial / hormone-positive cancers.
- "Starvation" diets (ketogenic, prolonged fasting, calorie restriction) during active treatment.
- Long courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics without gut restoration.
- SSRIs for treatment-related mood and menopausal symptoms when alternatives exist.
- Routine iron supplementation in chemotherapy-induced anemia without coordination of copper, erythropoietin, and the underlying erythropoietic stress.

The decision is the patient's, made with an oncologist informed of the framework. The protocol is what the patient runs regardless of the conventional-treatment choice.

## Cancer-stage modifiers

The phase protocols above apply across stages; the stage-specific modifiers are:

**Stage 0 (in-situ disease — DCIS, prostate intraepithelial neoplasia, cervical CIN).** Phase 1 prevention protocol intensified, with the explicit consideration that the over-diagnosis case ([[breast-cancer|*Breast Cancer*]], [[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]) is strongest at this stage. Coordinate with the surgeon on watchful-waiting where the lesion meets criteria.

**Stage I (small tumor, no nodal involvement).** Phase 2 active-cancer adjunct alongside conventional treatment is the default. The framework's case for declining treatment is strongest at Stage I when the cancer is also low-grade and slow-growing (Gleason 6 prostate cancer, low-grade DCIS, papillary thyroid microcarcinoma).

**Stage II–III (regional spread, larger tumor).** Phase 2 alongside conventional treatment, with full progesterone / aspirin / niacinamide / methylene blue / DCA stack at upper-range doses. The framework does not support declining surgical resection or first-line chemotherapy in this stage range for most cancers.

**Stage IV (distant metastasis).** Phase 2 at the upper end of every dose range. Conventional treatment in stage IV has variable evidence; the alongside-vs-instead-of decision becomes most patient-specific here. The framework's case for adjunctive palliation (aspirin for pain and inflammation, progesterone for fatigue and mood, methylene blue for cognitive support, baking soda for tumor-edema-related symptoms) is strong; the case for refusing chemotherapy in widely metastatic disease where the survival benefit is small and the toxicity is high is also stronger.

**Recurrence (after a clinical remission).** Phase 2 reactivated. The recurrence pattern is the field-management failure the dormancy literature predicts. Reassess every intervention from Phase 3 — diet compliance, thyroid status, progesterone dose, aspirin compliance, iron status, gut function — and intensify.

## Failure modes

Five characteristic ways the protocol goes wrong.

**1. Refusing all conventional treatment when adjunctive is the right call.** The most consequential failure. A patient with an aggressive cancer (high-grade, fast-growing, large burden, evidence of metastasis) who declines conventional treatment because of a framework-driven belief that surgery and chemotherapy will make things worse is one for whom the iatrogenesis-of-mainstream-treatment critique is being misapplied. The critique is real (see [[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]) but it is the critique of *iatrogenic excess*, not the critique of all intervention. The pediatric leukemia that responds to platinum, the testicular cancer that responds to cisplatin, the HER2-positive breast cancer that responds to trastuzumab — these are the treatments whose survival benefit is decisive and where the framework runs alongside, not instead.

**2. Chasing exotic supplements over basic metabolic restoration.** The protocol stack — thyroid, progesterone, aspirin, niacinamide, vitamin E, vitamin K, vitamin D, carrot, gelatin, sugar, salt — is unglamorous. The supplement industry sells more glamorous alternatives (high-dose curcumin, IV vitamin C, IV ozone, mistletoe, laetrile, intravenous DMSO). Some of these have marginal supporting evidence; most have less evidence than aspirin and progesterone, which are the highest-evidence interventions in the framework. The failure mode is to spend $5,000/month on exotics while the diet still has seed oils in it.

**3. Calorie restriction or fasting attempts.** The "starve the tumor" instinct survives in every cancer-patient population despite the Sauer-Dauchy / Schlappack literature ([[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]]) showing that glucose deprivation actively promotes metastasis. The intermittent-fasting and time-restricted-eating popularity adds to this; the ketogenic-anti-cancer literature (Seyfried in particular) makes the explicit case for fasting and dietary glucose restriction in cancer. The framework's position is unambiguous: cancer cells switch to glutaminolysis and free-fatty-acid oxidation when glucose is restricted, and the stress hormones the fasting produces drive systemic inflammation, lactate, and PUFA mobilization that worsens the cancer field. Eat. Keep the weight stable. The cachexia state is the danger, not the well-fed state.

**4. "Starving the tumor" thinking applied to high-dose vitamin C, IV chelation, exotic detox.** The variant of the starvation failure mode. The intuition that the body has "toxins" that need to be "flushed out" — through high-dose vitamin C, IV chelation, sauna detox protocols, juice fasts — substitutes a vague systemic intervention for the specific metabolic restoration the framework prescribes. Most of these interventions are at best neutral and at worst (IV chelation in particular) actively harmful. The framework's "detox" is the daily raw carrot, the intermittent cascara, the bowel motility, and the liver support — not a $200/session IV infusion.

**5. Stopping the protocol after a clinical remission.** The Phase 3 recovery and recurrence-reduction protocol is permanent, not temporary. The dormancy literature is the relevant frame: the cancer is not gone, it is held in dormancy by the conditions of the protocol. The protocol is maintained indefinitely. The failure mode is to return to the pre-cancer dietary and lifestyle baseline after a clinical remission, on the assumption that the cancer has been cured. The framework's reading is that this is the field-management failure that drives the high recurrence rates mainstream oncology reports.

The treatment-critique-side failure modes (over-treatment, over-screening, over-diagnosis, opiate dependence, "do everything as early as possible" mentality) are catalogued at [[cancer-treatment-iatrogenesis]].

## What to AVOID

Compact list, ordered by frequency in the patient population.

1. **Calorie restriction or prolonged fasting** during active cancer treatment. Eat. The Sauer-Dauchy literature.
2. **Ketogenic / very-low-carbohydrate diets** in active cancer. Glucose is not the enemy; lactate is.
3. **High-PUFA + low-saturated-fat "anti-inflammatory" diets** (the omega-3 marketing). Saturated fats and gelatin are the framework's anti-inflammatory fats.
4. **Megadose iron supplementation** in chemotherapy-related anemia without addressing the underlying erythropoietic stress. Coordinate copper and vitamin A; the anemia of inflammation is not iron-deficiency anemia.
5. **Raw cruciferous vegetables in volume in patients with documented dysbiosis or hypothyroidism.** Cooked and small portions are fine; raw kale smoothies daily are not. The goitrogen and the fermentable-fiber loads both worsen the gut and the thyroid in the wrong patient.
6. **Untested chelation protocols** (IV EDTA, oral chelators for "heavy metal detox" in cancer patients). At best neutral, frequently harmful.
7. **Opiate analgesia where alternatives exist.** Aspirin, magnesium, gabapentin, regional blocks, acetaminophen with caution. Reserve opiates for pain not controlled by alternatives.
8. **SSRIs and serotonergic antidepressants** in cancer patients. The serotonin-fibrosis-aromatase axis is what the protocol is suppressing.
9. **HRT in survivors of estrogen-receptor-positive cancers.** Including bioidentical estradiol marketed as "different from the WHI protocol." The case is in [[estrogen-and-cancer]].
10. **Fish oil and high-dose omega-3 supplements.** PUFA is PUFA.
11. **Soy in volume** (soy protein isolates, soy milk daily, edamame as a staple). Estrogenic.
12. **Routine PSA monitoring without a plan for what to do with a rising number** in older men. The over-diagnosis literature.
13. **Routine mammography in dense-breasted premenopausal women** in the absence of family-history risk. Substitute MRI or ultrasound where the situation allows.
14. **Long courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics** without gut restoration.

## Tracking signals

Daily and weekly, in addition to the oncology team's surveillance schedule:

**Morning oral temperature** trending toward 98.0–98.6 °F. Cold extremities, morning temperature below 97.6, and slow rewarming after a hot shower are the warning signs of thyroid inadequacy. Track daily through the dose-titration phase, then 2–3 times per week at maintenance.

**Resting pulse** trending toward 75–85. Track with the temperature.

**Mood, sleep, and energy.** Anxiety, insomnia, depression, and chronic fatigue rise as the stress-hormone axis rises and fall first as the metabolic restoration takes. The 2 a.m. wake-up driven by hypoglycemic cortisol-adrenaline surge is a specific signal — feed the liver before bed (milk and honey, fruit, a small bowl of ice cream) and the wake-up typically softens within a week.

**Weight stable** in the desirable range for the patient. Involuntary weight loss in a cancer patient is the cachexia signal and is its own mortality driver; chase it aggressively with sugar, gelatin, salt, and warmth. Voluntary weight loss in a previously-obese patient is the goal but the rate matters — slow (0.5–1 kg/month) is fine, rapid is not (it mobilizes stored PUFA faster than the protocol can protect against).

**Tumor markers, if applicable.** The patient's oncology team tracks these on schedule; the patient's role is to know the values and trends and to use rising markers as a signal to escalate the protocol intensity rather than to panic. CA 15-3 and CA 27-29 in breast cancer; CA 125 in ovarian; PSA in prostate; CEA in colorectal; AFP in hepatocellular. None of these are diagnostic alone, but the trends matter.

**Imaging response** on the oncology team's schedule. The framework's reading is that imaging stable or improving is the goal, and that the patient should not chase imaging at intervals shorter than the oncology team prescribes.

**Bowel function** daily, formed, complete, twice daily where the diet supports it. Constipation drives endotoxin reabsorption and is the gut-side failure mode the cascara / carrot / activated-charcoal stack is designed to prevent.

**Lab markers** quarterly through active treatment, semiannually in recovery:

- TSH below 1.5 (ideally close to 1.0), free T3 upper third of reference range, reverse T3 low
- Ferritin 30–70 ng/mL (no higher)
- 25-OH vitamin D 50–80 ng/mL
- HDL above 60
- Triglycerides below 100
- Prolactin below 10 ng/mL (men), within mid-range (women)
- hsCRP below 1.0 mg/L
- Liver enzymes (AST, ALT, GGT) baseline normal — important for the niacinamide dose monitoring above 1 g/day
- Complete blood count (white-cell count and lymphocyte differential as immune-surveillance proxy)

## When to escalate (to oncology, not to the protocol)

The protocol is not the right tool when:

- **Rapid clinical progression.** New symptoms, rising tumor markers, imaging changes that the patient and the oncology team agree are concerning. Coordinate with oncology about intensifying conventional treatment, not just the protocol.
- **Severe uncontrolled pain.** Aspirin and magnesium are the framework's first-line analgesics; if pain is escalating beyond what they control, the patient needs an opiate trial under pain-management supervision or a regional block.
- **Treatment-related toxicity.** Severe chemotherapy-induced nausea, neutropenic fever, radiation-induced enteritis, hand-foot syndrome, neuropathy progressing despite thiamine supplementation. The protocol is supportive; these are oncology emergencies.
- **Acute bleeding** in a patient on aspirin. Stop the aspirin and call the oncologist. Aspirin-induced GI bleeding is real and dose-dependent; the framework does not require the patient to bleed through it.
- **New thromboembolic event** (DVT, PE) in a cancer patient. The cancer-related hypercoagulability is well-established and aspirin alone is not adequate anticoagulation. Coordinate with oncology about heparin or DOAC therapy.
- **Sepsis or neutropenic fever.** Cancer treatment-related immunosuppression is real; sepsis is a medical emergency.
- **Decompensating cardiac, hepatic, or renal function.** Coordinate with the relevant specialty.
- **Psychiatric crisis** in a cancer patient. The framework's case against SSRIs is not a case against psychiatric care; severe depression, suicidal ideation, and psychiatric emergency need appropriate clinical management.

The framework adds the most when the patient is in surveillance, in stable disease, in the recovery phase, or in the prevention lane. It adds the least — and the patient is most exposed if the framework is wrong — in acute oncologic emergencies.
