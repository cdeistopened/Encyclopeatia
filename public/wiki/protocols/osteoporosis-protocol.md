# Osteoporosis Protocol

This is the action layer for [[osteoporosis]]. The mechanism page argues the case — bone loss is a failure of tissue renewal under the same hormonal current (estrogen, prolactin, parathyroid hormone, cortisol, serotonin) that drives soft-tissue calcification, with CO2 and vitamin K as the missing axis of calcium handling. The mainstream pharmacy (bisphosphonates, estrogen, calcitonin, denosumab, calcium-citrate megadoses) does not survive that reading. What follows is what to do instead, sequenced by phase, with the dose ranges Peat actually quoted, the K2 sub-form decision, the food-first calcium ladder, and the tracking template that lets a patient know whether the protocol is working before the DEXA reads it.

Phase matters. A 53-year-old with a borderline T-score and three years of perimenopausal symptoms behind her is not in the same protocol as a 71-year-old with a T-score of -3.2 and a fractured wrist last winter, and neither is in the same protocol as a 68-year-old man with low testosterone, gynecomastia, and a hip T-score of -2.7. The framework is the same; the proportions differ. Bone is a slow-turnover tissue and the metabolic field has to be sustained for twelve to twenty-four months for clinical signal to appear, so doing it once and stopping does not work and switching protocols every three months because nothing is happening yet is the most common reason the regimen fails.

What this protocol is not: an HRT alternative. Estradiol is not in the protocol at any phase or any dose, transdermal or oral or compounded. The [[menopause-protocol]] argument applies in full — the bone-protective signal Peat identified is progesterone-plus-thyroid-plus-K2, not "bioidentical estrogen at the right time." It is also not a substitute for individual medical guidance. The doses cited are the ones Peat published or wrote in email correspondence; the application to any specific person depends on co-morbidities, current medications (warfarin in particular interacts with the entire protocol), and labwork the clinician interprets. This is documentation of the framework, not personalized medical advice.

## Diagnosis Prerequisites

Before starting, gather the baseline. The DEXA is the conventional entry point and is worth having, with reservations — the radiation dose accelerates the same bone loss the scan is measuring, the soft-tissue water and fat content distort the reading, and soft-tissue calcification (the syndrome the same hormonal current is producing) can register as bone density on the scan and produce a falsely reassuring number. From [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm|*Bone Density: First Do No Harm* (2006)]]: "I think it would be reasonable to say that a woman with osteoporosis is usually better off when it's not diagnosed, because of the dangerous things prescribed for it." The protocol does not require a DEXA to start. What it requires is the metabolic baseline.

**Take the baseline of vital signs.** Morning oral temperature on waking, before getting up: target 97.8–98.2°F (anything under 97.6°F is hypothyroid until proven otherwise). Afternoon temperature one to three hours after lunch: 98.4–98.8°F. Resting pulse first thing in the morning: 75–90 bpm for an adult on a Peat-aligned diet (under 70 in a sedentary adult is hypothyroid). The protocol at [[metabolic-rate]] and the full tracking apparatus at [[temperature-tracking]] govern the metric. A patient whose temperature reads 97.0°F at 9 a.m. is in the protocol whose first phase is thyroid restoration, regardless of what the DEXA says about her hip.

**Take the symptom inventory.** Cold hands and feet; thinning lateral eyebrows; constipation; afternoon energy crash; insomnia particularly with 3 a.m. waking; thin, dry, brittle nails; thinning hair; new wrinkling of skin; height loss (a serial standing-height measurement against a doorframe is free); the kyphotic stoop of established vertebral compression; new dental enamel loss or gum recession; muscle cramping in calves at night (the magnesium signal). Catalog where the patient sits on every line. These markers move before bone density does and they are how the protocol's working state is read between annual scans.

**Take the history.** Age at last menstrual period (women), age at first menstrual period, history of any oral contraceptive or HRT use, history of any SSRI or proton-pump inhibitor or glucocorticoid use (all three are independent bone-loss drivers), any prior bisphosphonate exposure (the matrix damage lingers for years after discontinuation), prior fractures, family history of fracture, current diet (the PUFA exposure is the load-bearing question), current activity (excess endurance cardio is itself bone-loss accelerating via cortisol). For men: any history of androgen-deprivation therapy for prostate cancer, opioid analgesic use, alcohol intake.

**The labs worth having before starting.** Serum PTH (target the lower half of the reference range, not the middle — see [[parathyroid-hormone]]), 25-hydroxyvitamin D (target 50–60 ng/mL, not the 80–100+ some practitioners chase), RBC magnesium (more sensitive than serum, which is artifactually normal in tissue deficiency), serum prolactin (a direct bone-loss signal that should fall on progesterone and thyroid), free T3 and free T4 (TSH alone is inadequate — see [[hypothyroid-recovery]]), serum testosterone in men (total and free), DHEA-S in both sexes, fasting blood glucose and a simple lipid panel. Serum calcium itself is a poor marker — it is tightly defended by PTH and reads normal whether bone is dissolving rapidly or not.

## Phase 1: Early Postmenopausal (T-score 0 to -2.0, Roughly Ages 52–60, High-Risk Window)

The four-year window described in [[menopause]] — when ovarian, adrenal, and adipose estrogen production continues at near-premenopausal levels without monthly progesterone clearance — is the period of maximum tissue-estrogen exposure across a woman's lifetime, and the bone-loss rate of the first five years past LMP exceeds any other phase. A T-score that reads borderline at this stage is the early signal of the same chain that produces a -2.5 reading at 65. The intervention now is far cheaper than the salvage at 70.

**Progest-E, 3–4 drops once or twice daily, mucosal.** The canonical Peat dose for this phase. Progest-E in vitamin E oil delivers approximately 3 mg of progesterone per drop; three to four drops on the inner gum or under the tongue delivers 9–12 mg, absorbed through oral mucosa and bypassing the first-pass liver metabolism that destroys oral micronized progesterone's bioavailability. From the [[osteoporosis-harmful-calcification-and-nerve-muscle-malfunctions|*Osteoporosis, harmful calcification, and nerve/muscle malfunctions* (1996)]] newsletter: "Around the age of 50, when bone loss is increasing, progesterone and thyroid are likely to be deficient, and cortisol and prolactin are likely to be increased. Prolactin contributes directly to bone loss." Start at three drops on waking. Add a second three-drop dose at bedtime if hot flashes, night waking, or breast tenderness persist. The full phase-specific dosing through perimenopause is at [[menopause-protocol#phase-2-early-postmenopause-lmp-to-roughly-5-years-out-ages-5257]]. A 30-mL bottle of Progest-E contains roughly 600 mg of progesterone (Kenogen brand, ~3 mg/drop, ~200 drops per bottle); at six drops daily a bottle lasts about a month.

**Thyroid restoration to the metabolic target.** If morning temperature reads under 97.8°F or resting pulse under 75, restoration is non-negotiable. From [[osteoporosis-harmful-calcification-and-nerve-muscle-malfunctions]]: "Chronic hypothyroidism causes osteopenia, and in this connection, it is significant that women (as a result of estrogen's effects on the thyroid) are much more likely than men to be hypothyroid, and that, relative to men, women in general are 'osteopenic.'" The full protocol is at [[hypothyroid-recovery]]; for this phase the entry-level dosing is 5 µg of T3 (Cytomel or compounded liothyronine) two to three times daily with food, starting from a single 5 µg dose at breakfast and adding doses weekly until the morning temperature target hits 98.0°F+. T4-only therapy is the worst choice — estrogen-impaired livers convert T4 to T3 inefficiently and the conversion-failure pattern is precisely what produces postmenopausal bone loss. NDT at a quarter to half grain at breakfast is the alternative for patients who prefer the multi-component preparation.

**Vitamin K2 MK-4, 5 mg twice daily with fat-containing meals.** The single most under-prescribed nutrient in mainstream bone medicine, and the molecule that resolves the calcification paradox. From the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]]: "Vitamin K protects your arteries from calcification and allows the bone to use carbon dioxide to bind calcium into the bone." K2 carboxylates osteocalcin so the protein can bind calcium to the matrix; it blocks the PTH-driven lactic-acid production that dissolves bone; it oxidizes estradiol as a side benefit. Take with butter, full-fat dairy, or a meal containing animal fat. The MK-4 vs MK-7 sub-decision is below. Total daily 10 mg in this phase is the conservative entry; Peat referenced 1–10 mg "in the safe range as well as probably being therapeutic for the arteries" (see [[vitamin-k]]).

**Magnesium, 300–400 mg elemental at bedtime.** The cofactor for ATP that osteoblasts need to lay down matrix, the opponent of intracellular calcium overload that drives the catabolic phase, and a direct PTH-suppressor alongside dietary calcium. Forms that work: magnesium glycinate, magnesium chloride flakes dissolved in water, magnesium taurate, magnesium malate. Magnesium oxide is poorly absorbed; magnesium citrate raises stool frequency at therapeutic doses and Peat treated citrate as a calcium-loss promoter independently. Topical Epsom salts (20–30 minutes in a warm bath, twice weekly) supply roughly 100–200 mg transdermally without bowel irritation and were Peat's preferred route for women who couldn't tolerate oral magnesium. From [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|*Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis* (April 1985)]]: "A bias toward magnesium, rather than calcium, would protect against many conditions that occur in aging and stress."

**Vitamin D3, 2,000–4,000 IU daily.** Modest, not megadose. Target serum 25(OH)D of 50–60 ng/mL on annual labs. The activated vitamin D analogs (calcitriol) and the megadose protocols (10,000+ IU daily) drive respiratory alkalosis and hypercalciuria, which accelerates the same bone loss the supplement is supposed to address. See [[vitamin-d]] for the full mechanism. Mid-morning sunlight on bare skin for 20–30 minutes April through October (depending on latitude) covers the natural production layer. The supplement is the winter insurance.

**Two quarts of milk daily as the calcium baseline.** This is the protocol's calcium delivery. Two quarts of whole milk supply roughly 2,400 mg of calcium against a phosphate background that produces the Ca:P ratio of approximately 1.5:1 Peat identified as protective. From [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm]]: "Avoiding fluoridated water and the polyunsaturated oils, and drinking two quarts of milk daily (which will provide only 66 g of protein), and using some other nutrient-rich foods such as eggs and fruits, are probably the basic things to protect the bones." See the calcium ladder below for the dairy-intolerant pathway.

**Anti-PUFA across the protocol's full duration.** The PUFA load that drives the menopausal field is built across decades; the depletion clock is years long. See [[pufa-depletion]] for the full version. The bone-specific implication is that PUFA-driven inflammatory prostaglandins (PGE2 in particular) stimulate the same lactic-acid production in bone cells that PTH does, doubling the catabolic signal. Replace cooking fats with butter, ghee, coconut oil, beef tallow. Eliminate fish oil, flaxseed oil, and all "heart-healthy" seed oils. The aspirin component compensates for circulating prostaglandin signaling while the tissue PUFA turns over.

**Aspirin 81–325 mg daily with food, paired with vitamin K.** From the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 episode]]: "People seldom talk about it, but aspirin improves calcium retention in bones. It isn't as profitable as osteoporosis drugs like bisphosphonates, but it is very safe as long as you take Vitamin K with it." The aspirin-K pairing rule, worked out by Sarah Johanneson Murray on multiple KMUD broadcasts: 1 mg of vitamin K2 per 325 mg of aspirin. A baby aspirin (81 mg) does not require additional K beyond the protocol's baseline 5–10 mg; a 325 mg adult dose at the lower bone-protective end is well-covered by the same baseline. The mechanism is the same as the K2 piece — aspirin suppresses lactic-acid production in bone cells via a different pathway, stacking with K2's PTH-blockade.

**Bag breathing, 2–3 minutes, 3 times daily.** See the schedule below and the full mechanism at [[bag-breathing]].

## Phase 2: Established Osteoporosis (T-score -2.5 or Worse, Fracture History)

The patient has a confirmed diagnosis and either a worse-than-borderline T-score or a fragility fracture. The protocol intensifies on every axis — higher progesterone, K2 toward the upper Japanese-protocol range, mandatory thyroid restoration even if labs read "normal," and more frequent measurement. The first six months are the steepest part of the recovery curve and the dropout rate is highest there because the structural signal lags the metabolic one by twelve to eighteen months.

**Progest-E 6–8 drops twice daily, with night-time top-up as needed.** Doubled or tripled from the Phase 1 dose. From the [[the-progesterone-deceptions|*Progesterone Deceptions*]] referenced in [[menopause-protocol]], Peat acknowledged a 30-fold dose range across patients; in established osteoporosis the dose floor is roughly 40–60 mg daily (~12–20 drops) with periodic 100 mg surges during high-stress windows (illness, surgery, bereavement). Some practitioners use higher doses (100–200 mg daily) in the first year of severe osteoporosis with apparent clinical benefit — the data are observational, the upper bound is set by sedation and breast tenderness, both reversible on dose reduction. Slender women and women with persistent hot flashes typically need higher doses; heavier women may need still more because adipose tissue aromatizes the progesterone-protected estradiol pool. Titrate from the bottom up; the right dose is the one that controls symptoms and produces no breast tenderness, sedation, or breakthrough bleeding.

**Thyroid replacement is mandatory in this phase.** Even if TSH reads "normal," even if the patient has been told her thyroid is fine. The metabolic-baseline temperature target (98.0°F morning, 98.6°F+ afternoon, pulse 75–85) is non-negotiable. From [[osteoporosis-harmful-calcification-and-nerve-muscle-malfunctions]]: "Thyroid and progesterone, unlike estrogen, stimulate bone-building, and are associated with a decreased risk of cancer. It seems sensible to use thyroid and progesterone for their general anti-degenerative effects." Working dose for established disease: 1–2 grains of desiccated thyroid (Armour, Nature-Throid, or compounded NDT) at breakfast plus 5 µg T3 at lunch and another 5 µg mid-afternoon, or — for patients sensitive to T4 — 5–10 µg T3 three or four times daily as monotherapy. Recheck free T3 (target upper-third of reference range), reverse T3 (target below 15 ng/dL), and TSH (will read suppressed on adequate T3; this is the goal, not the failure). Co-occurring hypothyroidism is the rule in established postmenopausal osteoporosis, not the exception, and the bone-specific drugs do not reach this lever.

**Vitamin K2 MK-4 at the Japanese-protocol dose: 15–45 mg daily, divided.** The dose Japanese RCTs (Shiraki, Inoue, the multi-center trial in the late 1990s) used to demonstrate fracture-incidence reduction in established postmenopausal osteoporosis was 45 mg/day MK-4 (menatetrenone), divided three times daily with meals. Peat referenced this study line directly in [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science]]. The supplement form Sarah Murray named on the [[2012-09-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-phosphate-and-calcium-metabolism|2012 KMUD episode]] was Thorne Research liquid K, "one drop is 1 milligram. So we're talking about one to ten drops." For 15 mg/day, 5 drops three times daily with meals. For 45 mg/day in severe disease, 15 drops three times daily. The MK-4 vs MK-7 sub-decision is unambiguous in this phase: MK-4. (See sub-decision section below.) An adult who tolerates 45 mg/day MK-4 for the first six to twelve months can usually drop to 15 mg/day maintenance after the structural signal arrives.

**Magnesium 500–600 mg elemental nightly.** Pushed to the upper bowel-tolerance limit. Magnesium glycinate at 400 mg plus a 20-minute Epsom salt bath three times weekly is the working combination. Patients with chronic loose stool from oral magnesium can shift entirely to transdermal — magnesium chloride spray on the abdomen and inner thighs after a warm shower, or twice-daily Epsom soaks.

**Vitamin D3, 4,000–5,000 IU daily.** Pushed slightly higher than Phase 1, dosed to a serum 25(OH)D target of 60 ng/mL (not the 80–100+ some clinics target). The point is to keep PTH suppressed; once 25(OH)D is above 50 ng/mL, additional supplementation does not further suppress PTH and does begin to produce hypercalciuria.

**Calcium 2,400–3,000 mg daily from food.** Two quarts of milk plus daily cheese (4–6 oz), eggshell powder if dairy is restricted (see calcium ladder below), and a weekly serving of canned sardines or salmon with the bones intact. The Maasai reference Peat cited on the 2011 KMUD episode — "the Maasai people and other cattle-raising people often get 5,000 mg of calcium a day" — sets the upper-end physiological precedent; the protocol's 2,400–3,000 mg target is well within that range.

**Pregnenolone 100–200 mg daily.** The neurosteroid precursor pool that supplies progesterone, DHEA, and the protective brain steroids. From [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|*Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis*]]: "I think rational therapy for osteoporosis would include thyroid, vitamin A, bright light, and either pregnenolone or a mixture of progesterone and DHEA, with a diet rich in protein and all the salts." Pregnenolone 50 mg with breakfast and 50–150 mg with lunch, with a small amount of fat for absorption. Some patients need both pregnenolone and progesterone; others find pregnenolone-plus-DHEA sufficient. The combined regimen — progesterone-plus-pregnenolone-plus-low-dose-DHEA — is the maximal-bone-protective hormonal stack.

**Low-dose DHEA, 5–15 mg daily.** For postmenopausal women whose DHEA-S labs read in the bottom quartile of reference range. From the [[osteoporosis-aging-tissue-renewal-and-product-science]] newsletter: "DHEA, in low doses, has been shown to normalize the postload hypercalcemia of menopausal osteoporosis." Start at 5 mg with breakfast, lab-check DHEA-S and estradiol at 6 weeks to confirm the dose is not driving peripheral aromatization. Contraindicated in women with significant body fat (peripheral aromatase converts DHEA to estradiol) or persistent estrogen-dominance markers (breast tenderness, water retention, mid-cycle bleeding pattern carrying over into postmenopause).

**Aspirin 325 mg with food, twice daily, with vitamin K coverage.** Higher dose than Phase 1, requires explicit K coverage (2 mg K2 per 325 mg aspirin per Sarah Murray's formula on the [[2021-08-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-august-2021-kmud|2021 KMUD episode]]). The 15–45 mg K2 base of this phase covers it generously. Take with food and a pinch of baking soda if stomach sensitivity develops.

**Eggshell powder 1/4 teaspoon daily as supplemental calcium.** The form Peat endorsed because it releases CO2 on metabolism. Quarter teaspoon of finely ground eggshell powder is approximately 2,000 mg of calcium. See the calcium ladder below for preparation.

**Bag breathing, 5 minutes, 4 times daily** in this phase — higher frequency than Phase 1, with one session before bed as the standing prescription.

## Phase 3: Male Osteoporosis (Different Hormonal Profile)

Men's bone-loss physiology shares the parathyroid-CO2-K2-thyroid backbone with women's but the hormonal field differs. From the [[osteoporosis]] discussion of data gaps: "Peat described testosterone and DHEA as bone-protective in passing but never developed the male-osteoporosis case with the rigor he developed the female case. Men's bone loss accelerates with the decline of DHEA and testosterone and the rise of estradiol from increased aromatase activity in aging adipose tissue." The protocol substitutes male hormonal restoration for the female-specific progesterone-dominance pattern, while retaining the entire metabolic-substrate layer.

**The thyroid layer is identical to the female protocol.** Morning temperature 98.0°F+, resting pulse 75–85, free T3 in the upper third of reference range. From the [[prolactin-research|prolactin research brief]] citing Peat directly: "By the age of 50, men often show an excess of both prolactin and estrogen, and a deficiency of thyroid and testosterone." The same hypothyroidism-aromatase-prolactin pattern that drives female bone loss drives male bone loss, with testosterone failing on the same axis as progesterone fails in women. NDT 1–2 grains at breakfast plus T3 5–10 µg at lunch and mid-afternoon. The full dosing apparatus is at [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

**Pregnenolone 200–400 mg daily, divided.** The central hormonal restoration tool in male osteoporosis. Pregnenolone is the precursor to both DHEA and testosterone, and aging men typically run low on the entire pool. 100 mg with breakfast, 100–300 mg with lunch, taken with a small amount of fat. Pregnenolone alone, in this dose range, often raises serum DHEA-S into the middle quartile and serum free testosterone toward the lower end of the young-male reference range over 8–12 weeks.

**DHEA 25–50 mg daily** in older men with low DHEA-S labs. Higher than the female dose because peripheral aromatase activity in lean men is lower and the conversion-to-estradiol risk is less acute. Lab-check serum estradiol at 6 weeks — target male estradiol below 30 pg/mL. If estradiol rises into the 30–50 pg/mL range, add aspirin (an aromatase inhibitor) and consider reducing the DHEA dose by half. The classic Peat note from the [[dhea-research|DHEA research brief]]: "In a 1971 article, I mentioned DHEA as being one of the basic protective hormones like progesterone." For aging men, DHEA is closer to the central tool than for women.

**Low-dose progesterone (yes, for men too): Progest-E 2–4 drops once daily, mucosal.** Counterintuitive but consistently in Peat's male-protocol recommendations. Progesterone in men suppresses 5-alpha-reductase, lowers estradiol via the same aromatase-inhibition pathway as in women, and supports the broader anti-cortisol/anti-prolactin layer the bone-loss chain runs on. 6–12 mg daily (2–4 drops) is the typical male dose — far below the female dose because men don't have the same monthly-cycle progesterone deficit driving the requirement. From the [[progesterone|progesterone hub page]] and Peat's email correspondence: "men have been using progesterone for over fifty years for stress, cancer, and prostate issues. The dose is much smaller than for women, typically 2 to 4 drops per day."

**Testosterone status check is mandatory.** Total testosterone, free testosterone, SHBG, estradiol. Total testosterone in the bottom quartile of reference range with low free testosterone and an estradiol over 30 pg/mL is the male hypogonadism-with-aromatase-excess pattern that produces bone loss directly. Peat's framework does not endorse testosterone replacement therapy as a first move — the pregnenolone-DHEA-thyroid stack often raises endogenous testosterone production without the suppressive feedback that exogenous testosterone produces. If a six-month trial of the upstream restoration fails to move the testosterone labs, a clinician-supervised testosterone trial (transdermal 25–50 mg daily, NOT the 200 mg weekly injection protocol that produces wildly supraphysiological troughs and peaks) is a reasonable downstream consideration with aromatase monitoring.

**The K2, magnesium, vitamin D, calcium, anti-PUFA, aspirin layers are identical to the female protocol.** MK-4 at the Phase 1 dose (5–15 mg daily) for borderline disease, the Phase 2 dose (15–45 mg) for confirmed osteoporosis. Magnesium 300–500 mg nightly. Vitamin D3 2,000–5,000 IU. Two quarts of milk plus daily cheese.

**Bag breathing, same schedule** as the corresponding female phase.

## The K2 MK-4 vs MK-7 Sub-Decision

The most common question Peat received about the K2 protocol was which sub-form to use. The corpus answer is consistent but the rationale rewards working through. The choice matters more than most supplement debates because the dose ranges differ by two orders of magnitude.

**MK-4 (menatetrenone) is the form Peat used and recommended.** Short carbon-side chain, identical to the form mammals synthesize from K1 in tissues, half-life of about one hour in serum. Because it clears fast it requires multiple daily doses to maintain tissue saturation — the Japanese RCT protocol used 15 mg three times daily. The Thorne Research liquid Sarah Murray named on the 2012 KMUD episode is 1 mg per drop, dosed at 5–15 drops three times daily depending on phase. MK-4 has the dedicated osteoporosis RCT evidence base; MK-7 does not. From an email response Peat gave on the MK-4/MK-7 question, quoted in [[vitamin-k]]: "Taking the vitamin with a meal, it will absorb slowly and steadily, and I don't think will make much difference." The remark was dismissive of the urgency around the distinction, not endorsing MK-7 as equivalent.

**MK-7 (menaquinone-7) is the bacterial-fermentation form, half-life ~72 hours.** Marketed widely in the 2010s as a once-daily K2 because the long half-life keeps serum levels elevated between doses. Typical commercial dose: 100–200 µg/day. The case for MK-7 is convenience and serum K saturation between doses; the case against is that no equivalent dose has been shown to reproduce the MK-4 osteoporosis-trial outcomes, and the long half-life means that adverse responses (rare but reported — usually GI from the carrier oil) take days to clear. Peat was modestly skeptical of long-half-life synthetic vitamins in general, on the principle that physiological supply pulses preserve receptor responsiveness better than constant saturation does. He did not write a stand-alone critique of MK-7 — the corpus just does not name it as the form he would choose.

**The protocol decision.** Use MK-4 in any case of established osteoporosis (Phase 2) or male osteoporosis (Phase 3). The Japanese trial dose (45 mg/day, divided three times) is the documented effective regimen, MK-4 is the molecule actually tested, and the half-life requires the multi-dose schedule the trial used. For Phase 1 early-postmenopausal use, MK-4 at 5–15 mg/day divided twice is the conservative choice; MK-7 at 100–200 µg/day is the convenience choice some patients will prefer, and the bone-protective signal at that dose is plausible but unproven. Patients on aspirin paired with K should use MK-4 to match the multi-dose schedule with the aspirin doses; MK-7's long half-life makes the per-aspirin K coverage ratio harder to calibrate.

**What not to use: K1 alone.** Phylloquinone (the plant K) carboxylates the liver clotting factors but is less effective than K2 at carboxylating osteocalcin in bone. From [[vitamin-k]] citing Peat directly: "K1 is probably a little less active than K2. Both work; K2 works somewhat better." A high-potency liver-K1 supplement is the wrong tool for the bone protocol; pair K1 from kale and other greens with K2 from the supplement.

**Topical K2 as a workaround for GI sensitivity.** One Peat email correspondent reported severe abdominal pain on 2 mg oral K2; Peat's response was that the excipient oil was likely the issue and that he had switched to topical application. Topical K2 (the contents of a soft-gel capsule pressed onto the inner forearm or thigh skin) bypasses the gut. The route is unconventional but it is in the corpus.

## Calcium-from-Food Ladder

The protocol's calcium delivery is food, not isolated calcium tablets. Calcium citrate, calcium carbonate tablets in 1,000 mg boluses, and calcium-with-vitamin-D combination supplements do not appear in the protocol because the isolated post-prandial calcium spike they produce — without the protein, phosphate, magnesium, K2, and food matrix that dairy and bone-containing fish supply — deposits in soft tissue rather than bone. The food sources, in order of preference:

**Tier 1: Two quarts of whole milk daily.** Roughly 2,400 mg of calcium against a phosphate background producing the Ca:P ratio of approximately 1.5:1 that Peat identified as protective. Whole milk preferred over skim because the fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, K2 in pastured-cream content) ride along. Pasteurized is fine; raw is preferred when available and food-safety-managed, not because the calcium differs but because the lactase and immune factors are intact. Lactose-intolerant patients tolerate kefir, hard-aged cheeses (the lactose is fermented out), and small repeated doses across the day better than a quart at one sitting.

**Tier 2: Hard cheese, 4–6 oz daily.** Parmesan at 1,200 mg calcium per 100 g, cheddar at 700 mg per 100 g, Swiss at 800 mg per 100 g. A 100 g serving (about 3 oz) plus a quart of milk covers the 2,400–3,000 mg target. Cheese also delivers the K2 (MK-4 in particular) from the bacterial fermentation, the saturated fat, and the food-matrix phosphate-calcium ratio dairy provides.

**Tier 3: Eggshell powder, 1/4 teaspoon daily.** The supplemental form Peat endorsed because the calcium carbonate metabolism releases CO2 that stimulates rather than suppresses bone formation. From the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 KMUD episode]]: "Chemical analyses comparing eggshells to commercial supplements find that the lowest concentration of toxic heavy metals is in eggshells." Preparation: rinse eggshells, dry, bake at 200°F for 10 minutes to sterilize, grind to fine powder in a clean coffee grinder or mortar. Quarter teaspoon delivers approximately 1,500–2,000 mg of calcium carbonate. Stir into a glass of water with a few drops of lemon juice (to slightly ionize) and drink with a meal; do not stir into milk (the precipitate ruins the milk). Sarah Murray's note on the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 episode]]: "A quarter teaspoon of finely ground eggshell powder is about 2,000 mg of calcium."

**Tier 4: Sardines or canned salmon with bones intact, twice weekly.** A 3.5 oz can of sardines with bones delivers approximately 350 mg of calcium plus high-quality protein, omega-3 (in modest, non-pharmacologic amounts), and trace minerals. Useful especially in patients who cannot tolerate any dairy and prefer not to use eggshell. Also a vitamin D source.

**Tier 5: Greens, modestly.** Cooked kale delivers about 250 mg per cup (calcium oxalate complexes reduce bioavailability somewhat); collards about 270 mg per cup. Greens are the K1 source more than the calcium source. They are not a primary calcium delivery vehicle in the protocol but they fill out the diet.

**What's not in the ladder: calcium citrate, calcium carbonate tablets in 1,000+ mg boluses, calcium hydroxyapatite supplements, calcium-D combination supplements.** From the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 KMUD episode]]: "Citric acid itself causes you to lose calcium in your urine. Those supplements have anti-calcification effects." Calcium carbonate as a tablet has the right molecule but the wrong delivery — a 1,000 mg bolus produces the same vitamin-D-driven absorption pulse and arterial-deposit risk the literature has documented. Eggshell powder in food-with-meals doses is the right form of the right molecule.

## Bag-Breathing Schedule

The full mechanism and contraindications are at [[bag-breathing]]. For the bone protocol specifically the rationale is the [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]]-bone economy described in [[osteoporosis]] — bone mineral begins as calcium carbonate, osteocalcin's carbon-bound binding is vitamin-K-dependent, and tissue CO2 saturation is what makes the carbonate matrix form properly. Hyperventilation, respiratory alkalosis, low-CO2 states (induced by hypothyroidism, by HRT, by calcitonin, by activated vitamin-D analogs) drive bone loss in patterns that look nothing like a simple calcium-intake problem.

**The schedule:**

- **Morning, 2 minutes** while sitting up in bed before getting out. Establishes the day's CO2 baseline before coffee and breakfast. Small brown-paper lunch bag, paper not plastic, held loosely over nose and mouth, breathing normally (do not breathe deeply or fast). The bag becomes mildly uncomfortable in 60–90 seconds; that is the cue to remove it, take a few breaths of room air, and decide whether to repeat.
- **Mid-afternoon, 2–3 minutes** during the typical 3 p.m. energy dip. Pairs with the carbohydrate snack and a small amount of salt. Useful in patients who report cold hands and feet in the afternoon (the chronic-low-CO2 signal).
- **Pre-sleep, 3–5 minutes** as part of the bedtime sequence after the bedtime snack of milk-and-honey or fruit-and-cheese. Reduces the 3 a.m. cortisol-and-adrenaline surge that drives most of the day's nocturnal bone resorption.

**Stack with bedtime sodium bicarbonate, 1/4 teaspoon in water,** which raises bicarbonate alongside CO2 and supports the same alkali-load buffering that bones do during the day. From [[vitamin-k]] citing Peat: "Sodium bicarbonate increases calcium retention by bone." Not in every patient — those with hypertension or sodium-restricted diets should skip — but a low-cost adjunct for those who tolerate it.

**Phase 2 patients add a fourth session** (mid-morning, 2 minutes), giving four total daily sessions for the year of established-disease intensification. The marker that the practice is having an effect: warmer hands and feet within 60–90 seconds of starting a session, slower spontaneous breathing rate ten minutes after a session, and (over weeks) a longer comfortable breath-hold on the exhale (the control-pause score from Buteyko-method literature is a usable proxy for CO2 tolerance).

## What to AVOID

The mainstream protocol overlaps almost nothing with this one. The active avoidance list is therefore as long as the prescription list.

**Bisphosphonates as first-line therapy.** Alendronate (Fosamax), risedronate (Actonel), zoledronic acid (Reclast), ibandronate (Boniva). The drug class works by killing osteoclasts, which produces the appearance of preserved density on DEXA without restoring bone quality. From [[osteoporosis]]: "A bisphosphonate-treated skeleton accumulates microdamage; the matrix becomes brittle; atypical femoral fractures, osteonecrosis of the jaw, and esophageal complications appear in the trial data." A patient already on bisphosphonates for several years cannot simply discontinue and pivot — the drug retention in bone matrix is years long, and the metabolic-restoration protocol around an established bisphosphonate exposure is the working-with-what's-there variant. New starts on the protocol should not add a bisphosphonate. Patients with recent fragility fractures may pragmatically accept short-term (≤2 year) bisphosphonate while building the metabolic field; that is a clinician decision, not a protocol endorsement.

**Denosumab (Prolia).** The anti-RANKL monoclonal antibody. Similar mechanism (osteoclast suppression) by a different route, with the additional concern that discontinuation produces a rebound surge of bone resorption and a vertebral-fracture-cluster signal. Same active-avoidance stance as bisphosphonates.

**Estrogen replacement, of any form, route, or "bioidentical" rebranding.** Conjugated equine estrogens (Premarin), transdermal estradiol (Vivelle, Climara), oral micronized estradiol, compounded "bi-est" creams, vaginal-ring estradiol systemic delivery. From [[osteoporosis]]: "estrogen *accelerates* bone loss by raising prolactin, suppressing osteoblasts, and degrading the respiratory metabolism the skeleton depends on. The mainstream 'treatment' of bisphosphonate-poisoned osteoclasts and estrogen-driven cancers is the price paid for misreading the underlying biology."

**SERMs (raloxifene, tamoxifen) for bone protection.** The selective estrogen receptor modulator class is sometimes marketed as "estrogen for bones, anti-estrogen for breasts." The osteoclast-inhibition mechanism is the same as estrogen's, the bone-quality outcome is the same artifact, and the off-target effects (DVT risk for raloxifene, endometrial signal for tamoxifen) sit on top.

**Calcium-only supplementation in isolation.** Calcium citrate 600–1,200 mg tablets, calcium carbonate antacid-style boluses, calcium-and-vitamin-D combination products without K2 and magnesium. From [[osteoporosis]]: "isolated calcium supplements may *increase* cardiovascular events is exactly what Peat's framework predicts: a sudden bolus of mineral entering circulation, without the K2 to direct it to bone, deposits in the arteries that are already inflamed." If a patient is currently on calcium-citrate tablets, switch to food-source calcium plus eggshell-powder supplementation.

**High-dose vitamin D without K2.** 10,000 IU/day or higher daily doses, or the depot 50,000 IU/week protocols that some endocrinology clinics use to drive 25(OH)D into the 80–100+ range. Without K2 to direct the absorbed calcium into bone, the megadose D protocols drive arterial calcification and hypercalciuria. If a patient is currently on megadose D, reduce to 2,000–5,000 IU and start the K2 floor (5 mg MK-4 minimum).

**Megadose vitamin A as bone strategy.** Some practitioners have marketed retinyl-palmitate at 25,000–50,000 IU/day for hair, skin, and bone benefit. Peat used vitamin A but at modest doses (5,000–10,000 IU/day from food or supplement) — past the threshold, vitamin A inhibits thyroid and competes with vitamin D at the receptor. Weekly liver (4–6 oz) covers the vitamin A floor.

**SSRIs and SNRIs taken for hot flashes or perimenopausal mood.** The serotonin-osteoporosis link is in [[osteoporosis]] and the [[serotonin]] page. From the 2008 cohort data that produced the FDA warning: SSRI use is associated with a 30–50% increase in fracture risk in postmenopausal women. If a patient is currently on an SSRI, the metabolic restoration may reduce the need; do not discontinue an SSRI abruptly without a clinician-supervised taper.

**PPIs (omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole) long-term.** Proton pump inhibitors reduce stomach acid below the threshold needed to ionize dietary calcium. Long-term use is independently associated with osteoporotic fracture. If a patient has been on a PPI for more than 12 months, the gastroenterologist should be looped in on a stepwise reduction. The protocol cannot ionize calcium in a stomach the PPI is keeping at pH 6.

**Glucocorticoids long-term.** Prednisone-class drugs are independent bone-loss accelerators via direct osteoclast activation and indirect cortisol-pattern overlay. Patients on chronic steroid therapy for autoimmune disease, COPD, or rheumatologic conditions need clinician collaboration to minimize cumulative dose.

**Endurance cardio in excess.** Long runs, multi-hour cycling, marathon training. Chronic cortisol elevation from endurance exertion drives the catabolic side of the bone equation harder than the mechanical loading helps it. The bone-loading exercise that works is walking 30–60 minutes daily, modest resistance training 2–3 times weekly, and short bouts of weight-bearing activity (carrying groceries, climbing stairs, gardening). Postmenopausal triathletes have measurably lower bone density than sedentary postmenopausal controls — the cortisol load overwhelms the mechanical signal.

**Cooking with seed oils across the year-long protocol.** The single most common reason the protocol fails despite faithful supplementation is that the kitchen oil is still corn, soy, canola, sunflower, safflower, or "vegetable oil." Replace with butter, ghee, coconut oil, beef tallow. The PUFA tissue-depletion clock is years long; the kitchen-pantry switch happens once.

## 12-Month Tracking Template

The slow turnover of bone means a Peat-aligned protocol does not produce a DEXA change on the timescale most patients want. The signal arrives in layers, and the tracking has to follow the layers, not the DEXA.

**Daily (5 minutes, log on a paper card):** Morning oral temperature on waking; resting pulse first thing on waking; bedtime temperature and pulse if any energy crash that day. A simple spreadsheet or a paper card on the bathroom counter. Trend matters more than any single reading; a rolling 7-day average is the working figure.

**Weekly (15 minutes):** Symptom inventory check-in. Cold extremities (better/same/worse), insomnia (frequency, 3 a.m. waking), hot flashes (frequency, severity 1–10), constipation, mood, hair shedding rate, nail quality, energy. Standing height against a doorframe with a pencil mark — early vertebral compression shows up here before it shows up on imaging. Calf cramping at night (the magnesium signal). Photograph the doorframe marks every 6 months for a longitudinal record.

**Monthly (30 minutes):** Re-read the previous month's daily log for trend lines. Note any new fragility events (a tweaked wrist from a minor fall, a tender rib that won't quite resolve). Photograph posture from the side standing against a plain wall — the kyphotic stoop progresses subtly and the photograph catches what the mirror does not. Take a measurement of body weight, waist circumference, and grip strength (a $20 hand dynamometer from Amazon is the cleanest single proxy for systemic protein-and-energy status). Confirm protocol adherence — Progest-E drops, K2 doses, magnesium, T3 timing, calcium intake from food. The most common 90-day failure pattern is silent reduction in adherence; the monthly checkpoint catches it.

**Quarterly (lab + clinic visit):**
- Serum PTH (target lower half of reference range)
- 25-hydroxyvitamin D (target 50–60 ng/mL)
- RBC magnesium (more sensitive than serum)
- Serum prolactin (should fall on progesterone + thyroid)
- Free T3, free T4, reverse T3, TSH (T3 in upper third of reference, rT3 < 15 ng/dL)
- Fasting blood glucose and CRP
- For men: serum total testosterone, free testosterone, estradiol, DHEA-S
- For women in Phase 2 or 3: estradiol (should be in the postmenopausal low range), DHEA-S, progesterone (will read elevated post-Progest-E dose; the timing of the draw matters)
- Bone turnover markers if available: C-telopeptide (CTX, the resorption marker — falling on K2 + aspirin + progesterone) and procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide (P1NP, the formation marker — rising on T3 + progesterone). Costly and not always ordered by primary care; ask. Movement of CTX and P1NP precedes movement of DEXA by 6–9 months.

**Annually (DEXA repeat, with the caveats):** A repeat DEXA at 12 months in a patient whose metabolic markers have normalized typically shows stabilization rather than dramatic gain. By 18–24 months, modest density increases (1–3% at the spine, less at the hip) are common on the regimen. The clinically more meaningful signal is fewer fractures, better posture, restored height, and reduction in soft-tissue calcification visible on incidental imaging. From [[bone-density-first-do-no-harm]]: "X-rays accelerate the rate of bone loss. X-rays do their harm at any dose." Ultrasound bone-density measurement (calcaneal ultrasound) is the dose-free alternative and stimulates rather than damages bone tissue; ask for it if available. If only DEXA is offered, accept the trade-off but do not request mid-year scans — the radiation dose is worse than the data is worth.

**The metabolic signal arrives first, structural signal lags.** Within 4–12 weeks of starting the full protocol, the same patient should see basal temperature climbing toward 98.0°F, resting pulse rising into the 75–90 range, hair stopping its fall, cold extremities warming, mood lifting, sleep deepening. If these markers do not move, the protocol is not yet adequate and bone will not recover until they do. The fix is usually thyroid dose (under-replaced), progesterone dose (under-titrated), or PUFA persistence (cooking oil still wrong). Run the dose ladder up before declaring failure.

## Failure Modes

These are the patterns the clinician will see in a patient who reports "I tried the protocol and it didn't work."

**Insufficient progesterone dose.** The single most common failure mode. A 47-year-old with shortening luteal phases started on 3 mg/day (1 drop of Progest-E) is dosed at the lower-end Phase 1 floor and her symptoms persist because her requirement is the upper-end Phase 2 dose. The 30-fold dose range Peat acknowledged is real; the right dose is the one that controls symptoms without producing sedation or breast tenderness. Patients underdose because the supplement is expensive, because they read a 3-mg recommendation in a magazine, or because they fear "hormone therapy" generically. Titration must continue until either the symptoms resolve or the side-effect threshold is hit; stopping before either is the failure.

**K1 in place of K2.** Many patients buy a "Vitamin K" bottle from a generic supplement supplier and the label says "Vitamin K1 5 mcg." K1 carboxylates liver clotting factors but is less active than K2 for osteocalcin and matrix-Gla protein in bone. The dose is also two to three orders of magnitude too low — a 5 µg K1 tablet is one one-thousandth of the 5 mg K2 daily protocol floor. Read the bottle: it must say K2, and specifically MK-4 or MK-7 (the menatetrenone or menaquinone-7 sub-form). Quantity matters — single-tablet doses below 1 mg (1,000 µg) are inadequate for the bone protocol. The Thorne Research liquid K Sarah Murray named is 1 mg per drop, which is the working concentration.

**Ignoring thyroid status because TSH reads "normal."** The TSH reference range was widened in the 1990s, and many patients with a TSH of 3.5–4.5 (within "normal" by the lab) are clinically hypothyroid by the temperature-and-pulse measurement Peat used. Free T3 in the bottom quartile of reference range is the more informative single number; reverse T3 over 15 ng/dL is the conversion-failure signal. The bone protocol does not work in a hypothyroid metabolic field because the K2-osteocalcin-CO2-binding chain runs on respiratory metabolism the thyroid sets. The temperature and pulse are the cheapest, most accurate marker; if they are low, thyroid is the answer regardless of what TSH says.

**Continued PUFA-laden cooking oil.** A patient who follows the supplement regimen faithfully but is still cooking with canola, soy, safflower, sunflower, or "vegetable oil" is feeding the inflammatory prostaglandin pool that drives the lactic-acid-in-bone-cells pathway alongside PTH. The protocol's anti-PUFA piece is non-negotiable. Read the bottle in the kitchen; if it's not butter, ghee, coconut oil, beef tallow, or refined olive in small amounts, replace it before adding more supplement complexity.

**Calcium citrate or calcium carbonate megadose tablets layered on top of the protocol.** A patient who reads the protocol but cannot bring themselves to stop the 1,200 mg calcium-citrate tablet because "I've taken it for fifteen years" is supplying the soft-tissue-calcification substrate the rest of the protocol is trying to reverse. From the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 KMUD episode]]: "Citric acid itself causes you to lose calcium in your urine." If the patient cannot drop dairy and refuses to use eggshell powder, the calcium-from-supplement compromise is calcium carbonate 250–500 mg taken in small doses with meals — not the 1,200 mg single-tablet bolus.

**Bisphosphonate or denosumab carryover not factored in.** A patient newly diagnosed with osteoporosis who was on alendronate for the last 5 years has a skeleton with multi-year drug retention. The protocol's bone-formation signal (K2, T3, progesterone) works against the suppressed-turnover background the drug left. The DEXA in the first 12 months may not move because the drug-suppressed osteoclasts are still preventing the cleanup phase. Wait it out — by month 18–24 the underlying-metabolism signal usually dominates, and the structural improvement appears once the matrix has had a chance to renew.

**Coffee replaced by black-tea-with-milk or removed entirely.** Caffeine is bone-protective in the protocol via cortisol-suppression, aromatase inhibition (modest), and the dopamine-prolactin loop that lowers prolactin. The "coffee causes osteoporosis" mainstream claim is based on hyper-caffeinated cohorts who don't get calcium, magnesium, or anything else the protocol prescribes. Two to four cups of coffee daily with cream and sugar is fine and supportive. Removing coffee on the misguided belief that it pulls calcium is a Phase 1 failure pattern.

**Magnesium oxide as the supplemental magnesium.** Cheap, widely sold, and approximately 4% bioavailable. The bowel-irritation pattern is poor absorption, not "the right form too strong." Switch to magnesium glycinate, taurate, malate, or chloride. The Epsom-salt bath route bypasses the GI entirely and is the right answer for a patient with chronic loose stool on any oral magnesium.

## When to Escalate to Specialist

The protocol's documentation-not-medical-advice framing has a specific operational limit. The following situations require clinician supervision that the framework cannot substitute for.

**Recent fragility fracture.** A hip fracture in the last 6 months, a vertebral compression in the last 12 months, or a wrist fracture from a fall from standing height. Acute orthopedic management plus a bone-quality assessment that exceeds DEXA — high-resolution peripheral quantitative CT (HR-pQCT) where available, or at minimum a vertebral fracture assessment (VFA) added to the DEXA. A second fragility fracture within a year of the first is the indication for considering a short-term bisphosphonate trial alongside the metabolic protocol; this is a shared-decision conversation with an orthopedist or endocrinologist, not a unilateral protocol move.

**T-score below -3.5 with frailty.** The most fragile end of the disease severity range. Patients here are at high short-term fracture risk and the metabolic protocol's 12–24 month timeline is slower than the clinical risk window. Co-management with an endocrinologist who is willing to discuss the protocol's metabolic restoration alongside pharmaceutical fracture-prevention is the right configuration; an endocrinologist who insists on bisphosphonate monotherapy without dietary or hormonal restoration is the wrong fit and the patient should find a clinician who will work alongside the protocol rather than against it.

**Active malignancy or recent malignancy treatment.** Breast cancer with aromatase-inhibitor therapy (which is itself a bone-loss driver), prostate cancer with androgen-deprivation therapy, treatment-induced ovarian failure, multiple myeloma. The hormonal protocol's progesterone and DHEA components require oncology coordination — there is no abstract right answer outside that conversation.

**Hyperparathyroidism (primary or secondary).** A serum calcium above the reference range with an elevated PTH is primary hyperparathyroidism, which requires parathyroid scan, sestamibi imaging, and potentially surgical adenoma removal. Secondary hyperparathyroidism from chronic kidney disease has its own management track. The protocol's PTH-suppression strategy (calcium intake + magnesium + K2 + vitamin D) is not adequate to the surgical disease and missing the diagnosis costs years.

**Bisphosphonate-associated atypical femoral fracture or osteonecrosis of the jaw.** Both are documented adverse effects of multi-year bisphosphonate therapy and both require orthopedic or oral-surgical management. The protocol is the long-term restoration; the acute lesion has its own immediate care path.

**Severe chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 30).** Vitamin D activation requires functioning kidneys, calcium-phosphate balance is disrupted by uremic state, and the standard renal-osteodystrophy management overlaps but is not identical to the bone-protection protocol. Nephrology co-management is essential.

**Warfarin or other vitamin-K-antagonist anticoagulation.** The protocol's 5–45 mg/day vitamin K2 dosing is incompatible with the therapeutic warfarin window without explicit drug-dose adjustment. INR will need closer monitoring; warfarin dose will need uptitration to maintain therapeutic anticoagulation; some patients can transition off warfarin to a direct-acting oral anticoagulant (DOAC) which does not interact with K2 — that is the cardiology conversation. Do not start the K2 protocol in a warfarin patient without that conversation.
