# Constipation Protocol

Chronic constipation is one of the cleanest demonstrations of why Peat's framework cuts against the standard nutritional advice. The conventional prescription — more fiber, more water, more whole grains, more probiotics — assumes the problem is a deficit of bulk in the colon and a population imbalance in the microbiome. Peat reframed the same symptom as the visible end of a multi-factor metabolic failure: bile that does not flow because the liver is congested or the meal contained too little fat to trigger it, smooth muscle that does not contract because thyroid hormone is low and magnesium is depleted, intestinal water that does not arrive because gastric acid is insufficient and the stress system is diverting blood away from digestion, and a colon that does not signal evacuation because chronic adrenaline has overridden the parasympathetic tone that normally drives the gastrocolic reflex. The fiber-flooding prescription, applied to that picture, frequently makes the situation worse: fermentable fiber feeds the same gram-negative bacteria producing the [[endotoxin]] that drives the inflammation that slows transit in the first place. "Hypothyroidism's classical symptoms include chronic constipation," Peat told John Barkhausen in his 2010 *Politics and Science* episode on digestion — and the conventional answer treats neither the thyroid nor the bacterial inflammation downstream of it.

The protocol below is layered. Phase 1 buys immediate relief through magnesium, mechanical-clearance fiber, and the morning warm-fluid signal — interventions that are safe, fast, and address the most common single drivers. Phase 2 moves to root cause: thyroid evaluation, bile stimulation through fat at meals, an HCl trial if the picture suggests low gastric acid, and removal of the fermentable-fiber load that the standard advice tends to pile on. A severe-pattern section covers what to do when the picture persists past thirty days of correct execution. The fiber question — which Peat distinguished in a way the modern medical literature has flattened — gets its own section because it is the most commonly mistaken element. The frame throughout: constipation is a symptom, the symptom has identifiable mechanisms, and the right intervention depends on which mechanism is loudest.

## Diagnosis criteria

The clinical picture worth treating as "chronic constipation" in Peat's framework, drawn from the [[2010-04-26-politics-and-science-released-2019-politics-science-digestion|2010 *Politics and Science* digestion episode]] and the [[2019-09-10-jodellefit-jodellefit-how-to-fix-your-digestion-poop|2019 digestion interview with Jodelle Fitzwater]]:

- **Fewer than one bowel movement per day**, sustained for more than a few weeks. Peat's transit reference was the African comparison study he cited in 2019: cultures eating substantial potato and root vegetables ran about twelve hours from meal to elimination, while the average mid-twentieth-century American ran seventy-two. The target is closer to twelve to twenty-four hours, with one to two soft, formed movements daily.
- **Hard, fragmented, or pellet-shaped stool.** Bristol scale type 1 (separate hard lumps) or type 2 (sausage-shaped but lumpy). Type 3 or 4 is the target.
- **Straining to evacuate.** Peat's heuristic: a complete movement should arrive within five minutes of sitting down, without abdominal pressing.
- **Sense of incomplete evacuation.** The colon empties partway and the urge returns hours later, or never quite resolves.
- **Bloating that builds through the day** and rotten-smelling stool. This is the [[endotoxin]] signature — fermentation rising in the lower bowel as transit slows.
- **Co-symptoms pointing to upstream cause.** Cold hands and feet, morning oral temperature below 97.8°F, resting pulse below 75 bpm, dry skin, thinning hair, low mood — the thyroid pattern. Or post-prandial brain fog and headaches — the endotoxin pattern. Or fatigue worst within an hour of high-protein meals — the low-gastric-acid pattern.

Three or more of these together, sustained, is the protocol's target population. Acute constipation lasting a few days during travel or stress is a different problem and does not require a protocol.

## Phase 1: Immediate relief (1-2 weeks)

The goal of Phase 1 is to restore daily transit within one to two weeks using interventions that address the most common single drivers without depending on a diagnosis. Most people on this phase alone will see resolution and can move into Phase 2 maintenance.

**Magnesium, 300–600 mg in the evening.** Magnesium is the most reliable single lever for chronic constipation in Peat's framework. The deficit is near-universal in adults eating the modern diet, and the corrective dose works through three independent mechanisms: it relaxes intestinal smooth muscle (the same muscle that cannot contract rhythmically without adequate ATP, which magnesium is a cofactor for), it draws water into the bowel lumen osmotically when given as the oxide or citrate form, and it supports the thyroid and parathyroid pathways that govern peristalsis upstream. Glycinate or chloride are the preferred forms when the goal is systemic repletion plus a mild motility effect; oxide and citrate are stronger as osmotic laxatives but less well absorbed. Start at 300 mg taken with the evening meal or before bed. If no morning movement appears within two or three days, increase to 400–600 mg. The signal that the dose is correct is a soft, formed morning stool; the signal that the dose is too high is loose stool, which calls for stepping back. See [[magnesium]] for full repletion context.

**Vitamin C, 1–3 grams as needed.** Ascorbic acid produces the same bowel-loosening effect at higher doses and can be used in addition to or in place of magnesium when transit needs a faster nudge. Three grams in divided doses through the day is typical; some people respond at one gram. The collagen-supporting and anti-stress secondary benefits make it a reasonable parallel addition.

**Daily raw carrot salad.** Non-negotiable across both phases. One medium carrot, scraped or shredded lengthwise (lengthwise maximizes the surface area of indigestible fiber), tossed with a teaspoon of coconut or olive oil, a splash of vinegar, and a pinch of salt. Eaten about thirty to sixty minutes after the first solid meal. The carrot does three things at once that matter for constipation specifically: it provides mechanical-clearance fiber that does *not* ferment, it acts as a mild germicide on the gram-negative bacteria producing the endotoxin that drives slow transit, and it binds the conjugated estrogen and bile acids the liver is dumping after the meal. See [[carrot-salad]] for the full mechanism and [[endotoxin-reduction]] for how it integrates into the broader gut work.

**Warm water with salt, morning.** A cup of warm water with a quarter-teaspoon of salt shortly after waking. This is the simplest tool for activating the gastrocolic reflex — the parasympathetic signal that drives the morning movement most healthy people experience within an hour of getting up. The warmth dilates the splanchnic vasculature, the volume distends the stomach, and the salt restores the sodium-driven absorption that chronic adrenaline depletes. Caffeine works on the same reflex through a different mechanism.

**Coffee, one to two cups, with cream and sugar.** A second tool for the gastrocolic reflex, well-documented and consistent with Peat's framework. The cream and sugar matter — black coffee on an empty stomach raises adrenaline rather than easing it, and the resulting vasoconstriction can paradoxically slow rather than speed transit in someone whose stress system is already running hot. Coffee with food and sweetener acts mostly through its direct stimulation of colonic contraction.

**Well-cooked greens, sparingly.** Spinach, chard, beet greens, or cooked cabbage at one meal per day if tolerated. The cooking water can be retained or discarded depending on oxalate sensitivity; the magnesium that cooked greens are valued for largely extracts into the water. Raw greens — lettuce, kale salads, raw spinach — are explicitly excluded; Peat described raw lettuce as "one of the most mucus-promoting inflammatory agents" and treated raw cruciferous as a direct contributor to dysbiosis.

Phase 1 should produce visible improvement within three to seven days. If the picture has not begun to shift by day fourteen on full Phase 1 execution, the cause is upstream and Phase 2 is mandatory.

## Phase 2: Root cause (weeks 2-12)

Phase 2 moves the work upstream to the mechanisms Phase 1 was masking. The Phase 1 daily core continues throughout; these are additions and substitutions, not replacements.

**Thyroid evaluation as the first move.** Constipation in someone with cold extremities, low morning temperature, and a slow resting pulse is hypothyroidism until proven otherwise. The mainstream lab approach (TSH alone) misses most cases Peat's framework catches; the working picture combines morning oral temperature, resting pulse, Achilles reflex relaxation phase, and the clinical pattern (dry skin, thinning hair, slow mentation, cold intolerance, slow bowel) into a clinical diagnosis that can be acted on while waiting for or alongside lab work. Restoring thyroid function — T4 + T3 or T3-only dosing per [[hypothyroid-recovery]] — is upstream of nearly every constipation lever in Phase 1, including the magnesium response. A hypothyroid gut cannot keep itself sterile, peristalsis runs at a fraction of normal, bile production drops, gastric acid drops, and the colon's water-resorbing capacity rises (giving harder stool). See [[hypothyroidism]] for the diagnostic frame and [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for the dosing protocol.

**Healthy fat at every meal to stimulate bile.** Bile is the body's primary lubricant of the digestive tract and one of its main motility signals. Bile release is triggered by fat arriving in the duodenum; meals with insufficient fat produce insufficient bile and the next stool dries out before it reaches the rectum. The dietary correction is to ensure that every solid meal contains a meaningful fraction of saturated fat — coconut oil cooked into eggs, butter on potatoes or bread, full-fat dairy, cream in the coffee, the yolk of the egg eaten alongside the white. Low-fat diets aggravate the constipation pattern through this mechanism alone, independent of fiber and water. Peat's reference cases consistently combined adequate saturated fat with restricted polyunsaturated fat (see [[anti-stress-diet]] for the food baseline).

**Test for low gastric acid with an HCl + pepsin trial.** Stomach acid decline with age, with hypothyroidism, and with chronic stress is one of the most under-diagnosed contributors to slow transit. Without adequate acid, protein digestion is incomplete, the pyloric valve does not open on schedule, the duodenum does not signal bile release, and the entire downstream cascade runs late. Peat addressed this directly in a [[2018-10-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-medical-misinformation|2018 caller question]] where a listener reported that "a smaller dose of Betaine HCl, like 150–200mg, with the glycine/gelatin you recommend" had cleared up his digestion. Peat's response was direct: "Sure. Even strong vinegar or acetic acid helps the enzymes to function. The betaine hydrochloride definitely acidifies the stomach and works." The trial is straightforward: 150–500 mg of betaine HCl with a high-protein meal. If digestion improves and the meal is followed by a normal sense of fullness rather than bloating, low acid was contributing. If burning, warmth, or discomfort appears, the dose was too high or the cause is elsewhere. Vinegar in salad dressing or a tablespoon of lemon juice in water before meals is the gentler trial. See the [[2018-08-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-critical-thinking-in-academia|2018 critical-thinking episode]] for Peat's broader endorsement of HCl and pancreatic-enzyme support in older patients and those with low secretion.

**Reduce fermentable fiber.** The most counterintuitive Phase 2 move. People with chronic constipation on a "healthy" diet are frequently eating ten to twenty grams a day of psyllium, bran, oat fiber, FODMAP-rich whole grains, and "prebiotic" supplements — all of which ferment in the lower intestine and feed the bacterial populations whose endotoxin output is driving the inflammation that slowed transit in the first place. Removing the supplement load and switching the grain baseline to white rice, well-cooked potato (peeled), or sourdough (which has had most of its fermentable fiber pre-digested by the starter) commonly produces visible improvement within a week. This intervention runs *against* the conventional advice and is the single most predictable point of confusion. The fiber section below explains why.

**Sugar from ripe fruit, juice, honey, and milk.** Peat explicitly noted in his email correspondence that "sugar sometimes helps with constipation" — a counterintuitive observation rooted in the way well-absorbed carbohydrates support thyroid function, smooth muscle ATP, and the cellular water content that determines stool consistency. The dietary baseline through Phase 2 should include adequate fruit (well-ripened), milk if tolerated, and orange juice with meals. Restricting sugar in a constipated state, particularly when combined with adequate protein and salt, often worsens rather than helps the picture.

Expected response: by week six, daily soft-formed transit with no straining and resolution of the co-symptoms (cold extremities warming, mood lifting, brain fog clearing). If by week twelve the picture is still incomplete despite full execution, the severe-pattern section applies.

## Severe / refractory pattern

Chronic constipation persisting beyond thirty days of full Phase 1 + Phase 2 execution requires bridging interventions and a medical workup to rule out obstruction.

**Castor oil packs, externally.** A folded cotton cloth saturated with castor oil, applied warm to the lower right abdomen for thirty to sixty minutes, three to five times per week. The mechanism in Peat's framework is the same as the mechanism of cascara: anthraquinone-family compounds (ricinoleic acid in castor oil's case) that stimulate intestinal contraction without flogging the smooth muscle in the way habitual oral laxatives do. The topical application avoids the systemic effects.

**Cascara sagrada, low-dose evening, short-term bridge.** One or two capsules in the evening for two to four weeks while the upstream work catches up. Peat reversed the conventional warning about cascara causing "melanosis coli," attributing the pigmentation to chronic constipation itself rather than the herb. He treated cascara as preferable to senna or stimulant laxatives because its emodin is structurally similar to tetracycline and carries genuine anti-inflammatory and metabolic benefits in addition to the motility effect. See [[cascara-sagrada]] for the full mechanism and cautions. The intent is a bridge, not a permanent tool.

**Pharmaceutical laxative as last-resort bridge, NOT long-term.** Osmotic laxatives (polyethylene glycol, lactulose) for short-course use when the upstream work needs more time to act and impaction is a real risk. Stimulant laxatives (bisacodyl, senna) are the next tier down — useful for a one-off, problematic for daily use because of the well-documented colon-tolerance pattern that develops within months. The frame: a pharmaceutical laxative is a bridge across a crisis, not a permanent answer; the daily protocol is the answer.

**Colonoscopy to rule out obstruction.** Sudden change in bowel pattern, especially in someone over forty, requires a workup. Mass, stricture, diverticular disease, and inflammatory bowel disease all present with constipation that Phase 1 and Phase 2 cannot fix. The protocol assumes a functional cause; the workup rules out the structural one.

## The fiber question

Peat's distinction between mechanical-clearance fiber and fermentable fiber is the single most important point in the constipation protocol, and the point most consistently lost in the standard nutritional advice. The modern medical literature treats "fiber" as a single category — the difference between soluble and insoluble is acknowledged but largely flattened into "more is better." Peat treated the two functional categories as nearly opposite in their effects.

**Mechanical-clearance fiber is therapeutic.** Raw carrot, well-cooked bamboo shoots, well-cooked greens, mushroom cell walls — these have three properties in common: they are non-fermentable in the lower intestine, they pass through largely intact and act as a mechanical broom, and they often carry secondary antimicrobial compounds (the carrot's germicidal volatiles, bamboo's antiseptic structure, mushroom cell-wall mannans). They speed transit without feeding the bacterial populations that produce endotoxin. They are the fiber Peat endorsed.

**Fermentable fiber is the problem.** Psyllium, oat bran, fructo-oligosaccharides ("FOS"), inulin, most resistant-starch supplements, and the "prebiotic" category in general — these dissolve or get broken down by bacteria in the lower intestine, releasing short-chain fatty acids (acetate, butyrate, propionate) along with bacterial growth and gas. The functional-medicine field treats the short-chain fatty acids as beneficial; Peat treated them as toxins. "Acetic acid, butyric acid, and propionic acid all have their potential value, but they are also well known as toxins," he said in the 2021 SIBO episode. "You can get all your nutrients with things that are resistant to fermentation." The bloating, gas, and worse-not-better transit that many people experience when they "add fiber" for constipation tracks this distinction directly.

**The whole-grain question.** Standard medical advice for constipation recommends whole grains over refined. In Peat's framework the recommendation is inverted for refractory constipation: white rice, sourdough bread (pre-fermented), peeled potatoes, and fruit are the carbohydrate baseline; wheat bran, oatmeal, brown rice, and high-fiber whole-grain cereals are restricted because the fermentable fraction feeds dysbiosis. The point is not that whole grains are universally harmful — it is that they are the wrong tool for a constipation pattern driven by endotoxin and slow transit.

## What to AVOID

- **Chronic stimulant laxatives.** Bisacodyl, senna, or castor oil taken orally on a daily long-term basis. The well-documented tolerance pattern produces dependence within months and a colon that no longer contracts without the drug.
- **Daily psyllium or fiber supplements.** Bacterial fermentation, endotoxin production, and bloating worse than the original constipation in a meaningful fraction of users.
- **"High fiber" whole-grain diets** as the answer to refractory constipation. They feed exactly the bacteria producing the inflammation that drives the slow transit.
- **Probiotic supplements as the primary intervention.** Peat treated the entire probiotic doctrine as a category error — the goal is a sterile upper small intestine, not a populated one. Sterilizing what is there (carrot, bamboo, short-course antibiotic) is upstream of seeding new strains. See [[gut-restoration]] for the broader frame.
- **Mineral oil and chronic stool softeners.** Bind fat-soluble vitamins, suppress bile reabsorption, and treat the symptom without touching the cause.
- **Low-fat diet.** Suppresses bile flow and removes one of the two main motility signals.
- **Restrictive water-only fasting** as a constipation reset. The colon needs substrate and signal, not absence.

## Tracking signals

- Daily morning bowel movement, ideally within an hour of waking.
- Bristol scale type 3 or 4 — sausage-shaped, smooth, soft-formed, no pellets, no liquid.
- No straining; complete evacuation within five minutes.
- No abdominal bloating mid-afternoon or evening.
- Stool not strongly malodorous — Peat's heuristic was "the color and odor of a baby's."
- Co-symptom improvement: warmer extremities, better mood, clearer thinking within an hour of meals, improving morning temperature toward 98.0°F.

If transit normalizes but the co-symptoms persist, the gut work has succeeded but the upstream metabolic work (thyroid, [[anti-stress-diet]] baseline) is incomplete.

## Failure modes

- **Treating with fiber alone.** The single most common error. Magnesium-thyroid-bile is the lever; fiber is one accessory.
- **Ignoring thyroid.** Phase 1 produces relief in many people but Phase 2 thyroid restoration is what makes the relief permanent. Skipping it produces recurrent constipation every time Phase 1 is interrupted.
- **Chronic magnesium without addressing root cause.** Magnesium is a Phase 1 tool, not a lifetime answer. If the same dose stops working after months, the upstream cause has not been addressed and the magnesium is masking rather than correcting.
- **Adding probiotics or prebiotics to "support" the protocol.** Both work against it.
- **Switching to a low-FODMAP-but-still-low-fat diet.** Removes the fermentation problem but starves bile flow. The fix is low-fermentable-fiber *plus* adequate saturated fat.
- **Stopping coffee or salt to "be healthier."** Both are motility tools in Peat's framework; eliminating them removes Phase 1 leverage.

## When to escalate

A workup is mandatory, not optional, when any of the following appears:

- **Sudden change in bowel pattern** lasting more than two weeks, especially in someone over forty.
- **Blood in stool** — bright red on the surface, dark and tarry, or mixed in.
- **Unexplained weight loss** alongside the constipation.
- **Severe abdominal pain** that does not resolve with evacuation.
- **Vomiting or inability to pass gas** alongside no bowel movement for more than three days — possible obstruction.
- **Family history of colon cancer** with new constipation pattern.
- **Iron-deficiency anemia** appearing alongside constipation in an adult — occult bleeding workup required.

The protocol assumes a functional cause that responds to the daily core. Structural causes require imaging, scoping, and surgical or oncological care. Use Phase 1 as a stabilization layer while the workup is in progress; do not let it substitute for the workup.
