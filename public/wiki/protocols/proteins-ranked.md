# Protein Sources Ranked

Most dietary rankings of protein sources treat grams as the governing variable. A chicken breast scores well because it delivers twenty-eight grams for a hundred and sixty calories. Whey isolate scores better still because the ratio is cleaner. Tofu sits in the middle because it hits the same macro target with a different source. In Ray Peat's framework, this entire exercise misses the point. **The amino acid profile matters more than the grams.** Two foods that deliver the same number of grams of protein can produce opposite physiological effects depending on which amino acids are present and in what proportion, and the mainstream ranking routinely inverts the real hierarchy by ignoring that distinction.

The central villains in Peat's reading are three amino acids most nutritionists treat as unambiguously desirable: [[tryptophan]], [[methionine]], and [[cysteine]]. Tryptophan is the precursor to serotonin, which Peat associated with inflammation, fibrosis, and the generalized changes of aging. Methionine and cysteine inhibit thyroid function and suppress mitochondrial energy production. In his newsletter "Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging," Peat noted that "restricting only tryptophan, or only cysteine, produces a greater extension of the life span than achieved in most of the studies of caloric restriction." A food whose amino acid profile concentrates these three compounds is not a neutral protein source. It is a pro-aging signal delivered on every plate.

The central hero is [[glycine]], the simplest non-essential amino acid and the dominant constituent of [[gelatin]]. Glycine opposes serotonin at the tissue level, stabilizes cells against excitatory stimulation, inhibits lipolysis, protects the liver, and calms the intestinal lining. It is the biochemical mirror image of tryptophan, and in Peat's framework any ranking of proteins that does not privilege glycine over tryptophan is scoring the wrong test.

The single line that holds the whole framework together comes from Peat's [[gelatin]] article: muscle meats alone are **"essentially a refined food"** relative to what traditional populations actually consumed. A chicken breast, a pork chop, or a boneless steak represents a twentieth-century invention. Traditional cultures ate the whole animal, including the skin, feet, tails, joints, and connective tissue, which delivered glycine and proline in quantities that balanced whatever tryptophan came from the muscle. Modern butchering removed the balancing half of the package and left the pro-inflammatory half. The ranking below is an attempt to put the balance back.

## S-Tier: The Foundation

**Gelatin** is the protein Peat recommended most insistently across decades of writing, not because it is complete but because it corrects the imbalances that muscle meat creates. It is the only common dietary protein that contains no tryptophan at all, and only trace amounts of cysteine and methionine. Using it as a significant protein source shifts the amino acid balance away from the stress-and-aging profile of pure muscle meat and toward the traditional profile that sustained human health before modern butchering. Peat himself took this far enough to describe a personal experiment: after years of waking through the night, "the first time I had several grams of gelatin just before bedtime, I slept without interruption for about 9 hours." Ten to thirty grams a day is the practical range he recommended; up to a hundred grams a day appears in the therapeutic literature he cited.

**Milk** sits beside gelatin at the foundation because it solves a different problem. Two quarts of milk a day yields roughly two thousand milligrams of calcium in a 2:1 ratio to phosphate, which is the ratio Peat considered critical for suppressing [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]] and the cascade of inflammatory signaling that drives osteoporosis, arterial calcification, and the degenerative cluster he traced to chronic stress. The lactose in milk serves as the absorption cofactor for the calcium, and the saturated fat delivers vitamins A, D, E, and K2 in a matrix the body can use without supplementation. Peat's prescription was unambiguous: "I recommend at least two quarts of milk and two quarts of orange juice every day."

**Cheese** is milk concentrated. Aged hard varieties, particularly Parmigiano Reggiano, deliver the same calcium load in a package that has had most of its lactose fermented away and most of its tryptophan removed with the whey. "Cheese, by contrast, has the whey removed and therefore contains less tryptophan," Peat wrote. Four ounces of parmesan delivers roughly what a quart of milk delivers for calcium, with a cleaner amino acid profile and a higher menaquinone (vitamin K2) content. Cheese is also the lactose-intolerant adult's route to the same calcium benefit milk provides.

## A-Tier: Foundational With Caveats

**Eggs** were once, in Peat's framing, "the perfect food." Modern feed changed that. Between the 1930s and the 1970s, the polyunsaturated fat content of commercial eggs rose sharply as corn and soy replaced the traditional chicken diet, and Peat's recommended ceiling dropped accordingly. In the US, his recommendation was one or two eggs per day. In Mexico, where he knew the chickens and their feed, he ate four or five. The yolk delivers the fat-soluble vitamin package (A, D, E, K2), choline, cholesterol as the raw material for pregnenolone and progesterone, and a balanced amino acid profile. The caveats are three: always pair with carbohydrate to prevent the blood sugar crash that produces pseudo-allergic reactions, soft-cook the yolk to protect the oxidizable fats, and judge quality by taste rather than label (a fishy off-flavor signals flaxseed feeding and should disqualify the egg).

**Shellfish** sit in A-Tier for a specific reason: they are eaten as whole organisms. An oyster, a shrimp, a clam, a scallop. When you eat the whole body, you eat muscle, organ, and connective tissue in their natural ratio, and the resulting amino acid profile is balanced in the same way gelatin balances a steak. Peat stated the principle directly: "You can approximate that by eating shellfish, shrimps, and oysters, where you eat the whole body of the animal." Shellfish additionally carry the mineral density of seawater (zinc, selenium, iodine, copper) in a bioavailable whole-food matrix that inland foods cannot match. Weekly consumption, not daily, is the practical cadence.

**Liver** is the most nutrient-dense common food in the human diet and the one Peat most insistently recommended in weekly rather than daily portions. Four to six ounces of beef liver a week supplies more preformed vitamin A than a week of anything else, along with meaningful copper, B12, and the intracellular form of vitamin C. The reason for the weekly cap is the same as the reason for the recommendation: the concentration is so high that daily consumption begins to suppress thyroid function through chronic retinol loading, and the phosphate-to-calcium ratio drifts in the wrong direction. Peat's own practice, as he described it: "Typically about once a week, some oysters or liver."

## B-Tier: Acceptable With Balance

**Beef** and **lamb** are the two muscle meats Peat recommended consistently, and the reason is ruminant biology. The rumen, a specialized fermentation chamber in the cow or sheep's stomach, systematically biohydrogenates polyunsaturated fats from whatever the animal ate, converting roughly 98% of dietary PUFA into saturated fat before it reaches the animal's tissue. This is why beef fat and lamb fat remain safe even when the animals are grain-fed, and why Peat's guidance during radiation discussions was unambiguous: "Avoid pork and non-ruminants because those reflect whatever they ate and are usually highly unsaturated and anti-thyroid. Beef and lamb are saturated." Lamb arguably carries an edge because sheep spend more of their lives on pasture, but the two are close cousins in the ranking.

The caveat that applies to both is the gelatin rule. A boneless, lean cut of beef or lamb eaten by itself delivers the high-tryptophan, high-methionine profile of pure muscle meat. A lean cut consumed in a gelatinous broth, or served alongside chicken feet or pigs' ears, delivers something closer to the traditional whole-animal profile. Peat's suggested ratio was a tablespoon of gelatin for every half-pound of muscle meat, scaling up for larger portions.

**White fish** from low-fat ocean species is acceptable as a rotation protein. The rule Peat applied to fish was the inverse of the one he applied to supplements: avoid the high-omega-3 fatty fish that nutrition authorities most enthusiastically recommend. Salmon, mackerel, herring, and sardines concentrate polyunsaturated fat in a form that dominates whatever protein benefit the fish provides. Cod, sole, tilapia, and other lean white fish are acceptable in moderation, and their ocean-sourced mineral profile is a minor virtue.

**Goat and game meats** occupy the same general tier as lamb and lean beef. Goat shares ruminant biology with sheep and cattle. Game meats are generally lean enough that their limited fat load is not a concern, though the amino acid profile still needs gelatin balancing. These are rotation foods, not staples.

## C-Tier: Sparingly, With Full Awareness

**Pork** and **chicken** (and turkey) are the monogastric non-ruminants whose body fat directly mirrors whatever feed they ate. Modern corn-and-soy feeding has loaded both with linoleic acid at levels that make them functionally similar to the seed oils Peat spent his career warning against. Peat's account of the history was specific: "In the 1950s, the industry went through a change. They discovered they could economize on food for pigs and chickens by poisoning their thyroid glands so they wouldn't eat as much but would still get fat. They found that polyunsaturated fats suppress the metabolic rate."

Pork loin trimmed of visible fat is the least-bad pork cut. Chicken breast with skin removed is the least-bad chicken cut. Both can appear in a diet without causing immediate harm if they are kept infrequent, paired with gelatin, and balanced by the rest of the protocol. Neither is recommended as a staple. Peat's own practice was to cook pork skin for the gelatin in it and discard most of the fat, and to eat chicken primarily as broth where the bones contributed collagen and the meat was a secondary consideration.

## D-Tier: Protein Powders

All isolated protein powders fail the same test, which is that they strip the protein out of the food matrix that made it safe. Whey concentrates the tryptophan that cheese-making was designed to remove. Casein is the same in slower-release form. Pea, rice, and hemp isolates replace animal imbalance with plant imbalance, and in the case of hemp and soy carry real goitrogenic potential. BCAA and EAA supplements are the purest expression of the failure: free amino acids with no buffering from fat, sugar, or calcium, delivered directly into the bloodstream in a form that maximally provokes cortisol and insulin.

Peat's specific objection to processed protein powders extended to their manufacturing chemistry. "Powdered proteins contain two or three amino acids that are very unstable. When they break down, they can produce inflammatory toxins. Cysteine and tryptophan are the worst for breaking down during processing." The one protein powder that escapes this objection is gelatin, precisely because it contains neither cysteine nor tryptophan.

## F-Tier: Peat's Explicit "Avoid" List

**Soy** drew some of Peat's most forceful language. The isoflavones genistein and daidzein are phytoestrogens that bind human estrogen receptors and also activate aromatase, the enzyme that produces endogenous estrogen. Soy is goitrogenic, PUFA-loaded, and in infant formula "seriously harming lots of kids." Soy protein isolate, soy milk, tofu as a staple, and textured vegetable protein all sit outside the acceptable range. Small amounts of traditionally fermented soy as a condiment are tolerable; soy as a primary protein source is not.

**Beans and legumes** as primary protein sources were excluded for a different set of reasons. Peat devoted an entire 1993 newsletter ("The Bean Syndrome") to the lectins, oligosaccharides, and anti-thyroid compounds in common legumes. His verdict in a 2016 interview was succinct: "Potatoes are infinitely better than beans as a source of protein." When a tuber outperforms a food on the dimension that food is supposed to specialize in, the food has no place in the diet.

**Wheat gluten and seitan** combine the worst features of grain protein and isolated protein: high glutamate, low glycine, pro-inflammatory profile, and the gliadin that drives intestinal permeability independent of celiac disease.

**High-omega-3 fatty fish** (salmon, mackerel, herring, sardine, anchovy) are the foods mainstream nutrition most enthusiastically recommends and Peat most consistently warned against. The PUFA load dominates whatever protein benefit the fish provides. The ranking here is not the fish's fault. It is the consequence of applying the same lipid oxidation framework Peat applied to vegetable oils.

## The Gelatin Rule

The single most practical rule that follows from this ranking is that every muscle meat meal benefits from gelatin presence. Peat's phrase, worth memorizing, is that muscle meat alone is "essentially a refined food." The refinement is the removal of the connective tissue, skin, bones, and organs that traditional cultures consumed alongside the muscle. When traditional populations ate head cheese, oxtails, pigs' feet, chicken feet, and joint-bone soups, they automatically ingested roughly half their protein as [[glycine]] and proline, which balanced the tryptophan, methionine, and cysteine in the muscle. The modern diet privileges the expensive tender cuts and discards the very tissues that balanced them.

The practical correction is a tablespoon or two of powdered gelatin dissolved in a broth, sauce, or dessert at any meal containing significant muscle meat. Peat's specific guidance: "If a half-pound of steak is eaten, it would probably be reasonable to have about 20 grams of gelatin at approximately the same time." The gelatin does not replace the steak. It balances it. Without the balance, the steak is delivering a biochemical signal functionally similar to the cortisol-driven breakdown of muscle during extreme stress. With the balance, the steak is delivering the traditional amino acid profile that human metabolism evolved to handle.

Gelatin is not optional in this framework. It is the structural pairing that makes muscle meat tolerable. A diet built around chicken, beef, pork, or any combination of them, without gelatin to balance it, is a diet that generates the amino acid profile of chronic stress on every plate.

## The "Protein Without Carbohydrate" Rule

Running parallel to the gelatin rule is a second rule that applies to every protein meal regardless of source: never eat protein without carbohydrate. Peat's statement of the mechanism is direct: "Eating protein by itself can cause a big surge of cortisol. Preceding the protein with some carbohydrate makes the protein go farther, otherwise under the influence of cortisol a lot of protein is used just for energy."

A chicken breast eaten alone, however lean and however pure the protein, is a stress meal. A chicken breast eaten alongside a glass of orange juice, a piece of fruit, or a cup of milk is a normal meal. The difference is hormonal. Protein without carbohydrate triggers glucagon, cortisol, and adrenaline to maintain blood glucose; these same hormones mobilize free fatty acids, block glucose oxidation through the Randle cycle, and drive the body into the stress state the [[anti-stress-diet]] exists to prevent.

The practical fix is a short preload. Peat told Andrew Murray: "I think it's good to precede a big protein meal by maybe 15 or 20 minutes with fruits so that you get your liver stocked with some glycogen. That way, you don't respond so violently when you eat pure protein." A few ounces of fruit or orange juice fifteen minutes before a protein meal is enough to short-circuit the cortisol response. In practice, this aligns the protocol with the milk-and-orange-juice foundation: if milk and fruit are present at every meal, the protein is automatically preloaded, and the rule takes care of itself.

## Protein Targets

The numbers come from Peat's anti-stress diet protocol and are worth repeating alongside the ranking because the sourcing decisions above only matter if the total dosage is in range.

- **Hypothyroid floor:** 80 grams a day. "Until your metabolic rate is higher, 80 to 100 grams would be better" than pushing harder, because additional muscle-meat protein suppresses the thyroid further through tryptophan, methionine, and cysteine loading.
- **Military minimum:** 100 grams a day. Peat cited a military study finding that ordinary work required at least 100 grams of "good protein" for both men and women at military age.
- **Sedentary adult typical:** 120 to 150 grams a day. "I've always been very sedentary, but I have usually had close to 150 grams daily," Peat wrote in an email.
- **Athletic:** 150 grams and up, scaling toward "a gram per pound of body weight" for intense training. Recovery depends on carbohydrate more than protein.

The ceiling on protein is not a hard number. It is the point at which body temperature and pulse begin to fall, which is the signal that tryptophan and methionine are suppressing thyroid function. Broda Barnes, experimenting with a higher-meat diet, "found he had to double his thyroid dose" before he stabilized. The practical instruction is to watch the metabolic indicators rather than the protein log, and to use gelatin and dairy as the structural buffers that allow higher total protein without paying the thyroid cost. See the [[anti-stress-diet]] article for the full discussion of how protein fits inside the larger macronutrient framework.

The ranking above is not a scoreboard for counting grams. It is a guide to which sources deliver those grams in a form the body can use without triggering the stress cascade Peat spent fifty years identifying. A hundred grams of protein from whey isolate and chicken breast is a different meal than a hundred grams from milk, cheese, eggs, gelatinous broth, and weekly shellfish. The first is the amino acid signature of chronic stress. The second is the amino acid signature of traditional human nutrition, reassembled out of the foods a modern refrigerator can still supply.
