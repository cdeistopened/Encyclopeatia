# Cycle-Phase Eating

The cycle is not the problem. In a metabolically intact woman — eating enough, warm hands and feet, morning temperature near 98°F, resting pulse in the high seventies — the twenty-eight-day rhythm is barely felt. There is a mild salt and sugar craving around ovulation, a second one in the week before the period, two days of moderate flow, and the cycle begins again. The "PMS" of mainstream gynecology, the cramping and bloating and mood collapse the SSRI advertisements take for granted, is not the normal expression of cycling hormones. It is the noise produced by a hormonal field that has lost its baseline. Get the baseline back, and the cycle becomes a feedback channel — a monthly readout of whether the diet, the thyroid, and the stress load are right.

This protocol is the eating layer of that frame. It is not "cycle syncing." Peat never told a woman to eat a luteal-phase plate that differed dramatically from her follicular-phase plate. The staples are the same year-round, the same as on the [[core-diet-protocol|Core Diet Protocol]]: dairy, orange juice, salt, gelatin, eggs, ripe fruit, liver weekly, oysters weekly, the daily raw carrot. What changes across the phases is the *emphasis* — more salt and sugar at ovulation and premenstrually because that is when estrogen drives the cravings that warn of an oncoming aldosterone surge, more gelatin and broth in the follicular phase when the tryptophan-and-methionine load of muscle meat is hardest to clear, more progesterone (the substance, not the food) and more aspirin in the four to seven days before menstruation, more iron-aware feeding in the bleeding days when blood is lost. The instrument is the cycle itself. The adjustments are small. The framework is unchanged from the underlying diet.

What this article gives you: the hormonal arc of the cycle in Peat's reading, the phase-by-phase eating template, the "when something is broken" cases (anovulation, short luteal phase, full PMS, PMDD), the counter-arguments to the cycle-syncing and seed-cycling fads that have absorbed most of the popular conversation, and the data gaps that follow from running a framework that mainstream endocrinology does not share. The foundational pharmacology of [[estrogen]], [[progesterone]], [[serotonin]], and [[blood-sugar]] is referenced through hub links, not restated.

## The Cycle as a Calibrating Signal

A normal cycle in Peat's framework is the visible expression of a working metabolism. When it goes wrong, the symptom is upstream of the uterus. The corpus luteum is the most metabolically demanding endocrine organ a non-pregnant body produces — for two weeks every month it must make 20 to 30 milligrams of progesterone daily, which requires cholesterol, vitamin A, thyroid function, and enough mitochondrial respiration in the ovarian theca cells to keep the steroidogenic cascade running. Anything that suppresses metabolic rate suppresses the corpus luteum first.

Peat made the diagnostic point in his [[progesterone-summaries-progesterone-supplementation-dosage-of-progesterone|*Progesterone Summaries*]] newsletter: "Symptoms in cycling women are most common around ovulation and in the premenstrual week, when the estrogen/progesterone ratio is normally highest. At puberty, in the early twenties and in the late thirties and menopause are the ages when the ratio is most often disturbed — and these are also the ages when thyroid disorders are commonest in women." That sentence is the entire framework compressed. The cycle phases at which symptoms appear (mid-cycle and premenstrual) are the phases at which estrogen-to-progesterone runs highest. The decades at which the cycle goes wrong (puberty, early twenties, late thirties, perimenopause) are the decades at which thyroid function goes wrong. The eating protocol is built around protecting those two windows.

### The hormonal arc — corrected

The popular framing — "estrogen is the follicular hormone, progesterone is the luteal hormone" — is half right and half wrong. Peat corrected the standard description in his [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|*Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone* (2006)]] newsletter:

> The follicular phase is a time of low steroid production by the ovary, until near the end of the phase, just before ovulation, when estrogen rises. The luteal phase is a time of high estrogen and high progesterone synthesis. Many publications describe the follicular phase as a time of high estrogen, and the luteal phase as a time of low estrogen, roughly the opposite of the actual situation.

This matters for the eating protocol because the assumption that "estrogen is high in the follicular phase" produces the cycle-syncing recommendation to lean into estrogen-buffering foods (cruciferous vegetables, flax, fiber) in the first half of the cycle. In Peat's reading the ovary is quiet in the first ten days. Estrogen rises briefly at ovulation, falls, and then both estrogen *and* progesterone rise together through the luteal phase. The corpus luteum is the only thing standing between the woman and unopposed estrogen exposure in the second half of the cycle — and the corpus luteum is the structure that fails first when thyroid is low, when stress is high, or when the diet is short on protein, sugar, salt, or vitamin A.

A healthy 28-day cycle looks like this:

- **Days 1-5 (menstruation, also Days 1-5 of the new cycle).** Both hormones at their lowest. Endometrial sloughing. Cramping, when present, is a prostaglandin and serotonin signal. Iron is lost.
- **Days 6-13 (follicular).** Ovary quiet. Steroid production low. The body is in its most "baseline" state of the month.
- **Day 14 (ovulation, approximate).** Estrogen peaks. LH surge. Egg released. Brief progesterone rise begins. Temperature shifts up 0.4-0.6°F overnight.
- **Days 15-26 (luteal).** Corpus luteum active. Both estrogen and progesterone elevated, with the progesterone peak around days 21-23 in a 28-day cycle.
- **Days 27-28 (premenstrual).** Corpus luteum involuting if no implantation. Both hormones drop sharply over 48 hours. The premenstrual symptom window opens precisely here because the estrogen does not fall first — progesterone falls first, and unopposed estrogen produces the migraine, the bloating, the mood collapse, the carbohydrate craving.

The eating protocol is built to support the metabolic rate the corpus luteum needs to do its work, and to buffer the two windows (ovulation, premenstrual) where the estrogen-to-progesterone ratio runs highest.

## Phase 1 — Follicular (Days 1-13)

The follicular phase is the most metabolically forgiving window of the cycle. Steroid production is low. The body is recovering iron and blood volume from menstruation. Energy returns by day 4 or 5 in most women. Thyroid demand is at its baseline.

### What to eat

Run the [[core-diet-protocol]] as the template. Two to three quarts of milk daily, a quart of orange juice, salt to taste, coffee with cream and sugar, two or three real meals plus a bedtime snack, two to four eggs daily, liver four to eight ounces weekly, oysters weekly, daily raw carrot or well-cooked greens. The daily floor is 80-100 grams of protein.

Phase-specific emphasis:

- **Gelatin priority over muscle meat.** Muscle meats are heavy in tryptophan, methionine, and cysteine — three amino acids that, in excess, raise [[serotonin]], suppress thyroid, and drive inflammatory signaling. The follicular-phase body is rebuilding iron and red blood cells; the tendency in mainstream nutrition is to lean hard on beef and chicken to "rebuild." Peat's correction is to balance the amino acid profile with gelatin (10-20 g daily from bone broth, or powdered glycine 3 g before bed) and to favor dairy and eggs over muscle meat as the protein floor. The goal is not less protein — it is *better-balanced* protein. From the [[anti-stress-diet]]: "the protein that arrives from milk, cheese, eggs, gelatinous broth, and occasional liver and shellfish is, in Peat's framework, better balanced than the same grams from chicken breast or steak."
- **Iron replacement from food, not pills.** The 30-60 mL of blood lost in a typical period carries 15-30 mg of iron. Liver (4-8 oz weekly) replaces it without raising the systemic iron burden that supplements do. Oysters weekly add zinc, copper, and selenium — three cofactors the corpus luteum will need in the next phase. Skip non-heme iron pills unless a documented anemia exists; iron supplements raise oxidative stress and feed gut bacteria more than they raise hemoglobin.
- **Fruit and OJ for buffer.** Estrogen excitability rises slowly through the follicular phase. Fruit, orange juice, and ripe tropical fruit (mango, papaya, pineapple) supply fructose and glucose in the ratio that the liver glycogenates best, restraining the cortisol-and-adrenaline rebound that drives glycemic instability in the first half of the cycle. A cup of orange juice with breakfast, another in the afternoon, and a piece of fruit with cheese before bed is the daily floor.
- **Magnesium-rich foods.** Calcium-rich foods are already standard (milk, cheese, well-cooked greens). The magnesium pair — bone broth, dark chocolate (one or two squares of 80%+ daily), well-cooked dark leafy greens, coffee — supports the smooth-muscle relaxation that follicular-phase women sometimes miss when their period was crampy. Magnesium glycinate or chloride (300-400 mg elemental at bedtime) is reasonable if dietary intake is uncertain. From the [[hot-flashes-energy-and-aging|*Hot Flashes, Energy, and Aging*]] newsletter: "Magnesium deficiency drives PTH upward; PTH is the osteoclast-activating signal."
- **Low PUFA, hard.** The [[pufa|PUFA]] load is the slow-moving background variable that determines how the next month's luteal phase will go. The follicular phase is the time to be strict — no seed oils in cooking, no salad-dressing seed oil, no fish oil capsules, no "heart-healthy" mayonnaise, no nuts as a snack staple. Butter, ghee, beef tallow, coconut oil are the cooking fats. The depletion clock is measured in years (full tissue turnover ~4 years); each strict week shortens that clock.

### What modest carb tolerance looks like

A common confusion: Peat is sometimes read as recommending uniformly high carbohydrate intake across the cycle. That is too simple. In the follicular phase, the liver's insulin sensitivity is at its monthly low — estrogen has not yet risen enough to suppress glucose oxidation, but progesterone is also low, and progesterone is the hormone that *improves* glucose tolerance in the second half of the cycle. The follicular-phase carb tolerance is therefore "modest." A woman who eats four servings of fruit and a bowl of ice cream every day in the follicular phase may find that the same intake feels heavier in the third trimester of the cycle. The lever: eat the carbs *with* fat (cream, butter, cheese) and *with* salt, not as isolated fruit or juice on an empty stomach. Frequent eating beats large carbohydrate meals. Two real meals plus three to four small snacks (orange with cheese, ice cream, milk with honey, fruit with gelatin pudding) holds blood sugar without the insulin spikes that produce the rebound hypoglycemia.

### What not to do in the follicular phase

- Do not fast. Cycling women are especially vulnerable to the cortisol-and-adrenaline cascade that intermittent fasting and prolonged time-restricted eating provoke. The follicular phase is a low-progesterone window; progesterone is the buffer against cortisol catabolism. Run a 14-hour overnight fast at most. Breakfast within an hour of waking.
- Do not run a low-carb or ketogenic diet. The "ketogenic-improves-PCOS" message is widespread; the [[pcos]] mechanism page argues the opposite. Sustained low-carb pushes the body into the same Randle-cycle, free-fatty-acid, cortisol-elevated state that estrogen produces — exactly the wrong substrate for a corpus luteum that is preparing to make progesterone two weeks later.
- Do not run a low-salt diet. Salt is non-negotiable across all four phases.
- Do not load up on cruciferous vegetables expecting "estrogen detox." The mainstream cycle-syncing protocol recommends broccoli, kale, and Brussels sprouts in the follicular phase for "estrogen clearance via DIM." Peat's read: raw cruciferous in volume raises goitrogenic load and suppresses thyroid. Well-cooked greens in moderate volume are fine; the estrogen-clearance pathway that actually works is the daily raw carrot (fiber binds bacterial endotoxin and conjugated estrogen in the gut, preventing reabsorption — the [[natural-estrogens|*Natural Estrogens*]] observation that serum estrogen drops measurably within days on the carrot).

## Phase 2 — Ovulation (Day 14, ±2 Days)

Ovulation is a 24-48 hour window. Estrogen has peaked, the LH surge has fired, the follicle has released its egg, and the corpus luteum is forming. Temperature shifts up 0.4-0.6°F overnight as progesterone begins to rise. The mid-cycle salt and sugar craving Peat noted in [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|*Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity*]] appears now: "Many young women periodically crave salt and sugar, especially around ovulation and premenstrually, when estrogen is high. Physiologically, this is similar to the food cravings of pregnancy."

### What to eat

The cravings are a metabolic signal. Honor them.

- **Salt to taste, generously.** A pinch of salt on every meal, salted orange juice in the morning, sip salted water in the afternoon if a headache appears. From the same newsletter: "When women premenstrually increase their salt intake according to their craving, the water retention can be prevented." The same logic applies at ovulation. The standard advice — "restrict salt around ovulation to prevent bloating" — drives aldosterone up and worsens exactly the symptom it is meant to prevent.
- **Sugar with the salt.** A bowl of ice cream, a glass of orange juice with a teaspoon of honey, a small piece of fruit with cheese. Estrogen at its peak is mildly cortisol-driving and free-fatty-acid mobilizing — both of which a small sugar-and-salt dose interrupts.
- **Slightly higher carb tolerance.** Progesterone is rising now, which improves glucose oxidation in the next few days. A woman who notices that her body handles fruit, bread, or starchy meals more easily in the days around ovulation is not imagining it; the metabolic context is more permissive.
- **Energy peak — train accordingly.** Many women report their highest training capacity in the 48-72 hours after ovulation. This is the window for harder workouts, heavier lifts, longer walks. Energy availability is at its monthly peak. The follicular phase is for skill work and recovery; the ovulatory window is for output.
- **Fertility window.** If conception is the goal, this is the highest-probability 48 hours of the cycle. See [[fertility]] for the male-and-female protocol; the eating layer is the same as the rest of the cycle, with the addition that vitamin E (mixed tocopherols, 100-400 IU daily) and oysters once in the week before ovulation supply the cofactors for both sperm motility and corpus luteum formation.

### What not to do at ovulation

- Do not run a hard caloric deficit. The corpus luteum is forming right now; calorie restriction at the ovulatory window is the most reliable way to produce a short luteal phase the next month. From Peat's [[menses|menses email]]: "The need for T3 increases premenstrually, and is probably greater with diabetes." Cycling women on diets routinely produce short luteal phases the next month because they restricted at exactly the wrong window.
- Do not interpret the ovulation pain ("mittelschmerz") as a problem requiring NSAID prophylaxis. A 12-24 hour mild pelvic ache is a normal ovulation signal. Persistent pain (>72 hours) or sharp unilateral pain is a cyst signal — route to a clinician.
- Do not use OPKs (ovulation predictor kits) as the sole tracking tool if you are trying to conceive. They detect the LH surge, which precedes ovulation by 24-36 hours; the actual fertility window opens 5 days before ovulation (sperm survives in cervical crypts). Combine OPK with cervical mucus and basal body temperature ([[temperature-tracking]]) as in the [[fertility]] protocol.

## Phase 3 — Luteal (Days 15-26)

The luteal phase is the most consequential window of the cycle. The corpus luteum is producing 20-30 mg of progesterone daily; if that production is adequate, the next two weeks pass quietly, the period starts on day 28 with normal flow and no cramping, and the cycle is calibrating well. If progesterone is inadequate — short luteal phase (under 10 days), low peak (under 10 ng/mL), or rapid early decline — the woman enters the premenstrual symptom window with all the markers of estrogen unopposed: water retention, breast tenderness, mood collapse, sugar craving, migraine.

### What to eat

This is the phase where Peat's framework most diverges from mainstream "cycle-syncing." The mainstream advice for the luteal phase is to eat more whole grains, more legumes, more "complex carbs" to "support serotonin." Peat's read: serotonin is the symptom, not the cure. The luteal-phase eating template is closer to a slight intensification of the [[core-diet-protocol]] than a different diet.

- **Slightly higher carb tolerance — use it.** Progesterone improves glucose oxidation through the luteal phase. The fruit, OJ, milk-with-sugar, ice cream, and starchy-with-butter meals the body wants now are not "carb cravings to fight." They are the protein-sparing carbohydrate the body is asking for to keep cortisol low. A bowl of ice cream at 3 PM and a glass of warm milk with honey before bed is excellent luteal-phase eating. From [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|*Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity*]]: when women "increase their salt intake according to their craving, the water retention can be prevented."
- **Protein priority shifts from gelatin balance to absolute volume.** The luteal-phase protein demand is roughly the same total grams (100-120 g/day) but the *composition* shifts. More milk, more cheese, more eggs, more shellfish — the easily-digestible proteins that come pre-paired with calcium, fat, and steroidogenic cofactors. Muscle meat in the luteal phase is fine when balanced with gelatin, but the dairy-and-eggs floor matters more here than in the follicular phase. The lactose in milk, in particular, has a mild diuretic action that helps with luteal-phase water retention.
- **Salt liberally; sugar tolerance peaks.** The premenstrual salt craving — and the bedtime sugar craving — are the body asking for substrates to interrupt the cortisol-CRH-aldosterone cascade that drives premenstrual edema and mood collapse. Salt to taste, salted snacks, sugar-and-salt before bed. The bedtime carbohydrate snack (orange with cheese, ice cream, milk with honey, fruit with gelatin) is the single highest-leverage tactical move of the luteal phase. It prevents the 3 AM blood-sugar drop that produces the 4 AM cortisol surge that worsens the next morning's mood.
- **Aspirin, low dose, with food.** [[Aspirin]] (81-325 mg daily, with food and a daily green leafy vegetable for vitamin K1) is Peat's standard luteal-phase adjunct. It inhibits aromatase modestly, suppresses prostaglandin E synthesis (which drives cramping and breast tenderness), and chelates iron. From the [[menopause-protocol|Menopause Protocol]]: "Aspirin blocks aromatase, and caffeine stimulates progesterone production." Start at 81 mg with breakfast; raise to 325 mg in the four days before menstruation if cramping or breast tenderness is significant.
- **Vitamin E with the cheese course.** Vitamin E (mixed tocopherols, 100-400 IU daily, taken with a fat-containing meal) supports both the corpus luteum directly and the membrane integrity that PUFA-loaded tissues lack. Peat preferred mixed tocopherols ([[vitamin-e|vitamin E page]]) and noted in his [[vitamin-e|vitamin E email]] that "the products have been changing frequently in the last 15 years, so I think it's good to be cautious and use minimal doses." 100-200 IU is a reasonable luteal-phase dose; raise to 400 IU if breast tenderness or migraine is severe.
- **Coffee with cream and sugar, two to four cups.** Caffeine stimulates the metabolic rate and supports progesterone production. Always with cream and sugar — never black, never with sweeteners. The cream supplies fat for steroidogenesis and slows caffeine absorption; the sugar prevents the cortisol-and-adrenaline overshoot that strong black coffee produces.
- **Daily raw carrot.** Continues from earlier phases. Fiber binds gut endotoxin and conjugated estrogen, preventing reabsorption. The [[natural-estrogens|*Natural Estrogens*]] data: serum estrogen falls measurably within days of the carrot becoming a daily fixture, with corresponding symptom relief.

### Cyclic progesterone — the optional supplement layer

If the diet, salt, sugar, aspirin, and vitamin E together do not fully control luteal-phase symptoms, the addition of cyclic progesterone is the Peat-canonical next step. The pharmacology, history (Katharina Dalton lineage), and detailed dosing live on the [[pms]] page; the eating-context summary is:

- **Progest-E (progesterone in vitamin E oil), 3-4 drops (~10-15 mg) daily, days 14-28 of the cycle.** From the [[progesterone-summaries-progesterone-supplementation-dosage-of-progesterone|*Progesterone Summaries*]] dosage section: "Three to four drops, or about 10-15 mg of progesterone, is often enough to bring the progesterone level up to normal. That amount can be taken days 14 through 28 of the menstrual cycle; this amount may be repeated once or twice during the day as needed to alleviate symptoms."
- **Apply on gums, inner lip, or against the cheek.** Trans-mucosal absorption bypasses the liver's first-pass metabolism. The vitamin E carrier is the active vehicle — progesterone-in-water creams absorb poorly and erratically.
- **Repeat the dose 1-2× across the day if symptoms persist.** Migraine, breast tenderness, irritability, water retention — three or four drops at symptom onset, with another three or four six hours later if needed.
- **Drop in the bleeding days.** Days 1-13 of the next cycle: no progesterone. The cycle resets.

The dose is reasonably flexible. From the same dosage section: "smaller amounts are effective when estrogen production is low, and if estrogen is extremely high, even large supplements of progesterone will have no clear effect; in that case, it is essential to regulate estrogen metabolism, by improving the diet, correcting a thyroid deficiency, etc."

### What not to do in the luteal phase

- Do not restrict salt. The single most reliably-wrong piece of mainstream advice about premenstrual edema. Peat's clinical observation: "Seeing that salt restriction never helped women with cyclic edema, I explained the mechanisms of water regulation, first to just one woman... Since then (1978) I have explained this to many women, and it always works for estrogen-induced cyclic edema" ([[the-edema-of-stress|*The Edema of Stress*]]).
- Do not restrict carbohydrates. The luteal phase is when progesterone is improving the body's glucose handling. The "carb cravings = bad" framing routes women toward exactly the wrong response.
- Do not start an SSRI for premenstrual mood symptoms. Serotonin is the precursor of the mood-anxiety-bloat cascade, not the cure ([[pms]] expands on this). The SSRI relief women report is short-lived and the long-term cost is significant.
- Do not start oral contraceptives for "PMS." OCPs flatten the cycle entirely and substitute synthetic progestins (luteolytic in many cases) for the body's progesterone. The symptoms abate because the cycle abates; the underlying state worsens.

## Phase 4 — Menstruation (Days 1-5 of New Cycle)

Both hormones drop sharply over 24-48 hours as the corpus luteum involutes. Menstruation begins. The first two days of flow are usually the heaviest. Cramping, when present, is mediated by prostaglandin F2α and serotonin; the heavier the flow, the higher the prostaglandin signal, the harder the cramping.

### What to eat

The eating template inverts somewhat. The luteal-phase priority on continuous frequent feeding becomes a menstruation-phase priority on warming, salty, gelatin-rich, easily-digestible food.

- **Aspirin at first sign of cramping.** 325-650 mg with food at cramp onset. Prostaglandin synthesis inhibition is the most direct mechanism for cramp relief; aspirin is Peat's preferred NSAID for the safety profile reasons covered in the [[aspirin]] page and the [[pregnancy-nutrition]] page ("Aspirin has a good record of safety in pregnancy, paracetamol doesn't"). Repeat every 4-6 hours as needed during the first two flow days.
- **Gelatin-rich broth, daily.** A mug or bowl of bone broth (homemade or carefully-sourced) with extra salt, a teaspoon of gelatin powder if needed, sipped warm. Glycine calms the serotonin and stress signaling that drive cramping. Warm broth specifically: the menstruation-phase warm-food preference is not aesthetic, it is metabolic.
- **Salt liberally.** Sodium and water are being lost. A glass of salted orange juice on waking, salted water through the day, salt on every meal. The blood-volume support is more important now than at any other phase of the cycle.
- **Sugar with the salt.** Continued. Orange juice, milk with honey, ripe fruit, ice cream. The serotonergic surge of menstruation often produces a mood drop in the first 24-48 hours; sugar-and-salt with adequate sleep is the most direct counter.
- **Vitamin E, raised dose.** 200-400 IU mixed tocopherols daily through the bleeding days, taken with food. The membrane-protective and prostaglandin-modulating effects of vitamin E specifically reduce cramping in many women. Peat's [[vitamin-e-estrogen-antagonist-energy-promoter-and-anti-inflammatory|vitamin E article]] documents the menstrual-cramp use.
- **Iron-aware eating.** Liver (4-8 oz across the week), red meat (small portion at one meal), oysters once. The food sources rebuild hemoglobin without the oxidative cost of iron tablets. Skip non-heme iron pills unless a documented anemia exists. (Counter-argument: heavy menstrual bleeders may genuinely benefit from iron supplementation; check ferritin before deciding.)
- **Cyclic progesterone — stop at the first sign of flow.** If you have been running 3-4 drops Progest-E days 14-28, stop with the period. The cycle resets. The next progesterone dose is day 14 of the new cycle.
- **Magnesium glycinate at bedtime.** 300-400 mg elemental. Magnesium loss is highest during the first two flow days; supplementation supports the smooth-muscle relaxation that reduces cramping and the sleep quality that supports recovery.

### What not to do during menstruation

- Do not "bed-rest" or stop moving entirely. Gentle walking is supportive — circulation, warmth, the mild progesterone-like effect of light movement. Bed-bound days drive cortisol up, not down.
- Do not run a hard workout. The 48-hour window of the heaviest flow is for recovery; serious training routes through to the follicular phase 4-5 days later.
- Do not use acetaminophen as the primary cramp NSAID. Aspirin has a better safety profile (per Peat's [[pregnancy|pregnancy email]]) and a more direct prostaglandin-synthesis-inhibition mechanism. Acetaminophen depletes glutathione without the prostaglandin effect.
- Do not start hormonal contraception for "heavy periods." Heavy menstruation is, in Peat's [[menses|menses email]], the basic signal of hypothyroidism: "Hypothyroidism is the basic cause of heavy menstruation. Things strong enough to kill an embryo can't be harmless to a person." The intervention is thyroid, not synthetic progestin.

## When Something Is Broken — Diagnostic Cases

The above template assumes a working cycle with normal phase lengths. When the cycle has gone wrong, the eating layer remains the same, but the diagnostic flag tells you which upstream intervention to add first.

### Anovulation (no thermal shift mid-cycle)

The body completes a "cycle" — bleeding occurs — but no ovulation, no corpus luteum, no progesterone in the second half. This shows up on a basal body temperature chart as no mid-cycle thermal shift, and on the calendar as longer or unpredictable cycle lengths.

**Most common causes:** hypothyroidism, chronic stress, undereating, low body weight, PCOS (see [[pcos]]), perimenopause, coming off hormonal contraception, prolactin elevation, extreme exercise.

**Eating layer:** intensify [[core-diet-protocol]]. Salt to taste. Adequate calories — at least 2,000-2,400 daily for most women, more if active. Liver and oysters weekly. Stop fasting. Stop low-carb.

**Adjunct:** route to [[hypothyroid-recovery]] if morning temperature is consistently below 97.8°F or resting pulse below 75. Trial low-dose T3 (1 mcg every 2-4 hours, titrated). Pregnenolone (50-200 mg daily, oral, with breakfast and fat) can support the steroid substrate pool while thyroid is being restored.

**Cyclic progesterone in anovulatory cycles:** Peat's [[menses|menses email]] gives the practical heuristic: "If you know the date of the last menstruation, you could go by the calendar, so 6 or 10 or 14 days later might coincide with the ovulation cycle; if you have a sensation of ovulation, that would be a signal to start it, or if you see a sudden rise in morning temperature that could indicate ovulation. But if there's no cycle you can detect, just starting the progesterone could renew the rhythm." Start day 14 by calendar; the progesterone itself often restores the pituitary-ovarian rhythm.

### Luteal-phase deficiency (short luteal phase, <10 days)

The thermal shift occurs but progesterone drops early. The period starts before day 25 of the cycle. PMS is severe even when the rest of the cycle feels normal.

**Most common causes:** subclinical hypothyroidism, undereating in the days around ovulation, PUFA-loaded tissues, vitamin A insufficiency, recurrent stress.

**Eating layer:** the [[core-diet-protocol]] floor plus liver every week (vitamin A, the rate-limiting cofactor for cholesterol → pregnenolone conversion). Eggs daily. Oysters weekly. Stop fasting and low-carbing across the cycle, not just in the luteal phase.

**Adjunct:** cyclic progesterone (3-4 drops Progest-E, days 14-28) is the classical Peat first-line intervention for short luteal phase. Vitamin E 200 IU daily. Aspirin 81 mg with breakfast. T3 if temperature is low.

### Full PMS / PMDD

See [[pms]] for the full mechanism and intervention stack. PMDD ("premenstrual dysphoric disorder") is the medical-pharmaceutical label for severe PMS — a category created largely to support SSRI prescribing. In Peat's framework PMDD is PMS with a more inverted estrogen-to-progesterone ratio. The intervention is the same as for severe PMS: aggressive [[core-diet-protocol]], cyclic progesterone (often at 6-8 drops Progest-E daily through the luteal phase rather than 3-4), aspirin, salt to taste, sugar at bedtime, vitamin E, T3 if temperature is low.

The catamenial seizure case — the most extreme expression of the same hormonal pattern — is on the [[pms]] page. The continuity from "ordinary PMS" through "PMDD" to "catamenial epilepsy" is a continuity of dose, not a categorical difference of mechanism.

### Heavy bleeding (menorrhagia)

From the [[menses|menses email]]: "Hypothyroidism is the basic cause of heavy menstruation." Heavy and painful periods are a thyroid signal long before a uterine signal.

**Eating layer:** [[core-diet-protocol]] plus extra liver (8-12 oz weekly) plus oysters weekly. Aspirin 325 mg twice daily during the bleeding days reduces flow modestly through its prostaglandin effect. Continued cyclic progesterone in the cycle preceding the heavy period is the canonical intervention.

**Route to a clinician** if a period soaks through a pad-and-tampon combination in under an hour, if clots are larger than a quarter, or if a hemoglobin check shows iron-deficiency anemia. Fibroids ([[fibroids]]) are a common structural cause of heavy bleeding and a separate diagnostic question.

### Coming off hormonal contraception

The first three to six cycles after stopping the pill are often irregular, anovulatory, or absent. The pill flattens the cycle; the post-pill recovery is the brain and ovary rediscovering their rhythm. The eating template is the [[core-diet-protocol]] baseline plus deliberate patience — do not panic and do not run to a fertility clinic in months 1-3 unless there is a specific reason to. From the [[menses|menses email]], starting low-dose cyclic progesterone day 14 by calendar can accelerate the rhythm's return.

## Counter-arguments

**1. Mainstream "cycle syncing" / "seed cycling" / "luteal-phase carb-loading" frameworks.** The popular cycle-syncing protocol (Alisa Vitti, Lara Briden, "FLO living") recommends a different diet per phase — emphasizing cruciferous vegetables, sprouted grains, and fermented foods in the follicular phase for "estrogen detox," sweet vegetables and bee pollen at ovulation, roots and gourds in the luteal phase, and red meat plus warming spices at menstruation. Seed cycling (flax and pumpkin seeds in the follicular phase, sesame and sunflower in the luteal phase, two tablespoons daily) claims to "balance estrogen and progesterone via phytoestrogens and EFAs." Peat's framework rejects most of this. Phytoestrogens (flax, sesame, soy) add to the estrogen burden rather than balancing it. EFAs (sunflower, pumpkin, flax) are PUFA — antithyroid, pro-aromatase, pro-inflammatory. Raw cruciferous in volume is goitrogenic. The cycle-syncing protocol gets one thing right (eat with the cycle, not against it) and a long list of things wrong (which substances to emphasize). The Peat eating layer is closer to "same staples, slightly different emphasis" than to "completely different diet by phase."

**2. The serotonin-and-PMS hypothesis.** Mainstream gynecology treats premenstrual mood symptoms as a serotonin-deficiency state — the basis for SSRI prescribing during the luteal phase, sometimes daily, sometimes day 14-28. Peat treated serotonin as the symptom rather than the cure ([[serotonin]] hub). The premenstrual mood collapse is not low serotonin asking to be supplemented; it is high serotonin from estrogen-driven gut signaling. SSRIs suppress the symptom by extending the serotonin signal (paradoxically, the same intervention as raising serotonin); the underlying state worsens.

**3. The "estrogen replacement to balance progesterone" framing.** Some integrative-medicine protocols treat PMS or perimenopausal symptoms with bioidentical estradiol "to balance" progesterone — usually low-dose transdermal estradiol on top of oral or transdermal progesterone. Peat's framework rejects estrogen replacement at any dose for nearly all cases ([[menopause-protocol]] develops this). The exception is documented severe vaginal atrophy in postmenopausal women, where local estriol cream (the weakest natural estrogen, used vaginally only) is a defensible compromise.

**4. The intermittent-fasting and time-restricted-eating recommendations.** Several popular protocols recommend 14-16 hour daily fasts for "hormonal balance" or "metabolic flexibility." Peat's read on fasting (across the [[core-diet-protocol]], [[anti-stress-diet]], and the explicit [[fasting]] entry where it exists): fasting raises cortisol, drives free-fatty-acid mobilization, suppresses thyroid, and worsens the very metabolic state cycle-phase eating is meant to support. A 12-14 hour overnight fast is fine — anything longer is contraindicated for cycling women.

**5. The "ketogenic for PCOS" recommendation.** Widely promoted; the [[pcos]] page argues the opposite. PCOS in Peat's framework is a hypothyroid-stress-estrogen syndrome, not an insulin-resistance-as-primary-cause syndrome. Sustained low-carb pushes the body toward the same Randle-cycle and free-fatty-acid state estrogen produces.

**6. The DUTCH test and "estrogen detox" pathway analysis.** Functional-medicine practitioners increasingly order DUTCH urinary hormone testing and prescribe DIM, calcium-d-glucarate, sulforaphane, and similar "phase 1 / phase 2 detox" supplements based on the methylation, sulfation, and glucuronidation pathway readouts. Peat did not prescribe phase-pathway-targeted supplementation. The carrot fiber, the adequate protein, the thyroid restoration, and the saturated-fat-with-coffee daily routine are the estrogen-clearance pathway in Peat's framework. Whether the DUTCH-test-driven supplement stack adds anything beyond placebo is unsettled.

**7. The "every woman is different — find your phase" framing.** Sometimes used to defend cycle-syncing's many variations. Peat's framework accepts variation in cycle length (24-32 days is normal), in luteal-phase length (10-16 days), and in individual symptom patterns — but it does not accept that the *direction* of intervention varies. The substances that raise metabolic rate, lower estrogen, and support progesterone are the same for nearly every cycling woman. The dose varies, not the direction.

## Data gaps

**1. Pediatric and adolescent cycles.** Peat said relatively little about adolescent cycle-phase eating. Adolescence is one of the four windows of greatest hormonal disturbance he named in [[progesterone-summaries-progesterone-supplementation-dosage-of-progesterone|*Progesterone Summaries*]] — "at puberty, in the early twenties and in the late thirties and menopause" — but the explicit eating-protocol guidance for a 14-year-old with severe primary dysmenorrhea is thin. Cyclic progesterone in adolescents is plausible by the framework but not directly addressed by Peat's writing.

**2. Athletes and the high-training-volume cycle.** The intersection of cycle-phase eating with the metabolic demands of competitive training (rowers, runners, lifters) is undertheorized in the corpus. The female athlete triad (low energy availability + menstrual dysfunction + low bone density) overlaps significantly with Peat's framework but the explicit protocol mapping is sparse.

**3. Long-cycle and lunar-cycle exceptions.** Some women cycle on 35-45 day patterns; some experience cycle entrainment with moon phases (Peat noted dark cloudy winters lower progesterone production, in [[progesterone-summaries-progesterone-supplementation-dosage-of-progesterone|*Progesterone Summaries*]]: "Dark cloudy winters in England or the Pacific Northwest are powerful stressors, and cause lower production of progesterone in women"). The dietary adjustments for non-28-day cycles map naturally — phases proportional to actual length — but Peat's writing did not formalize this.

**4. The first-postpartum cycle and breastfeeding.** Postpartum cycling is highly variable. The first ovulation after birth often produces a short luteal phase. Breastfeeding suppresses ovulation through prolactin; once it returns, the early cycles are usually irregular for months. Eating-protocol guidance for the lactating-and-cycling woman is partially covered in [[pregnancy-nutrition]] but the cycle-specific layer is thin.

**5. Hormonal contraception interaction.** Cycle-phase eating on the pill is a category mismatch — the synthetic progestin and ethinyl estradiol have flattened the cycle, so "phase" is not a meaningful unit. The withdrawal bleed is not menstruation. Recovery from the pill is the more relevant frame; the [[fertility]] protocol addresses some of this.

**6. The PMDD subgroup that does not respond.** Some women with severe PMS/PMDD do not respond to standard Peat-protocol interventions — cyclic progesterone, salt, sugar, thyroid, aspirin, vitamin E — even at maximum doses across multiple cycles. The non-responder mechanism is not well-characterized in the corpus. Genetic variation in allopregnanolone sensitivity, severe estrogen receptor sensitization, and chronic uterine inflammation are candidate explanations but not directly addressed by Peat.

**7. Endometriosis and adenomyosis as cycle modifiers.** The [[endometriosis]] page expands on this, but the specific phase-by-phase eating modifications for endometriosis-driven cyclical pain are not fully developed. The general framework (lower estrogen, raise progesterone) applies; the specific layer is open.

**8. Race, ethnicity, and population variation.** Peat's corpus is largely silent on whether cycle-phase eating recommendations should vary across ethnic groups. The standard Western 28-day cycle is the implicit baseline. Variation in age at menarche, cycle length, and menopause age across populations is real but not addressed.
