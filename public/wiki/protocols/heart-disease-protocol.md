# Heart Disease Protocol

This is the action layer for the framework laid out on [[heart-disease]]. The mechanism — hypothyroidism driving adrenaline-mobilized free fatty acids into PUFA-permeable vessel walls, with cholesterol elevation as a symptom rather than a cause — is treated upstream. What follows is what to actually do.

**This is not emergency cardiology.** Crushing chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, a new irregular heartbeat, facial droop, slurred speech, or a sudden severe headache are reasons to call an ambulance, not to read a wiki. The protocol below is for the long arc: preventing a first event, managing stable known disease, recovering after a treated event, and renegotiating chronic medications with a physician. Nothing here replaces a cardiologist for acute coronary syndrome, decompensating heart failure, uncontrolled arrhythmia, or pregnancy with cardiac history. The framework adds the most in the cases mainstream cardiology under-treats: subclinical hypothyroidism with strong family history, stable angina managed pharmacologically without addressing metabolic substrate, post-stent patients on a statin they suspect is hurting them, women in early menopause with first cardiac symptoms.

## Risk stratification: which protocol are you running

1. **Primary prevention.** No diagnosed coronary disease, no event, no current cardiac medication. Possible risk factors: family history, elevated cholesterol on a routine panel, hypothyroid symptoms, PUFA-loaded diet, perimenopause, chronic stress.
2. **Known cardiovascular disease, no event.** Diagnosed stable coronary artery disease, angina, mild-to-moderate heart failure with preserved or reduced ejection fraction, controlled hypertension on medication, atrial fibrillation managed medically.
3. **Post-event or post-procedure.** Past myocardial infarction, post-stent or post-CABG, post-ablation, status-post valve repair. By definition under cardiology supervision and on multiple medications.
4. **Currently on a statin.** Crosses all three of the above. The statin conversation is a separate decision tree because the drug interacts with everything the protocol is trying to do.

Each section below specifies what is the same across strata, what changes, and what to bring to a cardiologist appointment rather than do unilaterally.

## Primary prevention

The leverage is highest here. Every intervention in the framework is a primary prevention intervention.

**Diet.** Run the full [[anti-stress-diet]]. The cardiovascular-relevant elements:

1. **Eliminate seed and vegetable oils.** Corn, soy, safflower, sunflower, canola, grapeseed. The highest-leverage change, because [[pufa]] is the substrate peroxidized in vessel walls and the load in adipose has a four-year half-life. Restaurant food is the hidden source. See [[pufa]].
2. **No fish oil supplements.** Same molecule, more concentrated. Fish oil has failed in every recent large trial (ASCEND, VITAL, STRENGTH) to reduce hard cardiovascular endpoints. See [[fish-oil]].
3. **Replace cooking oils with butter, ghee, tallow, and coconut oil.** Mediterranean reread: the protective component is olive oil, fish, vegetables, dairy, and meat — not the modern seed-oil inclusion. The PREDIMED active arm used about 50 mL of extra-virgin olive oil per day. That is the dose range, not "drown the salad."
4. **Two quarts of milk a day, 1% to 2%.** Delivers the ~2,000 mg of calcium Peat treated as the cardiovascular floor. Calcium suppresses parathyroid hormone (itself antithyroid and pro-vascular-calcification).
5. **A quart of orange juice a day.** Potassium (~2,000 mg/quart), magnesium, naringenin and hesperidin (mild aromatase inhibition; relevant because estrogen drives vascular permeability and clotting), and the sugar that supports liver T4-to-T3 conversion.
6. **Salt to appetite.** The PURE study (130,000 people across 18 countries) showed a J-shaped curve: intake below ~3 g/day sodium was *worse* than 4–5 g. The 1,500 mg DASH target is below the bottom of the safe range.
7. **Daily raw carrot.** Medium carrot with vinegar and salt. Binds endotoxin and excess estrogen; addresses the gut-inflammation axis the hsCRP-guided cardiology literature has begun to identify.

**Thyroid (if subclinical hypothyroid).** If morning oral temperature is below 97.8°F, resting pulse is under 70, cholesterol is over 220, or TSH is above 2.0, run [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for six weeks before adding hormone. If after that the temperature is still under 98.0 and pulse under 75, the entry point is desiccated thyroid (Armour, NP Thyroid, Nature-Throid) at one-quarter grain (15 mg) with the largest meal, increasing by 15 mg every one to two weeks. Most primary-prevention candidates land at one to two grains per day at steady state.

The Barnes-specific caution from [[broda-barnes]]: "It's very important to make sure your diet includes plenty of magnesium when you're starting to use thyroid," Peat told listeners on [[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|2019 *One Radio Network* on vitamin D and thyroid]]. Thyroid draws magnesium into skeletal muscle; coronary arteries can lose what they had been holding, producing transient spasm at small doses. Two patients Peat cited developed heart pain on 15 mg. The fix is not a smaller starting dose; it is a larger magnesium intake before and during the start.

**Magnesium.** 200 to 600 mg of elemental magnesium per day, in divided doses with meals. Magnesium chloride, glycinate, or topical magnesium oil applied to the legs. Oxide is poorly absorbed; citrate works but laxative effects limit the dose. Magnesium relaxes vascular smooth muscle, improves diastolic relaxation, reduces arrhythmia, and corrects the deficiency that statins exacerbate.

**Aspirin.** 81 mg daily with food. The mainstream evidence on primary-prevention aspirin has split (ASPREE, ARRIVE, ASCEND), but the framework's case is broader than the antithrombotic effect: aspirin inhibits aromatase, blocks arachidonic-acid prostaglandins, suppresses free fatty acid release, and shifts mitochondrial metabolism toward glucose oxidation. Co-supplement vitamin K2 (MK-4 1 mg/day or MK-7 100 µg/day) to offset chronic coagulation effects.

**Progesterone (women approaching or past menopause).** The highest-leverage single intervention for women in this stratum. From [[progesterone-not-estrogen-is-the-coronary-protection-factor-of-women|*Progesterone, Not Estrogen, is the Coronary Protection Factor of Women* (2006)]]: "Progesterone's effects are antagonistic to estrogen's. Progesterone decreases the formation of nitric oxide, decreasing edema; it strengthens the heart beat, by improving venous return and increasing stroke volume, but at the same time it reduces peripheral resistance by relaxing arteries (by inhibiting calcium entry but also by other effects, and independently of the endothelium) and decreasing edematous swelling." The WHI trial was *estrogen* and synthetic progestin, not bioidentical progesterone; the post-menopausal CV acceleration coincides with corpus-luteum progesterone loss.

Progest-E or equivalent, 30 to 100 mg/day sublingually or on the gums. Palpitations, edema, or post-menopausal hypertension reach the upper end. Topical creams absorb variably; oral/sublingual is more reliable. For women still cycling, dose luteal phase (day 14–28); post-menopausal, continuously. Physician oversight for any breast cancer history.

**Niacinamide.** 100 to 500 mg, two to three times per day with meals. From [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones* (2015)]]: "Niacinamide inhibits the release of free fatty acids from the tissues, and thyroid sustains the oxidation of glucose." Use the amide, not niacin (flushing, hepatic toxicity at high doses).

**Vitamin E.** Mixed-tocopherol vitamin E, 100 to 400 IU per day with a fat-containing meal. Protects stored PUFA from peroxidation during the slow tissue turnover. Avoid synthetic dl-alpha-tocopherol alone; a mixed-tocopherol formula with gamma- and delta-tocopherols is the goal.

## Known cardiovascular disease (no event yet)

If a stress test has documented ischemia, an echo shows reduced ejection fraction, an angiogram has found a stenosis, or stable angina is being managed pharmacologically, the protocol stays the same in structure but slows in titration and tightens in monitoring.

**Diet and supplements as above**, without exception. Restaurant food becomes a real problem: a single seed-oil-heavy meal spikes free fatty acids for hours.

**Thyroid: the Barnes approach, with cardiology consult.** Do not start thyroid unilaterally in known coronary disease. A 70% LAD stenosis with thin functional reserve can become symptomatic at a dose trivial in a healthier person. Have the conversation with a cardiologist open to the metabolic framework, and start at half the primary-prevention entry dose.

The case for thyroid in known coronary disease is direct: cardiac surgeons routinely administer T3 to brain-dead donor hearts before harvest. "Heart transplant surgeons have discovered that administering T3 to the brain-dead heart donor before removing the heart improves its viability and function in the recipient," Peat wrote in [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]]. The molecule that protects donor hearts protects native hearts.

Practical protocol for stable CAD with cardiology clearance:

1. **Start at 5 to 10 mg of desiccated thyroid (one-third grain) at the largest meal**, or 1 to 2 µg of liothyronine (Cytomel/Cynomel) twice daily with food.
2. **Load magnesium for two weeks first.** 500 to 600 mg per day, split through the day, before any thyroid dose increase.
3. **Increase by 5 to 10 mg of desiccated thyroid every two to three weeks**, tracking morning temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and symptoms daily.
4. **Stop at the first sign of chest pressure, worsening angina, or new palpitations.** Drop back, increase magnesium, re-examine.
5. **Target the same endpoints** (morning 98.0°F, resting pulse 75–85) but over months, not weeks.

**Progesterone for women with known CAD or heart failure** at the upper end of the range (100 mg/day, sometimes higher in heart failure with edema). The practical limit is drowsiness, not cardiovascular risk. From [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]]: "Szent-Györgyi found that estrogen treatment decreased the staircase effect, while progesterone treatment increased the staircase." Progesterone restores the function-builds-structure coupling that estrogen disrupts.

**Aspirin at full cardiac dose** (81 mg if tolerated, up to 325 mg where stronger antiplatelet effect is wanted), with continuing K2 co-supplementation.

**Niacinamide at the upper range** (500 mg, three times daily with meals). Free fatty acid suppression matters more here because substrate availability is what keeps failing myocardium running on dirty fat. The same logic underlies modern interest in pyruvate, lactate-clearance, and ketone-body work in heart failure.

**Bag breathing.** From [[heart-and-hormones|*Heart and Hormones*]]: "Another technique that is easy to use to lower blood pressure and improve heart rhythm is to breathe into a paper bag for a minute or two at a time, to increase the carbon dioxide content of the blood. This has a vasodilating effect, reducing the force required to circulate the blood, and reduces anxiety." Two or three minutes, two or three times daily.

**Pregnenolone and DHEA.** Pregnenolone 30 to 100 mg/day and DHEA 10 to 25 mg/day fill the steroid-conversion gap while thyroid and vitamin A are still working to restore it. DHEA in women can have androgenic side effects — start at the low end.

## Post-event or post-procedure

A patient who has had an MI, stent, CABG, or valve repair is on multiple medications already (DAPT, beta-blocker, ACE inhibitor or ARB, statin, possibly SGLT2 inhibitor for heart failure). The framework here is additive, not subtractive, and every change runs through the cardiologist.

**Continue the prescribed medications.** Beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, MRA antagonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors have substantial post-event mortality data. Spironolactone and eplerenone antagonize aldosterone, which the framework treats as a stress-cascade output driving fibrosis. SGLT2 inhibitors push fuel utilization toward ketones — closer to the framework's goal than fatty-acid oxidation, though glucose remains the preferred substrate.

**Dietary changes can be implemented immediately.** Strip PUFA, milk and orange juice in, daily carrot, salt to taste. The diet has no interaction with antiplatelet therapy and produces measurable CRP and lipid-panel improvement within weeks.

**Thyroid replacement: the conservative trial.** Start at half the primary-prevention dose (5–10 mg desiccated, or 1–2 µg T3), with a three-week interval between increases, and only after cardiology reviews the plan. The diagnostic case has to be made first: low morning temperature, low pulse despite no beta-blocker change, classic hypothyroid cluster, lipid panel readable as a thyroid panel. Same target, slower speed.

**Magnesium IV during the index hospitalization** is standard in some MI protocols. Continue oral magnesium glycinate 400 to 600 mg/day afterward.

**Progesterone in post-MI women** can be discussed with cardiology; *synthetic* progestin (medroxyprogesterone) is contraindicated — bioidentical progesterone is a different molecule from the one in the FDA cardiovascular warning. Framework prediction: protective. Literature: thin. Decision: physician-supervised.

**Aspirin and the antiplatelet question.** Post-stent patients are on DAPT (aspirin plus clopidogrel/ticagrelor) for six to twelve months, then aspirin alone. The framework supports continuing the aspirin component; do not stop DAPT early without cardiology.

**Bag breathing** as in the previous section.

## The statin question

This crosses all three strata above and is the single most common medication a person on the Peat side of the cardiology debate is wrestling with.

**Do not stop a statin abruptly without medical supervision**, especially after a recent event or stent. Observational data has linked acute statin discontinuation post-MI with rebound vascular inflammation and early event rates. The clean way is to renegotiate with a cardiologist open to the trade-offs and ask for a structured trial off the drug with monitoring.

**Mitigate the harms while still on the drug.** The mevalonate-pathway depletion is treated on [[coenzyme-q10]] and [[heart-disease]]. Practical add-ons:

1. **CoQ10 (ubiquinol form) 100 to 200 mg/day with a fat-containing meal.** Ubiquinol is the reduced (active) form, absorbed better than older ubiquinone. Many cardiologists now suggest CoQ10 as a first move for statin myalgia.
2. **Magnesium at the upper range** (500 to 600 mg/day). Statins exacerbate the magnesium-CoQ10 depletion cluster behind muscle and fatigue side effects.
3. **Vitamin K2** (MK-4 1 mg/day or MK-7 100 µg/day) to offset matrix-Gla-protein impairment from mevalonate-pathway disruption. K2 deficiency is behind some of the vascular-calcification signal in long-term statin users.
4. **Adequate dietary saturated fat** to provide substrate for dolichol-dependent processes the statin disrupts.

**Renegotiating with a cardiologist**, in roughly this order:

1. **Start with symptoms.** Myalgia, exercise intolerance, cognitive complaints, mood changes — documented side effects most cardiologists will respond to with a dose drop or drug switch.
2. **Bring data.** Recent lipid panel, hsCRP, fasting glucose (statins raise HbA1c slightly), thyroid panel (usually unmonitored).
3. **Discuss a dose reduction first**, not discontinuation. 40 mg to 10 mg of atorvastatin is a step the cardiologist can sign off on, and the side-effect burden often drops sharply.
4. **Propose a structured trial off the drug** if symptoms persist on the lower dose, with recheck at 6 to 12 weeks. Frame it as a clinical experiment, not a refusal of care.
5. **Substitute the framework protocol** during the off-drug window. Thyroid, magnesium, aspirin, the diet — the package does the work the statin was meant to do, by different mechanisms.

**The patient who refuses a statin from the start** has a different conversation. The Mendelian randomization evidence (below) is real; a patient with strong family history of premature MI, elevated [[lipoprotein-a|Lp(a)]], or [[familial-hypercholesterolemia|familial hypercholesterolemia]] is one for whom the framework's "lipids don't cause heart disease" position is hardest to defend. Refusing a statin in those cases without a fully executed framework protocol is taking a real risk.

## Lab monitoring

The labs the framework reads as a thyroid panel, not a lipid panel:

1. **Total cholesterol over 220 with TSH in upper half of normal, or temperature below 98°F:** classical thyroid sign. From [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud|*Thyroid Therapies: Confusion and Fraud* (2007)]]: "hypercholesterolemia was a diagnostic sign of hypothyroidism. Administering a thyroid supplement, blood cholesterol came down to normal exactly as the basal metabolic rate came up to the normal rate." Treat the thyroid; expect cholesterol to fall.
2. **Total cholesterol under 160:** the framework's red flag. Cancer, dementia, accident-mortality, and depression signals all appear below this threshold. LDL under 70 is below the protective range.
3. **hsCRP under 1.0 mg/L.** Above 3.0 is a vascular-inflammation signal no statin will reach; the diet and endotoxin work are more likely to.
4. **Homocysteine under 8 µmol/L.** B12, B6, and folate deficiencies push it up. Response: liver weekly, eggs, dairy.
5. **TSH under 2.0, free T3 upper half, free T4 mid-range, reverse T3 low.** Labs flag TSH above 4.5; many endocrinologists now treat above 2.0; the framework treats above 1.0 as suspicious in a symptomatic patient.
6. **Lp(a) at least once.** If above 50 mg/dL or 125 nmol/L, the framework's silence becomes a real gap; aggressive primary-prevention measures warranted. See [[lipoprotein-a]].
7. **ApoB if available.** Above 130 mg/dL in a hypothyroid-feature patient predicts substantial lipid response to thyroid replacement.
8. **Fasting glucose and HbA1c.** The [[diabetes]] partnership with CVD is direct. HbA1c over 5.7 changes urgency.

## Diet specifics for cardiology

The cardiology-relevant diet is the [[anti-stress-diet]] with one nuance: saturated-fat dose can be moderated without losing the mechanism, but PUFA elimination is absolute. A patient or cardiologist worried about saturated fat can run skim or 1% milk, measured butter, occasional cheese, with most fat coming from gelatin broth and lean meat. Protein, carbohydrate, calcium floors, and the daily carrot stay the same.

Mediterranean reread for cardiology buy-in: olive oil up to 50 mL/day, small fish (not large predators) once or twice weekly, dairy daily, eggs daily, vegetables cooked rather than raw, moderate wine. Divergence from marketed Mediterranean: rejection of whole-grain and legume staples; inclusion of butter and dairy/meat saturated fat.

The framework is *not* keto (depletes glycogen, shuts down T4-to-T3 conversion within days) and *not* vegan (removes dairy calcium, animal vitamin A, B12, and the protein quality the framework treats as cardioprotective).

## Counter-arguments

The framework makes strong claims against the dominant cardiology paradigm. The strongest rebuttals deserve full weight.

**Mendelian randomization and lifelong LDL exposure.** The most serious modern challenge. People with loss-of-function variants in PCSK9, NPC1L1, HMGCR, or LDLR have lower LDL from birth and lower lifetime cardiovascular event rates proportional to the LDL reduction. These are natural experiments unconfounded by industry funding or trial design. The simplest reading: LDL itself, integrated across decades, contributes causally to atherogenesis. A patient with familial hypercholesterolemia (untreated LDL above 200, family MI before age 50) is one for whom the framework should be a complement to, not a replacement for, lipid-lowering therapy.

**Statin secondary-prevention evidence.** In patients who have had a coronary event, the absolute reduction in recurrent events from statins is larger and more consistent than in primary prevention. Mechanism is debated (pleiotropic anti-inflammatory effects, plaque stabilization, modest LDL lowering), but the trial evidence is hard to wave off. A post-MI patient coming off a statin against medical advice is taking a real risk.

**GLP-1 cardiovascular outcomes.** Semaglutide and tirzepatide have shown CV outcome reductions (SELECT, SURPASS-CVOT) the framework has not engaged with. Mechanism appears to be weight loss plus direct vascular effects, both of which the framework can accommodate, but the magnitude is larger than predicted for a single pharmacological intervention.

**Heart-failure pharmacology actually works.** The HFrEF regimen (beta-blocker + ACE inhibitor/ARB + MRA + SGLT2i) has thirty years of mortality data. The framework is a complement, not a substitute: "give T3 instead of carvedilol" is not what Peat wrote.

**Modern lipidology has moved past total cholesterol.** ApoB and Lp(a) are better risk markers than total cholesterol alone. The framework's strong claims fall hardest on total cholesterol; less hard on ApoB; almost not at all on Lp(a).

**Selection bias in Barnes's no-heart-disease-deaths cohort.** The 30-year zero-CVD-death figure is real but the cohort was self-selected (patients sought out a thyroid-focused physician), Barnes set his own diagnostic criteria, and there was no matched control. He published in his own books, not peer-reviewed cardiology.

**LDL-lowering targets in very high-risk groups.** Established CVD, diabetes, or familial hypercholesterolemia patients are where the modern lipid-lowering protocols (statin + ezetimibe + PCSK9 inhibitor pushing LDL into the 30s) have shown the largest absolute benefits. The framework has not engaged with the PCSK9 data.

## Data gaps

**T3 dosing in established coronary disease.** The case for thyroid in heart-disease prevention is well developed; the case for T3 in a patient with a 70% stenosis is thinner. The cardiac-surgery literature on donor-heart T3 is encouraging but does not answer the dosing question in stable angina. The conservative-titration approach above is reasonable but untested against guideline-directed therapy.

**Atrial fibrillation.** Framework predicts AF downstream of hypothyroidism, hyperestrogenism, and PUFA-driven inflammation. Clinical evidence is sparse. Modern AF management has trial data for rate/rhythm control plus anticoagulation. Whether the metabolic protocol can replace, complement, or marginally improve standard care has not been tested.

**Coronary microvascular disease.** Modern cardiology increasingly recognizes microvascular dysfunction (especially in women) as distinct from large-vessel atherosclerosis. The framework predicts this well but specific therapeutic implications have not been worked out.

**Lp(a).** Largely genetic, mechanistically distinct, among the strongest predictors of premature coronary disease — and the framework gives it almost no attention. The protocol suggests checking it once; it does not have a specific answer for elevation.

**Endotoxin and gut barrier in CVD.** Peat's later work emphasized endotoxin as a driver of inflammation. Protocol implications (carrot, charcoal, antibiotics in severe cases, sugar for beneficial flora) are present in correspondence but never integrated into a unified gut-cardiovascular protocol. Active area in mainstream cardiology (the gut-heart axis literature); the two frameworks may be converging.

**Drug interactions.** Thyroid interacts with warfarin, several diabetes medications, and calcium channel blockers in ways that change effective doses. The protocol does not map all interactions; a patient on multiple drugs needs a physician to manage transitions.

**Women under 50 with cardiac symptoms.** The framework's account of late-life CAD in women is reasonably developed; pre-menopausal cardiac symptoms (microvascular dysfunction, SCAD, autoimmune vasculitis) much less so.
