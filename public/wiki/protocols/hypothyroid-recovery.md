# Hypothyroid Recovery

Hypothyroid recovery is multi-tiered. The thyroid tablet is the most conspicuous part of the protocol and the part that gets the most attention from physicians, patients, and online forums, but in the framework Ray Peat documented across fifty years of correspondence, the medication is necessary but not sufficient. A patient who swallows desiccated thyroid every morning on top of a diet built around seed oils, skipped meals, raw kale smoothies, ten thousand steps of compensatory cardio, a cold bedroom, and chronic vigilant cortisol will improve on paper and stall in life. The lab values move; the temperature does not; the hair does not grow back; the libido does not return; the cold hands stay cold. Then the dose gets raised. Then it stalls again. The diagnostic conclusion is escalation. The actual problem is that the medication is being asked to do work the rest of the patient's life is undoing in real time.

This protocol documents the other four pillars. Diet, micronutrient adequacy, warmth and circadian rhythm, and stress reduction are what allow whatever thyroid the gland still makes (and whatever supplemental thyroid is being added) to actually drive cells to oxidize glucose completely. They are the pillars that make the medication work. Many patients who execute them carefully discover that the medication dose required falls over time, sometimes to zero. Others remain on a stable maintenance dose for life and feel well there. Both are recoveries. The marker that recovery is happening is not the TSH; it is the morning temperature, the resting pulse, the warmth of the hands, the depth of sleep, the density of hair, the return of dreams, and the steadiness of mood — the constellation Broda Barnes catalogued before the blood tests existed and the constellation Peat defended against fifty years of pharmaceutical revisionism.

For the medication layer itself — what to take, how to titrate, dose patterns, cortisol pre-conditioning, when to escalate, when to back off — see [[thyroid-dosing]]. For the underlying biology of the gland, the hormone, and the conversion pathway, see [[thyroid]], [[hypothyroidism]], and [[metabolic-rate]]. For the diagnostic case against TSH-driven medicine, see [[tsh]]. This page covers everything else, and "everything else" is most of what actually determines whether recovery happens.

## The Five-Pillar Framework

Recovery rests on five pillars. Removing any of them weakens the structure; removing two collapses it. The order is partly sequential (you start eating before you start supplementing) and partly parallel (warmth and stress reduction begin on day one alongside the food).

1. **Thyroid medication, when needed.** Desiccated natural thyroid (Armour, Cynoplus, NP Thyroid) or low-dose T3 (Cytomel, Cynomel), dosed in microgram portions with food, titrated against waking and afternoon temperature and pulse rather than against TSH. Many patients never need this pillar; many require it for a season; some require it for life. The detailed dose decisions and titration loop live in [[thyroid-dosing]] and are not duplicated here.
2. **Pro-metabolic diet.** Whole milk, orange juice, ripe fruit, gelatin, eggs, weekly liver, white rice and potatoes with butter, coconut oil for cooking, salt to appetite, frequent meals, a bedtime snack. PUFA eliminated. Raw goitrogens cooked or removed. Soy and flax avoided. This is the [[core-diet-protocol]] and [[anti-stress-diet]] expressed as a thyroid-specific template below.
3. **Micronutrient adequacy.** Selenium, zinc, magnesium, vitamin K2, vitamin E, vitamin A, B-complex, calcium, and — with careful low-dose handling — iodine. The minerals and fat-soluble vitamins are what the thyroid gland uses to synthesize hormone, what the liver uses to convert T4 to T3, and what the receptors use to respond. Deficiency in any one produces a clinical picture indistinguishable from primary hypothyroidism.
4. **Warmth and circadian rhythm.** Morning sun, a warm bedroom, a warm bath in the evening, a bedtime snack that holds blood sugar through the night, minimized evening blue light, and a wake-up that lets the body warm into the day rather than blasting it with adrenaline and cold. Hypothyroidism is, mechanically, a heat-deficiency syndrome. The environment has to stop competing with the medication.
5. **Stress reduction.** Walking instead of running, bag-breathing for elevated lactate, sleep at seven to nine hours, no cold plunges, no fasting, no skipped meals, no over-exercise. The stress system runs on cortisol and adrenaline; these hormones suppress thyroid at the gland, the conversion step, and the receptor. A patient who is still grinding through a high-cortisol life will not recover, regardless of which prescription bottle is on the nightstand.

The remainder of this protocol details Pillars 2 through 5, plus the tracking template that detects whether the pillars are actually working, the expected recovery timeline, the most common stalls, and the conditions where the protocol is insufficient and medical escalation is required.

## Pillar 2 — Diet Template

The diet is the central pillar. Peat made this point in dozens of interviews and emails: "The diet has to come first, because exogenous thyroid raises metabolic demand, and a person who has not first fed their liver will crash on the first tablet" ([[2008-10-31-other-hope-for-health-hope-for-health-thyroid|2008 *Hope for Health*]]). The template below is the [[core-diet-protocol]] expressed for the thyroid-recovery use case. The full rationale for each item — why milk, why orange juice, why salt, why no PUFA — lives in [[core-diet-protocol]] and is referenced rather than rebuilt here.

### Daily baseline

1. **Two quarts of whole milk per day.** Roughly 2,000 mg of calcium, 65 grams of protein, 100 grams of lactose, and the fat-soluble vitamins that ride along with the cream. Calcium suppresses parathyroid hormone, which is itself antithyroid. Lactose feeds the liver. Whole milk, not skim — the saturated fat is part of the medicine. See [[milk]] for the full account.
2. **One quart of orange juice per day.** Fresh-squeezed or well-made not-from-concentrate. Fructose and glucose feed the liver; potassium supports the heart and steadies the nerves; the small amount of magnesium matters. Hypothyroid people are often potassium-depleted from chronic adrenaline drive, and juice corrects that more reliably than tablets. See [[orange-juice]].
3. **Ripe fruit through the day.** Two to four pieces of well-ripened, sweet, in-season fruit. Mangoes, peaches, melons, grapes, dates, papaya. Underripe fruit, raw greens, and high-starch tropical roots without preparation are not equivalent. See [[fruit]] for the ripeness rule.
4. **One to three tablespoons of coconut oil daily.** In coffee, in cooking, on toast, or eaten by the spoonful with honey. The saturated fat displaces tissue PUFA over months, supports thyroid function at the cellular membrane level, and helps with body temperature within hours of a dose. See [[coconut-oil]].
5. **One heaping tablespoon of gelatin daily.** Dissolved in a warm drink, stirred into yogurt, or cooked into broth. Glycine balances the cysteine and tryptophan load of muscle meats, supports liver glucuronidation (which clears excess estrogen), and softens the inflammatory edge of a high-protein day. See [[gelatin]].
6. **Two eggs daily.** The cholesterol is the precursor to [[progesterone]], pregnenolone, and the steroid hormones whose synthesis depends on adequate thyroid function. The choline supports liver function. See [[eggs]].
7. **Four to six ounces of liver per week.** Once or twice weekly, no more. The single most concentrated source of preformed retinol (vitamin A), copper, B12, folate, and bioavailable iron in the food supply. See [[liver]].
8. **White rice or potatoes with butter at every dinner.** A starch that feeds the liver overnight without the irritants of whole grains. See [[potatoes]] and [[white-rice]] in the foods catalogue.
9. **Iodized salt to appetite.** Liberal, not restricted. Low-salt diets raise aldosterone and adrenaline, both of which antagonize thyroid. A pinch of salt added to orange juice can be a quick recovery tactic during an adrenaline spike.
10. **A bedtime snack, every night.** A glass of milk with a teaspoon of honey, a small dish of ice cream, cheese and fruit, or a slice of toast with butter. The 2 to 3 AM cortisol surge that wakes hypothyroid patients is driven by falling blood sugar; the bedtime snack flattens it. See [[eat-before-bed]].

### What to avoid

1. **All industrial seed oils.** Soybean, corn, canola, sunflower, safflower, cottonseed, grapeseed, rice bran, and the "vegetable oil" of unspecified provenance that dominates restaurant cooking. Also fish oil — supplementing PUFA in pill form is no improvement on consuming it in a salad dressing. See [[seed-oils]] and [[pufa]].
2. **Raw cruciferous and goitrogenic vegetables.** Raw cabbage, raw kale, raw broccoli, raw cauliflower, raw mustard greens, raw collards, raw bok choy. Cooking deactivates most of the goitrogenic compounds. Eat them cooked or eat something else. Millet and cassava also goitrogenic and best avoided.
3. **Soy in any form.** Soy milk, edamame, tempeh, tofu, soy protein isolate, soy lecithin in processed foods. Antithyroid across every preparation, every fermentation status, every dose.
4. **Flax.** Both the seed and the oil. High in antithyroid lignans on top of being PUFA-loaded.
5. **Fasting or skipped meals.** Twelve hours without food depletes liver glycogen and shuts down T4-to-T3 conversion as a survival response ([[intermittent-fasting]]). Eat every three to four waking hours. The bedtime snack closes the longest window.
6. **Low-carbohydrate diets.** Keto, carnivore, paleo-without-fruit, Atkins. All of them suppress thyroid conversion within days and produce the "I feel fine but I am cold all the time" pattern that is pathognomonic for diet-induced hypothyroidism.

For the full meal-by-meal scheduling, calorie targets, and meal-template examples, see [[core-diet-protocol]] and [[anti-stress-diet]]. The point of the template above is that it tells you what to eat without asking you to re-derive the rationale every morning.

## Pillar 3 — Micronutrient Stack

The food template above covers most of what the thyroid system needs in food form. The supplemental stack is a backstop, not a replacement, and it is most useful in the early months when stores are depleted, in patients whose diet remains partly compromised, and in cases where a specific deficiency (selenium in the inland US, zinc in vegetarians, magnesium in coffee drinkers) is making the difference between stuck and unstuck.

**Selenium, 100 to 200 mcg daily.** The cofactor for the deiodinase enzymes that convert T4 to T3 in the liver. A selenium deficiency produces a clinical picture indistinguishable from primary hypothyroidism: low T3, normal T4, low temperature, low pulse. Soils across the inland US, Europe, and China are selenium-depleted, so food alone is unreliable. Best food sources are shellfish, ocean fish, and Brazil nuts (one or two per day, not a handful — Brazil nuts run highly variable in selenium content and a large dose can exceed the upper limit). Selenium also supports glutathione peroxidase, which is what clears the lipid peroxides that PUFA generates. See [[selenium]] (if catalogued) and [[shellfish]].

**Zinc, 15 to 30 mg daily.** Required for thyroid hormone synthesis inside the gland and for the conversion step in the liver. Oysters are the densest dietary source — six medium oysters supply 30 to 50 mg, several times the daily requirement. Liver, beef, and dairy are reasonable backups. Long-term zinc supplementation should be paired with copper (a 10-to-1 zinc-to-copper ratio is the classic balance), because high-dose zinc depletes copper over months. Liver once weekly supplies the copper.

**Magnesium glycinate, 200 to 400 mg daily.** Many hypothyroid patients are magnesium-depleted from chronic adrenaline drive (adrenaline pulls magnesium out of cells) and from coffee consumption (caffeine increases urinary magnesium loss). Symptoms of magnesium deficiency overlap heavily with hypothyroidism: muscle cramps, insomnia, anxiety, twitching eyelids, constipation. Glycinate is the gentlest form; citrate is laxative; oxide is poorly absorbed. Magnesium is also a cofactor for the ATP-using reactions thyroid hormone activates, so deficiency limits how much benefit any thyroid dose can produce.

**B-complex daily.** A standard B-complex with riboflavin (B2), niacinamide (B3), pantothenic acid (B5), pyridoxal-5-phosphate (B6), folate, B12, and biotin. Riboflavin in particular is required for the FAD-dependent steps in thyroid hormone synthesis; niacinamide supports the NAD-dependent steps in oxidative metabolism. The water-soluble B vitamins are cheap, well-tolerated, and have a wide safety margin. Liver weekly plus the B-complex covers most patients.

**Vitamin K2, 1 to 15 mg of MK-4 daily.** The form Peat preferred. MK-4 supports steroid synthesis (it shows up in the testes and adrenals at significant concentrations), suppresses excess clotting from PUFA-induced inflammation, and directs calcium toward bone and away from arteries. Doses up to 45 mg daily have been used in Japanese osteoporosis trials without toxicity. See [[vitamin-k2]] if catalogued.

**Vitamin E, 100 to 400 IU of mixed tocopherols daily.** Mixed tocopherols, not the synthetic dl-alpha-tocopherol alone. Vitamin E protects PUFA in cell membranes from peroxidation, supports thyroid function, and has documented estrogen-antagonist effects that matter for women whose hypothyroidism is being driven by estrogen excess. See [[vitamin-e]].

**Vitamin A, 5,000 to 20,000 IU of preformed retinol from food and small supplements.** Required at every step of thyroid hormone production, conversion, and steroid synthesis. Liver weekly covers most of the requirement. Carotene from vegetables does not count — hypothyroid people fail to convert carotene to retinol, which is why carotene-loaded palms are a classic Peat diagnostic sign. Egg yolks, dairy fat, and small cod-liver oil doses fill in gaps. Peat warned against megadose vitamin A and against synthetic retinoid drugs. See [[vitamin-a]].

**Iodine — caution.** Ordinary dietary iodine from iodized salt, occasional seafood, and dairy is enough for almost everyone. Megadose iodine protocols (Iodoral, Lugol's solution at 12.5 mg or higher) circulate in alternative health communities and can precipitate Hashimoto's flares by driving the gland harder than it can tolerate. If autoimmune thyroid disease is in the family history, or if antibodies are elevated on a recent panel, keep iodine supplementation to the trace amounts in a multivitamin and skip the megadose Lugol protocols entirely. See [[iodine]] (if catalogued) and [[hashimotos]] (if catalogued).

The whole stack costs roughly $30 to $60 per month from a competent supplement supplier. It is a small investment compared with what gets spent on thyroid medication appointments, lab draws, and the symptom-management drugs (statins, antidepressants, sleep aids, anti-anxiety prescriptions) that often appear when the underlying hypothyroidism is treated only at the gland.

## Pillar 4 — Warmth and Circadian Rhythm

Hypothyroidism is, mechanically, a heat-deficiency state. Cells fail to oxidize glucose completely to carbon dioxide and water, the heat byproduct of that reaction fails to appear, and the body runs cold. Every environmental input that helps the body retain or generate heat reduces the metabolic demand that thyroid hormone is trying to meet. Every input that drains heat out of the body or disrupts the circadian rhythm of cortisol, melatonin, and thyroid release adds to that demand.

**Morning sunlight, fifteen to thirty minutes within the first hour of waking.** A walk outside, breakfast on the porch, sitting near an open east-facing window. Morning red and orange wavelengths suppress nighttime melatonin, set the central circadian clock, support thyroid hormone production through the day, and improve sleep onset the next night. The contrast with hours-later sun exposure matters — the time-locked signal is what entrains the rhythm. See [[morning-sunlight]].

**A warm bath in the evening, 102 to 105°F for twenty minutes.** Raises core temperature, then allows the post-bath cooling that signals the brain to release melatonin. Effective enough to be used as a sleep aid in randomized trials. The Japanese tradition of the evening *ofuro* encodes this without any thyroid framework. A warm shower works less well because the immersion is what allows the temperature elevation.

**A warm bedroom, not a cold one.** The current sleep-hygiene consensus that the bedroom should be cold (60-65°F) was derived from studies of healthy young men with active thyroids and high metabolic rates, for whom a warm room felt overheated. Hypothyroid patients are running cold already, and a cold bedroom forces the body to spend the night generating heat against the room rather than restoring tissues. Set the thermostat to 68-72°F and use a heavier blanket. The marker of correct bedroom temperature is whether you wake warm or cold.

**A bedtime snack to hold blood sugar overnight.** A glass of warm milk with a teaspoon of honey, a small dish of ice cream, a piece of cheese with fruit, a tablespoon of coconut oil stirred into orange juice. Falling blood sugar at 2 to 3 AM triggers the cortisol-adrenaline surge that wakes hypothyroid patients, and the surge converts to a wide-eyed staring-at-the-ceiling pattern that no sleep aid handles cleanly. The food prevents the surge. See [[eat-before-bed]] for the mechanism and the full menu of options.

**Minimized evening blue light, two hours before sleep.** Screens, overhead LEDs, fluorescent kitchens, and ring lights for video calls all push blue-spectrum light into the retina at hours when the body is preparing for melatonin release. The opposite signal — incandescent bulbs, candlelight, dim red-spectrum lamps, screen filters set to warm — supports the rhythm. Hypothyroid patients are especially sensitive to evening light disruption because their cortisol curve is already deformed.

**A consistent wake time, with light immediately on waking.** Variable wake times within a four-hour window are tolerated by healthy thyroids and not tolerated by recovering ones. Pick a wake time and hold it within thirty minutes seven days a week. Pair it with morning light exposure within the first hour. The recovery is fragile in the early weeks; protect the rhythm.

The pattern across these practices is that warmth and circadian alignment reduce the metabolic load the thyroid system has to carry. They are not optional ornaments on the medication; they are the conditions under which the medication does measurable work.

## Pillar 5 — Stress Reduction

Cortisol and adrenaline antagonize thyroid at every level. They suppress TSH release from the pituitary, inhibit the deiodinase enzymes that convert T4 to T3 in the liver, displace thyroid hormone from its receptors, and accelerate hormone breakdown in the cytoplasm. A patient who is grinding through a high-cortisol life will not recover from hypothyroidism, no matter how carefully the medication is dosed. The stress pillar is what removes the headwind that the other pillars are working against.

**Walking instead of running.** Long-duration cardio elevates cortisol, depletes glycogen, raises catecholamines, and accelerates thyroid hormone breakdown. Twenty to forty minutes of unhurried walking, preferably outdoors in morning or afternoon light, produces the cardiovascular and mood benefits of exercise without the cortisol penalty. Strength training in short sets with adequate rest is also fine. The exercise pattern to avoid is the one most fitness culture endorses: long runs, HIIT, two-a-days, fasted cardio, training to failure as a regular practice. See [[walking]].

**Bag-breathing for elevated lactate or panic.** Cupped hands or a paper bag, breathing in and out at normal tidal volume for two to five minutes, raises blood CO2 and shuts down the hyperventilation that drives anxiety, lactate accumulation, and the cold-extremity vasoconstriction characteristic of late-stage hypothyroidism. Useful as an acute tool and as a discipline that retrains chronic over-breathing. See [[bag-breathing]].

**Avoid cold plunges.** The Wim Hof and ice-bath culture has popularized cold exposure as a longevity practice. For an already-hypothyroid patient running cold, the cold plunge is a thyroid-depleting stress, not a hormetic one. The brief catecholamine surge feels invigorating; the sustained metabolic cost is a setback. The same applies to cold showers, prolonged outdoor work in inadequate clothing, and the "embrace discomfort" school of self-improvement. Cold tolerance is a *result* of recovery, not a method for producing it. See [[cold-plunge-dont-do-it]].

**Sleep at seven to nine hours, with consistent timing.** Sleep deprivation raises cortisol, suppresses thyroid release, impairs glucose tolerance, and dysregulates appetite. The eight-hour target is not a luxury; it is part of the protocol. Patients who sleep five or six hours during recovery should expect slower progress and should not assume they can substitute caffeine for thyroid.

**No fasting, no skipped meals.** The fasting-as-medicine movement has produced one of the more durable myths in alternative health. For a hypothyroid patient, fasting depletes liver glycogen within twelve to twenty-four hours, shuts down T4-to-T3 conversion as a survival response, raises cortisol, raises adrenaline, and stalls every other variable the protocol is trying to move. Eat every three to four waking hours. Close the longest window with the bedtime snack.

**Minimize the chronic stressors that can be minimized.** The job that requires emergency vigilance every Tuesday afternoon; the relationship that produces nightly cortisol spikes; the financial situation that makes the bedtime snack feel like a frivolous indulgence; the news consumption pattern that keeps the sympathetic nervous system half-activated through the day. Some of these are not negotiable in the short term. Many of them have margins that can be adjusted. The protocol's tolerance for chronic stress is low, and patients who try to outrun a hostile life with thyroid medication discover the medication cannot keep up.

This is not a sermon about life balance. It is the recognition that the recovery framework has an upper limit on the cortisol load it can tolerate, and that the patient is the one who controls most of that load. The medication and the diet and the supplements work better when the day stops trying to defeat them.

## Tracking Template

The recovery is a moving target, and the only honest way to know whether the pillars are working is to measure. Peat's measurement set was simple, cheap, and ruthlessly informative. The blood tests fit at the edges; the temperature, pulse, and subjective markers do the heavy lifting.

### Daily

1. **Morning oral temperature.** Before drinking, eating, getting out of bed, or speaking more than necessary. Under the tongue until the thermometer beeps. Target: 97.8°F or higher, trending toward 98.0°F.
2. **Morning pulse.** Sixty seconds at the radial artery. Target: 75 to 85 beats per minute. Athletic bradycardia below 60 is not health.
3. **Mid-afternoon temperature.** Between 2 and 4 PM, the daily peak. Target: 98.6°F or slightly higher.
4. **Mood, 1 to 5.** A subjective rating taken at the same time each evening. Watch the trend more than the absolute number.
5. **Sleep quality, 1 to 5.** Subjective. Pair with notes on time-to-sleep, mid-night wakings, and waking energy.

Record everything in a spreadsheet or a paper notebook with one row per day. The full measurement protocol, the confounders that can ruin a reading (a recent hot drink, an active head cold, a menstrual phase shift, a thermometer with a dead battery), and the interpretation matrix for the patterns you will see, are in [[temperature-tracking]].

### Weekly

1. **Body weight, fasted, after the bathroom in the morning.** Once a week, not daily. Daily weight readings drive the kind of obsessive monitoring that itself raises cortisol.
2. **Waist circumference at the navel.** Once a week. More informative than weight for tracking the body composition shift that accompanies recovery.
3. **A written one-paragraph summary.** What changed this week. What stayed stuck. What needs attention.

### Monthly

1. **Labs if available.** A basic panel can include TSH, free T4, free T3, reverse T3, vitamin D, ferritin, a metabolic panel with lipids. Read them in light of the temperature and pulse trend, not against the lab's reference range. TSH below 1.0 is the Peat target; the lab's "normal" goes up to 4.5 and is not informative.
2. **Photos.** Front, side, back. Hair on the scalp, hair on the outer eyebrows, skin tone. The phone camera at the same time of day under the same light, monthly. The visual trend often shows what the scale and the lab miss.

## Recovery Timeline

The timeline below is an average across cases Peat documented and an average across what the temperature-and-pulse literature shows. Individuals vary; the order is consistent.

**Week 1 to 2 — Warming and mood.** Hands and feet less cold within days of the dietary baseline being executed. A first lift in mood as blood sugar stabilizes and the chronic cortisol pattern begins to soften. Morning temperature beginning to inch upward by 0.2 to 0.4°F. Vivid dreams returning for many patients. Sleep latency reducing.

**Weeks 2 to 8 — Temperature and pulse normalization.** Morning temperature crossing 97.8°F and trending toward 98.0°F. Afternoon temperature reaching 98.6°F on most days. Resting pulse climbing into the 70s and toward 75 to 85. Vivid dreams persisting. Less afternoon fog. Less caffeine required to function. Bowel pattern normalizing. Cold intolerance fading.

**Months 2 to 6 — Hair, skin, and libido.** Hair thickening along the outer eyebrows and at the temples — the classic Peat marker. Skin tone changing, shifting from sallow toward warm. Libido returning. Men noticing morning erections coming back. Women noticing more regular cycles, less PMS, less estrogen-dominant breast tenderness. Body composition shifting, often without weight change, as edema clears and lean tissue rebuilds.

**Months 6 to 12 — Full energy and fat redistribution.** Sustained energy through the afternoon without caffeine. Libido stable rather than intermittent. Body composition shift becoming visible to others. Less dietary rigidity required as the liver rebuilds glycogen reserves and the cell membranes begin to turn over their PUFA load. Many patients begin to need a lower thyroid dose at this stage; the medication should be re-titrated against temperature and pulse, not increased reflexively.

**Beyond twelve months — PUFA depletion shows.** The four-year half-life of stored PUFA in body fat means that the second year of recovery is generally easier than the first, and the third easier than the second. Cholesterol metabolism normalizes. Background anxiety that many hypothyroid patients carry for decades begins to lift for good. Cold tolerance returns — not because the patient is white-knuckling through the cold, but because metabolism actually generates heat now.

The timeline tells you what to look for and when. If you are not seeing the week 1 and week 2 markers, the diet is not being executed cleanly. If you are not seeing the month 2 to 6 markers, look at the common stalls below.

## Common Stalls and the Five Reasons

The protocol has predictable failure modes. Five of them account for the vast majority of stuck cases.

**1. Under-dosed thyroid.** The patient is on 25 or 50 mcg of T4-only and the prescribing physician is dosing against TSH. The TSH looks beautiful; the temperature is 97.0 and the pulse is 62. Resolution: re-titrate against temperature and pulse per the framework in [[thyroid-dosing]], and consider a switch from T4-only to a combination preparation. The conversation with the prescribing physician is critical; if they will not engage with the temperature and pulse data, find one who will.

**2. Persistent PUFA from old cooking habits.** The patient cleaned up the kitchen but still eats Sunday lunch at a restaurant that fries in soybean oil. The chicken in the supermarket is fed soy and corn so its tissue PUFA is high. The "healthy" granola, the salad dressing at work, the energy bar between meetings. PUFA is everywhere in the modern food supply, and the half-life of stored PUFA is roughly four years. Resolution: audit the kitchen again with skepticism, ask at restaurants, accept that two or three meals a week of hidden PUFA can flatten progress, and be patient — the tissues clean up slowly even under perfect compliance.

**3. Gut dysbiosis blocking conversion.** A large fraction of T4-to-T3 conversion happens not in the liver but in the gut wall and the bacterial flora. SIBO, persistent diarrhea or constipation, and the bloating-after-every-meal pattern point at a gut that is not converting properly. Resolution: raw carrot daily (the antibiotic-like effect Peat documented), well-cooked food, adequate protein, and where indicated a short course of antibacterials (tetracycline at low dose, or natural antimicrobials like coconut oil and aspirin) under medical guidance. See [[gut-health]] if catalogued.

**4. Estrogen excess in women blocking the thyroid receptor.** Estrogen suppresses thyroid at the gland, at the transport step (it raises thyroid-binding globulin, locking hormone away from tissues), and at the receptor. For women, this is the hidden variable that most often stalls recovery. Sources: PUFA-loaded body fat (estrogen is stored in adipose tissue), oral contraceptives, plastic and personal-care xenoestrogens, low progesterone of perimenopause. Resolution: weight loss if applicable, progesterone supplementation in some cases, and the estrogen-detox protocol in [[estrogen-detox]] and [[xenoestrogens]].

**5. Chronic unaddressed stressor.** The job, the relationship, the financial pressure, the news habit. Cortisol and adrenaline are running at high baseline and the thyroid system is being told, hormonally, that the famine continues. Resolution: identify the largest tractable stressor and reduce it. This is rarely a one-week intervention; it is a season of life. Patients who try to medicate around a chronic stressor without addressing it discover the medication cannot keep up.

The order to investigate stalls is roughly the order above. Most stuck cases will resolve when one or two of these reasons are corrected. Cases that remain stuck after careful work through all five point at the escalation criteria in the next section.

## When to Escalate

Several presentations require medical supervision and cannot be safely run on the protocol alone. The framework supports medical treatment in all of these cases; it does not replace it.

**Cardiac symptoms.** New palpitations, chest discomfort, irregular pulse, or unexplained shortness of breath during recovery require an immediate cardiology workup. Thyroid medication can precipitate atrial fibrillation in patients with underlying heart disease, and unrecognized hyperthyroid states can produce arrhythmias. Do not raise the dose, do not lower the dose, and do not change the protocol unilaterally — see a cardiologist.

**Severe hair loss, persistent edema, and cognitive decline suggesting myxedema.** Advanced hypothyroid decompensation can present with non-pitting edema in the face and lower legs, slowed reflexes, cold-induced confusion, and the puffy-faced appearance characteristic of severe untreated hypothyroidism. This is a presentation that warrants an endocrinology consultation, prescription thyroid hormone, and inpatient management in extreme cases. The food and lifestyle protocol supports recovery once medication is in place, but the medication is required first.

**Pregnancy planning, active pregnancy, or fertility treatment.** Dosing precision matters for the developing child. Thyroid hormone requirements rise by roughly 30 percent in the first trimester. Work with a physician who is willing to monitor temperature, pulse, and free T3 alongside TSH and adjust the dose proactively. The food and supplement layer of the protocol applies; the medication layer requires more careful supervision than a non-pregnant patient. See [[pregnancy-nutrition]] for the dietary specifics.

**Hashimoto's flare with rising antibody titers.** Active autoimmune destruction of the gland behaves differently from primary hypothyroidism. Iodine restriction (not megadose iodine), low-dose naltrexone, careful attention to gut barrier function, and selenium adequacy all matter more in Hashimoto's than in other presentations. Antibody titers should be tracked alongside the temperature-pulse data. See [[hashimotos]] if catalogued.

**Concurrent depression, anxiety, or psychiatric medication.** SSRIs, SNRIs, and antipsychotics interact with thyroid metabolism in ways that complicate titration. Lithium directly suppresses thyroid function. Patients on these medications should adjust the protocol with their prescribing psychiatrist's knowledge, not in opposition to it. The food, supplement, and lifestyle layers are compatible with psychiatric medication; the thyroid dose adjustments require coordination. See [[anxiety-depression-protocol]] for the parallel framework.

**Previous thyroid surgery or radioactive iodine ablation.** The gland is gone and exogenous hormone is mandatory. The protocol's other four pillars still apply and still improve outcomes, but the medication layer is non-discretionary. Work with the prescribing endocrinologist.

The framework's tolerance for "this patient is the exception" is low. Most stalled cases are not exceptions; they are one of the five common stalls above. But genuine medical escalation is a real category, and the patient who needs it should get it.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream endocrinology framework rejects most of what is on this page. The standard model is that TSH plus free T4 is sufficient diagnosis, that T4-only replacement is adequate for almost all patients, that combination T4-plus-T3 therapy is unnecessary and possibly harmful, that desiccated thyroid is imprecise and outdated, that temperature and pulse are too crude to titrate against, and that subclinical hypothyroidism in the 2.5-to-4.0 TSH range is not a clinical entity requiring treatment. The basis for this consensus is fifty years of TSH-driven literature, much of which Peat argued was calibrated to a fraudulent prevalence figure from the late 1940s (see [[tsh]] for the historical case). Both positions cannot be right, and the patient stuck between them has to choose which framework better matches the actual clinical picture in front of them.

The mainstream nutrition framework also rejects much of Pillar 2. Whole milk, orange juice, salt to appetite, weekly liver, no PUFA, no fasting, and a bedtime snack are all positions that current dietary guidance treats as risk factors for cardiovascular disease, obesity, or insulin resistance. The Peat counter-argument is that those guidelines were derived from epidemiological studies of populations already metabolically compromised by industrial seed oils, chronic stress, and undereating — and that prescribing the same restrictions to recovering hypothyroid patients makes the recovery slower, not faster. The empirical test is the patient's temperature and pulse over months, not a published meta-analysis.

## Data gaps

The protocol's evidence base is dominated by Peat's correspondence, Broda Barnes's clinical records, and patient self-reports. Randomized controlled trials of the integrated five-pillar approach do not exist. The component pieces — desiccated thyroid versus T4 alone, low-PUFA diets, vitamin K2 supplementation, morning light exposure — have varying degrees of trial support, but the integration is clinical practice rather than RCT-validated medicine. Patients who require the certainty of placebo-controlled trial evidence will not be satisfied by what is documented here. Patients who have been failing on the standard protocol for years and are willing to run a structured therapeutic trial against measurable endpoints (temperature, pulse, symptom resolution) are the audience for whom the framework was built.

The timing rules above (week 1-2 warming, months 2-6 hair) are averages across cases Peat described, not statistical distributions. Individual variation is wide. The order is more reliable than the calendar.
