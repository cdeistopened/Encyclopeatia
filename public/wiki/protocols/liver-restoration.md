# Liver Restoration

The liver was a central concern in Ray Peat's framework for a reason. No other single organ does more of the work that the rest of his physiology requires. The liver stores the [[blood-sugar|glycogen]] that prevents the cortisol-adrenaline cascade in the night and between meals. The liver converts T4 to active T3, producing roughly two-thirds of the body's circulating active thyroid hormone. The liver attaches glucuronic acid to [[estrogen]] and dumps it into bile so the gut can carry it out. The liver makes bile from cholesterol, treats [[PUFA]] as a toxin to be packaged into VLDL, performs gluconeogenesis from amino acids when glucose runs low, synthesizes most circulating plasma proteins, and runs the Phase I and Phase II [[detoxification]] machinery that clears drugs, hormones, and bacterial metabolites. A liver that is performing all these functions well is invisible. A liver that is overloaded, undersupplied, or fat-infiltrated is the engine of the metabolic decline Peat spent his career describing.

"Liver restoration" in this framework is not the wellness-industry's "liver cleanse" — no fasting, no milk thistle protocol, no coffee enemas, no parasite purge. The restoration is a sequenced rebuild of the inputs the liver runs on: glucose to fuel the conjugation enzymes, B-vitamins and minerals as cofactors, thyroid hormone to drive the deiodinase, saturated fat to displace stored PUFA over months, and a steady absence of the specific inputs that overwhelm the system (alcohol, acetaminophen, megadose retinol, mobilized stored PUFA from fasting). The mechanism page at [[detoxification]] explains what the liver actually does at the level of Phase I, II, and III enzymes; this protocol applies that mechanism to a person whose liver enzymes are elevated, whose imaging shows steatosis, whose body temperature is low despite a normal TSH, or whose metabolism is generally failing in a way that points back to hepatic capacity.

## What the Liver Does That's Relevant Here

Six functions matter for restoration, and each one breaks down in a recognizable way when the organ is in trouble.

**Glycogen storage.** A healthy adult liver holds roughly 80 to 120 grams of glycogen — enough to fuel the brain and red blood cells for six to eight hours of fasting. Beyond that, the body shifts to gluconeogenesis (breaking down protein) and lipolysis (releasing free fatty acids), both stress responses. A hypothyroid or fatty liver stores less glycogen, runs out faster, and triggers the cortisol-adrenaline cascade sooner. This is why Peat made the bedtime carbohydrate snack non-negotiable — see [[sleep-restoration]] for the mechanism — and why "small frequent meals" returns over and over in his recommendations for people whose metabolism is impaired.

**T4 to T3 conversion.** "Approximately two-thirds of the body's active T3 is produced not in the [[thyroid]] gland but in the liver," reads the canonical statement of this position. The conversion is performed by the deiodinase enzyme family and requires adequate glucose, selenium, and a hepatocyte that is not under inflammatory stress. The clinical signature of a liver that is failing at this step is the patient with a "normal" TSH and a "normal" T4 but a body temperature in the 97s and a resting pulse below 70 — the thyroid panel looks fine because the bottleneck is downstream of the gland.

**Estrogen glucuronidation.** Every estradiol molecule the body produces (or absorbs from food, water, plastic, or personal care) is eventually conjugated in the liver with glucuronic acid, dumped into bile, and excreted in the stool — *if* the system is working. UDP-glucuronate is built from UDP-glucose, which requires dietary carbohydrate. A hypoglycemic or fasting liver cannot produce enough UDP-glucuronate to keep up with the estrogen load, and estrogen recirculates. The estrogen recirculation problem is the through-line of Peat's view of female-specific symptoms (PMS, fibroids, heavy bleeding, perimenopause); see [[estrogen-detox]] for the full four-lever protocol that builds on liver restoration.

**Bile production and biliary excretion.** Bile is the export channel for cholesterol, fat-soluble vitamins, conjugated hormones, and Phase II metabolites. Sluggish bile flow (cholestasis) backs up the whole system — fat-soluble vitamins fail to be absorbed, conjugated estrogen sits in the liver and gets reabsorbed, and digestion of dietary fat becomes incomplete. Coffee, the bitter compounds in cooked greens, and adequate dietary fat (especially saturated fat from butter and coconut oil) all support bile flow.

**Gluconeogenesis and the Randle cycle.** The liver can manufacture glucose from amino acids when carbohydrate is short, but at a cost. Gluconeogenesis is cortisol-driven, catabolic, and produces ammonia that needs to be processed back through the urea cycle. A liver that is forced to gluconeogenically support the rest of the body — because the person is fasting, dieting, or chronically under-eating carbohydrate — is a liver running in stress mode. The fix is not "support gluconeogenesis"; it is to *spare* gluconeogenesis by supplying dietary sugar so the liver does not have to do it.

**Protein synthesis.** Albumin, ceruloplasmin, the clotting factors, the binding proteins for thyroid and steroid hormones, and most circulating enzymes are made in the liver. Low albumin and low ceruloplasmin are both Peat-recognized signs that the liver is failing at its protein-synthetic role, usually downstream of inadequate protein, inadequate copper (for ceruloplasmin), or chronic inflammation.

## Diagnosis Prerequisites

Before running this protocol, confirm the picture with both labs and symptoms. Labs alone miss the picture in two directions — early dysfunction shows symptoms before the enzymes rise, and a stressed liver can show normal enzymes if the hepatocytes are not yet leaking.

**Labs.** Elevated ALT and AST suggest hepatocyte leak; GGT suggests biliary stress and is the most sensitive single marker for alcohol-related and metabolic liver injury. A GGT in the 30s in a non-drinker is a yellow flag in Peat's framework even if it sits within the standard "normal" range. Alkaline phosphatase rises with cholestasis. Low albumin and low ceruloplasmin point to protein-synthetic failure. Elevated ferritin can indicate iron loading (hemochromatosis or transfusion-history) — a separate problem that demands its own approach.

**Imaging.** Ultrasound or MRI showing hepatic steatosis (fatty liver, NAFLD) puts the diagnosis on solid ground. The histological grades (mild, moderate, severe) correlate roughly with how long the restoration will take.

**Symptoms.** Afternoon energy crashes, waking at 3 to 4 AM, cold hands and feet, body temperature in the 97s, slow digestion of dietary fat (loose stools, gallbladder symptoms, pale stools), elevated estrogen symptoms in either sex, and a general sense of "running on cortisol" all point to the liver before they point anywhere else.

**The hidden-hypothyroid pattern.** A normal TSH and normal T4 with a low body temperature and a low pulse, especially when combined with one or more of the above symptoms, is the classic signature of failed peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion. The liver is the most likely site. Treating with T4 alone in this pattern often makes things worse; the bottleneck is downstream.

## Phase 1 — Reduce Load (Weeks 1 through 4)

The first phase is subtractive. Until the inputs overwhelming the liver are removed, no amount of supplementation will move the needle. Five categories of input need to come out before adding anything new.

**Seed oils, eliminated.** Polyunsaturated fats from corn, soy, cottonseed, safflower, sunflower, canola, and grapeseed oil are the single largest hepatic burden in the modern diet. They are stored in adipose and hepatocyte membranes for a tissue half-life of roughly two years, with full clearance taking about four — so the seed-oil-out work is a multi-year project — but the *new input* must stop now. Read every label. Restaurant fried food, salad dressings, mayonnaise, packaged baked goods, and most "heart-healthy" oils are all sources. Cook in butter, coconut oil, beef tallow, or olive oil. See [[pufa-depletion]] for the longer-arc tissue-clearance protocol.

**Alcohol, stopped during restoration.** Peat was emphatic that alcohol shifts liver metabolism away from oxidation and is "responsible for aneurysms as well as fatty liver." During an active restoration window, even moderate intake (a glass of wine with dinner) defeats the protocol. The interaction with stored PUFA is especially destructive: "If a person eats polyunsaturated fats, alcohol is more likely to cause oxidative reactions between iron and the fats." After three to six months of clean restoration work, small amounts of well-tolerated alcoholic beverages (a glass of wine with food, occasionally) become defensible; during the first phase, nothing.

**Acetaminophen, replaced with [[aspirin]] when an analgesic is needed.** Acetaminophen is hepatotoxic at doses much lower than the package indicates, especially in a liver already depleted of glutathione. Peat treated the displacement of aspirin by acetaminophen as "one of the most consequential pharmaceutical marketing victories of the twentieth century" — aspirin protects the liver, while acetaminophen poisons it. For someone using over-the-counter analgesics for headaches, joint pain, or menstrual cramps, switch the bottle in the medicine cabinet now. Aspirin requires accompanying [[vitamin-k]] if used at high doses; for ordinary analgesic use, a baby aspirin (81 mg) to a standard adult dose (325 mg) with food is the baseline.

**Megadose vitamin A supplements, stopped.** A weekly four-ounce serving of beef [[liver]] supplies the retinol the body needs. Capsule retinol — especially at 10,000 IU or more daily — overloads the same hepatocytes the protocol is trying to restore. Peat: "Vitamin A is highly unsaturated, in excess it suppresses the thyroid, so it has to be balanced with the thyroid." Stop the capsules; rely on liver and dairy fat for retinol.

**Fructose-overload, distinct from fruit, minimized.** The fructose problem in mainstream nutrition is the high-fructose corn syrup problem — pure refined fructose in soda, packaged sweets, and ultra-processed food, consumed in the absence of accompanying nutrients. Peat was clear that *fructose itself detoxifies the liver* — "fructose detoxifies many things that enter the liver, including alcohol" — but the experimental literature blaming fructose for NAFLD systematically combined fructose with PUFA. The practical instruction is: ripe fruit, fruit juice, honey, and table sugar are protective; the soda-and-packaged-food matrix that delivers them in the standard American diet is not. See [[fructose]] for the full disambiguation.

**Fasting, prolonged carb restriction, and intermittent fasting, stopped.** This is the most counterintuitive instruction for someone arriving from mainstream "liver detox" advice, and it is the most important. Fasting depletes liver glycogen, forces gluconeogenesis, mobilizes stored PUFA from adipose into a liver that cannot keep up with the substrate, and shuts down the Phase II conjugation enzymes that depend on UDP-glucuronate. Peat: "If you slow down the liver's activity — which happens in a fast — your brain and organs are exposed to chemicals diffusing out of fat and muscles. The best cleansing diet is one that increases your liver's action. Tasty food, sugar, and thyroid activate the liver." Three meals plus three snacks daily, never going more than four hours without food during the day, and a mandatory bedtime snack. The discipline runs against fashionable "metabolic flexibility" advice and is non-negotiable here.

## Phase 2 — Glycogen Restoration (Weeks 4 through 12)

Once the inputs are clean, the liver needs the substrate to rebuild its glycogen capacity. This is mostly a carbohydrate and protein problem, and the carbohydrate has to come in a form the liver can use without spiking insulin in a way that triggers reactive hypoglycemia four hours later.

**Small frequent meals, carb-plus-protein combo.** Peat: "Small meals help to increase the metabolic rate, single big meals increase fat storage. Frequent meals are helpful during hypothyroidism, and help to prevent obesity, but when the thyroid and liver are working, 2 or 3 meals a day are fine." During restoration, default to four to six eating events daily: three meals and three snacks. Every meal pairs a carbohydrate source with a protein source. Carbohydrate alone produces the hypoglycemic crash that drives the cortisol cycle; protein alone fails to refill glycogen.

**The orange juice and milk pattern.** A pint of orange juice and a pint of whole or 2% milk between meals supplies fructose (the most direct substrate for hepatic glycogen synthesis), glucose, sucrose, lactose, casein protein, calcium, and B vitamins simultaneously. Peat's own diet anchored on this combination for decades. The fructose in orange juice goes straight to the liver and converts to glycogen with less insulin response than a comparable dose of starch — exactly the metabolic profile a depleted liver needs.

**Ripe fruit between meals.** Tropical and stone fruits at full ripeness — mangoes, papayas, sapotes, oranges, ripe bananas, peaches, melons, pineapple — supply fructose, glucose, potassium, magnesium, and antioxidants in a matrix the gut handles well. Underripe fruit contains anti-metabolic compounds (chitinase inhibitors, raffinose, raw starch) that ferment in the small intestine; ripeness matters.

**The mandatory bedtime snack.** A small carbohydrate-and-fat snack 30 to 60 minutes before sleep — a glass of milk and a spoon of honey, a small bowl of ice cream, cheese and fruit, orange juice with salt — refills glycogen for the overnight fast. Without this, the liver runs out of glycogen at roughly 3 AM, the body releases cortisol and adrenaline to maintain blood glucose, and the resulting wake-up at 3 to 4 AM is the most reliable signal that the liver did not store enough for the night. See [[sleep-restoration]] for the full mechanism.

**Salt and gelatin to lower cortisol and support glycine.** Adequate salt prevents the aldosterone-cortisol surge that low-sodium diets produce. Adequate [[gelatin]] (from bone broth, slow-cooked cuts with skin, or unflavored gelatin powder in coffee or juice) supplies the glycine that the glutathione conjugation pathway needs. Roughly a third of total protein from glycine-rich sources is the target — muscle meat alone leaves the liver glycine-limited.

The signal that Phase 2 is working: body temperature drifts up by 0.5 to 1°F over the eight to twelve weeks, the 3 AM wake-ups stop, afternoon energy stabilizes, the cold hands and feet warm up, and labs (if rechecked at week 12) show ALT, AST, and GGT trending down even before the imaging changes.

## Phase 3 — Cofactor Supplementation (Weeks 4 through Ongoing)

Glycogen restoration and cofactor supplementation overlap — start adding the cofactors at week 4 when the foundation is laid, and continue them indefinitely. The B-vitamins and minerals below are the enzymatic cofactors that Phase I oxidation, Phase II conjugation, and the deiodinase conversion of T4 to T3 all depend on.

**B-vitamin complex, food-first.** Beef [[liver]] (four to six ounces weekly), eggs (one or two daily), [[milk]] (two to four cups daily), and well-cooked meat supply the full B-spectrum. The three that matter most for hepatic function are riboflavin (B2, for the FAD-dependent enzymes of Phase I), niacinamide (B3, for the NAD/NADH balance that drives the entire respiratory chain), and methylcobalamin (B12, for methylation and the regeneration of folate). A B-complex supplement is reasonable as a hedge during restoration but should be a low- to moderate-dose formulation; megadose B6 in particular can produce neuropathy.

**Choline, primarily from eggs and liver.** Choline is the limiting factor for VLDL packaging — the mechanism by which the liver exports fat into the bloodstream. Without enough choline, fat accumulates as steatosis even on an otherwise reasonable diet. Two egg yolks daily plus the weekly liver meal supplies the choline most adults need; supplemental phosphatidylcholine (1 to 2 g daily) is a reasonable addition during active fatty-liver reversal.

**Vitamin E, mixed tocopherols.** [[Vitamin E]] protects the unsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes (including the residual stored PUFA being slowly cleared) from peroxidation. A daily dose of 100 to 400 IU mixed tocopherols, taken with food, is Peat's general recommendation. The "mixed" matters — alpha-tocopherol alone displaces the other tocopherols and tocotrienols and can produce its own imbalance.

**Vitamin K1 + K2.** [[Vitamin K]] supports the carboxylation reactions in the liver that activate clotting factors and the calcium-binding proteins that keep calcium out of soft tissue. Cooked greens (kale, chard, parsley) supply K1; aged cheeses, butter from grass-fed cows, and natto supply K2. Supplemental K2 (100 to 200 mcg daily as MK-4 or MK-7) is reasonable, especially for anyone using high-dose aspirin.

**Selenium for deiodinase.** Selenium is the catalytic atom in the deiodinase enzyme. Two Brazil nuts weekly, or a serving of seafood, or 100 to 200 mcg of supplemental selenomethionine, covers the requirement. More is not better — excess selenium produces its own toxicity.

**Magnesium.** [[Magnesium]] supports ATP synthesis, glucose handling, and bile flow. Most people in the modern food environment are deficient. Supplement 300 to 600 mg daily as magnesium glycinate (well-tolerated) or magnesium chloride (less expensive) or as a topical magnesium oil. The bowel-tolerance limit is the practical ceiling.

**Vitamin A from food only.** Skip the capsules. Liver weekly, egg yolks daily, and full-fat dairy supplies the retinol the thyroid and steroidogenesis demand without overloading the hepatocyte.

## Phase 4 — Active Restoration (Ongoing)

The active layer is the set of substances and practices that *positively* support the liver — not just remove burdens or supply cofactors, but actively drive the hepatic capacity in the right direction.

**Beef liver, weekly.** Four to six ounces of beef liver, cooked quickly in butter, with orange juice and coffee, once a week. This is the single most concentrated source of preformed [[vitamin-a]], copper, B12, choline, and the "shotgun effect" of unknown cofactors that Peat valued. See the canonical [[liver]] page for the full rationale, including why daily liver is counterproductive and why the weekly cadence is precise.

**Aspirin, daily, with food.** A baby aspirin (81 mg) or low-dose adult aspirin (162 to 325 mg) daily with a meal, supplemented with vitamin K if used long-term at higher doses. Aspirin reduces inflammation, lowers liver enzymes, protects against hepatic cancer, and supports the same Phase II glycine-conjugation pathway it itself moves through. The displacement of acetaminophen by aspirin is one of the single highest-leverage interventions in this protocol.

**[[Coffee]], two to four cups daily, with cream and sugar.** Coffee is the single most-studied hepatoprotective beverage in the epidemiological literature. Heavy coffee drinkers have lower liver enzymes, lower rates of liver cancer, lower rates of NAFLD, and lower all-cause mortality. The mechanism is multiple — caffeine raises metabolic rate, the polyphenols induce Phase II enzymes, the bitter compounds stimulate bile flow. Drink it with cream and sugar to avoid the cortisol bump that black coffee on an empty stomach can produce. Take it with the liver meal to limit iron absorption.

**Thyroid optimization, if hypothyroid.** If the body temperature stays below 98°F and the pulse below 75 after eight to twelve weeks of glycogen restoration, the metabolism is still failing somewhere downstream of the diet — usually the thyroid. The decision tree from this point lives at [[thyroid-dosing]] and [[hypothyroid-recovery]]. The relevant point here is that the liver cannot convert T4 to T3 efficiently while it is fat-infiltrated or under-glycogenated, so any thyroid trial without first running Phases 1 and 2 of liver restoration will give an incomplete reading.

**Estrogen reduction in women (and men over 40).** Estrogen overload burdens the liver. Liver dysfunction worsens estrogen overload. The two reinforce each other, and breaking the loop requires running the [[estrogen-detox]] protocol alongside this one. The daily raw [[carrot-salad]], adequate progesterone (where indicated), elimination of xenoestrogens, and aromatase suppression all reduce the conjugation load the liver carries.

**Daily walking and warmth.** Walking in the morning sun raises metabolic rate without the cortisol of intense exercise. A warm body has higher mitochondrial activity, which produces the ATP that Phase II conjugation requires. Cold suppresses thyroid, slows the liver, and chronically activates the stress hormones that drive estrogen production. Heated baths, sauna at moderate temperatures, and adequate clothing in winter are all part of the protocol — not because sweat carries toxins out, but because the warm body's hepatocytes work faster.

## Phase 5 — Maintenance (Ongoing)

After three to six months of active restoration, most people see liver enzymes normalize, energy stabilize through the day, body temperature lift into the 98s, and the symptoms that pointed at the liver resolve. The protocol transitions from "active restoration" to "sustained anti-stress baseline." See [[anti-stress-diet]] for the full canonical version. The maintenance layer is:

- The Peat dietary baseline (milk, OJ, fruit, eggs, gelatin, weekly liver and shellfish, butter, coconut oil, salt, sugar, coffee).
- The daily raw carrot for estrogen-recirculation control.
- Daily aspirin with food.
- Coffee with cream and sugar.
- Mandatory bedtime snack.
- No alcohol on a regular basis (occasional glass with food is fine after the liver is restored).
- No acetaminophen unless absolutely required.
- Annual or semi-annual liver enzymes and thyroid panel to catch drift early.

The maintenance pattern is the Peat diet, full stop. The restoration protocol is a sequenced ramp into it for someone arriving from a damaged baseline.

## Tracking Signals

The protocol works when these signals improve together. If any one fails to move while the others do, look for the specific bottleneck.

- **Energy stable through the day.** No 2 to 3 PM crash, no need for an afternoon nap, no caffeine dependency to make it through the afternoon.
- **No 3 AM wake-ups.** Sleeping seven to nine hours without an adrenaline-mediated wake-up is the single most reliable indicator that liver glycogen is holding overnight.
- **Body temperature rising.** Morning oral temperature above 97.8°F by week 4, above 98.2°F by week 12, settling in the high 98s in maintenance. See [[temperature-tracking]] for the measurement protocol.
- **Resting pulse settling in the high 70s to mid 80s.** Lower indicates persistent hypothyroidism; higher indicates ongoing adrenaline stress.
- **Liver enzymes trending down.** ALT, AST, and GGT all decreasing toward the lower half of the reference range over 8 to 12 weeks.
- **Mental clarity returning.** The "brain fog" of a fat-infiltrated liver lifts as ammonia clearance improves.
- **Bowel regularity.** One to two complete, well-formed movements daily indicates the bile-and-fiber loop is working.

## Failure Modes

The protocol fails predictably when one or more of the following is happening.

**Continuing alcohol intake during restoration.** Even moderate intake (one drink with dinner four nights a week) prevents the steatosis from resolving and keeps GGT elevated. The first three to six months require none.

**Fasting attempts, intermittent fasting experiments, or skipped meals.** Each missed meal depletes glycogen, triggers gluconeogenesis, and forces the liver back into stress mode. People who arrive at this protocol from a "metabolic flexibility" framework often have the hardest time accepting the meal cadence; it is the most important single change.

**High-protein, low-carbohydrate dieting.** The amino-acid load forces the liver to gluconeogenically convert protein to glucose while simultaneously processing ammonia, exactly when it is being told there is no dietary glucose. This is a worst-of-both-worlds metabolic stress and produces visible elevations in ALT and BUN. The corrective is adequate carbohydrate at every meal.

**Acetaminophen for migraines or menstrual pain.** The package label permits 4 grams daily; even half that, taken weekly, depletes glutathione and damages the hepatocyte. Switch to aspirin (with adequate vitamin K) or address the underlying cause (estrogen excess for menstrual migraines, hypoglycemia for tension headaches).

**Continuing seed-oil intake.** Restaurant fried food, packaged baked goods, salad dressings, and "vegetable oil" cooking at home all deliver doses of linoleic acid that the restoration cannot outrun. The label-reading habit is non-negotiable.

**Megadose iron supplements without documented deficiency.** Iron loading is hepatotoxic. Most adult men and most postmenopausal women are at higher risk of iron excess than deficiency; supplemental iron should require a serum ferritin and a clinical indication, not a default multivitamin habit. Coffee with the liver meal blunts iron absorption from the meal itself.

**Fish oil supplements.** Concentrated EPA/DHA capsules are the highest-density PUFA dose in the modern supplement industry. They oxidize in the capsule, in the gut, and in the bloodstream. Peat was uniformly skeptical. Get omega-3s, if needed at all, from fresh seafood once or twice weekly — not from capsules.

## What to AVOID

- **Alcohol above occasional.** During restoration, zero. In maintenance, a glass of wine with food on occasion.
- **Acetaminophen frequent use.** Aspirin replaces it for almost every common indication.
- **Megadose vitamin A supplements.** Food sources only.
- **High-dose iron without documented deficiency.** Test before supplementing.
- **Fish oil supplements.** The PUFA delivery vehicle disguised as a health product.
- **Statins when alternative possible.** Cholesterol synthesis is a hepatic function the body needs; suppressing it pharmaceutically interferes with steroidogenesis and CoQ10 production. The conditions statins are prescribed for are usually addressable with the diet and aspirin alone.
- **Prolonged fasting, "metabolic resets," intermittent fasting protocols.** Each is a direct anti-input.
- **"Liver cleanses" and "detox" supplement stacks.** The mechanism is wrong, the cost is high, the substrate (milk thistle, NAC, glutathione precursors in isolation) does not address the energy-state problem upstream.

## When to Escalate

This protocol assumes a liver that is impaired but not in failure. Three categories of presentation require medical evaluation before or during the protocol.

**Emergency.** Jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes), severe abdominal fluid retention (ascites), confusion or altered mental status, vomiting blood, or black tarry stools. These indicate decompensated liver disease and require immediate medical care, not dietary protocols.

**Cirrhosis indicators.** Spider veins on the chest, palmar erythema, easy bruising, low platelet count, splenomegaly on exam, or fibroscan showing F3 to F4 fibrosis. The protocol still applies, but it is supplementary to medical management, not a replacement for it.

**Viral hepatitis active disease.** Hepatitis B or C in the active replication phase requires hepatology consultation. The dietary protocol supports the standard treatment but does not replace antiviral therapy where indicated. Peat himself was sharply critical of interferon-based hepatitis C treatment but did not argue against modern direct-acting antiviral approaches.

For everyone else — the much larger population with fatty liver, elevated enzymes, failed T4-to-T3 conversion, and the broad metabolic syndrome that points back to the liver — the protocol is the path.
