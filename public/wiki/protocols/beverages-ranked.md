# Beverages Ranked

Most dietary frameworks treat beverages as an afterthought: drink water, maybe a cup of coffee, avoid soda. Ray Peat's framework inverts this completely. In his writing and interviews, Peat named three beverages as the foundation of a pro-metabolic diet, and he treated them with the same seriousness a pharmacologist would reserve for prescribed medications. [[milk|Milk]], [[oranges|orange juice]], and [[coffee]] with cream and sugar were not accompaniments to food. They were food in the most therapeutically active sense, and the [[anti-stress-diet]] was built around drinking them in quantities that shocked mainstream nutritionists.

Water, meanwhile, occupies an unexpected position in the hierarchy. Peat explicitly contradicted the "eight glasses a day" rule, arguing that over-hydration dilutes electrolytes, raises antidiuretic hormone, stresses the kidneys, and blunts the metabolism the rest of the diet is trying to stimulate. Hydration, in his framework, comes from nutrient-dense beverages and the water already present in fruit, broth, and milk. The water you drink is not where the water you need lives.

The ranking below sorts beverages from S-tier (the foundational pharmacy Peat prescribed in quarts per day) down to F-tier (the short list of drinks he explicitly warned against). The tiering reflects Peat's actual writing, not modern Peat-adjacent folklore. Some of the placements are counterintuitive. A glass of Mexican Coca-Cola outranks a glass of green tea. A vodka and orange juice outranks a glass of Pinot Noir. The logic, in every case, traces back to the same core metabolic framework: support oxidative metabolism, suppress estrogen and serotonin, deliver calcium and sugar without triggering a stress response.

## The Three Foundational Beverages

> Before the full table, these three deserve their own callout. They are the only beverages Peat recommended in specific daily quantities, and the anti-stress diet collapses without them.

- **Milk (two quarts per day baseline).** Two quarts delivers roughly 2,000 mg of [[calcium]] in the 2:1 calcium-to-phosphorus ratio that suppresses parathyroid hormone, plus lactose as an absorption cofactor, balanced protein, naturally occurring progesterone, and fat-soluble vitamins A, D, and K2. Peat told KMUD listeners in 2008: "I recommend at least two quarts of milk and two quarts of orange juice every day. This will stimulate the thyroid, suppress inflammation, and help the liver regulate things." For weight loss the fat content shifts to 1% or skim; for maintenance, whole is fine. The one additive to avoid is carrageenan.
- **Orange juice (a pint to a quart per day, often more).** The vehicle for [[vitamin-c|vitamin C]] in its only safe form (balanced by bioflavonoids), plus fructose, potassium, magnesium, and the naringenin that makes orange juice a natural [[aromatase]] inhibitor. Pasteurized supermarket juice is acceptable; fresh is preferable when practical. Peat said explicitly: "You need orange juice first to get your thyroid going."
- **Coffee with cream and sugar (multiple cups per day, always with food).** A meaningful source of magnesium and niacin, a progesterone elevator, an aromatase inhibitor, an iron-absorption blocker, and one of the most strongly cancer-protective substances Peat studied. The cream supplies saturated fat; the sugar prevents coffee-induced hypoglycemia. Black coffee on an empty stomach is the single most common way Peat saw coffee go wrong.

## The Ranking

### S-Tier: Peat's Recommended Foundations

| Rank | Beverage | Peat's Take | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whole or 1% milk | Two quarts daily, minimum therapeutic dose | Whole for maintenance, 1% for weight loss. Carrageenan-free. |
| 2 | Fresh orange juice | A pint to a quart daily, often more | Pasteurized fine. Pulp-free easier to drink at volume. |
| 3 | Coffee with cream and sugar | Five or more cups a day was Peat's personal pattern | Always with food. Sugar is not optional; it prevents the stress response. |
| 4 | Other fruit juice (pomegranate, grape, tropical) | "A few other juices are good, for example watermelon" | Grape juice is specifically flavonoid-rich. Not a replacement for OJ. |

### A-Tier: Acceptable Supporting Beverages

| Rank | Beverage | Peat's Take | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Espresso with cream and sugar | Same rules as drip coffee | Same caveats apply. Pair with food. |
| 6 | Gelatin broth or bone broth | Daily, form open | Also a food. Glycine is inhibitory and supports sleep. See [[gelatin]]. |
| 7 | Mexican Coca-Cola | Explicitly OK as a stress rescue | Cane sugar, caffeine, carbonation (CO2), coca leaf extractives Peat called "really biologically active." |
| 8 | Coconut water | Acceptable electrolyte beverage | Potassium-to-sodium ratio is not ideal; Peat preferred juice plus salt. |

### B-Tier: Neutral, Fine in Moderation

| Rank | Beverage | Peat's Take | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | Sparkling water | Mild CO2 load, neutral | "In a crisis situation, it (or baking soda in water) can be helpful." |
| 10 | Still water, lightly salted | Use thirst, not a clock | Plain water with meals dilutes digestion; salt plus sugar is preferable when genuinely thirsty. |
| 11 | Tonic water | Quinine is useful for muscle cramps | A specific-use beverage, not a staple. |
| 12 | Hot cocoa (with milk, sugar, pure cocoa) | Acceptable milk vehicle | Use real cocoa, not a mix with carrageenan or hydrogenated oils. |

### C-Tier: Acceptable with Major Caveats

| Rank | Beverage | Peat's Take | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13 | Black coffee | Fine in principle, suboptimal in practice | Lowers blood sugar. "If he has pure black coffee, he'll end up lowering his blood sugar." (Sarah Murray, 2011 KMUD) |
| 14 | Pure distilled spirits (vodka, clear rum, gin, unaged tequila) | "Pure colorless highly distilled alcohol is the safest" | Best mixed with orange juice. Fructose accelerates ethanol oxidation by about 80%. |
| 15 | White wine | Less estrogenic than red | Still contains yeast-derived estradiol. |
| 16 | Red wine | Contains "enough estrogen to be harmful" plus histamine and resveratrol | Resveratrol is a stilbene structurally similar to DES. See [[alcohol]]. |
| 17 | Aged whiskey, bourbon, dark rum | Retains estrogenic compounds from the barrel | Closer to wine than to vodka on the hierarchy. |

### D-Tier: Not Recommended

| Rank | Beverage | Peat's Take | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 18 | Green tea | Tea polyphenols are estrogenic in Peat's framework | Caffeine is fine in isolation; the delivery vehicle is the problem. |
| 19 | Black tea | Same problem as green, plus tannins | The caffeine is not worth the polyphenol load. |
| 20 | Matcha | Concentrated polyphenols, worse than brewed tea | Highest flavonoid density per serving of any common tea. |
| 21 | Plant milks (almond, oat, rice) | Not nutritionally dense; contain seed oils and additives | No calcium-phosphorus ratio benefit. No lactose. Gums and carrageenan typical. |
| 22 | Soy milk | Genistein activates aromatase, raising estrogen | "Soybeans happen to have a substance that does exactly the opposite" of orange juice. |
| 23 | Kombucha | Fermented tea plus sugar, plus yeast | Combines three Peat concerns. Promotes [[endotoxin]] and [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]. |
| 24 | Vegetable juices (celery, green, kale) | Nitrates, oxalates, goitrogens, low nutrient density | Raw cruciferous juice in particular was a thyroid concern. |
| 25 | Diet soda | Artificial sweeteners plus phosphoric acid without the sugar that makes regular soda useful | No metabolic upside. Worst of both worlds. |

### F-Tier: Avoid

| Rank | Beverage | Peat's Take | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 26 | Beer | "Yeast can produce estradiol. They think the yeast fermenting is a major source of the breast development in beer drinkers." Plus hops ("a very powerful estrogen"). | Peat's mole anecdote: four ounces of beer daily for a few weeks caused visible estrogen-driven skin changes. |
| 27 | Energy drinks | Taurine is fine in isolation, but the combination of synthetic vitamins, artificial sweeteners, and caffeine without food is a stress response in a can | No food context, no sugar, no cream. Everything Peat warned against in coffee consumption. |
| 28 | Sports drinks | Cheap sugars plus high sodium plus artificial colors | Worse than orange juice plus salt, which does the same job with better ingredients. |
| 29 | HFCS-sweetened US soda | HFCS itself is fine per Peat, but the phosphoric acid and lack of nutrients make it a poor vehicle for sugar | Mexican Coke outranks US Coke primarily because the coca leaf extractives remain and the sucrose tastes better, not because HFCS is poison. |

## The Coffee Question

Of the three foundational beverages, coffee is the one most commonly botched by people trying to follow Peat's framework. The failure mode is consistent: black coffee, on an empty stomach, first thing in the morning. That specific combination is what produces the shaky, anxious, cortisol-spiked experience that anti-coffee advocates describe as "caffeine sensitivity." Peat's response was not to cut the coffee but to add the two ingredients that make it work: cream and sugar, ideally with food.

The mechanism is straightforward. Caffeine increases the metabolic rate, which increases the cellular consumption of glucose. If glucose is unavailable (overnight fast, no breakfast), the body compensates with adrenaline and cortisol. The "stimulating" feeling people associate with caffeine is often a blood-sugar crash in disguise. Sugar in the coffee preempts the crash. Cream supplies saturated fat and slows absorption. Food in the stomach provides the substrate the accelerated metabolism needs. Done this way, coffee stops being a stress drug and becomes what Peat argued it actually is: a pro-metabolic, anti-serotonin, progesterone-elevating, cancer-protective, iron-blocking nutritional source.

The full case for coffee lives at [[coffee]]. The rule for the ranking is simple: coffee with cream and sugar sits at the top of S-tier. Black coffee sits in C-tier, acceptable but suboptimal. The gap between them is larger than most people assume.

## The Water Question

The "drink eight glasses of water a day" rule is one of the few mainstream nutrition recommendations Peat directly contradicted. His objection was physiological, not contrarian. Plain water, consumed in excess of thirst, dilutes serum sodium, raises antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and aldosterone, and forces the kidneys to work against an electrolyte gradient they are designed to preserve. Over-hydration is a stressor. In his writing on blood pressure, Peat noted bluntly: "Drinking plain water can cause an extreme rise in blood pressure, especially in old people, and eating a meal (containing carbohydrate) lowers blood pressure."

The hydration the body needs comes from the same beverages the rest of the anti-stress diet is built around. Milk is roughly 87% water and carries minerals, protein, and lactose with it. Orange juice is roughly 88% water and carries sugar, potassium, and flavonoids. Gelatin broth is largely water and carries glycine and salt. A person drinking two quarts of milk, a quart of orange juice, a few cups of coffee, and a bowl of broth is already past any reasonable hydration threshold before touching a glass of water.

When plain water is genuinely needed, Peat's preference was to salt it lightly or to take it with food rather than between meals. Plain water with every meal dilutes digestive enzymes. Plain water without minerals, in volume, drives ADH up and metabolic rate down. The rule is to drink for thirst, not for a quota.

## The Alcohol Caveat

Alcohol is the category where Peat's framework is most likely to surprise people, because it inverts the cultural hierarchy. Wine, the drink of sophisticated moderation, contains yeast-derived estradiol, grape-derived phytoestrogens, and (in red wine) histamine plus resveratrol, which is a stilbene structurally similar to the notorious pharmaceutical carcinogen DES. Beer, the drink of cheerful excess, is worse still: the yeast-derived estradiol compounds with hops, which Peat called "a very powerful estrogen." The "beer belly" and the gynecomastia associated with heavy beer drinking are not calorie phenomena but endocrine ones, driven by estrogen-stimulated cortisol release.

The least harmful form of alcohol, in Peat's analysis, is pure distilled spirits. The distillation process strips away the yeast-derived estrogens, the phenolics, the histamine, and the phytoalexins that survive fermentation. What remains is ethanol and water. Mixed with orange juice, which supplies fructose (which accelerates ethanol oxidation by roughly 80%) and bioflavonoids, a vodka and OJ becomes roughly the least bad way to consume alcohol. The cocktail that connoisseurs sneer at is the one Peat's framework endorses.

Dark spirits (aged whiskey, bourbon, dark rum) sit between clear spirits and wine because the barrel aging reintroduces phenolic compounds. For someone who has decided to drink, the practical hierarchy is: clear spirits with fruit juice > white wine > dark spirits > red wine > beer. The full mechanism lives at [[alcohol]], including the remarkable case studies of [[pregnenolone]] and [[progesterone]] reversing alcohol dependency and cirrhosis.

The personal anecdote worth repeating, because it is so striking: Peat drank four ounces of beer daily for a few weeks and watched several moles grow rapidly. Two weeks after stopping, the moles dried up and fell off. Four ounces is roughly one-third of a bottle. The dose was small. The biological response was not. That anecdote is the reason beer sits in F-tier, not a folklore position.
