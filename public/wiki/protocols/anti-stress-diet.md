# The Anti-Stress Diet

Ray Peat rarely used the phrase "Peat diet," and he never accepted "bioenergetic diet" as a brand. The phrase he reached for, across decades of newsletters, interviews, and email correspondence, was the **anti-stress diet**. The distinction is not cosmetic. To call it anti-stress is to name the enemy the diet is built to fight. Cortisol, adrenaline, free fatty acids, [[serotonin]], estrogen, endotoxin, and the chronically elevated [[polyunsaturated-fats|polyunsaturated fats]] stored in human tissue for years on end: these are the forces that lower metabolic rate, burn through muscle, disrupt sleep, and accelerate every degenerative disease Peat spent fifty years studying. The diet is anti-stress because every food on the plate is chosen to suppress one of those forces, and every food excluded is excluded because it feeds one of them.

What the anti-stress diet is not is a meal plan. Peat resisted the impulse to write one. He gave gram ranges reluctantly, almost always followed by the caveat that the number depended on body size, metabolic rate, activity level, and the other nutrients in the day's food. The single lever he trusted was [[metabolic-rate|metabolic rate]] itself, and the feedback he trusted was body temperature and resting pulse rather than the bathroom scale. "My recommendation is to eat to increase the metabolic rate," he wrote in an [[diet-general|email exchange on weight loss]], "rather than any particular foods." The foods are instruments. The measurement is heat production.

This document lays out the framework as Peat actually expressed it, with his own numbers where he gave them and his own qualifiers where he refused. It is neither [[ketogenic-diet|keto]] nor low-fat nor a standard "clean eating" regimen. It is closer in spirit to a traditional dairy-and-fruit diet, built around two quarts of milk, a quart of orange juice, eggs, gelatinous soups, ripe tropical fruit, a small amount of saturated fat, and enough salt to satisfy appetite. Saturated fat and sugar sit side by side on the plate. Calcium comes from food, not pills. The body is kept warm, fed frequently enough to prevent a cortisol surge, and given the raw materials to rebuild muscle while shedding stored water and fat. If the temperature rises and the pulse settles in the high seventies, the diet is working. If it does not, more food, not less, is the first correction.

## The Foundational Principle: Raise Metabolic Rate

Every other recommendation in the anti-stress diet is a subordinate to a single thesis: **raise the metabolic rate**. When cells are oxidizing glucose efficiently and producing [[carbon-dioxide]] at a high rate, the body is in its protective state. When oxidation stalls and the cell reverts to burning free fatty acids or running on [[lactic-acid]] from incomplete glycolysis, the body is in its stress state. Peat treated these as two physiologies, not a spectrum of "energy levels." The anti-stress diet exists to keep the body in the first state and out of the second.

The villains are named explicitly and repeatedly across Peat's work. Cortisol breaks down tissue and converts protein into fuel. Adrenaline mobilizes free fatty acids into the blood. Those free fatty acids, through the mechanism [[randle-cycle|Philip Randle identified in the 1960s]], block the ability of cells to use sugar for energy. When the fatty acids in question are polyunsaturated, they additionally drive chronic inflammation, [[advanced-glycation-end-products]], and the conditions of aging and diabetes. "Stress tends to increase your circulating free fatty acids, activating the Randle effect, blocking the ability to use sugar for energy," Peat told Danny Roddy in a [[55-bioenergetic-nutrition-basics|Generative Energy episode on bioenergetic nutrition basics]]. "Increasing the sugar in your diet tends to lower the lipolytic activity, keeps the fat where it belongs, and prevents the Randle shift to fat oxidation. This maximizes your carbon dioxide production, which keeps your stress-producing lactic acid inhibited."

The tools are likewise specific. Sugar in the form of fruit, fruit juice, milk, and added sucrose or honey suppresses the release of free fatty acids and feeds the mitochondria their preferred substrate. Saturated fat from milk, butter, cheese, beef, lamb, and [[coconut-oil]] supplies energy without the long-range damage of PUFA. Calcium from dairy lowers [[parathyroid-hormone]] and anti-inflammatory stress signaling. Quality protein, heavy on dairy and gelatin, supplies amino acids without the tryptophan and cysteine load of muscle meat alone. Salt raises carbon dioxide production and quiets the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis. [[Thyroid]] hormone, when the diet alone is not enough, closes the loop.

Peat summarized the mechanism in a sentence Danny Roddy extracted from him in the same Generative Energy episode: **"Sugar is my current understanding of what is most protective."** That sentence, in the context of decades of physiology research, was the closest he ever came to reducing the diet to a single molecule.

## The Macronutrient Baseline

Peat gave macronutrient numbers grudgingly. He was explicit that the numbers depended on the individual and that metabolic rate, not intake, was the variable to manipulate. Still, a few figures recur across his emails and interviews, and they are precise enough to serve as a starting point.

### Protein

The floor is **one hundred grams a day**, and it comes from a military study Peat cited repeatedly. "The military did a big study on people of military age, both men and women, and found that to do just ordinary work, both needed at least 100 grams of good protein every day," he said in the [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 Ask the Herb Doctor episode on food]]. In a later conversation with Danny Roddy on [[55-bioenergetic-nutrition-basics|bioenergetic nutrition basics]], he clarified that the 100-gram figure was "for even little people working at a desk job; that was required for efficiency" and agreed that 120 to 150 grams was "more appropriate for an older person." For intense exercise, he wrote by email, "it's about a gram per pound of body weight."

Hypothyroid individuals are the exception. When metabolism is already low, the tryptophan, methionine, and cysteine in muscle meat can suppress thyroid further and worsen the condition. "Until your metabolic rate is higher, **80 to 100 grams would be better**," Peat wrote in an [[protein|email exchange on protein]]. "Replacing it with sugar, or very well cooked starch, would support thyroid function." This is the one context in which Peat ever told anyone to eat *less* protein, and the reason is specifically metabolic, not caloric.

Peat's own intake drifted high. "I've always been very sedentary, but I have usually had close to 150 grams daily," he wrote in the same email. He favored the traditional pattern of eating the whole animal, because muscle meats alone are "essentially a refined food" relative to what traditional populations actually consumed. The protein that arrives from milk, cheese, eggs, gelatinous broth, and occasional liver and shellfish is, in Peat's framework, better balanced than the same grams from chicken breast or steak.

The liver danger is real and worth noting. Broda Barnes, whose work on [[thyroid]] Peat frequently cited, experimented with a higher-meat diet and "found he had to double his thyroid dose" to keep his metabolism in range. Protein in excess, particularly from muscle meat, can displace carbohydrate and feed the cortisol cycle. The 100-gram floor is a floor. The ceiling is the point at which temperature and pulse start to drop.

### Carbohydrate

The carbohydrate range Peat endorsed is **180 to 350 grams a day** for a person with a healthy metabolism, scaling up to "closer to a pound" for someone with a very high metabolic rate. In the [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|first of his 2010 Ask the Herb Doctor episodes on sugar]], Andrew Murray asked how many grams of sugar was a good healthy amount. Peat answered: "It depends on your total caloric requirement. Some hypothyroid women can maintain their body weight on 700 or 800 calories a day. But if you have a healthy metabolism and can burn between 2,000 and 3,500 calories a day, then your carbohydrate requirement is going to be somewhere in the range of 180 grams to 350 grams."

By email he was willing to go higher. "I have often had a gallon of orange juice in a day, with 100 grams of other sugar, and didn't see any problem, even while being sedentary," he wrote. "If your metabolic rate is high, with a pound of sugar you will still have an appetite for quite a bit of fat and protein." His stated minimum for an average-size person was 180 grams, with a typical daily average "about 250 grams." For someone running a high oxygen consumption, he told Roddy, "it would be reasonable to expect that you need closer to a pound of carbohydrate per day."

The 100-gram experiment he ran on others was a failure. "I've experimented on people with a 100-gram carbohydrate diet, and I don't think that was even close to a good health level," he said. "I think it's probably around that range of 200 or 300 that's minimum."

### Fat

The fat recommendation is the number Peat gave the most tentatively. In a [[diet-general|general-diet email]] he put the range between 30 and 50 percent of calories. "If the fat is mostly saturated, from milk, cheese, butter, beef, lamb or coconut oil, I think it's usually o.k. to get about 50% of the calories from fat, but since those natural fats typically contain around 2% polyunsaturated fats, I try to minimize my PUFA intake by having more fruit, and a little less fat, maybe 30 to 35%."

Asked by Roddy whether he could put a gram number on saturated fat, Peat hedged. "Since I've never had the opportunity to have a pleasant tasting pure saturated fat, I would guess you could eat a lot of it." Pushed on a longevity-friendly middle ground, he landed on an ounce: "If you include oleic acid, stearic, and palmitic, I think something around an ounce would probably be ideal, but I don't think there would be any harm up to that." Roughly 28 grams a day of mixed saturated and monounsaturated fat, eaten alongside sugar and protein, was his practical target. By email, asked whether forty grams of fat a day was enough, he answered simply: "I think that's enough, having a little with the other foods is best."

### Calcium

Calcium is the one mineral Peat supplied a specific daily floor for. Sarah Johanneson Murray, his longtime interviewer on KMUD, put it this way in the 2016 food episode: "Calcium is very important for your heart. You need about 2,000 mg a day, which is basically two quarts of milk." Peat himself ran higher. "I always get at least 2500 mg and sometimes 5000 mg of calcium," he told Roddy in the [[55-bioenergetic-nutrition-basics|Generative Energy nutrition basics episode]]. The floor is two thousand milligrams. The ceiling, for someone drinking three quarts of milk and eating aged cheese and gelatinous broth, easily reaches five thousand. Calcium supplements are not the vehicle. Food is.

### Total Calories

Calories, in Peat's framework, are explicitly **not** the lever. The same [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 food episode]] contains the clearest expression. Asked to name an acceptable calorie figure for a sedentary woman, he answered: "I hope 2,000 is a ballpark. You can be very sedentary and still burn close to 3,000. Some people can be moderately active and get along on 1,700. Lots of people now are hypothyroid enough that they can move around with ordinary daily activity and still gain weight on 700 or 800 calories. You really have to look at your metabolic rate."

Peat's own intake illustrated the range. "I used to drink at least a gallon of 2% or 3% milk daily, and often ate more than 5000 calories," he wrote in one general-diet email, "but when I'm completely sedentary for more than ten hours daily, my energy requirement is much lower. The calorie intake should be balanced to your heat production and activity." Heat production is the governor. A diet that suppresses heat production is a failure at any calorie level. A diet that raises it is a success at any calorie level.

Peat rarely specified exact amounts, and when pressed he emphasized tolerance and temperature feedback. The numbers above are orientation points, not prescriptions.

## The Daily Plate

The foods that make up the anti-stress diet are few, and Peat repeated them so consistently across decades that the plate is almost memorizable. A person following it closely can expect most of their calories to come from milk, orange juice, eggs, gelatinous broth, ripe fruit, cheese, and a small amount of saturated fat.

### Milk: Two Quarts Minimum

The baseline is **two quarts of milk a day**, with three quarts or a gallon being Peat's own lifetime average. "Over the years I averaged a gallon a day," he wrote, "and I liked to eat butter, fat meat, ice cream, and thick cream in my coffee, so 1% milk had enough fat." In the [[2016-12-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-food|2016 food episode]] he described his daily intake: "Two or three quarts of milk, up to a gallon per day, usually 1%. Usually one big egg fried in butter. Orange juice, as much as is available—if it's good, at least a pint, preferably more. Typically about once a week, some oysters or liver. Almost every day, some kind of gelatinous soup, like kale soup or chicken soup with all the fat skimmed off."

The two-quart floor is tied directly to calcium. Two quarts of milk yields roughly two thousand milligrams of calcium, which is the cardiovascular and anti-stress minimum. Below that, Peat considered the diet nutritionally incomplete regardless of what else was on the plate.

Milk fat content is goal-dependent, and the brief contains a real tension that deserves honest treatment. Peat personally drank higher-fat milk for decades. But for anyone trying to lose excess body fat, he gave a different instruction by email: "I have heard from several people that they think I recommend drinking whole milk, which I don't, because the amount of fat in whole milk is very likely to be fattening when a person is using it to get the needed protein and calcium." Whole milk was his own preference. Low-fat milk was his prescription for weight loss. The quantity recommendation, two quarts, is consistent across both contexts. The fat percentage depends on whether the goal is weight maintenance or fat loss.

### Orange Juice: A Pint to a Quart, Often More

Peat's single most distinctive recommendation, and the one that most alarms mainstream nutritionists, is drinking a quart or more of [[oranges|orange juice]] daily. "Orange juice, as much as is available—if it's good, at least a pint, preferably more," he said on KMUD in 2016. By email he described his own intake as "probably over a quart on average," and he mentioned having drunk a gallon in a day without incident. "I find that I need almost a pint of orange juice to balance one egg."

Orange juice is not merely a sugar source. It is an [[aromatase]] inhibitor through its naringenin and hesperidin content, a potassium and magnesium vehicle, a calcium-compatible fluid, and a delivery system for vitamin C that arrives surrounded by the [[bioflavonoids]] that keep it from becoming a pro-oxidant. In Sarah Murray's gloss from the 2013 weight gain episode, "orange juice has chemicals that are anti-estrogenic, and milk calcium is very important as an anti-inflammatory and anti-stress agent. People who eat the same number of calories without milk are much more likely to be fat."

Pasteurized supermarket orange juice is acceptable. Fresh is preferable when practical, but the volume Peat recommended is difficult to sustain without the commercial product.

### Coffee with Cream and Sugar

Coffee is the third foundational beverage, and Peat drank it the traditional way. Not black. Cream and sugar were not concessions but active ingredients: the sugar suppressing adrenaline, the cream supplying saturated fat, the coffee itself stimulating metabolism. In his [[diet-general|general-diet correspondence]] he mentioned "cafe con leche several times a day" as a regular feature of his eating pattern. The beverage belongs in the diet. The additions are part of its effect.

### Gelatin and Gelatinous Broth: Daily, Form Open

Gelatin is one of the two places in the brief where Peat refused to give a gram target, and this document will not invent one. What he said consistently is that gelatin belongs in the diet daily, that it is best obtained from bone broth rather than from a powdered supplement, and that its function is to offset the tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine load from muscle meats. "35% of the amino acids in gelatin are glycine, 11% alanine, and 21% proline and hydroxyproline," Peat wrote in his [[gelatin-stress-longevity|article on gelatin, stress, and longevity]]. "A generous supply of glycine/gelatin, against a balanced background of amino acids, has a great variety of antistress actions."

Forms he endorsed: "I occasionally use some powdered gelatin for things like making marshmallows, but usually I get my gelatin from soup, such as ox-tails, lamb shanks, or chicken backs and wings." In the 2016 food episode: "Almost every day, some kind of gelatinous soup, like kale soup or chicken soup with all the fat skimmed off." He framed gelatin as a ratio to muscle meat rather than an absolute daily amount, and he preferred the form where the collagen was extracted by home cooking rather than bought as a powder.

Glycine itself is an inhibitory neurotransmitter. "Salt and gelatin are both very helpful for good sleep," Peat said in the nutrition basics episode. A nightly bowl of broth, or a glass of milk with a small amount of honey, serves both the amino-acid and the sleep-support function.

### Eggs: One to Two a Day

Peat ate "usually one big egg fried in butter" for breakfast. Sarah Murray's guidance in the 2016 food episode was to cap the daily intake at two: "You can't eat more than two eggs a day because they contain some PUFA." One to two eggs a day, fried in butter, balanced by the orange juice that covers the vitamin load, is the template.

### Liver and Shellfish: Weekly

Liver is a source of vitamin A, copper, and B vitamins that Peat considered difficult to obtain elsewhere, but he was cautious about quantity. "Too much liver can inhibit thyroid function," he noted by email, "so the amount depends on the individual. I think there are other reasons, but the high A content is the main one." His practical recommendation was roughly twice a month: "2 or 3 eggs would be o.k., though in the longer run it's good to eat liver about twice a month, limiting the daily eggs to one or two." In the 2016 food episode he described his own pattern as closer to weekly: "Typically about once a week, some oysters or liver." Four to six ounces once a week is the target most practitioners in his orbit settled on.

Shellfish fills the trace-mineral gap that dairy and muscle meat leave open. "Shellfish provide trace minerals that are often lacking from other foods," Peat wrote. "Mercury content is high in the big (old) fish, but not in the small shellfish or small fish such as cod and sole." Oysters once a week, or a combination of oysters, crab, and small fish, covers zinc, copper, and selenium.

### Coconut Oil: From a Teaspoon to Three Tablespoons

Coconut oil is the most dose-variable item in the diet, and the brief documents the range honestly. At the low end, for a woman burning only 700 calories a day because of severe hypothyroidism, Sarah Murray suggested three tablespoons a day and Peat pulled her back: "No, more like a small teaspoon three times a day." At the middle, Peat's own experiment with "about a tablespoon of coconut oil once a day" produced two pounds a week of weight loss for several weeks, without eating less. At the high end, Sarah's normal recommendation for someone with a healthy metabolism was three tablespoons a day.

The mechanism, Peat explained in his [[coconut-oil|article on coconut oil]], is the suppression of PUFA's antithyroid effect. "A small amount of coconut oil with each meal will increase the ability to oxidize fat, by momentarily stopping the antithyroid effect of the PUFA." The 1940s farming anecdote was one he repeated often: farmers tried to fatten livestock with cheap coconut oil and instead "made them lean, active and hungry." G. W. Crile's finding that "the metabolic rate of people in Yucatan, where coconut is a staple food, averaged 25% higher than that of people in the United States" was the epidemiological support.

Peat preferred refined, deodorized coconut oil to the virgin version or the coconut meat itself. "I advise against eating the solid parts of coconut, as a regular part of the diet, and recommend the deodorized refined oil, because so many people are allergic to the proteins (and starches) of coconut."

The practical tier structure: a teaspoon three times a day for anyone with a clearly depressed metabolism, a tablespoon a day as a middle setting, up to three tablespoons a day for a healthy athletic metabolism.

### Ripe Fruit and Well-Cooked Potato

Beyond orange juice, Peat favored ripe tropical and sub-tropical fruit: mangoes, papayas, lychees, sapotas, watermelon, and whatever local fruit was in season and actually ripe. Unripe fruit, because of its [[tannins]] and resistant starch, was a problem. Ripe fruit was a primary food.

For the starch category, a well-cooked potato with butter or cream served as a backup carbohydrate source. "Well cooked potatoes, with butter or cream, are a very good way to get carbohydrate," he noted in his general-diet emails. Bread, pasta, and grain-based starches appear nowhere in his recommendations.

### Cheese

Cheese does real work in the diet. It concentrates calcium, provides saturated fat in a well-tolerated form, and satisfies the appetite for salt when salted hard cheeses are chosen. "There are some very salty cheeses that help to satisfy salt appetite, for example pecorino or feta," Peat wrote, "sometimes I add salt to cheeses such as gouda, emmental, mozzarella, or cheddar." Aged hard cheeses like parmigiano reggiano were among his regular foods.

### The Daily Raw Carrot

The daily raw carrot is one of Peat's most distinctive prescriptions. A medium-size raw carrot, eaten with a small amount of salt and oil or vinegar, supplies insoluble fiber that binds to [[endotoxin]] and to excess estrogen in the gut and carries them out of the body before they can be reabsorbed. "A daily raw carrot often helps to balance progesterone, cortisol, and estrogen, by improving intestine-liver functions," he wrote. Sarah Murray elaborated that she knew "people who have lost weight just by eating a raw carrot every day, reducing endotoxin stress." The carrot is cheap, ordinary, and one of the most leveraged single items on the plate.

### Salt: To Taste, with a Floor

Peat almost never specified a salt number. His rule was "salt food to taste." But when Georgi Dinkov raised recent human studies showing that "anything less than five grams of elemental sodium daily activates chronically the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system," Peat's response in the nutrition basics episode was unqualified: "Oh, for sure. That tiny amount brings up your anxiety and inflammation. So salt is definitely anxiolytic and anti-inflammatory. A population in Mongolia, for example, eats an average of about 30 grams of salt per day and doesn't have hypertension."

Five grams of elemental sodium corresponds to roughly thirteen grams of salt, or about two and a half teaspoons. That is the floor Peat endorsed. The Mongolia reference establishes that, in the absence of kidney disease, intakes up to thirty grams of salt a day were acceptable if the person had the appetite. Salt was not the villain. It was one of the more reliable anti-stress tools on the plate.

### Ice Cream

Ice cream, for Peat, was food and medicine at once. "It has a good balance because it has fat, protein, and sugar. It can help a lot of people go to sleep," he said in the 2013 weight gain episode. In the 2016 food episode he described using it as a sleep aid: "I've seen similar things in people with cancer or extreme old age insomnia. When they drank a syrup-like concentrate of sugar in milk or ate a bowl of ice cream during the night, they could get back to sleep." Sarah Murray described a post-surgical patient who needed "up to a pound of sugar a day in various forms—marshmallows, sugar in milk—to lower the stress so he could sleep." Ice cream is the dessert form of the anti-stress diet's central mechanism.

## The Never List

Peat was more restrained about exclusions than the average diet author, but a handful of foods and practices were genuinely off-limits.

Polyunsaturated seed and vegetable oils are the first and most emphatic exclusion. Corn, soy, safflower, sunflower, canola, grapeseed, and the rest suppress thyroid function, accumulate in tissue for years, and drive the entire [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acid]] economy Peat identified as the core of stress physiology. "By 1950," he wrote in the coconut oil article, "it was established that unsaturated fats suppress the metabolic rate, apparently creating hypothyroidism." There is no acceptable cooking use for these oils in the anti-stress diet.

Fish oil supplements fall in the same category. The same PUFA that Peat avoided in seed oil form he also avoided in concentrated omega-3 capsule form. The idea that fish oil is anti-inflammatory was, in his reading, a confusion of acute prostaglandin suppression with chronic mitochondrial damage.

Soy was excluded with a specific mechanism. Where orange juice contains aromatase inhibitors that lower estrogen production, soybeans contain genistein, which activates aromatase and raises estrogen. Small amounts of traditionally fermented soy as a condiment were acceptable. Soy protein powder, soy milk, and tofu as staples were not.

Nuts and seeds as staples are excluded for their PUFA content. Small amounts occasionally are not a problem, but walnuts, almonds, sunflower seeds, and flax as daily foods work against the diet.

Fasting, intermittent fasting, and long stretches without food are all contraindicated. The mechanism is the one Peat described in the first sugar episode on KMUD. "Cortisol begins breaking down your tissues, turning protein into fat and carbohydrate for energy. Under that extreme condition, when you've been out of glycogen long enough to run on cortisol for about a day, your thymus will be dissolved just in a few hours." Any deliberate practice of skipping meals works against the diet's foundational purpose.

Protein without carbohydrate is another failure mode. "Eating protein by itself can cause a big surge of cortisol," Peat wrote by email. "Preceding the protein with some carbohydrate makes the protein go farther, otherwise under the influence of cortisol a lot of protein is used just for energy." A chicken breast on its own is a stress meal. A chicken breast after a glass of orange juice is a normal meal.

Raw cruciferous vegetables in volume, because of their goitrogen content, are also discouraged. Well-cooked broccoli or kale in a soup was acceptable. A daily kale smoothie was not.

Low-fat dieting in general and low-salt dieting in particular both fail for the same reason: both raise stress hormones. "The increase of adrenalin caused by salt restriction has many harmful effects, including insomnia," Peat wrote in his [[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|newsletter on salt, energy, and metabolic rate]]. "Many old people have noticed that a low sodium diet disturbs their sleep, and that eating their usual amount of salt restores their ability to sleep."

## Meal Timing

Meal frequency is a function of metabolic state. For a person whose thyroid is suppressed and whose liver is unable to store eight hours of glycogen, the instruction is **small frequent meals**, four to six a day, with no gap long enough to trigger a cortisol release. "Small meals help to increase the metabolic rate, single big meals increase fat storage," Peat wrote. "Frequent meals are helpful during hypothyroidism, and help to prevent obesity, but when the thyroid and liver are working, 2, 3, or 4 meals are good. For me, 2 meals and some snacks are most convenient."

The frequency-to-fewer-meals trajectory is important. Frequent feeding is a rescue protocol, not a permanent lifestyle. As temperature and pulse normalize and glycogen storage recovers, the number of meals naturally decreases. "As the metabolism gets more effective, you don't have to eat as often as when you are starting to change," Peat wrote. "At first, when glycogen isn't being stored, temperature will rise and fall situationally."

Protein is best eaten during the daytime, with sugar and fat weighted toward the evening. "It's better to take your protein during the day, sugar and fat in the evening," Peat wrote in an [[protein|email on protein]]. The logic is circadian. Protein meals raise cortisol if the glycogen stores are low; having those meals earlier in the day, when cortisol is already supposed to be high, causes the least disruption. Sugar and fat later, as the evening cortisol rhythm drops, support the overnight sleep state.

A small preload of fruit before a protein meal further reduces the cortisol surge. "I think it's good to precede a big protein meal by maybe 15 or 20 minutes with fruits so that you get your liver stocked with some glycogen," Peat told Andrew Murray. "That way, you don't respond so violently when you eat pure protein."

## The Randle Cycle in Practice

The Randle cycle is the most misunderstood piece of Peat's framework, and the anti-stress diet depends on getting it right. A naive reading of [[randle-cycle|the Randle effect]] says that fat and carbohydrate should be separated: eat one or the other in any given meal, because the two compete for cellular uptake. Peat did not eat that way, and he did not recommend that anyone else do.

His actual practice combined saturated fat and sugar constantly. Cream in coffee. Butter on potato. Ice cream as a dessert and a sleep aid. Whole milk, historically, as a daily beverage. The anti-stress diet is built around fat-plus-sugar pairings, not fat-or-sugar separation.

The resolution is in the mechanism. The Randle effect describes how elevated free fatty acids block glucose uptake. The question is what elevates free fatty acids chronically. Peat's answer, consistent across sources, is **large fat meals** and especially **PUFA**. "It's mostly from large fat meals, at first, but then it is increased by stress, and builds up over time," he wrote in a general-diet email about what activates the Randle cycle. In the [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 sugar episode]] he made the PUFA distinction explicit: "When the fatty acids involved in blocking the sugar are polyunsaturated, they produce long-range damage... the free fatty acids not only block sugar use instantaneously (Randle effect), but they produce these advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) associated with diabetes and aging. The chronic effect of a high-fat diet, if the fat is predominantly unsaturated, produces diabetes and all the things that result from glycated proteins."

Saturated fat eaten alongside plenty of sugar is not the pattern Randle described. The saturated fat is oxidized efficiently in the presence of glucose. The sugar suppresses the release of stored free fatty acids from adipose tissue. The Randle concern is about the stress state, in which PUFA is circulating freely because the body has mobilized it to replace the sugar it cannot use. The anti-stress diet interrupts that loop by supplying sugar continuously and by replacing PUFA with saturated fat in the tissue stores.

Peat's practical synthesis, from the nutrition basics episode with Roddy: "Sugar is my current understanding of what is most protective... Increasing the sugar in your diet tends to lower the lipolytic activity, keeps the fat where it belongs, and prevents the Randle shift to fat oxidation."

## The Morning Routine

The morning is the most dangerous window in the daily cycle. "In the morning, you have generally been exposed to several hours of darkness. Darkness is very stressful. Everyone is at their peak of exposure to stress hormones in the morning," Peat said in the [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 weight gain episode]]. "During stress, like starvation or the overnight fast, hormones mobilize energy production, involving increased free fatty acids." The task of breakfast is to interrupt that surge as quickly as possible.

The sequence Peat recommended is specific. **Orange juice first, with a little salt.** The salt is important because it helps the sugar absorb and further quiets adrenaline. A glass or two of orange juice on waking, before any other food, begins the process of lowering cortisol and restocking liver glycogen. "Your diet might be low in vitamin A, so it would be better to have eggs for breakfast, preceded with a generous amount of orange juice," he wrote in one general-diet email. Breakfast itself should follow within thirty minutes: typically an egg or two fried in butter, more orange juice, and coffee with cream and sugar.

The feedback signal for a correct breakfast is counterintuitive. The pulse should go down, not up, after eating. "That's good. That is the carbohydrate lowering the adrenaline. When the temperature goes up and pulse goes down, that's what breakfast should do," Peat said in the 2013 episode on Hashimoto's, temperature, and pulse. A rising temperature indicates increased heat production from food. A falling pulse indicates that adrenaline has dropped because the liver is restocked and the cortisol-adrenaline axis no longer has to fight for glucose.

The failure mode to watch for is eggs without carbohydrate. "Sometimes that happens when they eat eggs without enough carbohydrate. If you are hypoglycemic, eating eggs stimulates insulin, drops blood sugar further, and causes a surge of adrenaline. If you feel good when the pulse goes up, you're increasing thyroid. If you feel faint or shaky, that's shock from too much protein without carbohydrate." The pint of orange juice before the egg is not a garnish. It is the difference between a pro-metabolic breakfast and a stress meal.

## The Bedtime Snack

A bedtime snack is non-negotiable for anyone whose glycogen storage is compromised, which is most of the people who find their way to the anti-stress diet in the first place. The mechanism is the inverse of the morning routine. Going to bed with a full liver reduces the overnight stress hormone surge; going to bed on an empty liver guarantees it.

Peat's standard recommendation was some combination of sugar and a small amount of salt or protein. In the [[2015-02-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-uses-of-urea|2015 episode on urea]] he described it this way: "Having a carbohydrate meal late in the afternoon or before bed—like a glass of orange juice or milk with honey—helps. Sometimes the dose of sugar puts you to sleep for only an hour or two, so it's good to have another glass ready for when you wake up. Salty snacks at bedtime, like milk with salty tortilla chips or pork rinds, help stabilize blood sugar and energy production."

The sugar-plus-salt combination is the one that lowers adrenaline most reliably. "Sugar and salt will both help to lower adrenaline," Peat said in the 2010 sugar episode, "so they can both make you sleepy while they're present and circulating. People who have insomnia can often take care of the problem temporarily by taking some sugary and salty food at bedtime."

The three-in-the-morning wake-up is a specific protocol. People who reliably wake up at the same hour in the middle of the night are hitting the point at which their overnight glycogen runs out and stress hormones take over. Peat's intervention was preemptive. "I advised people to set an alarm to wake up shortly before their usual waking time and eat something with carbohydrate—milk, juice, or a cracker. Within a week, they were sleeping through the night." The alarm is moved earlier and earlier as the liver recovers its storage capacity, and eventually the wake-up disappears.

Ice cream at bedtime, milk with honey, orange juice with salt, or a small bowl of gelatinous broth all serve the same function.

## Signs the Diet Is Working

The measurements that matter are body temperature, resting pulse, sleep quality, and body composition. Scale weight is, at best, a lagging indicator. Morning temperature rising toward 98.6°F and drifting slightly above during the afternoon is the primary sign. Pulse in the range of 75 to 85 beats per minute at rest, with a modest post-breakfast decrease, is the second. "The optimum temperature for the brain and immune system seems to be around 101 degrees, maybe even a little higher," Peat told Andrew Murray in the temperature and pulse episode, referencing the upper end of the range that people with very strong metabolisms could sustain.

Warm hands and feet are a direct downstream signal. Hypothyroid individuals almost universally report cold extremities; a week or two into the diet, that symptom typically reverses. Sleep deepens, libido returns, and waist, thigh, and hip circumferences begin to decrease before scale weight moves. "We would have people measure their waists, thighs, and hips, and each week they would get smaller even as they gained weight," Peat said in the 2013 weight gain episode, describing the pattern where muscle mass was increasing faster than fat was decreasing.

Energy rising through the day, rather than crashing in the mid-afternoon, is the subjective confirmation. A correctly calibrated anti-stress diet produces more energy, not less, and the energy is sustained rather than reactive.

A simple cross-check Peat offered was urine evaporation. "In an average level of activity and humidity, a person will generally evaporate a liter per thousand calories burned. If you only have a missing liter of fluid in the day, you know you're only burning a thousand calories." The difference between fluid consumed and urine produced is a crude but useful proxy for metabolic rate.

If temperature drops after several weeks on the diet, the diagnosis is almost always that the person is eating too little. More food, not less, is the correction.

## Adaptations by Situation

**If you want to lose weight.** The weight loss variant of the anti-stress diet is covered in full at [[peatmaxxing-cut]]. The short version is that the quantities of protein, calcium, and sugar stay the same, the milk shifts to 1% or skim, the daily carrot is enforced, coconut oil is added to the low end of its range, and measurement shifts to waist circumference and morning temperature rather than scale weight.

**If you are hypothyroid.** The baseline diet is the first intervention. Give it three weeks minimum before adding thyroid supplementation. Protein drops toward the 80-gram floor, carbohydrate rises, meal frequency increases to every two or three hours, and the bedtime snack becomes mandatory. If after three weeks morning temperature has not moved above 97.5°F and resting pulse is still below 70, thyroid hormone is the next tool. Half a grain of Armour or a starting dose of 5 micrograms of T3 twice daily, taken with a meal, is the traditional entry point.

**If you are athletic.** Protein rises to a gram per pound of bodyweight for intense training. Carbohydrate can run to a pound a day or more for high metabolic rates. Salt intake rises with sweat losses, typically well above the thirteen-gram floor. Short concentric work beats endurance. Recovery depends on carbohydrate, not protein.

**If you are transitioning from keto.** The transition is slow. Two weeks minimum. Start by adding ripe fruit and a small amount of orange juice with each meal, then milk, then more starch and sugar. A sudden high-carbohydrate intake after months on keto can produce transient water weight gain and bloating as glycogen restocks. That is expected and resolves.

**If you are PUFA-loaded.** This is the patient, long-horizon case. Stored polyunsaturated fat decreases on a roughly four-year timeline. "When the polyunsaturated fats in the diet are reduced, the amount of them stored in the tissues decreases for about four years, making it progressively easier to keep the metabolic rate up, and stress hormones down," Peat wrote in a weight-loss email. [[Niacinamide]] at 100 milligrams, aspirin, and vitamin E function as supporting tools by lowering circulating free fatty acids while the tissue stores slowly turn over. The diet itself is the primary mechanism.

**If you are low-energy or brain-fogged.** A meal every two or three hours is the rescue protocol. Never skip the bedtime snack. Track morning temperature daily. Track resting pulse. Expect an early phase where the body is shedding stored water and replacing atrophied muscle; scale weight may rise in that window.

## Common Failure Modes

Under-salting is probably the most frequent failure. People come to the diet from a culture that treats salt as a poison and find it difficult to salt their food enough to satisfy appetite. The result is chronic low-level adrenaline elevation, poor sleep, and the sense that the diet "isn't working." The correction is more salt.

Cutting the sugar is the second. People come to the diet from a culture that treats sugar as the worst food in the world and hesitate to drink a quart of orange juice or add sucrose to coffee. Without the sugar, the free fatty acid suppression mechanism fails and the diet collapses back into a standard high-protein regimen with high cortisol.

Fasting for any reason, including for medical imaging or blood draws, works against the diet's purpose. Peat's emergency instructions for a morning blood draw were to drink orange juice and milk as soon as the needle came out.

Protein alone, without the carbohydrate preload, is the failure mode that turns ordinary meals into stress meals. The rule is simple: never a protein meal without sugar.

Olive oil as a "healthy fat" is a category error. Olive oil is high in oleic acid, which Peat considered acceptable, but the commercial product is frequently cut with seed oils, and even pure olive oil carries a meaningful PUFA percentage. Butter, coconut oil, and the fat already present in milk and cheese are the preferred saturated fats.

Fish oil for "recovery" or "inflammation" is the opposite of what the diet prescribes. Any fish oil at all, in any dose, is a step in the wrong direction.

Relying on supplements instead of the diet is the last and most subtle failure. The calcium comes from milk, not from a calcium citrate capsule. The vitamin C comes from orange juice, not from ascorbic acid powder. The potassium comes from fruit, not from potassium chloride. The diet is the intervention. Supplements are supporting tools for the cases in which food alone is not enough.
