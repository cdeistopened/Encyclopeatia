# Child Feeding

This is the eating protocol for the years between delivery and puberty. The maternal plate that determines the substrate the baby is born into lives at [[pregnancy-nutrition]]; the adult template the child diet converges toward lives at [[anti-stress-diet]] and [[core-diet-protocol]]. This page handles the in-between: feeding a newborn whose mother cannot breastfeed, weaning without setting up a lifetime of allergies, the toddler plate, and the school-age habits.

The framework inverts mainstream pediatric guidance. Breastfeeding is the unambiguous floor. After that, the priority foods are whole milk, ripe fruit, eggs, gelatin, small amounts of liver, and butter on everything. The foods to avoid are the ones the standard pediatric handout treats as harmless or essential: soy formula, iron-fortified rice cereal, fish-oil capsules "for brain development," industrial vegetable oils, low-fat dairy, fluoride toothpaste in toddlers, the vegetable-mush that crowds out animal foods. Salt is generous; sugar belongs with food. The two-quarts-of-milk plus a-quart-of-orange-juice baseline scales down with body size but never disappears.

Pediatric content is high-stakes. The acute syndromes — failure to thrive, anaphylaxis, dehydration, neurodevelopmental delay — live with a pediatrician, not with this page.

## When to Use This Protocol

Run as the **default daily template** birth through ~12 when: choosing how to feed a non-exclusively-breastfed infant; weaning; a toddler transitioning to a wider plate; a school-age child showing suboptimal-nutrition signals (recurring colds, constipation, low energy, growth concerns, mood instability, focus problems). Apply alongside ongoing well-child visits, not in place of them.

**Route to a pediatrician or emergency care immediately, not to this page, if:** failure to thrive; suspected anaphylaxis; dehydration from vomiting or diarrhea; suspected severe allergy or autoimmune presentation; neurological developmental delay or regression; chronic illness not improving with nutritional adjustment.

## Infancy: 0–6 Months

The biological default is breastfeeding from a mother on the [[pregnancy-nutrition|Brewer-Peat postpartum plate]] — three quarts of milk daily, a quart of orange juice, eggs, salt to taste, gelatinous broth, liver weekly, no industrial vegetable oils, no fish-oil capsules, thyroid in range. From a [[2014-09-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-field-biology|2014 field-biology episode]]: "Breastfeeding naturally, but with the mother well-fed, happy, and unstressed. When the mother is under stress or not properly nourished, the composition of the milk shows it. Good milk contains a lot of blood sugar converted into the lactose of the milk, hormones — some thyroid and progesterone — and a lot of immune factors. Even if a baby has an endocrine problem, as long as it's getting good milk, something like hypothyroidism often doesn't show up just because the milk is providing thyroid." The milk is downstream of the mother; "low supply" is more often a calorie-and-thyroid problem than a lactation-consultant problem.

The protective package extends past calories. From a [[2014-06-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vaccination-i|2014 vaccination episode]]: "Besides being good nutrition, breast milk is extremely low in iron. Iron accumulates in the environment and leads to oxidative injury. Milk provides nutrition with a relative absence of harmful iron. It also has antibodies and many other immune factors." The low-iron design (see [[milk]]) is doing the same protective work in the infant; the baby was born with stored iron from gestation and does not need supplementation for the first six months.

### When breastfeeding is not happening

If the mother cannot breastfeed, Peat's position across decades of correspondence is unambiguous: **whole cow's milk is the substitute, not soy**. Goat's milk is acceptable as a second option for confirmed cow's-milk-protein allergy.

From a [[2022-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-june-2022-kmud|2022 KMUD episode on the baby-formula crisis]]: "If they really couldn't produce milk, they could simply make a formula based on cow milk. When it isn't dehydrated, the reaction on the immune system is less harmful. To take a non-human milk and introduce it right at the very beginning will cause some antigenic differences, but at least if it's made from fresh cow milk, that's a lot safer than dehydrated. Every time you dehydrate a living substance, you kill it and oxidize some of the proteins, amino acids, and especially the fatty acids." Asked what to add to fresh cow's milk to make it newborn-appropriate: "You can adjust the amount of fat and water if it makes it more palatable to the baby, but just as is, it'll work."

The diluted-and-sweetened recipe pre-dating commercial formula — fresh whole milk, water to thin, a small amount of sugar to approximate breast-milk carbohydrate, sometimes a tablespoon of cream — is the historical referent. Exact proportions belong with a pediatrician; the principle (cow's-milk base, not soy, not dehydrated, fresh fat preserved) belongs here.

### The soy-formula problem

The full case against soy lives at [[soy]]; the three child-specific problems summarize here. **Estrogenic load** — Peat in a [[2016-02-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-iodine-supplement-reactions-hormones-and-more|2016 KMUD episode]]: "I think it has seriously harmed lots of kids, partly through the estrogenic effect." Soy-fed infants receive an isoflavone dose equivalent to several birth control pills adjusted for body weight, during the developmental window when the endocrine system is most sensitive to estrogenic signaling. **Goitrogenic effect** — isoflavones inhibit thyroid peroxidase during the period of fastest brain growth in a human life. **Manganese excess plus PUFA load** — soy formula's manganese is multiples of breast milk's, with animal evidence linking the excess to neurological injury; the linoleic acid drops the infant immediately into the PUFA problem the adult diet spends years trying to undo. From the same baby-formula episode: "The estrogenic factors associated with the proteins used in making the milk substitute cause deformed genitals in the developing young and later promote tumors of various sorts."

If cow's-milk allergy is confirmed, the order is goat's milk, then a hydrolyzed-casein or amino-acid-based formula coordinated with a pediatrician — not soy.

### Other early-life concerns

- **Fish-oil supplementation in formula.** The industry argument — newborn brain "shows evidence of n-3 deficiency" — inverts the physiology. The placenta excludes PUFA; manufacturers read the exclusion as deficiency; the supplement is rancid by the time it reaches the grocery store. From the [[2022-08-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-lipofuscin|2022 lipofuscin episode]]: "Within a few days of reaching the grocery store, these fats are degrading, oxidizing, and becoming toxic. The evidence suggests that what you're doing is feeding the baby rancid fish oil products."
- **Vitamin K at birth.** Standard intramuscular K at delivery is consistent with Peat's framework — protective against newborn hemorrhagic disease, supports later calcium handling.
- **Vitamin D.** Sunlight is the primary lever; supplementation 400–600 IU/day reasonable when sun is unavailable. One of the few supplements Peat recommended liberally.
- **Iron-fortified cereals.** The 4–6-month introduction of iron-fortified rice cereal is the food Peat singled out in [[irons-dangers|"Iron's Dangers"]]: "Some researchers are concerned that the iron added to cereals is contributing to the incidence of leukemia and cancers of the lymphatic tissues in children… At birth, they have a large amount of stored iron, and this decreases as they 'grow into' it." The fortification treats a non-problem and creates an oxidative one.

## Weaning: 6–12 Months

The transition from breast or formula to a wider plate is the most consequential six months in pediatric nutrition. The standard sequence — iron-fortified rice cereal, then pureed vegetables, then small amounts of fruit and meat — is, in Peat's reading, exactly backwards. The Peat sequence inverts it: animal foods first, ripe fruit alongside, grains late and minimal.

### What to introduce first

- **Ripe banana, mashed.** Calorie-dense, easy to digest, the potassium-and-magnesium package supports the same metabolic levers as the adult diet. Half a banana mashed with milk is a reasonable first solid.
- **Small amounts of orange juice.** From 5–6 months, a teaspoon diluted in water; build to an ounce over weeks. Flavonoids and potassium without a new protein.
- **Egg yolk, soft-cooked.** Around 6 months, the runny yolk of a 4-minute egg (no white). Vitamin A, cholesterol, choline, fat-soluble vitamin package. Whites wait until 9–12 months.
- **Bone broth and gelatin** from around 6 months, lightly salted. Two to three tablespoons added to mashed foods, building to a small cup. The glycine balances the methionine load of muscle meat.
- **Liver at 8–9 months.** A teaspoon of finely grated beef or chicken liver, cooked through, once or twice a week. Densest source of vitamin A, copper, B12, and iron in the right form.

### What to delay or avoid

- **Iron-fortified rice cereal as a first food** — the food Peat warned against in [[irons-dangers|*Iron's Dangers*]]. Refined carbohydrate plus oxidative iron load, displacing better calories.
- **Early vegetables** (pureed peas, broccoli, sweet potato, "baby greens"). The fiber and goitrogen load are not what a 6-month gut is built for. Well-cooked, strained greens can return around 12–18 months as a side, not a staple.
- **Commercial baby food in seed-oil carriers.** Read the label or skip the category.
- **Sugar-water and juice in a bottle propped in the crib.** Dental and behavioral problem. Sugar belongs with food.
- **Honey before 12 months.** Standard infant-botulism guidance applies.

## Toddler: 1–3 Years

The toddler plate is the pediatric anti-stress diet at scale. Whole milk is the central food (2–4 cups daily), cheese and eggs follow, ripe fruit at every meal, small amounts of meat and fish (with shellfish and liver weekly), bone broth or gelatin daily if possible, and butter or other saturated fat on every cooked food.

### The daily template

- **Milk: 2–4 cups whole**, pasteurized; grass-fed ideal, supermarket whole milk fine. The toddler scales down from the adult two-quart prescription with body weight, not down to nothing. The American tradition of weaning a toddler from milk onto juice boxes and Cheerios is exactly backward.
- **Orange juice: 4–8 oz daily**, fresh-squeezed or not-from-concentrate. The flavonoids and potassium damp the cortisol elevation toddlers run high on.
- **Eggs: 1–2 daily**, soft-cooked yolks, salted (see [[eggs]]).
- **Cheese: ~2 oz at meals** — calcium and protein with low lactose load. Aged cheese delivers K2.
- **Meat: 2–3 oz at one or two meals** — ground beef, lamb, chicken thigh — with bone broth or gelatin alongside to balance amino acids. Avoid lean chicken breast as a default.
- **Ripe fruit** — banana, ripe pear, melon, peach, pineapple, mango, blueberries. Underripe fruit ferments in the gut; ripe fruit does not.
- **Butter or coconut oil on cooked foods.** Eggs in butter, rice with butter, broth with butter melted in. The saturated fat is the antidote to PUFA.
- **Salt** — toddler diets are systematically under-salted under the "no salt under two" rule, which has no good evidence behind it. Toddlers sweat, lose sodium, and have growing kidneys; under-salting reproduces the hypovolemic, high-aldosterone state [[pregnancy-nutrition]] addresses in adults.
- **Sugar with food, not alone.** Honey on yogurt, dark chocolate after dinner, ice cream as dessert. The hazard is sugar-in-a-PUFA-carrier (industrial snacks) and sweetened drinks displacing milk, not the sugar molecule itself.

### What to avoid

Covered in the comprehensive list below — most important at toddler age: soy in any form, seed oils, sweetened drinks displacing milk, "low-fat" dairy, and fluoride toothpaste before reliable spitting (see [[fluoride]]).

## Child: 4–12 Years

The school-age plate is the [[anti-stress-diet]] at child scale. The same foods, in proportions adjusted for body size. Active children — and children should be active — burn through calories and minerals at a rate that adults underestimate; the temptation is to "limit treats" and add fiber, when the reverse is closer to right.

### Daily template, school-age

- **Breakfast.** Salted eggs cooked in butter, milk, fresh-squeezed orange juice, ripe fruit. Avoid the cereal-and-skim-milk default — refined starch with added iron, fat-stripped milk.
- **Lunch (school).** A packed lunch beats the cafeteria: cheese sandwich on real bread with butter, a hard-boiled egg, a thermos of milk, fruit, a small treat. The cafeteria default of seed-oil-fried entrees, "whole grain" plates, and "low-fat chocolate milk" (carrageenan, artificial sweetener) is the wrong plate.
- **Afternoon snack** — whole milk, fruit, cheese with crackers, ice cream once or twice a week. Active children run out of morning calories by mid-afternoon; the snack is non-optional.
- **Dinner.** Small portion of meat or fish, white rice or potatoes with butter, well-cooked vegetables (not raw), milk, fruit for dessert.
- **Bedtime snack.** Warm milk with honey, ice cream, or cheese with fruit. Prevents the overnight cortisol surge that drives morning grogginess.

### Specific foods and rationale

- **White rice and potatoes over whole grains.** Whole grains carry phytic acid, goitrogens, and fermentable fiber that produces endotoxin.
- **Butter and coconut oil for cooking, never seed oils.** The school cafeteria's universal seed-oil frying is one of the largest changes in the American childhood diet of the last fifty years.
- **Fish in small amounts.** Shellfish weekly (zinc, copper, B12, selenium with manageable PUFA). Avoid large-predator fish (mercury) and daily salmon (PUFA and farmed-fish contaminants).
- **Raw carrot.** Daily [[carrot-salad]] from age 3–4 onward — pulls endotoxin and excess estrogen out of the gut.
- **Liver weekly**, ½–1 oz in a form the child will eat (chopped in pasta sauce, pâté, small slice).
- **Coffee or cocoa with milk.** Fine in older children; Peat treated coffee as broadly protective.

## Common Pediatric Issues

- **Constipation.** Standard fiber-and-whole-grains advice runs in the wrong direction. The Peat correction: milk to bowel tolerance (mildly laxative when fat and lactose are intact), daily ripe banana or papaya, well-cooked carrot, [[magnesium]] glycinate 100–200 mg, occasional stewed prunes.
- **Recurring colds.** Usually under-fed in fat-soluble vitamins. Increase milk, butter, eggs, weekly liver; verify vitamin D. [[Aspirin]] is sometimes appropriate for fever in older children, coordinated with the pediatrician, never in chickenpox or other Reye's-risk illnesses.
- **Growth concerns.** "Failure to thrive" usually traces to calorie inadequacy. More food, denser food, milk specifically — not a high-protein shake, which can suppress thyroid further. Coordinate with a pediatric specialist for any growth crossing percentile lines.
- **ADHD-like presentations.** See [[adhd]]. Apply [[anti-stress-diet]] at child scale, remove PUFA snacks and artificial colors, ensure adequate salt and calories, evaluate thyroid (waking + post-breakfast temperature and pulse). Subclinical hypothyroidism is common in children diagnosed with attention disorders.
- **Recurring ear infections.** Often respond to a milk-and-fat-heavy plate, seed-oil removal, and endotoxin reduction (raw carrot, milk replacing sweetened drinks).
- **Childhood allergies and eczema.** Reflect a high-estrogen, high-PUFA, high-endotoxin background. Upstream corrections (maternal diet, then child's diet from weaning) usually outperform aggressive elimination diets, which often worsen the substrate.

## The Vegan / Vegetarian Child Concern

Peat was unambiguous that vegan diets are inappropriate for children. The complete-protein, B12, vitamin A, K2, and iron-in-the-right-form arguments compound when applied to a child growing 50% of their adult height in a few years. A lacto-ovo diet is workable with attention — 3+ cups whole milk, eggs daily, cheese, iron monitoring. A fully vegan child diet has no defensible whole-food substitute for preformed A (liver, butter), B12 (animal foods only), K2, or calorie-and-protein density. If a family commits to it, the corrections needed belong with a pediatric dietitian.

## The Grain Question

Peat was skeptical of grains and especially of "whole grain" emphasis. Applied to a child's plate:

- **Acceptable:** well-cooked white rice, well-cooked white potato with butter, sourdough or well-fermented bread in moderation, pasta with butter and Parmesan.
- **Avoid as a default:** industrial whole-grain bread, breakfast cereals (iron-fortified), oatmeal-as-staple. Phytic acid binds minerals; goitrogens suppress thyroid; fortification is oxidative.
- **Gluten in a child with a family history of celiac or autoimmune disease:** avoid until evaluated.

A child raised on milk, eggs, fruit, meat, and small amounts of well-cooked starch does not need a grain-centered diet.

---

## What to Avoid

- **Soy in any form** — formula, milk, yogurt, "cheese."
- **Seed oils** (sunflower, safflower, canola, soybean, corn, cottonseed, "vegetable oil") and the foods cooked in them — most fried foods, crackers, baked goods, restaurant fare.
- **Sugar-sweetened beverages with HFCS plus PUFA carriers** (most "juice drinks," "fruit punches").
- **Commercial baby food in PUFA carriers.** Read labels.
- **Iron-fortified infant cereal as a first food.** Liver and ripe fruit cover the niche.
- **Fish-oil capsules** "for brain development." Rancid; supplies oxidized PUFA.
- **Fluoride toothpaste in toddlers** who can't spit reliably. See [[fluoride]].
- **Vegetable mush displacing animal foods** in toddlers.
- **Nut-butter-heavy diets.** PUFA load, mycotoxin risk, displaces dairy.
- **Low-fat or skim dairy at any age.** The fat is the point.
- **Pediatric "reduce saturated fat" advice.** Cardiology guidance was never validated against pediatric outcomes; Peat regarded it as actively harmful.

## Tracking Signals

Watched across weeks and months, not days: steady growth along percentile lines; good color in face and lips; stable mood; active energy; chronic illness rare (colds resolve in 5–7 days, ear infections uncommon, eczema and asthma absent or mild); falls asleep within 20 minutes, sleeps through by age 2–3; teeth appear on schedule, well-mineralized, low cavity rate; morning temperature (when accessible in school-age children) around 98.0°F by mid-morning, resting pulse 80–100 bpm by age.

## Failure Modes

- **Vegan or rigidly vegetarian** child diets with inadequate dairy and no eggs. Most common ideologically driven failure pattern.
- **PUFA-laden snacks displacing real food** — goldfish crackers, granola bars, seed-oil-fried nuggets. High-PUFA, low-saturated-fat, low-fat-soluble-vitamin diet regardless of how much "fruit and vegetable" sits alongside.
- **Sweetened drinks replacing milk.** Calcium, protein, and fat-soluble vitamin loss compounds.
- **"Switch to skim milk after age two."** Default in many pediatric practices; strips the cream that delivers A, D, E, K2 to the developing brain and bones.
- **Under-salting active children** + sweating + growing = chronically high aldosterone, the same hypovolemia pattern adult Peat correction addresses.
- **Excessive fluoride exposure** in young children before they can spit reliably. See [[fluoride]].
- **Over-treatment of mild illnesses** — antibiotics for every ear infection, fever-reducers for every low fever. Pulls immune development backward and accumulates iatrogenic load.

---

## When to Escalate

Route to a pediatrician immediately for: failure to thrive (percentile crossing downward over 3–6 months); severe food allergies (anaphylaxis, severe eczema, GI bleeding); developmental delay or regression; chronic illness not improving with dietary correction; suspected autoimmune disease; growth or pubertal abnormalities; severe constipation unresponsive within 2–3 weeks; recurrent severe infections; feeding disorders. This is the daily eating template; acute syndromes belong with a clinician.

---

## Counter-arguments

**AAP guidance on first foods.** The AAP recommends iron-fortified infant cereal at 4–6 months and emphasizes early introduction of allergenic foods. The first-food order Peat's framework gives — fruit, egg yolk, broth, liver — conflicts with the iron-cereal default; the case against the cereal ([[irons-dangers|"Iron's Dangers"]]) rests on iron's oxidative load in children, a concern outside the AAP's framework. The recent reversal on early peanut introduction is closer to Peat's position.

**Pediatric saturated-fat restriction.** Many pediatricians still recommend low-fat dairy after age 2. The Peat objection is mechanistic — saturated fat's role in thyroid support, calcium handling, and fat-soluble vitamin delivery is not addressed by cardiology-derived restrictions, and the pediatric evidence base is thinner than the adult one.

**Soy formula in cow's-milk-protein allergy.** Pediatric guidance still permits soy as one option in confirmed allergy. The Peat-aligned position is to use hydrolyzed casein or amino-acid-based formulas instead; goat's milk is acceptable but cross-reacts with cow's milk in roughly 50% of allergic children.

**Vegan-child accommodations.** Major dietetic associations indicate "well-planned" vegan diets can work at all life stages. The Peat-aligned position disagrees specifically for children — complete protein, B12, vitamin A, K2, and calorie density are difficult to meet at growth-phase intensity without animal foods, and supplements are not whole-food equivalents.

---

## Data gaps

- **Long-term Peat-corrected pediatric outcomes.** No prospective cohort exists. The framework rests on extrapolation from adult evidence, Peat's clinical correspondence, and pre-1970s pediatric literature.
- **Exact infant-formula recipe from fresh cow's milk.** Peat's instruction ("adjust fat and water; as is, it'll work") is broad. The historic dilution-and-sugar recipes are reference points; precise composition belongs with a pediatrician.
- **Goat's-milk-formula equivalence in confirmed cow's-milk allergy.** Cross-reactivity is partial and incompletely characterized.
- **Toddler salt floor and ceiling.** No controlled-trial data; Peat's "salt to appetite" runs counter to handout-level low-sodium recommendations.
- **Pediatric thyroid testing thresholds.** Subclinical hypothyroidism is underdiagnosed; best-practice testing for children is not standardized in the Peat framework.
