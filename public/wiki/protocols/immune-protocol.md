# Immune Protocol — Cold and Flu

This protocol is the actionable layer on top of [[cold-flu]]. Read that article first — it explains *why* each intervention here is in the framework. The short version: a cold or flu is not "an invasion" you fight off, it is a metabolic state in which the body's cellular defenses have fallen below the threshold where viruses your body has been managing all year can be contained. The protocol does two things in tandem: it calms the inflammatory cascade that creates the cellular permissiveness, and it raises the bioenergetic state that makes cells stable enough to resist viral assembly. Killing the virus is not the goal; the body does that on its own if its energy state holds.

## The foundation

You cannot bolt acute immune defense onto a stressed metabolic baseline. The first item on the protocol is whether the [[anti-stress-diet]] is in place and whether morning temperature and pulse indicate adequate [[thyroid]] function. If those baselines are missing, "what to take when you feel a cold coming on" is downstream theatre. The full energy-state recovery protocol is at [[energy-restoration]] and the morning baseline rhythm at [[morning-routine]]. The phases below assume those baselines are at least partly held.

## Phase 1 — Year-round prophylaxis

These are the things you do regardless of season, year-round, that determine how often you get sick at all.

**[[Vitamin-d]] at 50–60 ng/mL serum.** This is the single most important number in Peat's protocol for Americans. The dose required to reach it varies — typically 2,000 IU/day in summer, 5,000 IU/day in winter, and 10,000 IU/day for several months if a deficit is being repaired. The form is D3 (cholecalciferol), never D2, never the activated calcitriol. Take with a fatty meal. Co-supplement with [[vitamin-k]] (at least 500 μg/day, more if also taking aspirin) and ensure adequate dietary [[calcium]] (1,500–2,500 mg/day from milk and cheese); without enough calcium, vitamin D cannot suppress parathyroid hormone, and the anti-inflammatory effect fails.

**[[Vitamin-a]] from food.** Most adults need ~5,000 IU/day, which a few eggs and dairy plus weekly liver provide easily. Don't lean on carotene — many people convert it poorly, and high carotene loads block utilization. If supplementing retinyl palmitate, take ~100 IU [[vitamin-e]] alongside to prevent oxidation.

**[[Carrot-salad]] daily.** One medium-sized raw carrot, grated, with a small amount of olive or coconut oil, vinegar, and salt, eaten with the first meal of the day. Peat treated this as a baseline defense against the gut-to-lung axis described in [[cold-flu]]. The carrot fiber is indigestible and antibacterial; the vinegar acetic acid suppresses gut fungi; the oil binds endotoxin for elimination.

**[[Milk]] as the calcium-and-D delivery system.** Two quarts a day for an adult provides the calcium-vitamin-D matrix that keeps cellular excitability low. If milk causes digestive issues, work down through cheese, yogurt, and gelatin to identify the tolerated form.

**[[Oranges]] (juice).** A quart or more of fresh orange juice daily, with a pinch of salt, provides sugar substrate for liver glycogen, bioflavonoids (naringenin, hesperidin, nobiletin) that suppress aromatase and inflammation, and the food-form vitamin C that supports collagen and adrenal function without the pro-oxidant problems of synthetic ascorbic acid.

**[[Coconut-oil]] 1–3 tablespoons daily.** Short and medium-chain saturated fats inhibit histamine release, support thyroid conversion, and — per the [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]] book — suppress viral expression at the cellular level. Use as cooking fat or melt into hot beverages.

**No PUFA.** Vegetable oils, fish oil, and PUFA-heavy nuts and seeds out. This is the slowest-moving lever (tissue PUFA stores deplete over 2–4 years) but the most structural. PUFA-loaded individuals are more inflamed at every viral load.

**Sunlight daily.** Direct sun on skin when possible, indirect daylight through windows when not. Light supports thyroid, vitamin D synthesis, and the bioenergetic systems Peat described as decisive in [[generative-energy|Generative Energy]] — "in the winter and at night the respiratory energy producing system is damaged, and the protective hormones decline."

**[[Thyroid]] function at baseline.** Morning oral temperature ≥97.8°F and resting pulse ≥75 bpm, taken before getting out of bed, indicate adequate thyroid. If either runs below, see [[hypothyroid-recovery]] — chronic low thyroid is the most common upstream driver of recurrent colds and flus.

## Phase 2 — Winter intensification (Oct–Apr in temperate zones)

These are the additions to the year-round protocol when day length drops, Vitamin D synthesis collapses, and the seasonal stress state begins.

- **Increase D3 to 5,000–10,000 IU/day** with adequate vitamin K (1 mg K2 per 5,000 IU D3 is a reasonable ratio).
- **Heavier coconut oil use** — switch to coconut oil as the primary cooking medium; add 1 Tbsp to hot beverages on cold mornings.
- **Salt the orange juice** more aggressively; sodium prevents the adrenaline–cortisol compensation cycle that drives winter sickness.
- **Earlier bedtime.** The light deficit is part of the winter immune problem; compensating with longer sleep helps.
- **A pinch of [[pregnenolone]] daily** (~50 mg sublingual) is what Peat himself did after his 1983 winter-sickness episode. Optional but well-grounded.
- **Cascara sagrada or magnesium citrate at night** if bowel transit slows — even mild stagnation increases endotoxin reabsorption.
- **Avoid:** intermittent fasting, low-carb dieting, cold plunges, prolonged cardio in cold weather. All four drive cortisol, all four worsen the seasonal immune state.

## Phase 3 — First symptoms ("I feel something coming on")

This is the highest-leverage phase. A cold caught at first symptoms can often be interrupted before it develops into systemic illness. The intervention is fast, multi-pronged, and bowel-first.

**Eat a [[carrot-salad|raw carrot]] immediately.** This is the Peat-specific move that mainstream cold protocols miss entirely. From his 2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode: "I've seen people who learned that at the first sign of a cold — sore throat or runny nose — if they ate a carrot, they could break the pattern and not develop the cold." Grate it, add a tablespoon of coconut oil and a teaspoon of vinegar and a pinch of salt, eat the whole thing.

**[[Aspirin]] 325 mg with food, dissolved in warm water.** Take with [[vitamin-k|vitamin K2]] (1 mg per 325 mg aspirin) to maintain coagulation. Peat treated aspirin as a broad-spectrum antiviral — "active against influenza, herpes, rhinovirus, and hepatitis" — and his preferred form for daily use was USP crystals dissolved in hot water rather than tablets, which can irritate the stomach.

**Salt + sugar + orange juice — do not skip a meal.** Falling blood sugar is, in Peat's framework, "the most powerful pro-inflammatory thing possible." A glass of well-salted orange juice, a small meal, milk with honey if appetite is absent. The goal is to keep blood glucose stable.

**A pinch of [[pregnenolone]] (50–100 mg sublingual).** This is the Peat 1983 intervention. From [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]]: "I took a pinch of pregnenolone. In about an hour, all of my symptoms had stopped, and for over a week I was healthy and energetic." Useful especially if the prodrome includes the chronic-borderline-shock pattern (cold extremities, low mood, sluggish digestion).

**Cascara sagrada at bedtime.** From the 2020 October KMUD episode: "Clean your intestines out." Cascara at first symptoms removes the endotoxin load that would otherwise amplify into systemic inflammation. Magnesium citrate works similarly if cascara isn't on hand.

**Extra Vitamin D — a single 10,000 IU dose.** Acute Vitamin D loading can shift the inflammatory cascade in a few hours. Take with food and your normal vitamin K.

**Bed early, light on waking.** Don't sleep in a sealed dark room — leave the curtains slightly open. The morning light exposure resets the circadian and cortisol cycles that the prodromal stress state is already deranging.

**What to avoid at first symptoms:** cold drinks, ice baths, skipped meals, intense exercise, [[acetaminophen]] (Tylenol), high-dose synthetic vitamin C tablets, fish oil, "letting it run its course" by under-treating.

## Phase 4 — Active illness day

If the cold or flu progresses past first symptoms — chest involvement, fever, sustained malaise — the protocol shifts to keeping inflammation in check while supporting the recovery state.

**Aspirin schedule.** 300–500 mg every 4–6 hours during waking hours, with 1 mg K2 per 325 mg aspirin. The bedtime dose has particular value — "sometimes just taking 300–500 mg before bedtime has time to get absorbed before you fall asleep" — to interrupt the overnight inflammatory cycle. Total daily dose can run to 2 grams during active illness without difficulty when K2 is co-supplemented.

**[[Niacinamide]] 100 mg every 4 hours.** Mast cell stabilizer, anti-histamine, stress-hormone modulator. Often paired with aspirin to lower temperature without forcing.

**[[Vitamin-d]] 5,000–10,000 IU/day during the illness.** This is acute-phase dosing, not maintenance.

**Salt + sugar + orange juice + milk + gelatin.** Small frequent meals. The classic Peat sick-day kit, drawn from his March 2020 episode: "Anti-inflammatory things: chicken soup (salt and gelatin), orange juice, milk, ice cream, aspirin." Gelatinous chicken soup — made from chicken backs, wings, and feet — is therapeutic. Salted chicken consommé at bedtime lowers adrenaline.

**[[Progesterone]] (women).** 1–4 drops of [[progest-e|Progest-E]] on the gums, two or three times during the day. Anti-inflammatory, anti-estrogenic, GABAergic, anti-fibrotic in the lungs. The protocol Peat would recommend for any inflammatory illness in a cycling or post-menopausal woman.

**Pregnenolone continued.** Continue the 50–100 mg/day dose from Phase 3.

**For nasal mucus:** hot milk with salt, coconut oil melted in the mouth, and the [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]]-rebreathing trick that Peat alluded to via the 1956 Goodman pharmacology textbook — slow nasal breathing in a closed-mouth posture, especially during sleep, raises CO2 enough to thin secretions.

**No NSAIDs other than aspirin.** No [[acetaminophen]] (Tylenol), no ibuprofen, no naproxen at acute-illness doses. Aspirin alone among the common NSAIDs has the mitochondrial, anti-estrogenic, and antiviral properties.

**Avoid:** fish oil (immunosuppressive — explicitly so), ice water, fasting, "broth diets" that omit the sugar substrate, prolonged darkness, isolation in a stuffy room.

## Phase 5 — Fever management

Fever is adaptive. It activates antibody production, accelerates phagocytosis, and signals that the metabolic machinery is mobilizing. The goal of the protocol is not to suppress the fever; it is to address the inflammation that drives the need for the fever.

- **Don't suppress moderate fever** (under ~103°F) — let the metabolic activation work.
- **Aspirin lowers the *need* for fever**, not just heat production. From Peat's March 31, 2020 episode: "It actually increases oxidative metabolism and, at least temporarily, will increase thyroid function. So when it reduces a fever, I think it's most often because it's remedying the thing that is causing the fever."
- **Hydrate with salted orange juice and milk — do not "starve a fever."** Starvation activates cortisol; cortisol shrinks the thymus.
- **Sponge bath only at >103°F sustained,** and only with tepid (not cold) water, focusing on neck and feet.
- **If breath shortening, lung edema, or altitude-sickness-like symptoms appear**, treat as serious — see escalation criteria below. Severe respiratory inflammation can compromise CO2 retention and tissue oxygen delivery.

## Phase 6 — Recovery (days 4–10 post-acute)

The recovery phase is where the wrong move can convert a one-week cold into a three-week post-viral state. The body is rebuilding its energy state from a substrate-depleted starting point.

- **Calories slightly elevated above baseline** for 7–10 days. The body is doing tissue repair; a normal-calorie day reads as a deficit.
- **Re-introduce exercise only after morning temperature and pulse return to baseline** (97.8°F+, 75 bpm+). Trying to exercise through a depressed temperature delays recovery.
- **Bedtime snack non-negotiable.** Recovery sleep stays calorie-dependent for at least two weeks. Milk + honey + a small piece of fruit before bed.
- **Continue carrot salad, vitamin D, aspirin (lower dose).** The post-acute window is when chronic post-viral patterns establish if the protocol drops too fast.
- **If symptoms recur within 2–3 weeks**, the underlying state is chronic — see [[cold-flu]] section on "Why some people get sick every winter" and read [[hypothyroidism]] / [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

## Phase 7 — When to escalate

The Peat framework is for home and outpatient management of typical colds and flus. It is not a substitute for emergency care. Escalate to medical evaluation if any of the following appear:

- Fever above 103°F sustained for more than 48 hours
- Oxygen saturation below 92% at rest, especially if not improving with rest and warm liquids
- Productive cough with green or yellow sputum plus chest pain — bacterial pneumonia risk
- Severe shortness of breath, especially with any altitude-sickness-like pattern (lung edema, cyanosis)
- Confusion, severe dehydration, inability to keep fluids down for more than 12 hours
- Symptoms of secondary infection (severe earache, sinus pain with fever, very tender swollen glands)
- Any cold/flu lasting more than 10–14 days without sign of recovery
- Immunocompromised patients, pregnancy, very young children, very elderly — lower the threshold for medical contact

In an inpatient setting, the Peat-aligned considerations — high-dose aspirin with vitamin K, intravenous glucose, CO2-supplemented respiratory support, [[losartan]] or [[cinanserin]] for severe inflammation, avoidance of pure-oxygen ventilation when CO2 retention is at issue — should be discussed with the treating physician where applicable. They are not self-administered.

## Counter-arguments

The standard cold-and-flu protocol most people grew up with includes several practices Peat opposed. The protocol above explicitly inverts them.

- **High-dose synthetic vitamin C is replaced by food-form C from orange juice.** See [[cold-flu]] for the full argument; the short version is that isolated ascorbic acid tablets can act as pro-oxidants and produce the symptoms they purport to treat.
- **Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is replaced by aspirin.** The Reye-syndrome reversal — see [[aspirin]] and [[reye-syndrome]] — flipped public perception of which drug was safer. Peat's reading of the Australian data was that the substitution went the wrong direction.
- **"Starve a fever" is replaced by "feed everything."** Falling blood sugar is the strongest pro-inflammatory event Peat named.
- **Cold exposure (cold plunges, ice baths) is opposed.** Cold drives adrenaline and cortisol; both suppress the thymus and worsen recurrent infection susceptibility.
- **Intermittent fasting is opposed during illness.** Same root critique. Fasting raises cortisol; cortisol shrinks the thymus.
- **Bed rest in a darkened room is partially opposed.** Sleep is important; sustained darkness deepens cortisol. Light exposure on waking, even brief, matters.
- **Annual flu vaccination** is, in Peat's framework, of questionable benefit and potential cost. This is the most contested element; the protocol above does not address vaccination decisions directly. See [[cold-flu]]'s Counter-arguments section for Peat's stated view, and consult your own physician and the contemporary public-health literature for a balanced picture.

## Data gaps

This protocol is an opinionated synthesis of Peat's positions; it leaves several common situations explicitly under-specified.

- **Pediatric dosing.** Aspirin and vitamin D doses in children require pediatric medical supervision; the adult-oriented dosing here does not extrapolate linearly.
- **Pregnancy.** Aspirin in pregnancy has specific medical guidelines that supersede this protocol; pregnant readers should consult them.
- **Immunocompromise.** Transplant recipients, chemotherapy patients, and biologic-drug-treated autoimmune patients have metabolic constraints not addressed here.
- **Long COVID / post-viral chronic fatigue.** The framework is the right starting place; the specifics require separate work.
- **Zinc lozenges at first symptoms.** Mainstream cold-flu protocols include them with reasonable evidence; Peat acknowledged zinc as immune-supportive but never wrote a focused piece on lozenge timing or dose. Not addressed here.
- **Specific viral pathogens** beyond the common cold and influenza — mononucleosis, severe shingles, hepatitis — need specialist input. The framework holds in principle but does not substitute for diagnosis.
- **Bacterial superinfection** requires antibiotics; the escalation criteria above name the warning signs.
