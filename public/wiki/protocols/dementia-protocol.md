# Dementia Protocol

The mechanism is at [[dementia]]; the argument that the dementing brain is a respiratory failure decades in the making, and that amyloid plaques are debris rather than disease, is not re-walked here. What follows is the action layer: what to do, in what order, at what stage, for the person across the kitchen table.

Stage matters more than diagnosis. Within each stage the order of operations is the same: stop the ongoing injury first (PUFA, estrogen, iron, prolonged fasting), restore the substrate (T3, glucose, salt, calcium), then add the agents that bypass damaged machinery (methylene blue, red light, aspirin, progesterone).

Peat reported reversals of "senile dementia" within days in specific cases — the 80-year-old man whose mind cleared on thyroid plus pregnenolone plus progesterone, the 52-year-old whose post-epileptic dementia resolved on progesterone alone. He did not promise these reversals in every case, and neither does the framework. A brain that has lost forty percent of its cortical volume will not regrow it. What can return is the function the remaining tissue is failing to perform because its mitochondria are running below the threshold thyroid and oxygen and glucose would let them run at.

Two non-clinical points govern everything that follows. The patient and the family are both subjects of the protocol — a caregiver timing five supplements through the night will fail before the medicine does. And consent and dignity are load-bearing: where the patient has lost the ability to consent, the protocol simplifies toward warmth, food, sugar, and light, and adds nothing the family cannot deliver calmly.

## Prevention Phase: Age 50 and Up

The prevention protocol is, almost entirely, the protocol for every other expression of the same respiratory defect. The midlife window (50–65) is when the load-bearing variables are still movable: tissue PUFA, iron stores, subclinical hypothyroidism, estrogen exposure. Interventions are inexpensive, well-tolerated, and consistent with the broader [[anti-stress-diet]].

**Anti-PUFA, lifetime.** The highest-leverage intervention is removing polyunsaturated oils from the kitchen and supplement cabinet. Out: corn, soy, safflower, sunflower, canola, grapeseed, "vegetable oil," and the entire fish-oil category. In: butter, ghee, coconut oil, refined olive oil in small amounts, the saturated fat in dairy and well-raised animal meat. The dementia-specific point — full mechanism at [[pufa]] — is that brain PUFA is the substrate from which iron-catalyzed [[lipid-peroxidation]] generates [[acrolein|acrolein]] and 4-hydroxynonenal, the aldehydes that poison cytochrome oxidase. Tissue PUFA turns over on a roughly four-year timeline; a midlife switch produces a measurably less peroxidation-prone brain by retirement.

Marine omega-3s are the preferred substrate for acrolein. From the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]]: "Acrolein is a very tiny fragment that comes mainly from the Omega-3 fatty acids... The fact that it comes from the oils that are being proposed as cures and treatments for Alzheimer's disease — fish oil supplements and other Omega-3 fats — are the main sources of acrolein." Fish-oil capsules are off the protocol at every stage.

**Treat subclinical hypothyroidism, do not wait for the TSH.** Diagnostics at [[hypothyroidism]]: morning temperature under 97.8°F, resting pulse under 75, slow Achilles reflex, cold extremities, thinning lateral eyebrows. A "normal" TSH does not rule out the syndrome. Entry-level: dietary support of thyroid function — 100+ grams protein, 250–350 grams carbohydrate, 2 quarts milk, removal of cruciferous vegetables in volume, daily raw carrot. If morning temperature does not climb above 97.8°F after several weeks, low-dose Cynomel (T3) is next: 5 mcg twice daily with food, titrated up until morning temperature reaches 98.0–98.4°F and resting pulse 75–85. From the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode]]: "Thyroid increases the cytochrome oxidase enzyme, which is the one that is very sensitive to stress, estrogen, carbon monoxide, and basically all of the toxins."

**Donate blood every three to four months.** From [[iron-cumulative-danger|*Iron: Cumulative Danger* (1997)]]: "People who regularly donate blood, on the other hand, have often been found to be healthier than non-donors, and healthier than they were before they began donating." A 500-mL donation removes ~250 mg of stored iron; the brain, which cannot unload iron once it has accumulated, gets a slow downstream benefit from the systemic decline. Postmenopausal women benefit most, men nearly as much. Four donations per year through the prevention window.

**Reduce dietary iron intake.** Stop iron-fortified flour, bread, and breakfast cereal. Avoid cooking acidic foods in cast iron. Coffee or tea with meals chelates dietary iron. From the iron newsletter: "Drinking coffee with iron-rich foods can reduce iron's toxic effects... Avoid food supplements which contain iron." Vitamin E 100 IU mixed tocopherols daily offsets the peroxidation that already-stored iron drives.

**Evening incandescent light, daytime sun, screens dimmed after sunset.** The cytochrome-oxidase activation by red wavelengths is the same mechanism used at later stages; in prevention the goal is to replace blue-shifted indoor lighting with 250-watt incandescent heat lamps in rooms where the most time is spent. Full mechanism at [[red-light]]. From the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode]]: "Exposure to ordinary visible light — the red, long waves of light that will pass right into your brain — happens to lift the copper atom just enough to restore its position in the cytochrome enzyme."

**Progesterone for postmenopausal women.** [[Progesterone]] destroys the estrogen receptor at the protein level, opposes local aromatase, supports brain neurosteroid concentration, and lowers chronic excitotoxic drive. Prevention dose: 5–10 mg in vitamin E (Progest-E style) most evenings, with breaks. Women already on supplemental estrogen are in a different protocol; the prevention point is to stop adding the substance that, in Peat's reading of the Seattle data, drives the female 2-3× incidence of Alzheimer's.

## Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI): The Inflection Window

MCI is the window where the protocol shifts from prevention to active reversal. Word-finding pauses lengthen. Names slip. Cognitive testing shows measurable deficit that does not yet meet dementia criteria. Mainstream prognosis quotes ~10–15% annual conversion to clinical dementia. In Peat's framework this is the highest-leverage window because the cortical substrate is still largely intact and the respiratory defect is doing most of the damage. Add to the prevention stack:

**Low-dose methylene blue, sublingually, mornings.** The full pharmacology and contraindications are at [[methylene-blue]]. For MCI: 1–4 mg of USP pharmaceutical-grade methylene blue as a 1% aqueous solution from a compounding pharmacy, sublingually with food. Urine turns blue-green within 2–4 hours; if it does not, absorption failed. Peat himself referenced even smaller doses — 20 to 40 micrograms — as the optimal range "analogous to thyroid hormone" (from his [Politics and Science 2015 interview](../../app/public/transcripts/politics-and-science/polished/2015-03-04-politics-and-science-politics-science-evolution.md)). Entry point: 1 mg every morning, raised slowly if no effect appears in three weeks.

**The hard SSRI contraindication.** Methylene blue is a potent MAO-A inhibitor at any therapeutic dose. **Do not take it if the patient is on an SSRI, SNRI, tricyclic, MAOI, triptan, tramadol, meperidine, dextromethorphan, or St. John's wort.** The FDA issued the warning after surgical fatalities. Fluoxetine washout is ~5 weeks; most other serotonergic drugs, ~2 weeks. Many "depressed" elderly patients on SSRIs are hypothyroid patients in cognitive decline; the SSRI is not addressing the substrate, and the cleaner path is to coordinate a taper and start methylene blue plus T3 plus progesterone as the antidepressant strategy.

**Aspirin, 81–325 mg daily with vitamin K2.** From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease as a Clue to Immortality* (2006)]]: "Aspirin protects against iron toxicity, clot formation, and reduces lipid peroxidation while blocking prostaglandin formation. Aspirin and other antiinflammatory drugs, taken for arthritis, have been clearly associated with a reduced incidence of Alzheimer's disease." Mechanism is multiple — aromatase inhibition, prostaglandin blockade, mitochondrial uncoupling, iron chelation. Peat's own dosing varied: "I think a little aspirin, regularly if not daily, is good prevention, if you are sure to get enough vitamin K... sometimes I take about 500 mg at night, but other times just a little." For MCI: 81–325 mg daily after the largest meal, paired with 1–5 mg K2 (MK-4). Full mechanism at [[aspirin]].

**Progesterone, raised dose for women.** For postmenopausal women with MCI, the dose rises from the prevention range to 20–40 mg most days, in vitamin E. Peat's most-quoted recovery anecdote sits here. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "A few years later, a man in his 80s showed the classical signs of senile dementia, with childishness, confusion, self-centeredness, and unstable emotions. A few days after getting a mixture of thyroid, pregnenolone, and progesterone, his mind was again clear, and he was able to work on a research project he had set aside years before." For men, 5–10 mg most days opposes local aromatase without disrupting libido or mood.

**Frequent feeding with the bedtime carbohydrate non-negotiable.** The MCI liver no longer bridges the overnight fast; adrenaline and cortisol rise in the small hours, free fatty acids mobilize, PUFA reaches the brain, and the morning is worse than the previous evening. Same protocol as [[energy-restoration]]: orange juice with salt within five minutes of waking; breakfast within thirty minutes; meals every two to three hours; a bedtime snack of milk with honey, ice cream, or salted cheese; juice on the nightstand for 3 AM. From the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode]]: "as your liver gets less efficient, many people can only go an hour or even less without suffering the symptoms of starvation. Some people wake up every one or two hours during the night with nightmares or heart pounding, which happens when the liver runs out of its sugar supply."

**Red light to the scalp, evenings.** A 250-watt heat lamp at three to four feet for 20–30 minutes per evening, or a near-infrared LED panel (810 nm and 660 nm) directly on the scalp for 10–15 minutes daily. From the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode]]: "Just in the last year, a group in the US did memory studies and showed that just shining some red light on the head improves memory."

## Mid-Stage Dementia

By mid-stage — disoriented in unfamiliar settings, confabulating, unable to manage finances or medications — the protocol shifts from "reverse decline" to "restore the recoverable function." The cortex has lost meaningful tissue volume; what can be restored is the metabolic rate at which the remaining tissue operates. A well-fed, warm, T3-supported mid-stage patient is dramatically more present than the same patient on a standard diet and SSRI.

**T3 micro-dosed through the day, including overnight.** The single most important intervention at mid-stage and the one that requires the most family coordination. Peat's reference dose for an elderly demented patient was 3 mcg of Cynomel (T3) per hour. From his [emails](../../app/public/transcripts/raypeatemails/thyroid.md): "[Giving 3mcg Cynomel/hour to 84-year-old grandmother with dementia] If someone is in a precarious condition, even smaller amounts at a time might be better... T3, sugar, and aspirin are the most heart-protective things." Pulsed micro-doses produce a stable thyroid signal without the spikes a single morning dose creates. Practical schedule:

- 3 mcg Cynomel every 1–2 hours during waking hours
- 3 mcg with the bedtime snack
- 3 mcg from the nightstand if the patient wakes overnight
- Total typically 25–50 mcg/day
- Titrate to 98.0–98.6°F oral temperature, 75–90 resting pulse
- Backstop with 1/4 grain (15 mg) Armour in the morning if T3 alone causes afternoon dips

For families that cannot run hourly dosing, splitting 4–5 doses across the day produces 80% of the benefit. The least useful schedule is a single morning dose: T3's half-life is short enough that once-daily produces a spike-then-slump that in a fragile cortex looks like worsening dementia.

**Full-dose methylene blue.** 4–10 mg daily, split morning and early-afternoon sublingual. Urine turns frankly blue. SSRI contraindication remains absolute; coordinate taper with the prescribing physician before raising the dose.

**Red light to the scalp, twice daily.** Morning and evening sessions of 20–30 minutes with the 250-watt heat lamp, or 10–15 minutes of direct near-infrared LED morning and evening. The lamp can warm the chair the patient reads or watches television in. Warmth itself is therapeutic — the "cold brain syndrome" Peat described reverses with warming.

**Aspirin 325 mg daily.** After the largest meal, with 1–5 mg K2. Some patients benefit from a 500 mg bedtime dose for sleep (Peat's own pattern). Watch for melena and easy bruising.

**Progesterone clinical dose.** 30–50 mg evening for postmenopausal women. Pregnenolone 50–100 mg morning for either sex; pregnenolone is the parent steroid and supports neurosteroids when mitochondrial steroidogenesis has failed.

**Low-dose naltrexone, 1.5–4.5 mg at bedtime.** [[low-dose-naltrexone|LDN]] is the modern oral version of the naloxone Peat referenced. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "I have proposed that the endorphins can cause or sustain some of the symptoms of aging. Naloxone appears to be a useful treatment for senility."

**The daily raw carrot.** One medium raw carrot grated lengthwise, with salt, a teaspoon of coconut oil, and vinegar. Binds endotoxin and estrogen in the gut. Cheapest intervention on the protocol; most under-used.

**Sleep architecture.** The bedtime snack and nightstand-juice protocol matter more here than at MCI. If sundowning is severe, 10–20 mg progesterone (oral or topical to the gums) at bedtime is reliably calming. Aspirin at bedtime is another Peat-referenced sleep aid.

## Advanced Stage: Palliative Bioenergetics

At advanced stage — the patient who no longer recognizes family, has lost speech, is bed- or chair-bound — the framework does not promise reversal of cortical atrophy and the protocol should not be operated as if it does. What it can still do is keep the patient warm, fed, calm, and comfortable, and reduce the catecholamine surges that produce agitation and sundowning. Interventions simplify, they do not disappear.

**Warmth, continuously.** Room 72–76°F. Layered blankets. Hot water bottle on the abdomen. The cold cortex is the agitated cortex.

**Sugar in palatable forms, all day.** Orange juice through the morning. Milk with honey. Ice cream when chewing is hard. Refined sugar in coffee. This is not a moment for nutritional purity; it is a moment for stable blood sugar, which translates directly into a calmer patient.

**Salt to taste, generously.** Salted broth, salted cottage cheese, salted hard cheese. Restricted-sodium hospital diets at this stage are catastrophic for comfort.

**Gelatinous broth daily.** Bone broth or chicken-back stew. Glycine is calming, sleep-supporting, and reduces the tryptophan and methionine that drive serotonin and estrogen-amplified inflammation.

**T3 continued, modestly.** 3 mcg every 2–3 hours during waking, 15–25 mcg/day total. The goal is metabolic stability and the warmth that comes with it.

**Methylene blue, low dose, mornings only.** 1–2 mg sublingual. SSRI contraindication still holds. The case for raising the dose weakens at end-stage; the case for keeping a low dose continues.

**Progesterone, calming dose.** 20–40 mg evening for women; 5–10 mg for men. Anti-agitation effect is real and family-noticeable.

**No fish oil, ever.** Decline firmly if a well-meaning clinician offers it.

**Red light, comfort-focused.** Heat lamp at four feet during quiet seated time. Warmth and light are inseparable benefits.

**Family logistics.** Here the protocol stops being chemistry and becomes care. A caregiver awake at 3 AM dosing T3 is a caregiver whose own metabolism is failing. Sleep for the caregiver is part of the protocol. Respite. Predictable meals. Dose frequency should never exceed what the family can sustain without becoming patients themselves.

## Iron-Lowering Strategy

The iron mechanism is at [[dementia]]; the actions are concentrated here because mainstream medicine does not run them.

**Blood donation, every 3–4 months for any able adult.** A 500-mL donation removes ~250 mg of iron; four donations annually drains approximately one gram. Brain iron tracks systemic iron with a lag. Postmenopausal women and adult men benefit most. The Red Cross every-8-weeks limit is conservative; quarterly is sustainable for most non-anemic adults.

**Therapeutic phlebotomy.** For patients with elevated ferritin (>200 ng/mL men, >150 women) a physician can order phlebotomy on a hemochromatosis schedule — 500 mL every 1–2 weeks until ferritin reaches 50–80, then maintenance every 2–3 months.

**Stop iron-fortified flour, bread, breakfast cereal, and cast-iron cookware for acidic foods.** Read labels; the fortification is regulatory legacy. Stainless steel, enameled cast iron, or glass replace iron pans.

**Coffee or tea with meals.** Polyphenols in both chelate dietary iron. From the [[iron-cumulative-danger|iron newsletter]]: "Drinking coffee with iron-rich foods can reduce iron's toxic effects."

**IP6 (inositol hexaphosphate), 500–2000 mg between meals.** Water-soluble oral iron chelator, reasonable safety. Take on an empty stomach so it chelates stored rather than dietary iron. Three to six months at the higher dose lowers ferritin measurably.

**100 IU vitamin E daily (mixed tocopherols), weekly shellfish.** From the iron newsletter: "Take about 100 units of vitamin E daily; your vitamin E requirement increases with your iron consumption... Use shrimp and oysters, etc., to prevent the copper deficiency which leads to excess storage of iron." Oysters weekly cover the copper.

## Counter-arguments

**Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine).** The standard mainstream prescription for early-to-mid Alzheimer's. The mechanism — slowing acetylcholine breakdown — pushes a metabolically failing cortex harder rather than restoring its capacity. From [[the-problem-of-alzheimers-disease-as-a-clue-to-immortality|*The Problem of Alzheimer's Disease*]]: "The specific approaches of this orientation — to energize but quiet the brain — are diametrically opposed to some of the 'therapies' for Alzheimer's disease that have been promoted recently by the drug industries: Things to increase stimulation, especially to increase cholinergic excitation."

**Lecanemab and donanemab (anti-amyloid antibodies).** Plaque-clearing antibodies have shown modest slowing of cognitive decline in randomized trials, with substantial side effects (ARIA — brain edema, microhemorrhage), at ~$26,000/year and biweekly infusions. The framework predicts the trial result: modest slowing without modifying the underlying respiratory defect, side effects from clearing what is dropped cargo rather than disease.

**Ketogenic diet for cognition.** A substantial wellness-medicine literature recommends ketosis for early Alzheimer's on the reasoning that ketones bypass the impaired glucose oxidation of the dementing brain. The framework reads this backward: ketosis requires fat oxidation (less CO2 per oxygen than glucose), relies on lipolysis (which mobilizes stored PUFA into circulation where it reaches the brain), and suppresses thyroid function within weeks. Some MCI patients report short-term cognitive clarity on keto; in this framework that is the adrenaline of glycogen depletion, not recovery. The protocol here is the opposite — frequent glucose, stable liver, no fasting.

**Hormone replacement therapy in early menopause.** The "critical window" hypothesis in mainstream endocrinology suggests estrogen started early in menopause may be protective. The WHIMS data Peat cited applied to women starting after 65. The Peatian position uses progesterone instead at any age; women already on HRT should taper under medical supervision rather than stop abruptly.

**Methylene blue's evidence base is small.** The strongest critique of the protocol is that the methylene blue recommendation runs ahead of the human trial data. TauRx LMTX (2016) failed its primary endpoint in mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's; secondary analyses suggested benefit in patients not on standard medications. Recommending methylene blue for dementia in 2026 is recommending an experimental intervention with modest published evidence. The protocol treats it as worth running at low dose because the downside is low and the mechanism aligns with everything else on the stack; a cautious reader could reasonably decline.

**Mainstream neurology will not write the prescription.** A neurologist asked to prescribe Cynomel for a euthyroid-on-paper demented patient, or methylene blue for cognitive decline, will typically decline. The protocol assumes either a Peat-aware prescriber or a family willing to source through compounding pharmacies. 
## Data gaps

**Stage-specific dose-response.** Doses above are clinical-experience-derived and corpus-anchored, not trial-validated. The dose at which methylene blue benefits MCI is unknown. The dose at which T3 benefits mid-stage dementia is unknown. Peat's micro-dosing schedule has not been compared head-to-head with single-daily dosing.

**Combination effects.** The protocol stacks 7–10 interventions. Which are load-bearing and which are merely additive is unmeasured.

**Reversibility by stage.** Peat's reversal anecdotes concentrate in early-to-mid disease. Advanced cortical atrophy is presumed structural; the inflection point is not specified.

**Iron-drainage kinetics in the brain.** Blood donation drains systemic iron measurably; brain iron is presumed to follow with a lag, but the actual time course under sustained donation has not been imaged.

**Methylene blue interactions.** Co-administration with thyroid is widely practiced and observationally benign; whether the two are additive, redundant, or compensatory at standard doses is unstudied. Long-term human safety at 1–4 mg daily over decades is uncharacterized.

**Caregiver burden as confounder.** A protocol running 4–8 daily doses requires a sustaining caregiver. A controlled trial would have to isolate caregiver attention itself, a substantial therapeutic intervention.

**The Lewy-body and frontotemporal subtypes.** Built on Peat's writing about Alzheimer's specifically. Application to Lewy-body (REM behavior disorder, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism) and frontotemporal (behavioral disinhibition, language) dementias is plausible but not corpus-grounded.
