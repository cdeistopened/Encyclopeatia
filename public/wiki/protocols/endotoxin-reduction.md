# Endotoxin Reduction Protocol

This is the action layer on top of [[endotoxin]] and [[detoxification]]. Read those first — they explain *why* bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is the constant background metabolic opponent in Peat's framework, and *why* the liver's conjugation enzymes spend most of their capacity on internally generated load before they have any room left for external compounds. The short version: gram-negative bacteria in the lower intestine continuously release LPS, that LPS leaks across the intestinal barrier in proportion to inflammation and slow transit, and the liver's first job every minute of every day is to neutralize that flow before it reaches the systemic circulation. The estrogen-serotonin-cortisol cascade that Peat traced through nearly every degenerative condition begins here.

The protocol below is **preventive, not corrective**. It is what someone in reasonable metabolic health does every day to keep the load low, plus what someone in an acute endotoxemic state does to bring it back down. It is not a "detox cleanse" — Peat was hostile to that concept (see [[detoxification]]). The point is to reduce the daily input to the liver, not to mobilize stored toxins or run colonics. The single most common implementation error is treating "endotoxin reduction" as a one-week project; the correct frame is a daily practice that runs for life.

## The daily core

Three habits, every day, indefinitely. None of them require a supplement bottle.

**Raw carrot salad, once daily, after the first meal.** One medium carrot, scraped or julienned lengthwise (not crosswise — lengthwise shredding maximizes the surface area of indigestible fiber). Tossed with about a teaspoon of olive or coconut oil, a splash of vinegar, and a pinch of salt. Eaten about thirty to sixty minutes after the first solid meal of the day, when the bile has been triggered and the carrot fiber can bind the conjugated estrogen the liver is in the process of dumping. The carrot must be **raw** — cooking destroys the antiseptic fiber structure that gives it its therapeutic effect (see [[carrot-salad]] for the full mechanism). Peat ate one every day for over twenty years; it is the single most leveraged habit in his framework.

**A second antiseptic-fiber source most days.** Carrots get tiresome after years; rotation prevents abandonment. The acceptable alternates are well-cooked [[bamboo-shoots]] (canned, pre-boiled — fresh bamboo requires elaborate prep to destroy cyanogenic compounds) added to soups, curries, stir-fries, or stews, and well-cooked white-button mushrooms (cooked at least an hour to destroy agaritine; raw or lightly cooked mushrooms are not safe). These share the carrot's non-fermentable, mildly germicidal fiber but survive cooking. Aim for one of these on most days, two on heavy-fiber-need days.

**One soft, formed, complete bowel movement daily, ideally two.** Transit time is the lever that controls how long endotoxin sits in contact with the intestinal wall. The target is roughly twelve to twenty-four hours from meal to elimination, with stool that holds its shape but is not hard or fragmented. Constipation lengthens bacterial exposure and increases LPS absorption. Chronic loose stools indicate the wrong food choice or active dysbiosis and warrant investigation, not symptom-suppression. The interventions for slow transit — magnesium glycinate or chloride at 300–600 mg/day, [[cascara-sagrada]] at a low evening dose, and adequate fluid — sit in Phase 1 rather than the daily baseline because most people on the carrot-and-substrate baseline will normalize without them.

Around these three habits, the [[anti-stress-diet]] baseline is assumed: adequate protein with substantial gelatin (the glycine bottleneck for glutathione conjugation), adequate sugar from ripe fruit and milk (the UDP-glucuronate substrate the liver needs to conjugate estrogen), salt to appetite, daily sun and walking, no PUFA.

## Why the carrot works (the mechanism, compressed)

The full mechanism lives at [[endotoxin]] and [[carrot-salad]]; the compressed version here is enough to make daily compliance feel less arbitrary. Three effects operate at once.

First, the carrot's non-fermentable insoluble fiber **mechanically binds** conjugated estrogen, secondary bile acids, and bacterial metabolites in the bile-saturated upper small intestine and carries them out before bacterial beta-glucuronidase in the lower intestine can deconjugate them. This is the lever for interrupting the enterohepatic recirculation of estrogen that Peat documented dropping hormone levels "a day or two after" the carrot habit began.

Second, root vegetables produce their own antimicrobial compounds as a defense against soil bacteria and mold. Eaten raw, these compounds **survive intact** through the entire digestive tract and act as a mild germicide on the gram-negative bacteria producing the LPS. Peat described it as a "mild antibiotic from stomach to colon" that, unlike pharmaceutical antibiotics, suppresses the toxin-producing bacteria selectively without flattening the rest of the flora. The carrot does what people hope a probiotic supplement will do, except by reducing the bacterial population rather than adding to it.

Third, the **non-fermentability** of the fiber is itself the therapeutic property. Pectins, gums, psyllium, oat bran, fructo-oligosaccharides, and most "prebiotic" fibers ferment in the lower intestine and feed the same bacteria producing the endotoxin. The carrot fiber refuses to feed them. The vinegar in the dressing briefly lowers gut pH, the oil provides a mild anti-microbial fatty-acid layer, and the salt makes the whole preparation palatable enough to eat every day for years.

The combined effect, well-documented in Peat's own reports and in the fertility-clinic anecdote he repeated for thirty years, is a measurable hormonal shift within days: lower estrogen, lower cortisol, higher progesterone, higher T3.

## Bowel transit management

The carrot does not work in a constipated gut. Slow transit gives bacterial beta-glucuronidase more time to deconjugate the estrogen the carrot is supposed to be removing, and gives the bacterial cell-wall debris more contact time with the intestinal wall.

**Target.** One soft, formed, complete bowel movement daily, ideally two. The most reliable indicator is morning urgency that resolves within five minutes on the toilet and produces a stool that holds its shape on the way down. Straining, fragmented stool, pellets, mucus, or undigested food in stool are signals to intervene.

**First-line interventions for slow transit.**
- **Magnesium** at 300–600 mg/day as glycinate, chloride, or carbonate (avoid oxide, which is poorly absorbed and laxative-only). Magnesium normalizes peristalsis, supports the smooth-muscle ATP machinery the intestine depends on, and corrects the deficit most adults sit at chronically.
- **[[Cascara-sagrada]]** at one or two capsules in the evening as a gentle herbal laxative. Peat reversed the conventional warning about long-term cascara use, attributing the "melanosis coli" pigmentation to chronic constipation rather than the herb. The mechanism is anthraquinones (emodin) that stimulate peristalsis directly. He also noted emodin and tetracycline-class antibiotics share a structural family and overlapping anti-inflammatory properties.
- **Adequate fluid.** A quart of orange juice, a quart of milk, plus water with meals; deficit-driven constipation usually resolves in days.
- **Thyroid support.** Slow transit is one of the cleanest signs of hypothyroidism. Morning oral temperature below 97.8°F and resting pulse below 75 bpm point to thyroid as the upstream lever — see [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

**Chronic loose stools** indicate the *opposite* problem: usually an undigested-fiber food in regular rotation (raw onions, garlic, lentils, beans, large amounts of cruciferous, high-FODMAP supplements) or active dysbiosis. Anti-diarrheal medication is symptom-suppression; the lever is removing the offending food and considering a Phase 1 protocol below.

## Phase 1 — Severe endotoxemia

This is the active-treatment phase for someone in an acute endotoxin-driven state: post-antibiotic dysbiosis, an autoimmune flare with somatic markers, severe brain fog with cold extremities and 4 a.m. cortisol wake-ups, IBS-D or IBS-C with documented SIBO, or the clinical pattern Peat described in fertility patients before the antibiotic trial. The daily baseline runs in parallel; these are additions, not replacements.

**Daily raw carrot, every single day.** Non-negotiable. If carrot tolerance is poor, halve the dose and build up; if absolutely intolerable, use cooked bamboo shoots as substitute (less effective but still in-category).

**Activated charcoal at 1–3 grams, twice weekly.** This is an emergency-medicine tool used intermittently, not daily. The dosing in Peat's reference (V.V. Frolkis's 43% lifespan-extension study in old rats) was approximately 1 cup per day for ten days of each month — a heavy intermittent regimen, not a continuous one. For human use, 500–1,000 mg taken on an empty stomach two hours away from meals, supplements, and medication, twice weekly during the acute phase. Charcoal binds LPS, bile acids, and bacterial metabolites in the gut lumen and carries them out in stool. It is profoundly non-selective — it will bind vitamins, minerals, and oral drugs along with the toxins, which is why the timing and intermittence matter. See [[activated-charcoal]] for the full caution set. Stool will turn black; this is harmless. Pair with adequate magnesium or cascara because charcoal can constipate.

**Cooked greens or antiseptic-fiber food at every meal.** Bamboo shoots in soup, well-cooked mushrooms in eggs, cooked cabbage or spinach (cooking water discarded if oxalate sensitivity is a factor — the magnesium extraction does happen in cooking water, but the oxalates leach into the same water). The goal in the severe phase is mechanical clearance every few hours, not just once daily.

**Targeted short-course low-dose antibiotic — under clinician coordination only.** This is the most clinically loaded element of the protocol and the one most often misread. Peat's framework supports **targeted, short-course, low-dose** antibiotic use in a dysbiotic state and is opposed to broad-spectrum, long-course use. The reference cases are doxycycline 50–100 mg per day for 5–7 days, or minocycline at similar dosing, or the older fertility-clinic protocol that produced the hormonal shift (lower estrogen, higher progesterone, lower cortisol) in infertile women on a brief antibiotic. Peat's own usage pattern, recorded in his email correspondence, was to break tablets into fourths or halves and dose to symptom — a first dose of 100 mg, then 50 mg if the symptom response was clear, then taper as the symptom resolved. Vitamin K (from liver, kale, or a supplement) must be co-supplemented because antibiotics suppress the gut bacteria that produce vitamin K. This element of the protocol requires a clinician who is willing to prescribe and monitor; it is not appropriate for self-administration. See [[antibiotic-question]] below for the framework.

**Thyroid optimization in parallel.** A hypothyroid intestine cannot keep itself sterile — peristalsis slows, digestive secretions drop, bacterial colonization creeps up from the colon into the small intestine. Restoring thyroid function (T4 + T3 or T3-only dosing per [[thyroid-dosing]], with morning temperature and pulse as the dose-finding signals) is upstream of the gut work and often resolves recurrent endotoxemia on its own.

**Aspirin at 325 mg with food once or twice daily, with co-supplemented vitamin K.** [[Aspirin]] suppresses the nitric oxide and prostaglandin response that endotoxin triggers, acts as a mild laxative by improving intestinal motility, and reduces the inflammatory amplification downstream of LPS exposure. Not a primary lever but a useful adjunct in the severe phase.

Expect the severe-phase additions to run for two to six weeks, not months. The exit criterion is reduction of the somatic markers (clearing brain fog, warming extremities, normalizing the 4 a.m. wake-up, falling joint pain, normalizing stool form) and an audit of whether the daily baseline is now sufficient.

## Phase 2 — Maintenance

This is the steady-state once the acute phase has resolved or for someone who has never been in an acute phase. The daily core (carrot, antiseptic fiber rotation, transit management) is the entire protocol most of the time.

**Daily raw carrot.** Same as Phase 1.

**Antiseptic-fiber rotation.** Cooked bamboo shoots and well-cooked mushrooms cycled through the week so carrot fatigue does not erode compliance.

**Charcoal at most once weekly, often less.** Not a daily supplement. The maintenance frequency is "when needed for a known trigger" — after a heavy restaurant meal, when traveling, during an obvious gut upset — rather than scheduled. Long-term daily charcoal use binds minerals and fat-soluble vitamins and is net-harmful in the maintenance state.

**No standing antibiotic.** The antibiotic question reopens only when symptoms recur; the default in maintenance is the carrot-and-substrate baseline alone.

**Active food choices that don't feed lower-bowel fermentation.** Easily digested sugars (ripe fruit, juice, honey, milk) absorbed in the upper small intestine before they reach the bacterial zone; saturated fats with starches (butter on potatoes) to suppress bacterial growth on what does reach the lower bowel; calcium with phosphate-heavy foods (cheese with bread) because calcium blocks the phospholysis that breaks fiber down for bacteria. The mineral context is part of the protocol — a high-calcium, low-phosphate diet is itself an endotoxin-reduction strategy.

## The antibiotic question

Peat's position on antibiotics is the most counterintuitive element of his framework and the most easily caricatured. The short version: he was opposed to broad-spectrum long-course antibiotic use in the conventional model, and supportive of targeted short-course low-dose use in specific dysbiotic states.

What he supported was the clinical pattern documented in the fertility-clinic study he cited repeatedly: a brief antibiotic course in infertile women produced "less fatigue, fewer premenstrual symptoms, lower estrogen, higher progesterone, lower cortisol" — a fertility-pattern hormonal shift produced not by hormone replacement but by interrupting the bacterial production of endotoxin upstream of the liver. The same principle applies to the targeted use of doxycycline or minocycline in dysbiotic patients today. The tetracycline class, which includes doxycycline and minocycline, has additional anti-inflammatory and anti-collagenase properties beyond its antibacterial effect; Peat noted minocycline showed signal in Alzheimer's prevention studies, and doxycycline at sub-antimicrobial doses has documented effects on biofilm and matrix metalloproteinase activity.

What he opposed was the conventional pattern: broad-spectrum antibiotics at full dose for weeks, prescribed for unclear indication, without attention to vitamin K, without rebuilding the gut substrate afterward, and without the upstream thyroid and dietary work that prevents the dysbiosis from recurring. He described the failure mode directly: "It sterilizes the intestine. If you don't pay attention to hormones governing digestive enzymes, a sterile intestine is a good place for fungus." A scorched-earth antibiotic on a hypothyroid patient on a high-PUFA low-sugar diet is a recipe for chronic recurrence, not for resolution.

His personal-use pattern, documented in his email correspondence, was idiosyncratic and not directly transferable as a prescription: he would break tablets into fourths or halves, dose to symptom (a first dose of 100 mg, smaller subsequent doses as the symptom decreased, stopping when there was no detectable signal), and supplement with vitamin K1 or K2 from liver, kale, or capsule throughout. The replicable element is the **principle** — targeted, low-dose, symptom-titrated, vitamin-K-supported — rather than the specific dose pattern.

The clinical translation: this is a clinician-coordinated intervention. The protocol calls for finding a practitioner willing to prescribe doxycycline 50–100 mg/day for 5–7 days (or minocycline at similar dosing) in a documented dysbiotic state, with vitamin K co-supplementation, with the gut-substrate work running in parallel, and with explicit exit criteria. Self-administration is not appropriate. The risk profile of even short-course tetracyclines (photosensitivity, esophagitis if not taken upright, rare hepatotoxicity, the standard tetracycline-class cautions) and the requirement for accurate identification of the underlying state both demand professional oversight.

## Foods that worsen endotoxin

The corollary list is the set of foods that feed lower-bowel fermentation, increase the daily LPS input, and bottleneck the protocol.

**Raw cruciferous in large amounts.** Raw cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, and brussels sprouts contain fiber that ferments aggressively and isothiocyanates that suppress thyroid. Small amounts cooked are fine; salads built on raw cruciferous are net-negative in this framework.

**Raw onions and garlic.** Both contain fermentable fibers and sulfur compounds that ferment in the lower intestine. Cooked is acceptable; raw should be limited. Peat noted that raw garlic "is about as hard on the stomach as on the germs" — the antimicrobial action is real but the gastric irritation is significant.

**Legumes.** Beans, lentils, peas, and soy carry fermentable oligosaccharides that bypass the small intestine and feed lower-bowel bacteria directly. Peat described "the bean syndrome" as a specific endotoxin-driven inflammatory state. Even soaked, sprouted, and well-cooked legumes carry residual fermentable load.

**Whole grains and high-bran foods.** Bran, oat fiber, psyllium, and "high-fiber" cereals provide exactly the fermentable substrate the protocol is trying to deny the bacteria. Peat: "Practically all of the fibers used as food additives — carrageenan, guar gum, oat bran, and even psyllium — have been identified as carcinogens for the intestine and possibly other organs." This is the inversion of the conventional fiber recommendation that takes most people the longest to absorb.

**[[Carrageenan]] and food-additive gums.** Carrageenan synergizes with endotoxin on nitric oxide, prostaglandins, and toxic free radicals, and damages the mucosal defenses that normally keep LPS in the lumen. Read labels; it appears in almond milk, ice cream, deli meats, and many "natural" packaged products.

**High-PUFA foods.** Polyunsaturated fats increase intestinal permeability directly and provide the arachidonic-acid substrate for the inflammatory prostaglandins that endotoxin generates downstream. The PUFA-endotoxin interaction is the central amplifier — see [[pufa-depletion]] for the long-term clearance protocol.

**Underdigested protein from low gastric acid.** Protein that reaches the lower intestine undigested feeds putrefactive bacteria. Adequate stomach acid (often supported by salt, B1, and addressing chronic stress) and adequate digestive enzyme output are upstream of this; chronic acid-blocker use is a known driver.

## Tracking signals

The protocol works if these change, in roughly this order:

- **Mental clarity** within 1–2 weeks. Endotoxin-driven brain fog tends to lift as serum LPS drops; thinking feels sharper and faster.
- **Mood stability** within 2–4 weeks. The 4 a.m. cortisol wake-up weakens; afternoon crashes flatten; baseline anxiety eases.
- **Body temperature stable** at ≥97.8°F morning oral, ≥98.6°F mid-afternoon, within 1–3 months. Reflects the thyroid-side improvement that follows endotoxin reduction.
- **Joint pain reduction** within 2–6 weeks for non-structural pain; prostaglandin-driven inflammation drops with LPS clearance.
- **Easier digestion** within 1–2 weeks. Less bloating, less gas, more consistent stool, less reactivity to individual foods.
- **Reduction of food sensitivities** within 1–3 months. Most "intolerance" patterns are downstream of endotoxin-driven gut permeability; as the barrier reseals, the reaction profile narrows.
- **Stool form normalizes** within 1–3 weeks. Soft, formed, complete; one to two per day.

If the daily baseline runs for six to eight weeks with no movement on these markers, the protocol is being broken somewhere — usually by hidden fermentable fiber, by inadequate substrate (low calories, low salt, low sugar), or by upstream hypothyroidism that has not been addressed.

## Failure modes

The protocol fails in predictable ways. Watch for these.

**Over-relying on charcoal.** Daily charcoal binds fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), minerals (calcium, magnesium, zinc), and beneficial dietary compounds along with the toxins. It is an acute tool, not a daily supplement. The maintenance-phase frequency is at most weekly, often less. People who treat charcoal as a daily habit end up mineral-deficient.

**Ignoring constipation while running the carrot.** The carrot only works in a moving gut. Constipation lengthens the contact time and reverses the protocol's effect — the carrot fiber that should be carrying conjugated estrogen out instead sits with it long enough for bacterial deconjugation to occur. Treat slow transit before scaling carrot intake.

**Using probiotics as the primary intervention.** Peat was skeptical of probiotic supplements. The argument is that the lower intestine should not be heavily colonized in a healthy state — the bacterial load there should be modest. Adding bacteria to a dysbiotic state is, in this framework, mostly theatre. The lever is reducing the substrate (fermentable fiber, undigested protein, slow transit) the existing bacteria feed on, not adding more bacteria. There may be a narrow case for specific strains in narrow conditions, but the default in the protocol is no probiotics. (Fermented foods are a related but distinct question — Peat tolerated some, was skeptical of others; see [[yogurt]] and the fermented-foods section in [[detoxification]].)

**Taking antibiotics without rebuilding the substrate.** A doxycycline course on a hypothyroid PUFA-loaded patient on a low-carb diet sterilizes the intestine briefly and then reseeds with the same dysbiotic pattern within weeks. The antibiotic only works as part of the larger protocol — substrate, thyroid, carrot, transit, and only then the targeted course.

**Treating endotoxin reduction as a project with an end date.** This is a daily practice, not a cleanse. The bacteria do not stay reduced; the carrot has to be eaten today, again tomorrow, and again the day after.

**Aggressive fasting as a "reset."** Fasting mobilizes stored PUFA into a slowed liver, increases gut permeability, and depletes the UDP-glucuronate substrate the liver needs to conjugate the estrogen the protocol is trying to clear. Peat was explicit: "If you slow down the liver's activity — which happens in a fast — your brain and organs are exposed to chemicals diffusing out of fat and muscles. The best cleansing diet is one that increases your liver's action."

## What to avoid

- **Probiotic supplements** as a primary intervention. Skeptical default.
- **Fermented foods** during active dysbiosis. Yogurt, kombucha, sauerkraut, and kefir add lactic-acid-producing organisms to a state already characterized by excess fermentation. Reintroduce, if at all, in maintenance.
- **Raw onions and raw garlic** as regular foods. Cooked, fine; raw, limited.
- **Beans, lentils, soy** as protein staples. The fermentable-oligosaccharide load is too high.
- **High-FODMAP foods** in active state. Onions, garlic, wheat, apples, pears, stone fruits, honey in excess — the FODMAP list overlaps heavily with Peat's avoid-during-dysbiosis list.
- **High-bran whole grains, psyllium, oat bran.** The "fiber" recommendation inverts.
- **Carrageenan and gums.** Read labels.
- **Coffee enemas, colonics, juice fasts.** Peat dismissed these explicitly. They mobilize without clearing and stress the intestinal mucosa.

## When to escalate

The protocol assumes a patient with manageable symptoms doing the daily work over weeks to months. It is not appropriate as primary care for the following — see a clinician immediately.

- **Persistent diarrhea** for more than a week, especially with blood, mucus, or weight loss. Possible inflammatory bowel disease, infectious colitis, or C. diff; needs workup.
- **Blood in stool.** Always a clinician question. Hemorrhoidal bleeding is usually benign but should be confirmed; melanic or tarry stool is a red flag for upper GI bleeding.
- **Unexplained weight loss** of more than 5% body weight over a few weeks. Endotoxin reduction does not cause weight loss in a properly fed person; weight loss in this context points to malabsorption, undiagnosed inflammatory disease, or other pathology.
- **Severe brain fog progressing to confusion** or new neurological symptoms. The endotoxin-encephalopathy pattern is real but rare; new neurological signs need imaging and a clinician.
- **Severe abdominal pain, fever, vomiting.** Acute abdomen — emergency care.
- **Suspected SIBO** with chronic bloating, abnormal breath tests, persistent food reactivity despite the protocol. Worth a gastroenterology consult; rifaximin (a non-absorbed antibiotic) has documented efficacy and fits the "targeted, short-course" frame Peat would have endorsed.
- **Suspected autoimmune flare** with new joint involvement, skin changes, fatigue out of proportion to load. The protocol supports background work but rheumatology should rule out specific diagnoses requiring disease-modifying therapy.

The general principle: the protocol is for the chronic background load, not for acute decompensation. When acute, get help.

## Counter-arguments

The endotoxin-reduction framework runs against multiple lines of mainstream nutritional and gastroenterological orthodoxy, and an honest protocol page has to surface the disputes rather than paper over them.

**The "more fiber is better" consensus.** Mainstream nutrition treats fiber as universally beneficial — soluble for cholesterol, insoluble for transit, fermentable for short-chain-fatty-acid production. Peat's framework inverts most of this: fermentable fiber is the substrate for the bacteria producing the toxin. The disagreement is partly definitional (Peat's "antiseptic fiber" is a narrow category) and partly empirical. The short-chain-fatty-acid case for fermentable fiber is real but, in Peat's view, the LPS produced alongside the SCFAs is the dominant signal. Mainstream gut-microbiome research is mostly hostile to the Peat position.

**The probiotic and prebiotic industry.** A multi-billion-dollar industry rests on the premise that adding bacteria to the gut is therapeutic. Peat treated the lower intestine's correct state as one of modest bacterial load, not high diversity. The "diversity is health" claim that drives the field is, in his framework, mostly artifact of the disease state the field is studying.

**The biome-rebuild model.** Functional medicine often runs a "kill-clear-rebuild" cycle (antimicrobial → binder → probiotic). The Peat framework keeps the antimicrobial and binder phases and replaces the probiotic rebuild with substrate work (sugar, salt, gelatin, thyroid). The disagreement is whether the bacterial population reseeds correctly on its own given the substrate, or whether it requires active reseeding. Peat's bet was on the substrate; clinical opinion is mixed.

**The antibiotic-overuse consensus.** Mainstream medicine is correctly cautious about antibiotic overuse driving resistance. The Peat framework's endorsement of low-dose short-course tetracyclines in dysbiotic states sits uneasily with this consensus. The resolution is in the specifics: short courses at sub-antimicrobial doses produce different selection pressure than broad-spectrum long courses, and the tetracycline-class anti-inflammatory effects may be the dominant therapeutic mechanism at low doses. The point remains contested.

## Data gaps

- The dose-response curve for raw carrot intake is not characterized. Peat ate one daily; some people seem to benefit from more, some less. No formal study has measured the relationship between carrot mass and endotoxin or estrogen markers.
- The optimal frequency of activated-charcoal dosing in maintenance is unclear. The Frolkis lifespan-extension study used a heavy intermittent regimen (10 days/month); no replication has tested lower frequencies.
- The actual prevalence of clinically significant SIBO in the population Peat called "everyone has endotoxin" is unknown. The breath-test diagnostic standard is imperfect.
- Long-term safety data for the low-dose intermittent tetracycline pattern Peat described is sparse. The conventional safety data is for full-dose continuous courses.
- The interaction between the antibiotic phase and pregnancy or breastfeeding is not addressed in Peat's framework and warrants extreme caution.
- The maintenance-phase question of whether daily carrot alone is enough indefinitely, or whether occasional cycling onto bamboo shoots and mushrooms is required, has no formal answer.
