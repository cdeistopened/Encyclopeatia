# Migraine Prevention Protocol

This is the prevention layer paired with [[migraine]]. The condition page sets out the mechanism (estrogen-histamine-serotonin elevation on a hypothyroid liver, blood-sugar volatility, ammonia/CO₂ imbalance, and inherited susceptibility), the acute-rescue logic (sugar + salt + caffeine + progesterone, with [[cyproheptadine]] for serotonin-dominant attacks), and the case against chronic triptan use. This page is the work to stop attacks from starting.

Most "prevention" advice in the conventional literature is symptomatic — daily prophylactic drugs taken to blunt a system that keeps trying to fire. Peat's framework inverts the question. A migraine is a brain that has been pushed past its energy threshold by some combination of low blood sugar, hormonal stress, sleep debt, vasoactive dietary load, and detoxification overflow. Prevention means raising the threshold by restoring the metabolic substrate, suppressing the estrogen-histamine-serotonin spike that crosses it, and identifying the personal trigger that fires most often. Triptans and CGRP inhibitors become a residual tool for the migraines that get through, not a daily commitment.

Sequencing matters. The most common failure pattern is layering hormonal interventions or pharmaceuticals on top of a metabolism still running on intermittent fasting, a quart of coffee, no [[salt]], and seed-oil dinners. The protocol below puts the substrate work first, then identifies the pattern, then titrates pattern-specific levers, then asks whether residual attacks justify a conventional prophylactic. People who skip the trigger-inventory step and go straight to "what supplement do I take" often spend a year chasing the wrong lever.

## Trigger Inventory

Before any intervention, two to four weeks of structured tracking. The point is not to confirm that you have triggers — almost every migraineur has several. The point is to identify which one fires most often in your case, because the rest of the protocol pivots on this.

Track daily for at least one full menstrual cycle if you are a cycling woman, four weeks otherwise:

1. **Meals.** Time, composition, whether you went more than 4 hours without food.
2. **Sleep.** Hours, wake-time consistency, whether you woke between 1 and 4 a.m.
3. **Cycle phase** (if cycling). Day of cycle, days from ovulation, days to next period.
4. **Stress arc.** Subjective stress 1–5; whether the day was high-tension or a "letdown" after a stressful stretch.
5. **Food log of suspect items.** Red wine, aged cheese, cured meats, MSG-heavy restaurant food, aspartame-sweetened beverages, chocolate, citrus on empty stomach.
6. **Weather.** Barometric drops, heat, humidity changes.
7. **Hydration and salt.** Glasses of water, whether food was salted normally.
8. **Migraine days.** Whether one occurred, prodromal window (food cravings, neck stiffness, fatigue 12–48 hours before), severity 1–5, duration, what aborted it.

At the end of the window, look for the dominant cluster. Most people find one of six patterns leads:

- **Hypoglycemic pattern.** Attacks fire 3–6 hours after a missed or light meal, after exercise without refueling, in the late morning of skipped breakfast, or as 3 a.m. wake-ups that become full migraines by 6 a.m.
- **Menstrual pattern.** Attacks cluster predictably in the late luteal phase (days 25–28), on the first day of menstruation, or at ovulation. Often accompanied by other [[pms]] symptoms.
- **Sleep-debt pattern.** Attacks fire on the third or fourth day of insufficient sleep, after a single very late night, or after a time-zone shift.
- **Dietary serotonin-histamine pattern.** Attacks fire within hours of red wine, aged cheese, cured meats, chocolate, or large amounts of raw fruit on an empty stomach. From Peat's [[fruit|fruit email]]: "The pectin in raw apples causes the intestine to release serotonin into the blood, so well cooked apples have much less effect."
- **Stress-letdown pattern.** The classic "weekend migraine" or "vacation migraine" — attacks fire on the first day off after a high-stress stretch, or the morning after a major deadline. Cortisol falls, vascular tone drops, and the brain crosses the threshold in the relaxation phase.
- **Weather/dehydration pattern.** Attacks correlate with barometric drops, heat exposure, salt restriction, or the day after heavy alcohol.

Many people have two or three patterns active simultaneously. Rank them by frequency. The pattern that fires most often gets the most aggressive emphasis layer below; the secondary patterns get monitored but not chased.

## The Daily Core Stack

Six interventions that everyone runs regardless of pattern. These constitute the metabolic substrate without which no pattern-specific lever will hold.

**1. The bedtime snack — mandatory.** See [[eat-before-bed]] for the full practice. The shortest version: 30 to 60 minutes before sleep, a small carb-and-salt snack — 4–6 oz orange juice with a pinch of salt, a glass of warm milk with honey, a small bowl of ice cream, or salty broth. The function is to pre-load liver glycogen so the 1–4 a.m. cortisol-adrenaline cascade never starts. From the [[headaches-and-migraines|*Headaches and Migraines* email]]: "An inefficient liver that, among other things, doesn't store enough glycogen to last the whole night, is a common cause of both headaches and shakiness." For the hypoglycemic pattern this is the single highest-leverage intervention. For every other pattern it is the foundation that lets the rest work.

**2. Magnesium glycinate, 200–400 mg evening.** [[Magnesium]] is the one supplement the conventional migraine literature and the Peat framework agree on. The mechanism is dual: magnesium is a cofactor in glucose oxidation (without it, glucose goes to lactate instead of CO₂), and it directly stabilizes vascular smooth muscle and NMDA receptor activity. Glycinate is the most bioavailable form for daily use; magnesium chloride or sulfate baths are an alternative absorbed through skin. Citrate is acceptable but more laxative. Start at 200 mg with the evening meal; titrate to 400 mg if stools tolerate. Avoid oxide and aspartate. Peat noted in the [[headaches-and-migraines|migraine email]] that "magnesium has a great variety of restorative functions" — the corpus does not give a migraine-specific dose, but the conventional 200–400 mg range is consistent with his broader recommendations.

**3. Aspirin, 81–162 mg daily.** Low-dose [[aspirin]] does three things relevant to migraine: it inhibits prostaglandin synthesis (the inflammatory mediators behind vascular dilation and pain), it lowers serotonin's effect on platelet aggregation, and from the same email file Peat noted: "Aspirin's similarity to benzoate and phenylacetate suggests that it might sometimes help to remove ammonia." Take with milk or food to protect the stomach. People with bleeding disorders, ulcer history, or upcoming surgery should clear this with a clinician. Half a baby aspirin (40 mg) is enough for many people who get GI symptoms from larger doses. Do not stop abruptly during a migraine-prone window — the prostaglandin rebound can precipitate an attack. Taper if discontinuing.

**4. Ripe-fruit-and-salt baseline.** The dietary foundation. A quart of orange juice or other ripe fruit daily, with salt deliberately added to food. Fruit supplies fructose (which buffers liver glycogen more efficiently than glucose alone), potassium, and the alpha-keto acids Peat noted "can bind ammonia, becoming ordinary amino acids" ([[estrogen-aging-radiation-migraine-and-energy|*Estrogen, Aging, Radiation, Migraine & Energy*]]). Salt — Peat repeatedly emphasized that the standard low-salt advice destabilizes blood sugar and amplifies adrenaline. Salt food to taste; if you crave it, eat more of it. The bedtime broth variant is the salt-and-snack combined.

**5. No PUFA cooking oils.** Eliminate soybean, corn, canola, safflower, sunflower, grapeseed, "vegetable" oil, and most commercial baked goods. Cook in butter, coconut oil, beef tallow, or ghee. The mechanism is in [[pufa-depletion]] — circulating polyunsaturated fatty acids amplify estrogen's effects, suppress thyroid, and feed the inflammatory prostaglandin cascade behind vascular headache. From [[natural-estrogens|*Natural Estrogens*]]: "the accumulation of the 'essential fatty acids,' the polyunsaturated oils, in the tissues promotes the action of estrogen in a variety of ways." This change does not stop migraines next week. Circulating free fatty acids drop within two weeks; tissue stores take years. It is the substrate that lets every other lever work harder.

**6. Adequate sleep, defended.** Seven and a half to nine hours, consistent wake time, dark room, warm enough to keep feet from getting cold. The sleep-debt pattern is the most underrated trigger in conventional advice that fixates on diet. A single night of four-hour sleep on the wrong cycle day can produce a 48-hour migraine in a susceptible woman. Treat sleep as non-negotiable infrastructure, not a hobby. The bedtime snack above is half the sleep practice; the other half is timing and protection. See [[sleep-restoration]] if sleep is itself the problem.

## Cyproheptadine Evening

For the serotonin-dominant pattern — the person whose attacks reliably follow red wine, aged cheese, raw fruit on empty stomach, chocolate, or the day after a serotonin-elevating event (sunburn, intense exercise, a tryptophan-rich meal) — a small evening dose of [[cyproheptadine]] is the most targeted preventive available in the Peat toolkit. The full pharmacology and dose framework is on the [[cyproheptadine]] page. The migraine-specific summary: start at **0.25 to 0.5 mg in the evening**, increase by 0.25 mg every five to seven days until either the migraine pattern softens or morning grogginess becomes limiting. Most preventive responders settle at 1 to 2 mg evening.

Peat's framing in the [[headaches-and-migraines|*Headaches and Migraines* email]]: "Is cyproheptadine available where you are? It's probably the safest of the antiserotonin drugs." And in another exchange in the same file: "Cyproheptadine, a small amount at bedtime, reduces night stress, might help the headaches and shakiness." The drug has a long mainstream history as a pediatric migraine prophylactic; the Peat-specific contribution is the fractional dosing and the framing as a tool to interrupt a self-perpetuating serotonin cycle while the dietary and metabolic substrate rebuilds.

The durability signal matters. Peat repeatedly noted that small intermittent doses produce extended periods of relief without continuous dosing. A patient who runs cyproheptadine evenings through one or two migraine-prone weeks and then drops it often finds the off-drug interval lengthening as the underlying physiology recovers. This is not the pattern of a drug that has to be taken forever to work. Tracking whether the off-drug interval is growing is a better progress measure than tracking the dose.

If sedation is limiting at any dose, drop back. The starting dose is the smallest fraction of a tablet that produces an effect.

## Menstrual-Pattern Emphasis

For women with cycle-tracked attacks clustering in the late luteal phase or at menstruation, [[progesterone]] is the most leveraged single addition. Peat documented this consistently across forty years of writing. In the [[transdermal-progesterone-for-premenstrual-syndrome|*Transdermal Progesterone for PMS* (1983)]] paper: "We have used transdermal progesterone therapy in two hundred women suffering from the full range of premenstrual symptoms, including migraine, acne, depression, mastalgia, edema, and lethargy, and we found that nearly all of the women, applying the lotion themselves, are able to find the appropriate dosage for controlling their symptoms."

The mechanism is in [[estrogen-aging-radiation-migraine-and-energy|*Estrogen, Aging, Radiation, Migraine & Energy*]]: "Ammonia has also been found to be increased during migraine attacks. I suspect that progesterone's sometimes dramatic effect on migraine involves ammonia and energy metabolism... Progesterone's ability to stop the local excitation of nerve cells spares ATP."

**Dosing for the menstrual pattern:**

- **Form:** Progest-E (vitamin E vehicle) or another high-quality oil-based progesterone. Avoid the cream bases sold in supplement aisles — most are sub-therapeutic in dose density.
- **Phase 1 trial:** 25 to 100 mg daily, applied to inner wrists, gums, or vaginally, on **days 14 to 28** of the cycle (ovulation through the day before bleeding). For women whose attacks are most reliable on cycle days 25 through 2, run the higher end of the dose through the perimenstrual window.
- **Acute rescue layer.** If an attack starts mid-cycle, the [[headaches-and-migraines|migraine email]] case applies: "100 mg of progesterone in oil can usually stop it, by stopping the wastage of glucose." A 100 mg bolus at prodrome is often abortive; up to several hundred mg over the next 12 hours is within the safety range Peat described.
- **Titrate by symptom, not by lab.** Two weeks of trial; if attacks soften or shift later in the cycle, you have the right pattern. If no change, the pattern is not estrogen-progesterone-driven and the lever is wrong.

**Thyroid optimization** runs in parallel. Peat consistently linked premenstrual migraine to underlying [[hypothyroidism]] — "estrogen-histamine-serotonin rise on a background of hypothyroid liver malfunction" ([[anti-depressants|anti-depressants email]]). A hypothyroid liver does not clear estrogen, does not store enough glycogen for the overnight, and converts T4 to T3 poorly. Morning oral temperature consistently below 98.0°F or resting pulse below 75 indicates the thyroid axis should be addressed before judging the progesterone response. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]] for the staging.

**Estrogen-load reduction.** Run the full [[estrogen-detox]] protocol concurrently — eliminate [[xenoestrogens]], add the daily raw carrot, address gut transit, support liver phase I and phase II detox. The carrot detail is in [[natural-estrogens|*Natural Estrogens*]] in Peat's own words: "Because of my own experience in finding that eating a raw carrot daily prevented my migraines, I began to suspect that the carrot fiber was having both a bowel-protective and an antiestrogen effect. Several women who suffered from premenstrual symptoms, including migraine, had their serum estrogen measured before and after the 'carrot diet,' and they found that the carrot lowered their estrogen within a few days, as it relieved their symptoms." A medium peeled carrot daily with a little salt or vinegar, eaten between meals.

## Hypoglycemic-Pattern Emphasis

For the person whose attacks fire after missed meals, light eating, intense exercise without refueling, or as middle-of-night wake-ups that escalate to morning migraine, the entire intervention is feeding density and timing.

**Frequent small meals, never long fasts.** Eat every 3 to 4 hours during waking hours. Three meals plus two or three snacks; a snack is a glass of milk, a piece of fruit with cheese, a small bowl of ice cream, or salted broth with gelatin. Intermittent fasting protocols are contraindicated for the hypoglycemic pattern — the eight-hour eating window is exactly the duration that produces the rebound hypoglycemia attacks fire on. From the [[headaches-and-migraines|migraine email]]: "Low blood sugar increases the absorption of toxin from the intestine as well as activating nerve reflexes and inflammation."

**Orange juice and salt at first prodrome.** The earliest warning of a hypoglycemic-pattern attack — neck stiffness, food craving, vague fatigue, irritability 6 to 24 hours before pain — is the window where the attack is most easily aborted. From the same email: "A very large amount of sugar will usually relieve a migraine; ice cream (about a quart) or milk shakes with some fat and protein make it easier to assimilate the sugar without stomach upset. Caffeine sometimes makes the aspirin and sugar more effective." A glass of OJ with salt at the first signal; if the prodrome continues, scale up — milkshake, ice cream, salted soup. Treating the prodrome aggressively is cheaper than treating the migraine.

**Eat-before-bed mandatory.** Non-negotiable for the hypoglycemic pattern. See [[eat-before-bed]]. Set an alarm 30 minutes *before* the historical 3 a.m. wake-time and eat preemptively if the pattern is established; this breaks the cycle within a week.

**Thyroid check.** A hypoglycemic pattern in an adult is almost always downstream of a [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid]] liver with reduced glycogen capacity. Track morning temperature and pulse; if temperature is consistently under 97.8°F or pulse under 70, the thyroid axis is the upstream lever. Symptomatic management of hypoglycemic migraine will work but the substrate work compounds it.

## Stress-Letdown Pattern Emphasis

For the weekend or vacation migraineur — the person whose attacks fire on the first day off, the morning after a deadline, or the start of a holiday — the issue is not stress itself but the *transition* from high to low sympathetic tone. Cortisol falls, vascular tone collapses, and the brain crosses the threshold in the relaxation phase. Conventional advice ("manage your stress better") gets the direction backwards; the work is to soften the transition.

**Anticipate the transition.** Identify your high-to-low pattern (Friday evening, last day of a project, first hour of a vacation) and treat the 24 hours before and after as a managed window. Eat the bedtime snack. Drink an extra glass of orange juice on the day. Take the daily aspirin and magnesium without skipping. Sleep at the regular time, not late.

**Maintain cortisol stability with food, not caffeine.** Sudden caffeine drops on the weekend (skipping the workday coffee routine) are a frequent letdown trigger. If your workweek is four to five cups of coffee daily, keep at least two cups on the weekend. Conversely, do not chain-load coffee on the weekend to "catch up" — the cortisol-caffeine spike followed by an empty-stomach crash is the same hypoglycemic substrate as the missed-meal pattern.

**Light movement through the transition.** Twenty to thirty minutes of walking on Saturday morning, slow and aerobic, maintains the vascular tone the sudden sit-down would have dropped. Not strenuous exercise — which spikes lactate and adrenaline and can itself precipitate an attack — but walking, light gardening, household tasks. The point is gentle sympathetic load, not training stress.

**Bag breathing for the prodrome.** If the letdown prodrome appears (neck stiffness, fatigue, the characteristic "I can feel one coming") and an attack has historically followed within hours, ten minutes of [[bag-breathing]] raises CO₂ and counteracts the hyperventilation-driven vasoconstriction that often opens the attack. Not a substitute for sugar and salt — combine.

## Dietary Trigger Elimination

For the dietary-serotonin-histamine pattern — the person whose attacks reliably fire within hours of specific foods — a structured four-week elimination trial. Run this only after the daily core stack is in place; the substrate work alone resolves dietary sensitivity in a substantial fraction of people, because the histamine-clearance pathway becomes more robust on a well-fed liver.

**Eliminate for four weeks:**

- **Red wine** (and to a lesser extent dark beer, aged spirits)
- **Aged cheese** (parmesan, blue, aged cheddar, brie). Fresh cheeses (mozzarella, ricotta, cottage) are usually fine.
- **Cured meats** (salami, prosciutto, bacon, smoked fish, hot dogs). Nitrates are the suspect.
- **Aspartame** (Diet Coke, sugar-free gum, "light" yogurts)
- **MSG-heavy foods** (most restaurant Chinese, processed soup mixes, bouillon cubes, flavored chips)
- **Chocolate** (particularly dark, particularly on empty stomach)
- **Raw onions, fermented foods** (kimchi, sauerkraut), large doses of probiotic supplements — the histamine load matters more than the bacterial profile

**Reintroduce one at a time.** After four migraine-free or migraine-reduced weeks, reintroduce a single suspect at a time, in a small portion, on a low-other-stress day. Wait 72 hours before judging — delayed reactions are common. Either it triggers an attack or it doesn't. If it doesn't, return it to the diet and trial the next item a week later.

The point is not lifelong elimination but identification of the two or three personal triggers that account for most attacks. Most people find one or two of the list above are reliable triggers and the rest are not personally relevant. Knowing which two means you can avoid them at meaningful cost, not avoid all of them at the cost of social and dietary flexibility.

A note on fruit. Peat's [[fruit|fruit email]] flagged that "the fruits you mention all seriously increase serotonin. A sore throat is a quick effect, but some people get migraines from them. The pectin in raw apples causes the intestine to release serotonin into the blood, so well cooked apples have much less effect." If raw apples or pears reliably precede attacks, switch to cooked apples (compote, applesauce) and well-ripened tropical fruit (mango, papaya).

## The Prophylactic Medication Question

When the core stack plus pattern-specific emphasis produces a partial response — fewer or less severe attacks but more than one per month residual — the question of conventional prophylactic medication becomes pragmatic rather than ideological. Topiramate, propranolol, amitriptyline, candesartan, and the newer CGRP inhibitors (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab) each have established prophylactic efficacy. The Peat-framework critique is not that they fail to reduce attacks but that they treat the symptom while leaving the substrate intact, and several carry significant tradeoffs (topiramate cognitive effects, propranolol blunting of thyroid response, amitriptyline anticholinergic burden and serotonin recycling).

The decision logic:

- **If attacks reduce by 50%+ on the Peat protocol alone**, hold conventional prophylactic in reserve. Track for three to six months. The protocol typically deepens over that window.
- **If attacks reduce but residual frequency disrupts work or life**, layer a conventional prophylactic at the lowest effective dose. The protocol substrate often allows a lower prophylactic dose than would be needed without it, with fewer side effects.
- **If attacks do not reduce after three months of consistent core stack**, reconsider the pattern identification. The wrong pattern emphasis produces a flat response. Re-run the trigger inventory; the lead pattern may be different from what the first month suggested.
- **CGRP inhibitors** are the newest class and have the cleanest side-effect profile of the modern prophylactics. For a patient with disabling chronic migraine who has run the protocol and still has weekly attacks, a CGRP trial is reasonable. The cost is high; insurance authorization usually requires documented failure of two older prophylactics.

The framing matters. A patient on the protocol plus a low-dose conventional prophylactic is not a protocol failure. They are a person whose threshold has been raised by both mechanisms and whose residual attacks are fewer than either alone would have left them. The fixation on "natural" versus "pharmaceutical" obscures the practical question — which combination produces the lowest attack burden at the lowest total side-effect cost.

Triptan use is the harder case. The condition page covers the rebound issue in detail. The short version: occasional triptans (a few times per month) for severe attacks that get through the protocol are reasonable. Daily or near-daily triptan use produces medication-overuse headache, which is itself a migraine pattern that no amount of protocol work can suppress until the triptan is tapered. A patient on the protocol who is also taking triptans 15+ days per month is in a different problem and needs a clinician-supervised triptan taper before the protocol can be properly judged.

## Tracking Template

Daily tracking through Phase 1 and Phase 2; weekly through Phase 3.

**Daily:**
- Morning oral temperature (post-wake, pre-anything)
- Resting pulse (one minute, seated)
- Hours slept; wake quality 1–5
- Meals — time, composition, did you go >4 hours without food
- Bedtime snack — yes/no, what
- Stress 1–5
- Cycle day (if applicable)
- Any prodrome signal (neck stiffness, food craving, fatigue 6–24h pre-attack)
- Migraine day yes/no; severity 1–5; duration; what aborted

**Weekly:**
- Number of migraine days that week
- Number of prodrome events (whether or not they progressed)
- Trigger candidates noted in the inventory (which foods, which sleep nights, which transitions)
- Cycle-phase tally if cycling

**Monthly:**
- Migraine days per month, trend line
- Severity-weighted score (days × severity)
- Rescue medication use (triptan days, OTC analgesic days)
- Pattern hypothesis update — is the lead pattern holding, or is a different one surfacing

The single most useful tracking output is the migraine-days-per-month trend over three months. A genuine response is a downward trajectory; a flat line means the pattern identification is wrong or compliance is partial.

## Phase 1 — Baseline + Core Stack (Months 1–3)

Aim: get the substrate in place and identify the dominant pattern. No pattern-specific intervention yet beyond the immediate dietary cleanup.

- Week 1: Begin tracking. Eliminate PUFA cooking oils. Start the bedtime snack.
- Week 2: Add 200 mg magnesium glycinate evening. Add 81 mg aspirin daily.
- Week 3: Begin the daily raw carrot. Begin the quart of orange juice daily. Add salt to food deliberately.
- Week 4: First trigger inventory review. Identify lead pattern.
- Weeks 5–8: Tighten compliance on the core stack. Begin to address sleep consistency. Continue tracking.
- Weeks 9–12: Second pattern review. Compare migraine days to baseline. If reduced ≥30%, hold and continue. If unchanged, recheck compliance — most "no response" in Phase 1 is actually partial compliance.

Expect to see prodrome events become catchable (you notice them and intervene before pain) before you see migraine days drop. The interval between prodrome and pain typically lengthens as the substrate improves, which is what makes prodromes catchable.

## Phase 2 — Titrate by Pattern (Months 3–9)

Add the pattern-specific emphasis layer for the lead pattern identified in Phase 1.

- **Menstrual lead:** Begin Progest-E days 14–28, 25 mg titrating up. Run [[estrogen-detox]] in parallel. Address thyroid if morning temperature is low.
- **Hypoglycemic lead:** Shift to four to five meals/snacks per day. Tighten the bedtime snack. Check thyroid markers (TSH, free T3, free T4, reverse T3) and address if hypothyroid. Pre-emptive OJ-and-salt at the historical prodrome window.
- **Serotonin/dietary lead:** Run the four-week elimination trial. Add evening cyproheptadine 0.5 mg, titrating up.
- **Stress-letdown lead:** Identify weekly transition window; defend it deliberately. Add walking on the transition day. Maintain caffeine intake on weekends. Bag breathing for prodromes.
- **Sleep-debt lead:** Run [[sleep-restoration]] in parallel. Sleep becomes a hard infrastructure commitment.
- **Weather/dehydration lead:** Track barometric pressure with a phone app; pre-emptive sugar-and-salt on drop days. Increase daily salt by half a teaspoon. Add a glass of orange juice with salt before any extended outdoor heat exposure.

Track for at least eight weeks before adjusting. Progesterone responses can take two cycles to be clear. Cyproheptadine responses are typically visible in two to four weeks. The dietary elimination is at four weeks.

Re-evaluate at three and six months on the pattern layer. If migraine days are down 70% or more, begin Phase 3. If down 30–70%, hold and consider adding a second pattern's emphasis (most people have a secondary pattern that surfaces once the primary is addressed). If down less than 30%, re-evaluate the pattern hypothesis itself.

## Phase 3 — Maintenance and Lowest Effective Doses (Month 9+)

The goal is the lowest dose that holds the response. The protocol is designed to make itself less necessary over time as the substrate rebuilds.

- **Cyproheptadine** — try off-drug intervals. Most responders find the effect persists for several days after stopping; the intermittent-dosing pattern Peat described becomes the norm.
- **Progesterone** — for menstrual pattern responders, the dose typically stabilizes and may decrease as the underlying estrogen-progesterone ratio normalizes through diet, thyroid, and gut work. Some women maintain a small mid-luteal dose indefinitely; some can drop it entirely after a year.
- **Aspirin** — usually continued at 81 mg indefinitely unless contraindicated. The cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory benefits extend beyond the migraine indication.
- **Magnesium** — continued indefinitely. Modern food supply is magnesium-poor; this is replacement, not pharmacotherapy.
- **Bedtime snack** — for many people it becomes optional on metabolically calm days but stays in the toolkit for stress, illness, or cycle phase as needed.
- **Conventional prophylactic** (if layered in Phase 2) — taper attempt at 12 to 18 months of stable response, in coordination with prescribing clinician. The protocol substrate often allows successful taper at this stage.
- **Dietary triggers** — most people identify two to three personal triggers in Phase 2 and avoid those indefinitely; the others return to the diet without incident.

The maintenance phase is also when the protocol stops being a project and becomes a way of eating, sleeping, and pacing. The tracking can drop to weekly notes or a simple migraine-days-per-month tally. The trigger inventory gets re-run if a new pattern surfaces (life stress changes, perimenopause, a new medication).

## Failure Modes

**Skipped meals.** The most common reason a hypoglycemic-pattern protocol fails. Most people underestimate how often they go more than four hours without food and overestimate how much the morning coffee counts as breakfast. If migraine days are not falling and the pattern was hypoglycemic, audit the meal log for actual eating frequency, not intended frequency.

**Chronic triptan use producing rebound.** A patient using triptans 10–15+ days per month has medication-overuse headache layered on top of migraine. No amount of protocol work will resolve this until the triptan is tapered. The taper itself produces a 4–8 week window of increased headache before the rebound clears. Best done in coordination with a headache-specialist clinician.

**Ignoring sleep debt.** The protocol that fixes diet but leaves a chronic four-to-five-hours-per-night sleep pattern in place will produce a partial response and a frustrated person who concludes the diet work didn't work. Sleep is not optional; it is the substrate the diet is feeding.

**Stopping aspirin abruptly.** The prostaglandin rebound from sudden cessation of daily aspirin can precipitate an attack within 24 to 72 hours. If discontinuing, taper over two weeks (alternate days, then every third day, then stop).

**Trying everything simultaneously.** A patient who starts the bedtime snack, progesterone, cyproheptadine, magnesium, and the dietary elimination on the same Monday cannot tell which lever is working. Sequence the additions weekly. The patient response that informs the protocol is lost when six variables change at once.

**Wrong pattern emphasis.** The most subtle failure mode. A patient with serotonin/dietary lead pattern on a menstrual-pattern emphasis layer will see partial response (the daily core stack helps everyone) and conclude the protocol "kind of works." Re-running the trigger inventory at month three is the corrective.

**Misidentified hypothyroidism.** Many people with apparent hypoglycemic, menstrual, or letdown pattern migraine have an underlying [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid]] substrate that no pattern-specific lever fully resolves. Morning temperature consistently below 97.8°F and pulse below 70 is the signal. Addressing thyroid before judging the rest of the protocol is the path of least frustration.

**Stopping the protocol after the first migraine on it.** A migraine in month two of the protocol is not protocol failure. The substrate work has not yet rebuilt the liver, the gut, the thyroid, the estrogen clearance pathway. Three months is the minimum window for an honest judgment.

## When to Escalate

Most migraines are not medical emergencies. A small subset are. The following warrant same-day medical evaluation regardless of where you are in the protocol:

- **Status migrainosus** — a migraine attack that does not break in 72 hours despite treatment. Requires medical intervention to break the cycle (IV fluids, dihydroergotamine, sometimes brief steroid course). Continuing the protocol alone is not adequate.
- **Aura with new or progressive neurological deficits** — weakness on one side, speech difficulty, vision loss in one eye, confusion. Migraine with aura is common, but new aura features or aura that persists after the headache resolves needs neurological evaluation to rule out stroke or TIA. Migraine with aura also carries an elevated stroke risk profile independent of acute symptoms; clinical follow-up is warranted.
- **Thunderclap headache** — sudden, severe, worst-headache-of-life onset over seconds. Subarachnoid hemorrhage until proven otherwise. ER, not next-day clinic.
- **Sudden change in established migraine pattern** — a person with a 20-year history of monthly menstrual migraines who develops daily attacks of different character, or sudden new aura where none existed before, needs neurological evaluation. Pattern change is the medical signal.
- **Headache with fever, neck stiffness, photophobia in combination** — meningitis screen.
- **Headache after head trauma** — concussion evaluation if not already done.
- **Migraine in pregnancy** — most of the protocol substrate (food, salt, sleep, progesterone, magnesium) is pregnancy-safe and often becomes more effective in pregnancy. Aspirin and cyproheptadine should be cleared with the obstetrician. Triptans are usually avoided in the first trimester.

The protocol does not replace clinical evaluation for any of the above. It is a framework for the much larger population of migraineurs whose attacks are recurrent, established, and amenable to substrate work.
