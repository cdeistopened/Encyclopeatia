# Cold Plunge — Don't Do It

The cold plunge is the wellness practice of the late 2010s and early 2020s — Wim Hof's breath-hold-and-ice-water protocol on the public side, Andrew Huberman's *Huberman Lab Podcast* on the protocol side, a chest freezer in a garage on the consumer side. The pitch is consistent across sources: a 2-to-5-minute immersion at 50–60°F triggers a 2.5× rise in dopamine that persists for hours, activates brown adipose tissue, raises norepinephrine, builds stress tolerance, and improves mood. Ray Peat, who died in 2022, never wrote a newsletter titled *Against the Cold Plunge*. He did not appear to have named Wim Hof or Huberman directly anywhere in the corpus we have indexed. But his framework, built over fifty years on the central claim that *cold is what hypothyroid people already are*, points unambiguously the other direction. This page is the counter-practice in the encyclopedia — the one that names a popular thing and explains the opposing position. The argument is not that cold exposure is unprecedented in human experience; it is that deliberately inducing cold in a population whose dominant metabolic disorder is hypothyroidism aggravates the disorder rather than treating it.

## The popular practice

The protocol, in its widely shared form, is straightforward: 1 to 5 minutes of immersion at 39°F–60°F, ideally first thing in the morning before food and caffeine, several times a week, at least 11 minutes per week in total ("Søberg minimum"). The Wim Hof variant pairs the cold dose with a preceding round of hyperventilation and breath-holds; the standard Huberman variant skips the breathwork and treats cold immersion as a deliberate-stress training tool on its own.

The claims attached to the practice are several. First, the dopamine response — Søberg's University of Copenhagen study measured a 2.5× rise in circulating dopamine after head-out 14°C immersion, with the elevation persisting for hours. Second, the brown-adipose activation — Cypess and colleagues' 2009 *NEJM* paper rediscovered functional brown fat in adult humans, and the literature that followed treated repeated cold dosing as the lever to grow brown-fat depots, raise basal metabolic rate, and improve glucose tolerance. Third, the norepinephrine and stress-tolerance frame — the cold dose is theorized to train the autonomic system to stay composed under arousal. Fourth, the subjective claim of focus, alertness, and mood elevation that anyone who has stood under cold water can confirm short-term.

These are the strongest versions of the case. The page that explains why Peat's framework rejects them is this one.

## Why Peat's framework opposes it

The argument begins from the [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid baseline]]. In Peat's clinical reading, the dominant metabolic disorder in the population that buys cold tubs is the disorder that makes the body unable to tolerate cold. "First, their fingers, toes, and nose will get cold, because adrenalin, or adrenergic sympathetic nervous activity, will increase to keep the brain and heart at a normal temperature, by reducing circulation to the skin and extremities. Despite the temperature-regulating effect of adrenalin, the reduced heat production resulting from decreased T3 will make a person susceptible to hypothermia if the environment is cool" ([[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|*TSH, Temperature, Pulse Rate, and Other Indicators in Hypothyroidism*, 2007]]). Cold hands and feet are, in the [[broda-barnes|Barnes-and-Peat]] diagnostic frame, a presenting sign of low thyroid function. Deliberately inducing the cold state in an organism already running on adrenaline to defend its core temperature asks the catabolic machinery to do more of what it is already doing too much of.

The mechanism of the dopamine and norepinephrine spike is, in Peat's reading, the [[stress|stress cascade]] itself. Cold shock drives adrenaline; adrenaline mobilizes [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] from adipose tissue; in the modern body the released fats are overwhelmingly [[polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]], which then suppress mitochondrial respiration and the conversion of T4 to T3. "When the tissues are saturated with those antithyroid fats, metabolism slows, especially when any stress, such as cold or hunger, increases the concentration of free fatty acids in the blood stream. Stress and hypothyroidism increase the formation of serotonin, which is an important factor in producing the torpor of hibernation, and lowering the body temperature. The polyunsaturated fatty acids themselves directly contribute to the formation of [[serotonin]]" ([[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|*Unsaturated Fatty Acids: Nutritionally Essential or Toxic?*]]). The cascade Huberman frames as "trained eustress" is the cascade Peat traced step by step from adrenaline through free fatty acids through reverse-T3 through serotonin into the torpor reaction of small mammals approaching hibernation. The dopamine pulse is real. So is the catabolic loop running underneath it.

The brown-fat case is, in Peat's framework, real but conditional. Sustained brown-fat thermogenesis requires high circulating T3; the chronic cold dose suppresses T3 conversion through the FFA pathway just described, so the very tissue the protocol is theorized to grow is the tissue the cold dose disables in the population that needs it most. The brown fat that does activate produces heat by uncoupled oxidation — metabolically expensive, and on a PUFA-saturated substrate it generates the spontaneous peroxidation Peat traced as a major contributor to aging. The hibernation literature Peat cited — squirrels that cannot enter hibernation without dietary PUFA, lizards whose body temperature drops several degrees on a PUFA dose — uses the same molecular machinery in reverse: cold and PUFA together produce the metabolic downshift cold and PUFA together produce.

The hormesis-tolerance frame — the claim that deliberate brief stressors train the system upward — is the case the [[hormesis]] page in this vault is built around and need not be repeated here. The short version, in Peat's language: intermittent stress signals are not the same as chronic energy state, the heat shock proteins and norepinephrine and cortisol pulses that hormesis advocates point to as evidence of adaptive benefit are, in his reading, the molecular evidence that the cell has been forced into a survival metabolism it will not fully come back from. "Brief cold exposure drives [[cortisol]] and adrenaline, mobilizes [[free-fatty-acids]] (overwhelmingly [[polyunsaturated-fats|PUFA]] in modern adipose, which then poison mitochondrial respiration), and produces the same thymic atrophy any chronic stressor produces" ([[hormesis|*Hormesis*]] vault entry). The lineage runs back to [[hans-selye|Hans Selye]], whose original cold-exposure experiments in the 1930s — long before any wellness influencer named the practice — produced the alarm reaction, the thymic involution, and the adrenal hypertrophy that became the General Adaptation Syndrome. Cold was a *stressor* in Selye's lab. Peat inherited the frame and never updated it in the direction of the modern wellness reading.

What Peat repeatedly recommended in the slot modern protocols fill with cold is *warmth*. "Increased body temperature improves sleep, especially the deep slow wave sleep. A hot bath, or even warming the feet, has the same effect as thyroid in improving sleep. Salty and sugary foods taken at bedtime, or during the night, help to improve the quality and duration of sleep" ([[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|*Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities*]]). Warm baths, sunlight, warm rooms, warm meals, salt, sugar to feed the [[liver]] and prevent the 3 a.m. adrenaline spike, [[carbon-dioxide|bag breathing]] to dilate vessels in cold extremities — the practice list is a list of supports for the temperature the [[thyroid]] is trying to maintain. Cold is the framework's central problem. Deliberately inducing it makes the central problem worse.

## The exceptions, if any

What would *not* be opposed under this framework: a brief cold splash on the face (the diving-reflex response activates the vagus nerve and lowers heart rate at a dose too small to run the FFA cascade meaningfully); a cold compress on an injury for swelling control; the *contrast* practice of traditional Russian and Finnish bathing culture, where the load-bearing input is the heat that preceded it; stepping into a cool room after vigorous outdoor work in summer.

Peat himself, in the corpus indexed here — 180 newsletters, 253 transcripts, 244 emails, 82 articles, 2 books, 48 newspaper letters — did not appear to have endorsed any cold-immersion practice directly. He did not write against the cold plunge as a named target either. The closest he came was the recurring framing of cold as the *stressor* — the demand on the system, the diagnostic indicator of low thyroid function, the experimental input that produced thymic involution in Selye's rats — never as a therapeutic input. The synthesis here is from the broader cold-stress corpus, not from a direct statement on the Wim Hof or Huberman protocol.

## What to do instead

The practice list this vault recommends in the slot occupied by the cold plunge:

- **Stay warm.** The morning room is warmer than 65°F. The hands and feet are warm to the touch by mid-morning. If they are not, the fix is [[thyroid]], sugar, salt, and food, not exposure training.
- **[[morning-sunlight|Morning sunlight]].** The direct retinal and skin dose of early-day sun raises body temperature, lowers serotonin, supports the [[red-light]] effects on mitochondrial respiration, and is the practice that occupies the same morning-routine slot that wellness culture fills with the cold plunge.
- **[[walking|Walking]].** The only exercise Peat unambiguously endorsed. Concentric, low-intensity, sustained, outdoor, daylight. Raises CO2 production, raises body temperature, and does not run the catabolic cascade that hard interval training does.
- **[[eat-before-bed|Eating before bed]].** The salt-and-sugar snack that prevents the 3 a.m. adrenaline surge — the same surge a cold plunge deliberately induces. The most-recommended practice in the corpus.
- **Warm baths.** The Peat-endorsed bath is hot, not cold, and is paired with salt or magnesium when used as a sleep aid.
- **Warm meals on cold mornings.** Oatmeal, coffee with milk and sugar, eggs, hash browns. Cold cereal on a winter morning is, in this framework, working against the [[thyroid]] the morning meal is meant to support.

## Counter-arguments

The case for deliberate cold has a real research literature, and a complete page has to give it its due.

**Søberg's dopamine data.** The 2.5× dopamine elevation after head-out 14°C immersion is reproducible. Proponents read it as a mood-elevating intervention with hours-long aftereffect. Peat's reading: dopamine is co-released with norepinephrine under stress; the elevation is part of the catecholamine pulse, not a separate signal, and the same elevation can be produced by intense exercise, caffeine, or amphetamine without endorsement following automatically.

**Cypess and the brown-fat literature.** The 2009 *NEJM* paper and the follow-on cold-exposure work (Van Marken Lichtenbelt, Saito, Yoneshiro) demonstrate that repeated cold dosing raises brown-fat activity and modestly improves cold-induced thermogenesis and glucose disposal. Peat's framework does not deny the activation; it disputes the interpretation. The relevant question is whether the activation is the most efficient route to the underlying outcome (metabolic rate, glucose tolerance, body composition) when a thyroid-supportive diet plus warmth gets there without the catabolic cost — and whether the activation is worth running on the PUFA-saturated substrate Peat's framework treats as the source of the problem, not the solution.

**Huberman's deliberate-stress frame.** The argument that brief controllable stressors train the autonomic system has parallels in [[exercise]] physiology that are not contested. Peat's framework distinguishes between activities the well-fed cell *performs and is built by* (skilled work, walking, the concentric muscular efforts of a high-thyroid teenager) and stressors the same cell *survives at the cost of structure* (cold, fasting, exhaustive exercise, sleep deprivation). The biphasic curve in the cold-exposure literature is, in Peat's reading, a curve over baseline metabolic state, not a property of the cold dose.

**The Wim Hof breathing-plus-cold protocol.** Kox et al. (*PNAS*, 2014) showed modulation of inflammatory response to endotoxin in trained practitioners. The hyperventilation half of the protocol does the opposite of what Peat's [[carbon-dioxide|CO2-elevation]] practice is built to do — it lowers CO2 sharply, causing the vasoconstriction and alkalosis the Buteyko lineage documented. The cold half adds the FFA cascade above. The combined effect on inflammatory markers is interesting; long-term metabolic outcomes have not been measured.

**Habituation.** Advocates note the subjective intensity of cold exposure drops with practice and frame it as adaptive training. Peat's framework reads the same observation differently: the diminishing alarm response is the system running out of the [[cortisol]] and adrenaline it had been mounting in defense, and the "tolerance" is the [[learned-helplessness|exhaustion phase]] of Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome arriving on schedule.

## Data gaps

The honest version of this page has to name what neither side knows.

- **Long-term thyroid markers in chronic cold-exposers.** Free T3, reverse T3, TSH, and morning body temperature in cohorts who have practiced 3-to-5-times-weekly cold immersion for five-plus years have not, to our knowledge, been published. The mechanism Peat's framework predicts (chronic T3 suppression via the FFA-PUFA pathway) is testable and untested.
- **Dose-response curves.** Cold-exposure studies cluster at the wellness-protocol dose (1–5 minutes, 40–60°F, 3–5×/week). Below that dose, the question of whether brief cold-water rinses cross the stress threshold at all has not been resolved. Above that dose, the question of where the inflection point sits has not been mapped.
- **Individual variation by baseline metabolic rate.** Peat's framework predicts that the cold dose damages a hypothyroid cell and is absorbed without scar by a high-thyroid cell. The protocol literature treats subjects as fungible. Stratified analyses by baseline temperature, pulse, free T3, and morning cortisol have not been published in this literature.
- **Gender differences.** Women have lower cold tolerance than men at every age, store more body fat, run lower body temperatures, and respond more sharply to thyroid changes. Most cold-plunge popularizers are men; most cold-plunge research subjects are men. The practice has been generalized to women without the framework adjusting for the [[estrogen|estrogen]]-and-[[free-fatty-acids|FFA]] interactions Peat's [[hot-flashes-energy-and-aging|*Hot Flashes*]] essay traced in detail.
- **The brown-fat-and-PUFA interaction.** Whether cold-induced brown-fat thermogenesis on a low-PUFA substrate produces the same lipid-peroxidation cost it does on a modern high-PUFA substrate is the experiment that would settle a large piece of this disagreement. It has not been done.
