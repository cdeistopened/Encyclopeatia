# Eat Before Bed

Of all the dietary recommendations Ray Peat made across forty years of writing and interviews, the bedtime snack is the one he repeated most often. It is the practice he reached for first when callers described 3 a.m. wake-ups, night sweats, nightmares, restless sleep, or the dawn anxiety that the medical literature calls "early-morning insomnia." The mechanism is straightforward and lives in [[blood-sugar]], [[sleep]], and [[stress]]: liver glycogen runs out three to four hours into sleep, [[blood-sugar|blood sugar]] starts to fall, and the body fires [[adrenaline]] and then [[cortisol]] to mobilize glucose from muscle and other tissue protein. The wake-up is not a sleep problem. It is a metabolic emergency the body is solving by cannibalizing itself. The bedtime snack pre-loads the tank so the emergency never starts.

## The Practice

The full practice is small, deliberate, and takes less than five minutes to execute.

**Timing.** 30 to 60 minutes before lying down — not the moment you turn out the light. You want some absorption before sleep onset but no full stomach working against you. If you wake during the night, eat again immediately; do not lie there and try to "get back to sleep" while [[cortisol]] climbs.

**What to eat (pick one):**

1. **Orange juice with salt.** 4 to 6 oz of [[oranges|orange juice]] with a generous pinch of [[salt]]. Fast onset, dairy-free, and the salt blunts the adrenaline curve more than the juice alone. Peat's most-mentioned formula. "If he woke up around 2:00 or 3:00 AM and had a glass of orange juice with a little salt, that might help," he confirmed in a [[2012-08-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hot-flashes-night-sweats-the-relationship-to-stress-aging-pm|2012 Ask the Herb Doctor episode on night sweats]].
2. **Warm [[milk]] with [[honey]].** 8 oz of whole milk with a tablespoon of honey or sugar. The lactose and honey supply glucose and fructose; the fat slows absorption; the calcium suppresses parathyroid hormone. "Having milk at bedtime lowers stress hormones," Peat told Patrick Timpone in a [[2014-01-01-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-patrick-timpone-dr-peat-answers-questions-regarding-health-die|2014 interview]]. This is the default if you don't know where to start.
3. **A small bowl of [[ice-cream|ice cream]].** Peat's literal recommendation more than once, and the most-prescribed version for callers who resist eating "real food" near bed. The combination of fat, protein, and sugar makes it slow-releasing. In a [[2018-06-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-positive-thinking-sleep-and-repair|2018 Ask the Herb Doctor episode]] he recommended "a fairly low-protein, high-carbohydrate supper right before bed, like ice cream or salty chicken broth."
4. **Salty broth or chicken consommé.** "A good chicken consommé with plenty of salt will lower your adrenaline," he told Timpone. Useful when sweet foods feel wrong or you wake in the night and don't want sugar. Add [[gelatin]] for extra calming effect from glycine. Avoid marrow bones — high iron.
5. **Hard cheese plus fruit.** A small portion of cheese with a piece of fruit. Calcium plus a slow sugar curve. Good for people who find milk bloating.

**Quantity.** Small. A snack, not a meal. 100 to 200 calories is enough. The goal is to top off the liver, not to load the stomach. A full meal too close to sleep raises [[insulin]] sharply, which can drive a delayed hypoglycemic dip an hour after sleep onset and wake you anyway.

**The two active ingredients.** Sugar and salt. Peat's clearest statement of the mechanism is from his [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|*Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities* (2009)]] newsletter: "Salty and sugary foods taken at bedtime, or during the night, help to improve the quality and duration of sleep. Both salt and sugar lower the adrenalin level, and both tend to raise the body temperature." Every other variant of the snack is a delivery vehicle for those two.

**Variants by metabolic state.** A [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid]] person with a small liver-glycogen capacity may need the snack every night and an additional middle-of-the-night dose for several weeks before the liver rebuilds. A [[thyroid|metabolically healthy]] person who skipped dinner or had a hard workout may need it only situationally. People recovering from [[anxiety-depression]] or chronic insomnia should treat it as non-negotiable for at least a month before judging. Peat told a 2013 Hashimoto's caller that for predictable wake-ups, setting an alarm 30 minutes *before* the usual wake time and eating preemptively often broke the pattern within a week.

**What NOT to use.** Anything heavy in [[pufa|PUFA]] (nut butters, seed oils, fatty pork, most commercial baked goods) — the free fatty acids that release during digestion block glucose oxidation and defeat the entire purpose. Hard-to-digest starches at full dinner volume — they sit in the gut and ferment, raising [[endotoxin]] and serotonin overnight. High-protein-only snacks (a cup of cottage cheese, a protein shake) — protein alone provokes an insulin surge without enough carbohydrate to ride it out, which fires the same hypoglycemia–adrenaline cascade the snack was supposed to prevent. [[coffee|Caffeine]] is obvious; alcohol is less obvious but reliably wrecks the second half of the night.

## Why It Works

The mechanism is the [[stress|nocturnal stress cascade]] explained in full in [[sleep]] and [[blood-sugar]]. Compressed to its operational core:

1. The brain and heart pull glucose from the bloodstream continuously through the night.
2. The [[liver]] replenishes blood glucose from its glycogen reserve.
3. In most adults this reserve covers three to four hours, sometimes less in [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid]] or estrogen-dominant states.
4. When glycogen runs low, [[adrenaline]] fires to squeeze the last glucose out of the liver. If that isn't enough, [[cortisol]] follows and breaks down muscle protein into amino acids the liver converts to glucose via gluconeogenesis.
5. Both hormones are mentally arousing. The 3 a.m. wake-up with heart racing, anxious thoughts, and cold sweat is the felt experience of that hormonal surge.

Peat described his own version of this discovery in [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|*Solving Some of the Problems of Aging* (1991)]]: "I began to wake up every night, usually between 2 and 4 AM... I got in the habit of drinking milk or orange juice to raise my blood sugar enough to lower my adrenalin (and cortisol) so that I could return to sleep." The snack works in two directions at once. It supplies the substrate the liver was about to run out of, and the salt and sugar both directly suppress the adrenaline that would have fired in compensation.

The longer-term effect is what makes the practice transformative rather than palliative. Each night that the liver is spared the dawn catabolic event, it rebuilds capacity. Peat repeatedly described the timeline: "In a week or so, you can get your liver stocked up with enough glucose to sleep through the night" ([[2012-08-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hot-flashes-night-sweats-the-relationship-to-stress-aging-pm|2012, Ask the Herb Doctor]]). The bedtime snack is training wheels for a [[liver]] that has been chronically depleted. After three to six weeks of consistent use, many people find they no longer need it on most nights — but the option remains for stressful days, late nights, or recovery from illness.

The calcium-and-protein circuit is the second arm. From [[2011-07-04-its-rainmaking-time-it-s-rainmaking-time-life-supporting-substances|*It's Rainmaking Time* (2011)]]: "Having some salty soup or a glass of milk at bedtime helps. Milk (protein and sugar) inhibits the rise of PTH and cortisol. Salty soup (especially with gelatin) helps prevent the rise of aldosterone. This prevents the deterioration of mitochondria during the night." The dairy version of the snack does double duty — glucose for the liver, calcium for [[parathyroid-hormone|PTH]] suppression.

## Tracking Signal

The bedtime snack is one of the few interventions in the Peat corpus that produces a same-night signal. If it is going to work for you, it usually starts working the first night.

**Within 24 hours:**
- Fewer or no 3 a.m. wake-ups.
- If you do wake, you fall back asleep within 15 to 30 minutes (not 60 to 120).
- Warmer feet and hands at sleep onset.
- Less dawn anxiety; fewer vivid stress dreams in the final third of the night.

**Within 1–2 weeks:**
- Morning [[temperature-tracking|temperature]] climbs by 0.2 to 0.5°F.
- Morning pulse stabilizes (less day-to-day variance).
- Reduced or eliminated night sweats.
- Cognitive clarity within an hour of waking, without coffee — the signature of a liver that did not have to run gluconeogenesis overnight.

**Within 1–2 months:**
- Sleep architecture stabilizes; the practice becomes optional on metabolically calm days.
- For women with cyclic insomnia ([[pms]]/[[menopause]]), the late-luteal-phase wake-ups soften or disappear.

**Failure modes — when the signal does not appear:**
- *Snack too large or too late.* A heavy meal 10 minutes before bed disrupts sleep onset through digestive activity. Pull the timing back to 30–60 minutes and reduce volume.
- *PUFA-laden snack.* Almond butter, granola bars, most ice creams with vegetable oil, peanut butter cookies — these defeat the purpose because the [[pufa|polyunsaturated fats]] released during digestion block glucose oxidation overnight. Switch to a clean version (whole milk + honey, or homemade ice cream from cream + sugar + eggs).
- *Pure protein with no carb.* Cottage cheese alone, a protein shake — produces an insulin spike without sustaining glucose. Add fruit, honey, or juice.
- *Severe [[hypothyroidism]].* If the liver cannot store glycogen even with the snack, the wake-ups persist. The snack alone is not sufficient; thyroid status and the broader [[anti-stress-diet]] need attention. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]].
- *High evening caffeine, alcohol, or [[endotoxin|gut irritation]].* The snack cannot overcome a stimulant load already in the system. Address those first.

## Counter-arguments

**Intermittent fasting orthodoxy.** The dominant wellness recommendation of the 2010s and 2020s is to stop eating by 6 p.m., extend the overnight fast as long as possible, and skip breakfast. Peat treated this protocol as the most destructive popular dietary practice of his era and an active driver of the very cortisol, [[insulin]] resistance, and nocturnal anxiety it claims to prevent. The bedtime snack is the most explicit refusal of fasting culture in his framework. The lifespan-extension data from caloric restriction in rodents does not translate to humans, who experience the same metabolic downshift as iatrogenic [[hypothyroidism]] and chronic [[stress]]. See [[intermittent-fasting]] for the full case.

**The "liver detoxes at night" framing.** Several functional-medicine traditions assign each organ a "cleansing window" and treat eating after 7 p.m. as interference. The framing has no specific mechanism behind it. The liver does not need to be empty to do phase I and phase II detoxification — it needs cofactors (glucose, glycine, sulfate, B vitamins), most of which are *supplied* by a small carb-and-protein snack. Peat's [[liver]] page covers this in full.

**Weight-loss orthodoxy.** "Don't eat before bed because you'll store it as fat." The implicit model is that a 100-calorie evening snack is calorically additive in a way the same 100 calories at breakfast would not be. Peat's response was that a snack which prevents the nocturnal cortisol surge also prevents the cortisol-driven [[fat storage]], visceral fat deposition, and muscle catabolism that follow chronic 3 a.m. wake-ups. The bedtime snack is a weight-stabilizing tool, not a weight-gain tool, in his framework. The exception is people who use "bedtime snack" to mean half a pint of Häagen-Dazs or a sleeve of cookies — at which point the issue is portion, not principle.

**Dentists.** Sugar at bedtime is the worst time of day for tooth enamel because saliva production drops during sleep. This is a legitimate trade-off. Rinse the mouth or brush briefly after the snack, or favor the salty-broth variant on nights when you don't want to brush twice. Some Peat-influenced practitioners suggest a small piece of cheese after a sweet snack to neutralize the pH.

**Mainstream sleep medicine.** Standard sleep-hygiene advice tells patients not to eat for two to three hours before bed and to keep the bedroom cool and dark. Peat's framework inverts all three: eat close to bed, keep the bedroom warm enough to maintain foot temperature, and use bright incandescent light in the evening rather than darkness. The specific case against this orthodoxy is laid out in [[sleep]].

## Data Gaps

- **Optimal sugar/protein/fat ratio.** Peat gave qualitative guidance ("low protein, high carbohydrate") but never a clean macronutrient ratio. The 1:1:1 sugar/fat/protein of ice cream works empirically; whether it is optimal versus higher-carb or higher-fat versions is not settled.
- **Individual variation by liver-glycogen capacity.** A trained athlete with a large liver may not need the snack at all. A frail elderly person with hepatic [[insulin]] resistance may need a larger or repeated dose. There is no clinical heuristic for predicting where on the spectrum any individual sits short of trial.
- **Interaction with [[thyroid]] status.** People with frank [[hypothyroidism]] often need the snack to be supplemented by thyroid restoration before sleep fully consolidates. The relative contribution of each is unclear and likely individual.
- **Women versus men.** Peat consistently linked menopausal hot flashes and night sweats to nocturnal hypoglycemia and recommended the same bedtime carbohydrate. Whether the dose, timing, or composition should differ by sex, cycle phase, or [[progesterone]] status is not systematically addressed in the corpus.
- **Children and elderly.** The corpus advice is implicitly adult. Children seem to benefit (Peat occasionally mentioned giving a sleepy child warm milk and honey) but the framework for very young children has not been worked out. Elderly people, who lose the most glycogen capacity and sleep continuity, may need the most aggressive version.
- **Long-term cessation.** Whether the rebuilt glycogen capacity persists indefinitely after the snack is dropped, or whether the snack must remain in the rotation as a stress-state contingency, has not been studied.
