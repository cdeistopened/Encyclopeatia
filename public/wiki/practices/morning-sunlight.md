# Morning Sunlight

Most people who treat morning sunlight as a health practice picked it up from contemporary chronobiology: bright light hits the suprachiasmatic nucleus, suppresses melatonin, fixes sleep onset that evening. Ray Peat would have agreed with the recommendation and disagreed about why it works. His framework is broader. Light regulates cellular energy production through the copper enzyme cytochrome c oxidase ([[red-light]]); UVB on cholesterol-containing skin produces cholecalciferol ([[vitamin-d]]); bright light suppresses the stress hormone of darkness ([[melatonin]]), which Peat treated as anti-metabolic rather than as a benign sleep regulator. Morning sunlight, on his framework, is the daily restoration of a metabolic state that long nights and indoor living have systematically degraded.

## The practice

Get outside within the first hour after waking, after breakfast rather than before. Stay outside long enough to feel warm, not long enough to redden. Do not wear sunglasses. Expose as much skin as the season and modesty allow — face, neck, forearms in winter; chest, back, legs in summer. Do not apply chemical sunscreen for the duration of the dose; if you stay out beyond the comfort window, retreat to shade or cover up rather than block UVB chemically. The order is important: orange juice with salt and a real breakfast precede the light exposure, not the other way around. From [[morning-routine|the morning routine protocol]]: "Light exposure on an empty stomach is itself a cortisol amplifier. Orange juice and food come first, then the walk."

**Timing.** Ten to twenty minutes within the first ninety minutes after waking is the baseline. The window matters less than the consistency. Peat's framework predicts that brief, repeated exposure beats one long bout: from [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|*Using Sunlight to Sustain Life*]], "Frequent short exposures to bright light is almost as valuable as continuous sunlight, and it is less likely to cause skin aging."

**What to expose.** Both eyes and skin, doing different jobs. Eyes mediate the circadian and melatonin-suppression effects through the retinohypothalamic tract and, on Peat's reading, also through direct deep photoreception by structures near the pineal. From [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|*Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis*]]: "Pineal researchers planted a photoelectric cell in a sheep's brain, near the pineal gland... and the device clearly indicated when the animal moved from shade to sunlight." Skin mediates the vitamin D and red-light effects: UVB on cholesterol-containing skin makes [[vitamin-d|cholecalciferol]]; orange-red wavelengths penetrate deeply enough to reach mitochondria in muscle, bone, and marrow.

**Quantities for vitamin D.** UVB drops off rapidly with sun angle. Peat's working numbers from the [[2016-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vitamin-d|November 2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]]: "From 10:00 AM to 2:00 PM in the summer at high latitudes, it only takes 15 or 20 minutes to get enough." Outside that midday window, especially at latitudes above 37 degrees in winter, UVB is too low to make meaningful vitamin D regardless of how long you stand in the sun. The morning practice is therefore better understood as a circadian and red-light intervention; vitamin D needs a separate midday dose in season, or supplementation in winter.

**What to avoid.** Sunglasses block the retinal light that drives the melatonin-suppression effect — Peat's framework treats subtracting pieces of the natural spectrum as net harmful at non-damaging brightness. Window glass blocks most UVB: useful for the red-and-orange-without-UV regime Peat preferred for sensitive skin, useless for vitamin D synthesis, which needs a direct path. Chemical sunscreens with PABA derivatives absorb into the skin and react with light. From [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|*Using Sunlight to Sustain Life*]]: "several recent studies have found that the sun-blockers, which decrease the ordinary skin damage caused by ultraviolet rays, actually increase the risk of developing melanoma, by causing mutations when the cells' chromosomes interact with the sunscreen and the light." If a barrier is needed, Peat preferred reflective minerals (zinc oxide, titanium oxide) and topical antioxidants (coconut oil, vitamin E) over absorbed filters.

**Latitude effects.** Above 37 degrees latitude — roughly the line from San Francisco through Washington D.C. — winter UVB is functionally absent for several months even at solar noon. The introduction to the [[2016-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-vitamin-d|November 2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]] put the public-health version: "In England and many countries north of 37 degrees latitude, there is insufficient sunlight, especially in the winter months, to generate Vitamin D. Rickets was a fairly common condition and still is in some parts of the world." The implication for the practice: morning light is still worth doing for the circadian and red-light effects, but vitamin D in winter at high latitude must come from oral supplementation or food (see [[vitamin-d]]).

**Indoor backup.** When weather or latitude makes morning sun impossible, Peat's substitute was bright incandescent light — several 100-to-250-watt bulbs aimed at the work area for the first hour of the day. From [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|*Using Sunlight to Sustain Life*]]: "If artificial light is bright enough, it is as effective as sunlight at stopping the stress reaction, but people seldom use lights that are bright enough." A 250-watt infrared reflector bulb at three or four feet, with the user facing it for ten to fifteen minutes, is the closest indoor analogue. LED and fluorescent replacements are explicitly inferior in his framework — narrow bands missing the metabolically active orange-to-red region ([[red-light]] for the long version).

## Why it works

The mechanisms below are covered at length on their own pages; this section names the unique interaction that morning exposure (specifically) produces.

**Cytochrome c oxidase reactivation ([[red-light]], [[mitochondria]]).** Cellular respiration runs through a copper enzyme disabled by darkness, blue-only light, PUFA-loaded cardiolipin, and stress. The Leningrad rabbit experiments Peat cited showed mitochondria swelling within minutes of lights-out and exploding by 3 or 4 a.m. — the same hours human mortality peaks. Morning sun delivers the orange-and-red wavelengths that re-oxidize the copper centres. Morning timing does at the front of the day what evening incandescent light does at the back: interrupts the cumulative darkness damage. From [[2012-02-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cancer-treatment|the February 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor*]]: "Animal experiments show the enzyme gets damaged by eight hours of darkness. We shouldn't be in total darkness for longer than it takes to sleep."

**Melatonin suppression ([[melatonin]]).** Mainstream chronobiology treats melatonin as a beneficial sleep regulator and morning light as its appropriate off-switch. Peat treated melatonin as an anti-metabolic stress hormone whose effects include thyroid suppression, thymus atrophy, vasoconstriction, and sex-organ shrinkage. The morning-light suppression is therefore, on his framework, the daily reset of a hormone that should never have been elevated overnight in the first place. From [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|*Using Sunlight to Sustain Life*]]: "It is the hormone of darkness and winter, and is produced in the pineal gland by any stress which increases adrenalin. Adequate sun light suppresses the formation of melatonin." Morning sun continues the suppression that evening incandescent light started.

**Serotonin clearance ([[serotonin]]).** The nocturnal serotonin peak that Peat associated with stroke, depression, and winter sickness is dose-dependent on darkness. From the August 2011 *EastWest Healing* (cited in [[red-light]]): "The peak serotonin in the blood is at midnight in the winter. The enzymes that produce it and detoxify it are under the influence of light. People get sick, tend to have strokes, and depression is exacerbated under the influence of darkness and high serotonin." Light suppresses serotonin production by activating mitochondrial enzymes that route tryptophan metabolism away from the serotonin pathway. Morning light is the daily clearance signal.

**Vitamin D synthesis ([[vitamin-d]]).** UVB on cholesterol-containing skin produces cholecalciferol. Morning sun at high latitudes outside summer produces effectively none of this — the angle is too oblique — but morning sun at lower latitudes and in summer contributes meaningfully. The practice should not be sold primarily on the vitamin D ledger because the timing is wrong for that effect; the vitamin D effect is a midday-window phenomenon. The other effects (circadian, red-light, melatonin) are what justify the morning timing.

**The phase-advance window.** Light in the first hours after waking advances the circadian phase — earlier sleep onset that night. Light late in the day produces phase delay. Peat does not contradict the standard chronobiology; he reframes it. The hormones his framework cares about (cortisol, thyroid, serotonin, melatonin) all key off the same light-dark cycle, and aligning it earlier generally lowers stress hormones and raises metabolic tone. For people running cortisol-dominant patterns with 3 a.m. wakings, the bigger lever is the bedtime snack ([[blood-sugar]], [[sleep]]) — not the light timing.

## Tracking signal

The practice is working when the following stabilize, in roughly this order:

- **Body temperature rises into the day.** Oral temperature taken before getting out of bed should drift upward over weeks toward 36.6–36.8 °C (97.8–98.2 °F). Static or falling temperature suggests the dose is too small, that unaddressed [[hypothyroidism]] is bottlenecking the response, or that the eat-first-then-light sequence is inverted.
- **Pulse stabilizes in the 75–90 bpm range at rest.** Peat used pulse alongside temperature (see [[temperature-pulse-tracking]] in the practices queue). Light alone will not move pulse meaningfully; the combination of food + light + warmth will.
- **Sleep onset drifts earlier within one to two weeks.** If onset has not moved after two weeks of consistent morning light, the bottleneck is elsewhere (bedtime caffeine, late screens, low overnight blood sugar — see [[sleep]]).
- **Mood lifts within the first week.** Standard SAD-light-box literature reports 3–7-day response; Peat's framework predicts the same timing but routes the mechanism through cortisol normalization and serotonin suppression rather than serotonin elevation.
- **Skin produces a light tan over weeks.** Skin that never tans despite consistent exposure is either getting too little UVB (glass, sunscreen, oblique angle) or signalling low skin cholesterol (see [[vitamin-d]]).

**Failure modes.**

- **Too late in the day.** Light after solar noon, especially after 4 p.m., shifts from phase-advance to phase-delay. The practice is morning-specific.
- **Through glass for vitamin D.** Window glass blocks UVB. Sitting near a sunny window helps the red-light and melatonin effects but does nothing for cholecalciferol. Direct exposure required if vitamin D is the goal.
- **Sunburn.** Genuinely rare in dawn light — UV erythema scales with solar angle, and the morning angle is shallow enough that burn risk only appears once the sun is well above the horizon (light skin, low latitude, after 10 a.m.).
- **The PUFA confound.** Peat treated UV damage as conditional on dietary [[polyunsaturated-fats]] in the skin. From [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|*Using Sunlight to Sustain Life*]]: "two groups of rabbits were fed diets containing either corn oil or coconut oil... The animals that ate corn oil developed prematurely wrinkled skin, while the animals that ate coconut oil didn't show any harm." Skin tolerance for sun rises as PUFA falls.
- **The wrong sequence.** Light on an empty stomach raises cortisol. Breakfast first, light after.

## Counter-arguments

**Mainstream dermatology: sun is carcinogenic.** The American Academy of Dermatology recommends broad-spectrum sunscreen daily on all exposed skin. Peat's position was that the cancers most associated with sun exposure (basal cell, squamous cell) are far less lethal than melanoma, that melanoma correlates more strongly with [[estrogen]] and chemical pollutants than with sun, and that the sunscreen-and-shade campaign has produced rising vitamin D deficiency with uncertain net cancer effects. From [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|*Using Sunlight to Sustain Life*]]: "The incidence of melanoma is consistently lower at greater elevations, where ultraviolet light is more intense, than at lower elevations. It is common for melanoma to develop on relatively shaded areas." The public-health consensus remains pro-sunscreen.

**The seed-oil-context argument.** Peat held that UV damage tracks PUFA loading, not UV itself; the right intervention is therefore [[pufa-depletion|reducing dietary PUFA]] rather than blocking the sun. The framework predicts that the sun-damage relationship should be weak in low-PUFA populations and strong in high-PUFA populations. The dermatology literature does not control for dietary fat composition, so the claim remains essentially untested.

**Sunscreen advocacy.** The interaction between absorbed chemical filters and skin biology is real (PABA derivatives are photoreactive; mineral filters are not). How much of the population-level melanoma trend traces to sunscreen versus to the simultaneous rise in PUFA intake versus to better diagnosis is unresolved, but Peat read the rising melanoma rates of the sunscreen era as suggestive of a sunscreen contribution.

**Mainstream chronobiology says the operative wavelength is blue, not red-orange.** The intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells driving circadian entrainment peak around 480 nm. From this position, morning sun's value is its blue content. Peat's framework does not dispute that blue-sensitive ganglion cells exist; it argues the deeper-penetrating red-and-orange wavelengths are doing simultaneous metabolic work the blue-only model does not register. Practical recommendations overlap; mechanistic interpretations diverge.

## Data gaps

- **Optimal minute count by skin type and latitude.** Peat gave the 15–20-minute summer-midday number for vitamin D; he did not give a morning-light dose curve for the circadian and red-light effects. Contemporary practice converges on 10–30 minutes within the first ninety minutes of waking, but this is folk consensus, not measured.
- **Retinal exposure threshold.** How much light into the eye is enough to suppress melatonin on a winter morning when sky brightness is well below summer noon? Peat did not specify.
- **Season-by-latitude dose curve.** UVB drops below the threshold for vitamin D synthesis at high latitudes for several months per year; the integration with the morning-timing window is poorly characterized. Morning sun in winter at 45° latitude is effectively a circadian-and-red-light practice with no meaningful vitamin D contribution.
- **Interaction with red light therapy devices.** Peat did not live to see the proliferation of 660/850 nm LED panels. Whether a daily red-LED-panel session substitutes for morning sun's circadian and metabolic effects — and whether retinal exposure from a panel suppresses melatonin — is essentially unstudied.
- **Pediatric exposure protocols.** Peat rarely specified light protocols for children. Infant eyes are more sensitive to all light; the framework predicts benefit, but specific pediatric dosing is not in the corpus.
- **Sunscreen-PUFA interaction.** The framework's most testable prediction — that sun damage scales with dietary PUFA — has not been directly studied in humans with controlled fat-composition diets.
