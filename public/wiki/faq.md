# FAQ — Bottom-line Peat-framework answers

Each Q is phrased the way a reader actually asks it. Each A is 2–4 sentences: bottom line first, then nuance, then a link to the deep article. The deep article carries the mechanism, sources, and counter-arguments — this page is just the routing layer.

This is reportage of what Ray Peat said and wrote, not medical advice. If you're in an acute crisis (chest pain, suicidal thoughts, can't keep fluids down, sudden neurological symptoms), call a doctor or 911 first and read the encyclopedia second.

## Foods + Diet

### How much sugar should I eat?
As much as your metabolism can actually oxidize — for an active adult that often lands somewhere between 200–400g/day from mixed sources (fruit, OJ, milk, honey, white sugar in coffee). The "sugar causes diabetes" line is the inversion Peat spent decades correcting: in his framework, starch plus PUFA plus a sluggish thyroid causes diabetes, while protective sugars from ripe fruit, OJ, and milk support thyroid function and glucose oxidation. Watch for the signal that you've gone past your oxidation rate — anxiety, racing pulse, cold extremities — and add salt + protein + saturated fat with the next dose. Full picture: [[blood-sugar]] + [[glucose]] + [[sucrose]] + [[fructose]] + [[core-diet-protocol]].

### Is coffee good or bad?
Good, with the right preparation: three to six cups a day, always with sugar and milk or cream, ideally with food, never on an empty stomach. Peat treated coffee as one of the most effective anti-stress tools available — the caffeine plus niacinamide plus magnesium plus polyphenols suppress free fatty acid release and protect the liver — but the orthodox "black coffee, fasted" preparation defeats every one of those mechanisms by mobilizing PUFA from storage. Full picture: [[coffee]] + [[caffeine]] (via [[coffee]]) + [[anti-stress-diet]].

### Should I drink milk if I'm lactose-intolerant?
Usually yes, with workarounds, because most adult lactose intolerance reflects upstream gut and thyroid problems that the protocol resolves over months rather than a fixed genetic ceiling. Try warm milk with salt, small split doses across the day, A2-only milk if available, or a lactase enzyme; raw milk works for some who can't tolerate pasteurized. Persistent intolerance after a few months of metabolic support is rare in Peat's case notes. Full picture: [[milk]] + [[raw-milk]] + [[hypothyroidism]] + [[parathyroid-hormone]].

### How much orange juice is too much?
Peat regularly recommended a quart or more per day for adults under stress — somewhere between 500ml and 1.5L, depending on bodyweight, activity, and how cold you run. The point isn't the OJ specifically but the fructose + potassium + magnesium + small amount of protein it delivers without endotoxin burden. Pulp-free, fresh-squeezed or 100% juice, never from concentrate with added "natural flavors." Full picture: [[oranges]] + [[fructose]] + [[anti-stress-diet]].

### What fats should I actually cook with?
Saturated fats: butter, ghee, coconut oil, tallow, beef suet, cocoa butter. Olive oil is acceptable raw or low-heat in small amounts but is not in the "core" rotation; everything labeled "vegetable oil" (soy, corn, canola, sunflower, safflower, grapeseed) Peat considered metabolic poison whose effects accumulate over decades. The cooking-fat question is downstream of the lifetime-PUFA question. Full picture: [[butter]] + [[coconut-oil]] + [[ghee]] + [[tallow]] + [[fats-ranked]] + [[seed-oils]] + [[pufa]].

### How much salt should I use?
Use it generously, by taste — Peat repeatedly told correspondents to salt food until it tastes good and then a little more. Low-sodium diets raise aldosterone and adrenaline; people who think they're "salt-sensitive" are usually low-thyroid and high-aldosterone, and they feel better when they salt more, not less. Iodized table salt or sea salt both work; avoid the low-sodium potassium-chloride substitutes. Full picture: [[salt]] + [[aldosterone]] + [[hypothyroidism]].

### Are eggs good or bad?
Good — eggs were a daily staple in Peat's recommendations for most adults, valued for cholesterol, choline, vitamin A, and complete protein. The caveat is the polyunsaturated content of the yolk, which rises sharply when hens are fed soy and corn; pastured eggs from low-PUFA-fed birds are better, and two to four per day is a reasonable working range. Don't fear the yolk — fear the seed-oil feed. Full picture: [[eggs]] + [[cholesterol]] + [[pufa]].

### How much meat should I eat?
Peat preferred gelatin-rich cuts (oxtail, shanks, skin, marrow) and organs (especially liver, weekly) over plain muscle meat, because muscle meat alone is high in tryptophan and cysteine relative to glycine. A practical pattern: muscle meat once a day, liver once a week (about 4 oz), bone broth or gelatin daily to balance the amino acid profile. Pork is acceptable but Peat warned about its higher PUFA fraction from modern feed. Full picture: [[beef]] + [[lamb]] + [[liver]] + [[gelatin]] + [[proteins-ranked]].

### Should I eat liver, and how often?
Yes — about 4 oz of beef, lamb, or chicken liver once a week was Peat's standard recommendation. It delivers a uniquely dense package of vitamin A, copper, B12, folate, and other cofactors that supplements imitate poorly. Twice a week is fine for short stretches if you're correcting a deficiency; daily is too much vitamin A for most people. Full picture: [[liver]] + [[vitamin-a]] + [[copper]] + [[vitamin-b12]].

### Are vegetables bad?
No, but most "leafy greens" carry tradeoffs Peat would rank below better options. Well-cooked white potatoes, mushrooms (cooked thoroughly), bamboo shoots, the daily raw carrot salad, and ripe fruit are his favored plant foods; spinach, kale, and other oxalate-heavy greens were used sparingly. The famous carrot salad isn't about the carrot — it's about the indigestible fiber binding and exporting endotoxin and surplus estrogen. Full picture: [[carrot-salad]] + [[potatoes]] + [[mushrooms]] + [[bamboo-shoots]] + [[vegetables-ranked]] + [[endotoxin]].

### What about soy and beans?
Peat was sharply against soy in all forms (tofu, tempeh, soy milk, soy protein isolate, soy oil) for its phytoestrogen, PUFA, and goitrogen load. Other beans (black, pinto, lentils) are less bad but still carry endotoxin-promoting fibers and antinutrients; if used, they should be well-cooked and well-tolerated, not staple. The protein-from-beans idea was something he treated as a marketing artifact, not a metabolic recommendation. Full picture: [[soy]] + [[beans]] + [[endotoxin]].

### Should I avoid gluten and whole grains?
Whole wheat: Peat was skeptical, especially of the daily-bread habit, because of the gluten-endotoxin-prolactin axis plus PUFA from the germ. White rice (well-cooked, with butter and salt) was his preferred starch when starch was used at all. Symptomatic gluten reactions are real, but he treated them as downstream of low thyroid and damaged gut rather than as the prime cause. Full picture: [[whole-wheat]] + [[white-rice]] + [[oats]] + [[carbs-ranked]] + [[endotoxin]].

## Supplements

### Should I take vitamin D, and how much?
Yes, in winter or if you don't get midday sun on bare skin most days; somewhere between 2,000 and 5,000 IU/day is the typical range Peat discussed, with the target serum 25(OH)D around 50–60 ng/mL, not the higher ranges some functional-medicine practitioners push. Higher doses are sometimes used short-term to correct documented deficiency. Take it with the fat-soluble cluster (A, K2) and magnesium for calcium handling. Full picture: [[vitamin-d]] + [[vitamin-a]] + [[vitamin-k]] + [[calcium]].

### Should I take vitamin K2?
Yes, especially if you supplement vitamin D — Peat regarded K2 (MK-4 form, the kind in egg yolk, butter, and liver) as essential for directing calcium into bone and out of soft tissue. Food-first is fine if you eat liver, butter, and egg yolks regularly; supplement 1–5 mg MK-4/day if your diet is thin in those foods. K2 is one of the cheapest insurance policies against the arterial calcification that vitamin D alone can accelerate. Full picture: [[vitamin-k]] + [[vitamin-d]] + [[calcium]].

### What about vitamin E?
Use it carefully and only briefly when needed — mixed tocopherols, never alpha-tocopherol alone, around 100–400 IU as a short course to mop up PUFA peroxidation products, not as a permanent daily habit. Peat treated vitamin E as a rescue tool for someone unwinding a PUFA load, an estrogen flare, or fertility problems, not as a forever supplement. Most commercial vitamin E is also dissolved in soy oil — read the label. Full picture: [[vitamin-e]] + [[pufa]] + [[lipid-peroxidation]].

### Should I supplement magnesium?
Most adults benefit, because food magnesium has dropped over a century of agriculture and stress depletes it fast. Magnesium glycinate, magnesium chloride, or epsom-salt baths are the usual delivery routes; 200–600 mg/day elemental is a reasonable working window. Magnesium oxide is poorly absorbed; if you only get diarrhea, you took too much or used the wrong form. Full picture: [[magnesium]] + [[stress]].

### Should I supplement iron?
Almost never. Peat was emphatic that adults — especially men and post-menopausal women — accumulate iron lifelong, and that excess iron drives oxidative stress, infection susceptibility, and the very "anemia of chronic disease" that gets misread as iron deficiency. Eat liver, eat eggs, and check ferritin before assuming an iron supplement is the answer; a ferritin in the 30–80 ng/mL band is healthier than the 200+ that gets called "fine." Full picture: [[iron]] + [[liver]] + [[copper]].

### What about calcium?
Get it from milk, cheese, and well-cooked greens — Peat strongly preferred dietary calcium to calcium pills, partly because dairy delivers the phosphate ratio and vitamin K2 cofactors that bare calcium carbonate does not. If you can't tolerate dairy, eggshell powder or oyster shell with magnesium and K2 is closer to food. Calcium pills alone (the orthopedic standard) move calcium into arteries, not bone. Full picture: [[calcium]] + [[milk]] + [[cheese]] + [[parathyroid-hormone]].

### Should I take vitamin C?
Useful as a food (orange juice, peppers, kiwi), modestly useful as ascorbic acid powder in doses of 500–2,000 mg/day during illness or pollution exposure, but Peat didn't promote it as a daily megadose the way Linus Pauling did. He preferred to get it with the food matrix (flavonoids, fructose, potassium) that surrounds it in fruit. Watch for additives in supplement forms — corn-derived ascorbic acid is fine, but some "buffered" versions hide soy or sodium loads. Full picture: [[vitamin-c]] + [[oranges]].

### Should I take B vitamins?
Niacinamide (not niacin) is the single B Peat recommended most often — 50–500 mg/day to suppress free fatty acid release, support NAD+, and calm the brain. B1 (thiamine) is useful for many conditions; B6 should be the P5P form and used cautiously; B12 from food (liver, dairy) usually suffices. A "B-50 complex" is rarely what Peat prescribed — it's typically too much B6 and not enough niacinamide. Full picture: [[niacinamide]] + [[vitamin-b12]] + [[liver]].

### What's the deal with methylene blue?
A specific tool, not a daily supplement — Peat discussed methylene blue at 0.5–4 mg doses for mitochondrial support, especially in dementia, depression, or recovery from sepsis. It's an electron donor that bypasses parts of damaged respiratory chain. Don't combine with SSRIs (serotonin syndrome risk), and dose low: more is not better with this molecule. Full picture: [[methylene-blue]] + [[mitochondria]] + [[dementia]].

### Should I take aspirin?
For most adults over 30, Peat thought yes — a baby aspirin or a half regular tablet (about 80–160 mg) with food, daily or near-daily, is one of his most-recommended interventions. Higher therapeutic doses (1–4 g/day, split) for cancer, dementia recovery, or acute illness, always with food and ideally with baking soda or vitamin K to protect the stomach and clotting. The aspirin-causes-stomach-bleeding story is real but small relative to the anti-cancer, anti-estrogen, anti-clotting benefits over decades. Full picture: [[aspirin]] + [[prostaglandins]] + [[estrogen]].

## Hormones

### Should women take progesterone, and how?
Often yes after age 30, almost always after menopause, and frequently for PMS, fibroids, endometriosis, and miscarriage prevention. Peat's preferred delivery was Progest-E (vitamin-E-dissolved natural progesterone) applied to the gums or skin in the luteal phase, scaled to symptoms — anywhere from one drop (about 3 mg) to several drops several times a day in acute situations. The synthetic "progestins" (medroxyprogesterone, drospirenone) are not progesterone and carry different, worse profiles. Full picture: [[progesterone]] + [[progest-e]] + [[pms]] + [[menopause]] + [[fibroids]] + [[endometriosis]].

### Should I take T3 or NDT (natural desiccated thyroid)?
If your symptoms and labs both point to hypothyroidism, yes — but Peat preferred Cynomel (pure T3) or a mix like Cynoplus (T4+T3) over levothyroxine alone, because T3 is the active hormone and many people convert poorly. Typical maintenance doses he discussed: 5–15 mcg T3 split across the day, sometimes alongside 1 grain of NDT or its equivalent. Levothyroxine-only is the orthodox default and is often inadequate. Full picture: [[thyroid]] + [[hypothyroidism]] + [[hypothyroid-recovery]] + [[tsh]].

### Should I take pregnenolone?
Often yes for adults over 35 with stress, brain fog, or hormone-cluster symptoms — 5–30 mg/day was Peat's typical range, taken with food and ideally with vitamin E or fat. Pregnenolone is upstream of both progesterone and DHEA, so it's a gentler nudge than dosing the downstream hormones directly. Buy a brand without soy oil filler. Full picture: [[pregnenolone]] + [[progesterone]] + [[dhea]] + [[stress]].

### Should I take DHEA?
Cautiously, and usually only after age 40 or with documented low levels — 5–15 mg/day is a conservative starting dose; doses sold over-the-counter (25, 50 mg) are too high for most women and many men. DHEA can convert downstream to either testosterone or estrogen depending on aromatase activity, so it's safer to support thyroid + lower aromatase (PUFA depletion, aspirin, progesterone) first, then add DHEA. Full picture: [[dhea]] + [[aromatase]] + [[estrogen]].

### Should I do "natural HRT" instead of conventional HRT?
The Peat framework is not bioidentical-estrogen HRT — it's the opposite. Estrogen unopposed is the metabolic problem; the protocol is progesterone (often), low-dose pregnenolone, thyroid as needed, plus aspirin and food choices that lower endogenous estrogen production. Most "bioidentical HRT" clinics still center estrogen because that's what the marketing is built on; ask whether the protocol leans on progesterone first. Full picture: [[progesterone]] + [[estrogen]] + [[estrogen-detox]] + [[menopause-protocol]].

### Should men supplement testosterone?
Peat's standard answer was no, fix the upstream problems first — low thyroid, high prolactin, high estrogen, high cortisol, low pregnenolone, PUFA load — and testosterone usually rises on its own. Exogenous testosterone shuts down the body's own production and tends to aromatize into estrogen, especially in men with belly fat or PUFA tissues. Reserve TRT for documented hypogonadism that hasn't responded to a year of upstream work. Full picture: [[testosterone]] + [[aromatase]] + [[prolactin]] + [[pufa-depletion]].

## Conditions

### How do I know if I'm hypothyroid if my TSH is "normal"?
Use body temperature and resting pulse first, labs second. Peat's working thresholds: oral temperature should reach 98.6°F by mid-morning, resting pulse 75–85 bpm at rest; cold hands and feet, brittle nails, dry skin, slow gut, thin outer eyebrows, miscarriage, and chronic fatigue all point at hypothyroidism even when TSH sits between 1.0 and 4.0. The "TSH-only" diagnostic standard misses most of the people Peat would have called hypothyroid. Full picture: [[hypothyroidism]] + [[tsh]] + [[temperature-tracking]] + [[broda-barnes]].

### How should I treat depression?
Treat it as a metabolic and inflammatory problem — restore thyroid, restore blood sugar across the day, drop PUFA, raise CO2 (bag breathing, altitude, exercise), use aspirin and progesterone if appropriate, get bright light early, and only then consider serotonin-related drugs (and Peat's view of SSRIs was very negative). Many depressions resolve within 2–6 months on the metabolic protocol without ever touching a psychiatric drug. Full picture: [[anxiety-depression]] + [[anxiety-depression-protocol]] + [[serotonin]] + [[mental-health-cluster]].

### What causes anxiety, and how do I stop it?
Lactate, adrenaline, free fatty acids, serotonin, and estrogen all push the brain toward anxiety; the protocol is to lower each. Concretely: eat every 2–3 hours with sugar plus protein plus salt, do bag-breathing to raise CO2 and lower lactate, avoid caffeine alone, use niacinamide (100 mg), magnesium, and (if appropriate) progesterone, and address the upstream thyroid status. The Xanax-or-meditation framing misses the metabolic substrate entirely. Full picture: [[anxiety-depression]] + [[anxiety-depression-protocol]] + [[lactic-acid]] + [[carbon-dioxide]] + [[bag-breathing]].

### How do I reverse hair loss?
Lower DHT exposure to the scalp by lowering the things upstream of it — drop PUFA, drop estrogen, drop prolactin, raise thyroid, raise progesterone — and apply topical OTC tools (caffeine, progesterone, niacinamide solutions) at the scalp rather than chasing finasteride. The female-pattern and male-pattern story converge on the same metabolic substrate; PFS (post-finasteride syndrome) is one reason Peat hated the drug. Full picture: [[hair-loss]] + [[hair-loss-protocol]] + [[dht]] + [[progesterone]] + [[post-finasteride-syndrome]].

### How do I lose weight on this framework?
By restoring metabolic rate, not by eating less — high thyroid burns more calories at rest, which is what makes long-term fat loss possible without rebound. The "peatmaxxing cut" pattern: keep sugar high (200–300g/day from OJ, fruit, dairy), drop starch and PUFA hard, eat liver weekly, do light walking + occasional concentric resistance, and let the temperature/pulse rise. Caloric deficits without metabolic restoration tank the thyroid and rebound. Full picture: [[peatmaxxing-cut]] + [[hypothyroidism]] + [[randle-cycle]] + [[free-fatty-acids]].

### What about IBS, bloating, and gut issues?
Peat treated gut symptoms as endotoxin + low thyroid + wrong fibers, not as a "microbiome" puzzle to be fixed with probiotics. The standard intervention: daily raw carrot salad (or well-cooked bamboo shoots), well-cooked white rice or potatoes instead of fibrous grains, drop salads-and-beans as staples, use small doses of activated charcoal or cascara if needed, and treat the upstream thyroid. Most IBS resolves on this stack within a few months. Full picture: [[carrot-salad]] + [[endotoxin]] + [[bamboo-shoots]] + [[activated-charcoal]] + [[cascara-sagrada]].

### How do I treat PMS and bad periods?
Progesterone (Progest-E) in the luteal phase, full stop, is the single most-recommended intervention. Underneath that: lower estrogen exposure (PUFA depletion, gelatin, liver, carrot salad, aspirin), raise thyroid status (temperature 98.6°F target), eat sugar through the day to stabilize blood sugar, and the cycle itself usually normalizes. Hormonal birth control suppresses the symptoms by suppressing the cycle, but does nothing for the underlying estrogen excess. Full picture: [[pms]] + [[progesterone]] + [[estrogen-detox]] + [[progest-e]].

### How should I handle menopause?
Menopause symptoms (hot flashes, anxiety, insomnia, bone loss, joint pain) map to estrogen-dominance-without-progesterone, not to "low estrogen" — that's the framing inversion that makes orthodox estrogen-replacement seem necessary. Peat's protocol: Progest-E daily (often 30–100 mg), pregnenolone (10–30 mg), thyroid as needed, vitamin D + K2, plus the standard anti-PUFA, anti-endotoxin diet. Most women feel better in 2–8 weeks. Full picture: [[menopause]] + [[menopause-protocol]] + [[progesterone]] + [[pregnenolone]].

### How do I improve fertility?
Both partners, simultaneously — fertility is a couples problem, not a women's problem. For both: drop PUFA, raise thyroid, eat liver weekly, drop alcohol and weed, raise sugar intake to oxidize properly, fix sleep. For women: add progesterone in the luteal phase, vitamin E, possibly low-dose aspirin. For men: zinc from oysters or shellfish, vitamin E, drop PUFA hard for at least three months before trying. Full picture: [[fertility]] + [[pregnancy-nutrition]] + [[progesterone]] + [[pufa-depletion]] + [[oysters]].

## Practices

### How important is sleep?
Central, but treated as downstream of metabolism rather than as a separate "pillar." Most chronic insomnia resolves when you eat a sugar + salt + protein snack at bedtime (small bowl of ice cream, glass of milk with honey, or cheese with OJ), keep the room cool and dark, get bright light in the morning, and address the underlying thyroid. Melatonin pills Peat regarded with skepticism — they suppress the body's own production and shift the problem. Full picture: [[sleep]] + [[sleep-restoration]] + [[melatonin]] + [[morning-routine]].

### Should I be doing red light therapy?
Useful but secondary — bright incandescent or red LED light (the warm-spectrum 600–700nm band) improves mitochondrial function, lowers TSH, and supports skin and thyroid. A few minutes a day of direct exposure to a 250W reptile-grade incandescent or a dedicated red-light panel is the typical practice. Don't chase the equipment until you've fixed sugar, PUFA, and thyroid — the lighting is the multiplier, not the foundation. Full picture: [[red-light]] + [[mitochondria]] + [[hypothyroidism]].

### What kind of exercise is OK?
Walking, swimming, easy cycling, and short concentric resistance work — exercise that doesn't drive lactate, cortisol, and adrenaline through the roof. Peat was openly skeptical of "cardio for fitness" past the point where breath stays nasal and conversation is possible; he was also skeptical of heavy eccentric work (downhill running, drop-set lifting) because of the muscle damage and inflammation it generates. Move daily, lift occasionally, never to exhaustion. Full picture: [[exercise]] + [[lactic-acid]] + [[stress]].

### Is intermittent fasting bad?
Yes, in Peat's framework — fasting drives free fatty acids out of storage, raises cortisol and adrenaline, suppresses thyroid (T3 drops within 24 hours), and trains the body to run on stress hormones rather than sugar. Eat every 2–4 hours from waking; the breakfast-within-an-hour, snack-before-bed pattern is the opposite of orthodox fasting protocols and is exactly the point. Full picture: [[intermittent-fasting]] + [[free-fatty-acids]] + [[blood-sugar]] + [[cortisol]].

### What about keto and carnivore?
Peat regarded both as stress diets that work short-term by mobilizing cortisol and free fatty acids and fail long-term by tanking thyroid, depleting glycogen, raising aldosterone, and damaging the gut lining. Some people report symptom relief because they've cut out PUFA-laden snack foods and gluten — the relief is real, the framework explaining it is wrong. The Peat fix delivers the same benefits without the metabolic cost by keeping protective sugars in. Full picture: [[randle-cycle]] + [[free-fatty-acids]] + [[blood-sugar]] + [[hypothyroidism]].

## Tests

### What thyroid panel should I ask for?
The full panel: TSH, free T3, free T4, reverse T3, plus thyroid antibodies (TPO, Tg) at least once. TSH alone is the diagnostic that misses most of the people Peat would call hypothyroid; the ratio of free T3 to reverse T3 tells you whether you're actually getting active hormone into the cells. Pair the labs with morning and afternoon oral temperature and resting pulse — labs alone lie often, the combination rarely does. Full picture: [[tsh]] + [[hypothyroidism]] + [[temperature-tracking]] + [[thyroid]].

### Is high cholesterol bad?
No — Peat regarded cholesterol as one of the body's most protective molecules, the substrate for every steroid hormone including progesterone, pregnenolone, DHEA, and testosterone. Total cholesterol of 200–280 mg/dL is closer to optimal than the 150-target the orthodox doctor will push you toward. The exception is when cholesterol is high because thyroid is so low that the body can't convert cholesterol into hormones — the fix there is thyroid, not a statin. Full picture: [[cholesterol]] + [[hypothyroidism]] + [[heart-disease]] + [[pregnenolone]].

### What blood markers should I track?
Beyond the thyroid panel: ferritin (target 30–80, not the 200+ that gets called "normal"), fasting glucose and HbA1c, prolactin (especially men over 40 and women with PMS), DHEA-S, total and free testosterone (men), progesterone-and-estradiol mid-luteal (cycling women), vitamin D 25(OH), CBC with differential, and a basic metabolic panel. Add CRP and homocysteine occasionally to track inflammation. The blood-panel analyzer in the plugin walks through the specifics. Full picture: [[hypothyroidism]] + [[iron]] + [[prolactin]] + [[dhea]] + [[blood-sugar]].

## The framework itself

### Where do I start if I'm completely new to this?
Three foods, three drops, three subtractions, three measurements — that's the first month. Add: a quart of OJ a day, daily raw carrot salad, liver once a week. Drop: all "vegetable oils," all soy products, all whole-wheat staples. Measure: morning oral temperature, resting pulse, weekly bodyweight. Don't add supplements or hormones in month one; let the food and the measurements teach you what's actually happening. Full picture: [[core-diet-protocol]] + [[anti-stress-diet]] + [[carrot-salad]] + [[oranges]] + [[liver]] + [[temperature-tracking]].

### Is this safe?
For most healthy adults, yes — the food protocol is conservative and within the range of every traditional diet that included fruit, dairy, gelatin, and organ meats. Hormones (T3, progesterone, pregnenolone) are real drugs with real interactions and should ideally be supervised; aspirin at therapeutic doses needs the standard "with food, watch for bleeds, stop before surgery" caveats. The riskiest move is usually not starting the framework — it's stopping the framework's existing medications without coordination. Full picture: [[core-diet-protocol]] + [[aspirin]] + [[thyroid]] + [[progesterone]].

### What if my doctor disagrees?
Expect it — Peat's framework inverts orthodox positions on PUFA, sugar, cholesterol, estrogen, iron, TSH cutoffs, and SSRIs. The productive move is to bring the doctor your symptoms, your home temperature and pulse logs, and a full thyroid panel (not just TSH), and let the labs speak. Some doctors will work with you; many won't, and a functional-medicine or integrative practitioner familiar with NDT and Progest-E may be a better fit than fighting your conventional GP. Full picture: [[hypothyroidism]] + [[tsh]] + [[cholesterol]] + [[broda-barnes]].

### What about my kids?
Peat addressed this only obliquely in writing, but the consistent thread is: full-fat dairy (especially milk and cheese), ripe fruit and OJ, gelatin in broths and homemade gummies, eggs, well-cooked white potatoes and rice, liver in small amounts mixed into meatballs or pâté, lots of salt and butter, no seed oils, no soy formula. Children's metabolic rates are higher than adults' — they need calories and sugar, not portion control. Aspirin and Reye's syndrome: avoid aspirin in children under ~12 during a febrile viral illness; outside that window the risk is small and overstated. Full picture: [[milk]] + [[eggs]] + [[gelatin]] + [[fertility]] + [[pregnancy-nutrition]] + [[aspirin]].

### When should I stop self-treating and call a doctor?
Chest pain, sudden severe headache or neurological symptoms, suicidal ideation with a plan, can't keep fluids down for more than a day, signs of clot (one-sided leg swelling and pain, sudden shortness of breath), a baby's fever in the first months of life, or any acute mental break — call urgent care or 911 first and read the encyclopedia second. The Peat framework is a chronic-metabolism framework; it doesn't replace emergency medicine. Full picture: [[anxiety-depression]] + [[heart-disease]] + [[mental-health-cluster]].
