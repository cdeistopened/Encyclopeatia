# Prostaglandins

The prostaglandins are the central hinge between Ray Peat's PUFA argument and his theory of disease. They are the molecules that translate accumulated dietary fat into the immediate, signaling-level events that mainstream medicine recognizes as inflammation, pain, fever, vasoconstriction, vasodilation, platelet aggregation, smooth muscle contraction, uterine cramping, labor, tumor growth, and the local production of estrogen. They are eicosanoids — twenty-carbon, oxygenated derivatives of polyunsaturated fatty acids — and they are made on demand, wherever and whenever a cell is stressed. They do not circulate in the bloodstream the way endocrine hormones do. They act locally, briefly, and at extraordinarily low concentrations, which is why mainstream pharmacology has tended to treat them as benign signaling molecules in need of fine-tuning rather than as the toxic byproducts of a substrate that the body should not contain in the first place.

Peat treated them differently. The framework on this page rests on an inversion: the prostaglandins are not, in his reading, a regulatory system that occasionally misfires. They are stress products. Their normal function is to amplify damage already in progress, and the apparent diversity of their effects — that PGE2 promotes cancer in one tissue and induces labor in another, that PGF2-alpha drives menstrual cramps and PGI2 dilates vessels — reflects the diversity of contexts in which the same underlying chain reaction operates, not the existence of a sophisticated multi-channel hormonal system. The reason aspirin opposes such a wide range of conditions is that all those conditions share the same upstream substrate. Block the COX enzyme, deplete the [[pufa]] that feeds it, or substitute the body's endogenous [[mead-acid]] for the dietary arachidonic acid that should never have entered the system, and the entire downstream cascade collapses together.

The chemistry begins where the [[lipid-peroxidation]] page leaves off. Arachidonic acid sits in cell membrane phospholipids, esterified at the sn-2 position; phospholipase A2, activated by estrogen, stress hormones, and a long list of inflammatory triggers, cleaves it free; cyclooxygenase (COX) inserts molecular oxygen across two of its double bonds to form the unstable prostaglandin endoperoxide PGH2; tissue-specific enzymes then convert PGH2 into the various series-2 prostaglandins (PGE2, PGF2-alpha, PGD2, PGI2/prostacyclin) and thromboxanes (TXA2). The same enzyme acting on dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA) produces the series-1 prostaglandins; acting on EPA, it produces the series-3 prostaglandins. Aspirin inactivates COX by transferring an acetyl group to a serine in the active site, blocking the substrate's entry. In every functional sense relevant to Peat's framework, this is a chain reaction running on a substrate that warm-blooded organisms should not be storing in their tissues, and aspirin is the chemical surrogate for an endogenous protection system ([[mead-acid|Mead acid]] and its non-toxic eicosanoid derivatives) that PUFA accumulation has progressively displaced.

## The Biochemistry: Series 1, 2, and 3

The prostaglandins are grouped into three series according to the number of double bonds in the final molecule, and this number tracks the unsaturation of the parent fatty acid. The series classification is biochemically clean but, in Peat's reading, clinically misleading — it has been used to claim that some prostaglandins are "good" and others "bad," when in his framework none of them are signals the body needs.

The **series-2 prostaglandins**, the dominant ones in modern bodies, are made from [[arachidonic-acid|arachidonic acid]] (20:4 omega-6), itself elongated and desaturated from dietary [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] or absorbed directly from animal foods. The pathway is COX-1 or COX-2 → PGH2 → PGE2, PGF2-alpha, PGD2, PGI2, and TXA2. PGE2 is the universal amplifier, the molecule most often referenced in cancer, fever, pain, labor, and the estrogen-prostaglandin loop. PGF2-alpha drives uterine contraction and is the basis of menstrual cramps and the older clinical induction of labor. PGI2 (prostacyclin) is a vasodilator and antiplatelet agent produced primarily by endothelium. TXA2 is its opposite: a vasoconstrictor and platelet aggregator made by platelets themselves. The balance between PGI2 and TXA2 is the basis of the "low-dose aspirin for cardiovascular prevention" hypothesis, which depends on the empirical observation that COX-1 in platelets is acetylated irreversibly (platelets cannot synthesize new enzyme) while COX in endothelium recovers within hours.

The **series-1 prostaglandins** are made from dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA, 20:3 omega-6), an intermediate in the linoleic-to-arachidonic conversion. PGE1 is the principal series-1 prostaglandin and is, in the mainstream literature, treated as mildly anti-inflammatory, vasodilator, and antiplatelet. The mainstream argument for borage and evening primrose oil supplementation rests on the claim that providing DGLA shifts the balance away from arachidonic-derived series-2 products and toward series-1 products. Peat treated this as an industry-friendly story that obscures the underlying problem: the substrate pool is still a PUFA, and the conversion of DGLA to arachidonic acid by delta-5 desaturase is rapid in most humans, so supplementing the precursor mostly just adds to the arachidonic load.

The **series-3 prostaglandins** are made from eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5 omega-3) by the same COX enzymes that make the series-2 products from arachidonic acid. PGE3 and TXA3 are the principal series-3 molecules, and the marketing case for fish oil rests almost entirely on the claim that they are less inflammatory than their series-2 counterparts. Peat granted the surface-level pharmacology — series-3 prostaglandins do bind their target receptors with lower affinity than the series-2 versions — but argued that the framing misses what fish oil actually does. EPA and DHA, being more unsaturated than arachidonic acid, peroxidize more readily and generate larger quantities of acrolein, malondialdehyde, isoprostanes, and neuroprostanes than the omega-6 chain does (see [[lipid-peroxidation]] for the full chemistry). The "anti-inflammatory" effect of fish oil is not a substitution of better signals for worse ones; it is the immunosuppressive effect of breakdown products that happen to dampen inflammatory markers in the short term while accumulating tissue damage on the four-year timeline of adipose turnover.

This is the unique signal of the prostaglandin page that the [[pufa]] hub only gestures at: in Peat's framework, the series distinction is a red herring. Series-2 PGE2 is the central villain; series-3 PGE3 is a different villain with worse oxidation chemistry; series-1 PGE1 is a transient way station that mostly converts to arachidonic acid anyway. The category that Peat treated as genuinely different is the eicosanoid series derived from [[mead-acid]] (5,8,11-eicosatrienoic acid, the omega-9 PUFA the body synthesizes when no dietary omega-3 or omega-6 is available). Those derivatives — Peat consistently noted from "Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer" onward — "behave very differently from the familiar prostaglandins, as far as they have been compared." The Mead acid eicosanoids are the body's endogenous protective lipid signaling system. The series-1/2/3 prostaglandins are what colonizes the same biochemical niche when dietary PUFA suppresses Mead acid synthesis.

## The Aspirin Mechanism: COX Acetylation

The aspirin story Peat returned to most often was that the standard explanation came late and incomplete. From "[[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer]]": "A few years later, aspirin was found to inactivate the enzyme that forms prostaglandins, by the transfer of the acetyl radical to the enzyme. This became the orthodox 'explanation' for what aspirin does, though it neglected to explain that salicylic acid (lacking the acetyl radical) had been widely known in the previous century for its very useful antiinflammatory actions." The COX-acetylation mechanism is real and elegant: aspirin's acetyl group transfers to serine-530 in COX-1 (and serine-516 in COX-2), and the resulting bulky modification physically blocks the channel through which arachidonic acid would otherwise enter the enzyme's active site. Because aspirin acetylates covalently rather than binding reversibly, a single dose disables every enzyme molecule it reaches, and recovery depends on de novo protein synthesis. Platelets, lacking nuclei, cannot synthesize new COX, which is why low-dose aspirin produces an antiplatelet effect that outlasts the drug's plasma half-life by days.

But the COX story does not explain salicylic acid, willow bark, meadowsweet, or the entire pre-1899 history of salicylate medicine. Salicylic acid has no acetyl group to donate. It does not acetylate anything. Yet it was the basis of the most widely used antiinflammatory drug of the nineteenth century, treated everything from rheumatic fever to gout, and remains the active metabolite of aspirin after the acetyl group has been transferred. Peat's reading is that aspirin's effects are a composite of two molecules — the acetyl group disabling COX and the salicylic acid acting on a broader set of stress systems including [[aromatase]], NF-kB, GAPDH, mitochondrial respiration, and the lipolysis cascade that liberates free fatty acids in the first place. From "[[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer]]": "The polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as linolenic, linoleic, arachidonic, EPA, and DHA, have many directly toxic, antirespiratory actions, apart from the production of the prostaglandins or eicosanoids. Just by preventing the release of these fatty acids, aspirin would have broadly antiinflammatory effects." The COX block is one of several mechanisms by which aspirin reduces the prostaglandin signal; the inhibition of phospholipase-mediated arachidonic acid release is another, and the displacement of arachidonic acid from the active site by the structurally similar salicylic acid is a third.

The Mead acid hypothesis ties these threads together. If the prostaglandins are what the body produces in the absence of an endogenous protective lipid system, and if salicylic acid is structurally similar enough to the Mead acid family to occupy the same COX active sites that arachidonic acid would otherwise enter, then aspirin is functioning as a chemical substitute for the protection that a PUFA-free diet would have provided endogenously. From the "[[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer]]" newsletter: "If salicylic acid and the structurally similar antiinflammatories, local anesthetics, muscle relaxants, expectorants, and antihistamines, act as surrogates for the absent Mead acid family, and thereby act as defenses against all the toxic effects of the unstable fats, it would explain the breadth and apparent coherence of their usefulness." The framework reads aspirin as the pharmacological replacement for a missing physiological protection, not as a chemical antagonist of an essential signaling system.

The COX-1/COX-2 distinction enters here. COX-1 is the constitutive isoform, present in most cells under normal conditions, responsible for housekeeping prostaglandins that maintain gastric mucosal blood flow, renal tubular function, and platelet aggregation at injury sites. COX-2 is the inducible isoform, switched on by inflammatory cytokines, endotoxin, growth factors, oncogenes, and stress hormones, responsible for the prostaglandin surges in inflammation, fever, pain, cancer, and tissue injury. The pharmaceutical pitch for COX-2 selective inhibitors (celecoxib, rofecoxib) was that they would block inflammatory prostaglandins while sparing the gastroprotective ones, eliminating aspirin's stomach side effects without losing the antiinflammatory benefit. Peat's reading is that the pitch was incoherent from the start: COX-2 in vascular endothelium produces prostacyclin (PGI2), which opposes the platelet thromboxane TXA2 made by COX-1; selectively blocking COX-2 therefore shifts the platelet-endothelium balance toward thrombosis, which is why rofecoxib (Vioxx) was eventually withdrawn after producing tens of thousands of excess cardiovascular events. The aspirin newsletter notes the structural giveaway: COX-2 inhibitors are "remarkably similar to the 'designer estrogens'" like tamoxifen, and "rather than protecting the circulatory system as aspirin does," they "damage it." The pharmaceutical industry, in Peat's framing, produced an inferior, more expensive, more dangerous drug to replace a generic whose patent had expired ninety years earlier.

## PGE2 as Universal Amplifier

Of the four series-2 prostaglandins, PGE2 occupies the central position in Peat's account of disease. It is the prostaglandin most directly implicated in cancer growth, in fever, in pain sensitization, in labor induction, in the activation of aromatase, in the recruitment of inflammatory cytokines, in the cachexia of advanced cancer, and in the local immunosuppression that allows tumors to evade detection. The reason PGE2 functions as a universal amplifier is that it acts through four different G-protein-coupled receptors (EP1, EP2, EP3, EP4) coupled to different intracellular cascades, so the same molecule can produce vasodilation in one tissue, contraction in another, immune activation in a third, and immune suppression in a fourth. From "[[regeneration-and-degeneration-types-of-inflammation-change-with-aging|Regeneration and Degeneration]]": "Two prostaglandins, PGE2 and PGF2a, potently produce inflammation in fetal rabbits, but not in adult rabbits." The age-dependent reactivity is itself a Peat signature — prostaglandins do not act on a fixed receptor system; they act on a tissue whose own state determines how the signal is interpreted.

The connection to estrogen is the most consequential of PGE2's downstream effects, and it operates through the [[aromatase]] cascade. In breast tissue, in fat, in bone, in brain, in nearly every tissue Peat examined, PGE2 activates the aromatase enzyme that converts androgens into estrogen. The full mechanism lives on the [[aromatase]] page; the prostaglandin-side framing is in "[[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone]]" (2007): "In the breast, COX-2 converts arachidonic acid into prostaglandins, which activate the enzyme aromatase, that forms estrogen from androgens." From "[[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer]]": "Since the polyunsaturated fats and prostaglandins stimulate the expression of aromatase, the enzyme that synthesizes estrogen, aspirin decreases the production of estrogen. So many of aspirin's effects oppose those of estrogen, it would be tempting to suggest that its 'basic action' is the suppression of estrogen."

The loop runs in both directions. PGE2 activates aromatase, producing estrogen. Estrogen activates phospholipase A2, liberating arachidonic acid from cell membranes. The liberated arachidonic acid feeds COX, producing more PGE2. From "[[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer]]": "Estrogen increases lipid peroxidation, and maintains a chronically high circulating level of free fatty acids, mainly PUFA, activates the phospholipases that release arachidonic acid from cells leading to formation of prostaglandins and isoprostanes, and increases the enzymes that form the inflammation-promoting platelet activating factor (PAF) while suppressing the enzymes that destroy it, and increases a broad range of other inflammatory mediators, interleukins, and NF-kappa B." Estrogen and PGE2 amplify each other indefinitely, and the loop is one of the structural features of the aging organism.

The pain dimension of PGE2 is the basis of aspirin's analgesic action. PGE2 sensitizes peripheral nociceptors to other inflammatory mediators; aspirin reduces the sensitization by reducing the substrate. The fever dimension operates through PGE2 acting on the hypothalamic preoptic area; aspirin's antipyretic effect is downstream of the same COX inhibition. The labor dimension operates through PGE2 (and PGF2-alpha) inducing cervical ripening and uterine contraction; this is why exogenous PGE2 (dinoprostone, brand name Cervidil) is used clinically to induce labor, and conversely why aspirin in late pregnancy can prolong labor and is generally avoided in the third trimester.

The cancer dimension is the one Peat pressed hardest. From "[[prostate-cancer|Prostate Cancer]]": "The prostaglandins were discovered in prostatic fluid, where they occur in significant concentrations. They are so deeply involved with the development of cancers of all sorts that aspirin and other prostaglandin inhibitors should be considered as a basic part of cancer therapy. The prostaglandins have local and systemic effects that promote cancer growth." The local effects include the activation of aromatase to produce a tumor-supporting estrogen environment, the recruitment of inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, the local suppression of natural killer cell activity, the induction of angiogenesis that supplies the tumor with blood vessels, and the stimulation of cell proliferation through EP2 and EP4 receptor signaling. The systemic effects include cachexia, immunosuppression, and the thrombotic complications that often determine cancer mortality. Peat's recommendation that aspirin should be considered a basic part of cancer therapy rests on the integration of all of these effects into a single substrate-blocking intervention.

The connection to [[endotoxin]] is the final piece. Bacterial endotoxin activates COX-2 expression through TLR4 and NF-kB, producing the PGE2 surge that drives the fever, cachexia, and immune dysregulation of sepsis. From "[[endotoxin-stress-depression|Endotoxin, Stress, Depression]]": "Endotoxin and prostaglandins activate these same systems, and progesterone and aspirin are among the protective factors that can oppose those effects... CRH is promoted in the hypothalamus (and in many other tissues) by inflammation, endotoxin, serotonin, interleukins, and prostaglandins, but also by the perception of unavoidable difficulties. Besides cortisol, progesterone and androgens are internal factors that decrease the activity of CRH." The intestinal-bacterial source of PGE2 production is, in Peat's framework, one of the dominant chronic stimuli driving the prostaglandin cascade across the adult lifespan, which is why his anti-prostaglandin protocol overlaps so completely with his anti-endotoxin protocol — the raw carrot salad, calcium-heavy dairy, antibiotics when warranted, and aspirin all reduce the same downstream PGE2 surge.

## Why Series-3 PGs from Fish Oil Are Still Net-Harmful

The mainstream argument for fish oil supplementation rests on a partial truth that Peat treated as more dangerous than a complete falsehood. EPA and DHA do compete with arachidonic acid for COX, and the series-3 prostaglandins produced when COX acts on EPA are less inflammatory than the series-2 products from arachidonic acid. PGE3 binds the EP receptors with lower affinity than PGE2; TXA3 is a weaker platelet aggregator than TXA2. Short-term fish oil supplementation does reduce inflammatory markers in randomized trials. None of this is contested.

What Peat contested was the framing. From "[[the-great-fish-oil-experiment|The Great Fish Oil Experiment]]" (2007): "The prostaglandins are so problematic that their suppression is helpful, whether the inhibition is caused by aspirin or vitamin E, or by fish oil." Fish oil reduces inflammatory prostaglandins not because it provides better signals but because the underlying mechanism is immunosuppression — the same mechanism by which X-ray therapy reduced rheumatoid arthritis symptoms in the 1930s while killing the patients. The breakdown products of EPA and DHA peroxidation include acrolein, the isoprostanes, and the neuroprostanes, all of which produce damage that does not register on the short-term inflammatory markers but accumulates as lipofuscin, cardiolipin remodeling, and the bioenergetic decline catalogued on the [[lipid-peroxidation]] and [[age-pigment]] pages.

The isoprostane and neuroprostane chemistry is the key. From "[[the-great-fish-oil-experiment|The Great Fish Oil Experiment]]": "Polyunsaturated fatty acids, especially those that can be turned into prostaglandins, are widely involved in causing inflammation and vascular leakiness. EPA and DHA don't form ordinary prostaglandins, though the isoprostanes and neuroprostanes they produce during lipid peroxidation behave in many ways like the more common prostaglandins." Isoprostanes are formed nonenzymatically when peroxyl radicals cyclize on arachidonic acid; neuroprostanes are the corresponding DHA products. Both bind prostanoid receptors. Both constrict vessels. Both amplify inflammation. The route is not COX but spontaneous oxidation, and the more unsaturated the substrate, the more easily it occurs. The PUFA-loaded organism that has switched from omega-6 to omega-3 dietary sources has not reduced its eicosanoid burden; it has shifted the burden from a regulated COX pathway to an unregulated peroxidation pathway, and the latter is harder to block.

Peat also pressed the immunosuppression argument categorically. Fish oil's documented short-term reduction in inflammatory markers in rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and ulcerative colitis is the same effect, in his reading, that cortisol produces, that radiation therapy produces, and that the immunosuppressive intravenous lipid emulsions developed for organ-transplant patients produce. Suppressing the immune system reduces the inflammatory signs of disease while increasing the long-term risk of cancer, opportunistic infection, and tissue degeneration. The trials are short; the harms are long. From "[[suitable-fats-unsuitable-fats-issues-in-nutrition|Suitable Fats, Unsuitable Fats]]": "Besides inhibiting enzymes and being converted into prostaglandins, the polyunsaturated fatty acids have direct effects, as signals (or interference with signals) on many tissues." The signal-interference effects of EPA and DHA include the same cytochrome oxidase poisoning, mitochondrial swelling, and respiratory inhibition that arachidonic acid produces, and the cytotoxicity of EPA and its metabolites, Peat noted, is not even fully inhibited by vitamin E.

The clinical implication is uncomfortable. The fish oil supplements marketed as cardioprotective and anti-inflammatory are reducing short-term inflammatory markers by mechanisms that, on a multi-decade timescale, increase the load of peroxidation products in tissues, accelerate the formation of lipofuscin, suppress immune surveillance against developing cancers, and shift the bioenergetic state of mitochondria toward the impaired respiration that Peat identified as the substrate for nearly every degenerative disease. The "anti-inflammatory" effect is real and precisely the problem.

## Clinical Applications: PG-Driven Conditions

The list of conditions in which Peat identified prostaglandin excess as a structural cause runs through nearly the entire spectrum of modern chronic disease. The unifying logic is that any condition with an inflammatory, fibrotic, vascular-permeability, or estrogen-amplification component is, in part, a prostaglandin condition.

**Dysmenorrhea and reproductive pain.** Menstrual cramps are produced by PGF2-alpha (and PGE2) acting on uterine smooth muscle. The clinical observation that NSAIDs are effective against dysmenorrhea is the cleanest single demonstration of the prostaglandin mechanism in routine clinical practice. Peat extended the same logic to other reproductive pain syndromes — endometriosis (where ectopic endometrial tissue produces local prostaglandins and estrogen through aromatase), fibroids (estrogen-driven, prostaglandin-amplified), and PMS more generally.

**Pregnancy and labor.** Exogenous PGE2 (dinoprostone) and the synthetic PGE1 analog misoprostol (Cytotec) are clinically used to induce labor by ripening the cervix and stimulating uterine contraction. Endogenous PGE2 and PGF2-alpha rise sharply at the onset of labor and are part of the cascade that initiates parturition. Peat's reading of this is not that prostaglandins are reproductive signals but that the stressed uterus at term liberates arachidonic acid through phospholipase A2 in patterns determined by hormonal context, and the prostaglandins produced are the same molecules that would amplify any other tissue stress. Aspirin in late pregnancy can prolong labor; low-dose aspirin earlier in pregnancy can improve uterine circulation and fertility outcomes, as Peat noted in "[[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer]]": "Aspirin, like progesterone or vitamin E, can improve fertility, by suppressing a prostaglandin, and improving uterine circulation."

**Rheumatoid arthritis and autoimmunity.** RA is the prototypical inflammatory condition, with synovial PGE2 and PGI2 production driving the pain, swelling, and joint destruction. The COX-2 selective inhibitors were originally developed for RA, and the failure of those drugs to outperform aspirin in long-term outcomes — combined with their cardiovascular toxicity — is one of the cleaner clinical demonstrations that the entire prostaglandin pathway needs to be reduced, not selectively shifted. Peat treated RA as a downstream consequence of endotoxin, PUFA accumulation, and estrogen excess, with the prostaglandin cascade as the executor.

**Cancer (breast, prostate, colon, others).** The PGE2-aromatase-estrogen loop is the central engine in hormone-sensitive cancers. The local prostaglandin environment in tumors recruits suppressor immune cells (Treg, MDSC), promotes angiogenesis through VEGF, and stimulates proliferation through EP2/EP4 signaling. Long-term epidemiological data on aspirin and colon cancer prevention — the only intervention with a consistent randomized-trial signal for reducing colorectal cancer incidence — fits Peat's framework cleanly. Peat's recommendation that high-dose aspirin (6 grams or more daily, with adequate vitamin K) be part of cancer treatment derives from this account. His mention of "inflammatory breast cancer" remitting overnight on aspirin in his own clinical experience is on the [[aspirin]] page.

**Cardiovascular disease.** The platelet TXA2/endothelial PGI2 balance is the basis of low-dose aspirin for cardiovascular prevention. The deeper Peat framing is that atherosclerosis is itself a chronic inflammatory and fibrotic process in which PGE2 promotes the foam-cell formation and TXA2 promotes the thrombotic events that close coronary arteries. Aspirin reduces both. The trade-off is the bleeding risk, which Peat treated as the practical reason for vitamin K co-administration and for finding the lowest effective dose for the indication.

**Fever and acute pain.** The classical NSAID indications. PGE2 acting on the hypothalamic preoptic area raises the temperature set-point; aspirin lowers it by blocking the local synthesis. The cleanest demonstration of how short the prostaglandin signal is — a few hours of aspirin at appropriate dose, and the fever breaks.

**Brain and neurodegenerative conditions.** Prostaglandin synthesis in the substantia nigra contributes to Parkinson's pathology; in the hippocampus and cortex, to the chronic inflammation of Alzheimer's. From Peat's aspirin newsletter and his Parkinson's discussions: aspirin protects neurons by blocking the local PGE2 surge and the acrolein-mediated synuclein cross-linking. The neuroprostane chemistry (DHA peroxidation products that act on prostanoid receptors) is heavy in the Alzheimer brain and is partly the substrate Peat had in mind when he warned against fish oil supplementation in older adults.

**Diabetes and metabolic syndrome.** The PUFA-stress-prostaglandin loop that Peat described in "[[fats-functions-malfunctions|Fats, Functions and Malfunctions]]" runs through PGE2 amplification of insulin resistance and the inflammatory state of obesity. The clinical observation in *Science* (2001) that aspirin reverses obesity- and diet-induced insulin resistance through IKK-beta is one demonstration; the broader pattern is that anything that reduces the prostaglandin load also improves glucose tolerance.

**Shock, sepsis, multi-organ failure.** The acute endotoxin-PGE2 surge is part of what produces the vascular leak, hypotension, and organ dysfunction of septic shock. Peat's reading of multiple organ failure as primarily a PUFA-and-endotoxin phenomenon includes prostaglandins as the immediate downstream executor.

## Counter-arguments

The mainstream framing of prostaglandins is the central counter-narrative this page is written against, and the disagreements deserve to be named directly.

**The lipid mediator regulatory framework.** The dominant mainstream account treats prostaglandins as part of a sophisticated, multi-channel, finely tuned lipid signaling system, on a par with the cytokine network or the neurotransmitter system, and treats the goal of pharmacology as the selective modulation of specific receptors in specific tissues. The discovery of EP1-EP4 receptor subtypes, the characterization of the specialized pro-resolving mediators (resolvins, protectins, maresins, lipoxins) from omega-3 substrates, and the active-resolution concept developed by Charles Serhan's group all push this framework forward. Peat's reading was that the proliferation of receptors and resolvers is exactly what one would expect of an organism whose biochemistry is improvising responses to a substrate it should not contain. The specialized pro-resolving mediators are, in this reading, additional PUFA breakdown products marketed as solutions; whether they represent genuine homeostasis or another iteration of the omega-3 rebrand is the kind of question the framework expects to remain open.

**COX-2 selective inhibitors as targeted therapy.** The argument for the coxibs was that COX-1 produces gastroprotective and antiplatelet prostaglandins, COX-2 produces inflammatory ones, and selective inhibition of COX-2 would deliver aspirin's benefits without aspirin's gastrointestinal toxicity. Vioxx's withdrawal after producing tens of thousands of cardiovascular deaths is the most direct empirical refutation. The deeper mechanistic problem is that COX-2 in vascular endothelium produces prostacyclin, which opposes platelet thromboxane; blocking COX-2 selectively shifts the platelet-endothelium balance toward clotting. Mainstream pharmacology has partly absorbed this lesson; celecoxib remains on the market but with cardiovascular warnings, and the failure of the COX-2 program has shifted resources toward lipoxygenase inhibitors and lipid-resolution agonists. Peat's framework predicts those programs will fail for the same reason: the substrate is the problem, not the enzyme.

**The omega-3 cardiovascular and cognitive consensus.** Large meta-analyses of marine omega-3 supplementation continue to produce mixed results, with the most recent trials (VITAL, ASCEND, REDUCE-IT) generally not showing clear primary-prevention mortality benefits. The DHA-supplementation programs for infant brain development (added to formula since the early 2000s) have not produced the cognitive gains originally promised. The Peat framework's prediction — that benefits would be modest at best and harms would accumulate on a multi-decade timeline — has not been clearly refuted by the available evidence, but neither has it been clearly confirmed at the population level.

**The "essential prostaglandin" argument from animal physiology.** Some mainstream physiologists argue that prostaglandins are essential for renal autoregulation, mucosal blood flow, platelet aggregation at injury sites, and the cardiovascular adjustments of fetal life. The clinical observation that chronic high-dose NSAID use produces renal toxicity and gastric ulceration is sometimes cited as evidence that the prostaglandin system is genuinely necessary. Peat's counter is twofold: first, that the Mead acid eicosanoid system performs the same housekeeping functions in PUFA-deficient organisms with apparently better outcomes; second, that the chronic-NSAID toxicities reflect the displacement of an enzyme system the body has come to depend on under conditions of PUFA load, not the loss of a genuinely irreplaceable signaling network.

**The clinical efficacy of aspirin and other NSAIDs is real.** The mainstream and Peat agree on this. The disagreement is about what aspirin is doing. The COX-inhibition framing treats aspirin as a chemical antagonist of an essential signaling system whose long-term suppression risks gastric, renal, and bleeding complications. The Peat framing treats aspirin as a chemical surrogate for an endogenous protection system that PUFA accumulation has displaced, with the implication that the practical risk of long-term aspirin is small relative to the practical risk of the prostaglandin load it opposes, and that vitamin K co-administration and PUFA depletion address most of the residual concerns.

**Population-level prostaglandin biology.** Some of Peat's claims rest on extrapolations from rabbit, rat, and mouse data that have not been fully reproduced in human studies. The claim that PGE2 and PGF2-alpha produce inflammation in fetal but not adult rabbits, for instance, is a Peat-cited observation that has not been integrated into mainstream developmental biology textbooks. Whether the framework generalizes cleanly across species in every detail or whether some of its predictions hold mainly in the model systems Peat worked with is not fully settled.

## Data gaps

Peat's prostaglandin account is coherent at the level of substrate and downstream effects but has several open margins where the framework's predictions have not been fully tested.

**Dose-response and threshold effects.** The relationship between dietary PUFA intake, tissue arachidonic acid concentration, baseline COX expression, and steady-state PGE2 production has not been characterized at the level of detail that would allow individualized risk prediction. The clinical implication — that lower PUFA intake reduces prostaglandin load proportionally — is broadly supported but the dose-response shape is unknown.

**Mead acid eicosanoid pharmacology.** Peat's framework places considerable weight on the claim that Mead acid derivatives are anti-inflammatory and act on cannabinoid-related receptors, providing the endogenous protection that PUFA suppresses. The biochemistry of those derivatives — sometimes called the "n-9" or "omega-9" eicosanoids — is genuinely under-studied in the mainstream literature. The framework predicts they would emerge as significant if researchers without industry conflicts of interest investigated them, but the evidence base is thin.

**Series-3 vs. series-2 long-term outcomes.** The mainstream and Peat frameworks make different predictions about what happens to a population that shifts from omega-6 to omega-3 PUFA over a generation. The framework predicts that the increased peroxidation load from more-unsaturated substrate will produce net harm despite reduced inflammatory markers; the mainstream framework predicts net benefit. The relevant data — long-term outcomes in populations with sustained high omega-3 intake — are partial and confounded.

**The neuroprostane and isoprostane biology.** The peroxidation-derived eicosanoids are biomarkers in current research but their pharmacology is less developed than the COX-derived prostaglandins. Whether they bind specific receptors with comparable affinity to the canonical prostaglandins, and what their tissue-specific effects are, is partly open. Peat's prediction that they "behave in many ways like the more common prostaglandins" is broadly supported but not exhaustively characterized.

**Pediatric and developmental questions.** Prostaglandins are involved in ductus arteriosus closure, in lung surfactant production, in early developmental signaling. Whether a child raised on a low-PUFA diet would be expected to have impaired versions of those processes, or whether the Mead acid system would supply equivalent function, is not addressed in the corpus.

**The aspirin allergy syndrome.** A subset of patients develop respiratory symptoms (Samter's triad: asthma, nasal polyps, aspirin sensitivity) when exposed to aspirin or other NSAIDs, generally attributed to the diversion of arachidonic acid away from COX and toward the lipoxygenase pathway, producing leukotrienes that drive bronchoconstriction. Peat's framework treats this as evidence that the underlying PUFA load is high enough that blocking COX exposes the lipoxygenase pathway, with the predicted resolution being PUFA depletion rather than aspirin avoidance. The clinical testing of that prediction has not been done in any systematic way.

**Prostaglandin-receptor blockade as an alternative to substrate depletion.** The pharmaceutical industry is actively developing receptor antagonists (EP4 inhibitors for cancer, EP2 modulators for various indications) as a more selective alternative to COX inhibition. Whether selective receptor blockade will produce the same benefit-to-harm profile as substrate depletion, or whether it will reproduce the COX-2 selective failure on a different scale, is the next phase of the empirical question.
