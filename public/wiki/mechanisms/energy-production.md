# Energy Production

"Energy production" is the umbrella term for every chemical pathway by which a cell builds ATP, the universal phosphate-bond currency that drives biosynthesis, contraction, transport, and signaling. In mainstream biochemistry the umbrella covers a half-dozen substrate paths — glycolysis, the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain, beta-oxidation of fatty acids, ketone-body oxidation, amino-acid catabolism, the pentose phosphate shunt — and the textbook treats them as roughly fungible: ATP is ATP, the cell selects whichever substrate is available, and the choice is one of supply rather than quality. Ray Peat used the same term but inverted the emphasis. For Peat, the *path* by which ATP is produced determines whether the cell is being built up or torn down, because each path leaves a different waste product behind, and the waste products do completely different things to the protein-water phase that constitutes the cell. The four major paths produce ATP at very different costs in [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] output, [[lactic-acid|lactate]] accumulation, and reactive-species leak — and those differences, compounded over the decades of an adult life, are the difference between the metabolism of a child and the metabolism of a diabetic.

This article sits as the synthesis hub between [[metabolism]] (the umbrella above it), [[oxidative-metabolism]] (the favored mode), [[glycolysis]] (the failure mode), [[mitochondria]] (the organelle), and [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]] (the gateway enzyme). It does not re-derive the mechanism each of those entries covers — the lipid-peroxidation chain, the cardiolipin substitution at cytochrome oxidase, the Lewis-acid chemistry of CO2, the Randle substrate competition, the Warburg lineage — but maps them onto a single question: where ATP comes from, what comes out with it, and which sources of ATP are compatible with the kind of structure-building energy flow Peat treated as the substrate of health.

## ATP, the energy currency

ATP — adenosine triphosphate — is the molecule the cell uses to do everything mechanical and most things chemical. Three phosphate groups are strung end-to-end off the adenosine, each bonded to the next by a phosphoanhydride linkage that stores roughly seven kilocalories per mole of free energy. The cell hydrolyzes the terminal phosphate to ADP and inorganic phosphate when it needs to drive a reaction; the released free energy couples to muscle contraction, active transport, kinase phosphorylation, ribosomal protein synthesis, and the thousand other reactions that would not proceed spontaneously. ADP is then re-phosphorylated to ATP by one of the energy-producing pathways, and the cycle repeats roughly every few seconds for every ATP molecule in the cell. A resting adult turns over approximately one body weight of ATP per day — not as net synthesis but as the sum of all the hydrolysis-and-rephosphorylation cycles. The total cellular pool at any instant is small; the throughput is enormous.

Peat treated ATP less as a free-floating "energy molecule" and more as a participant in the structured-water/oriented-protein phase the cell maintains. In [[gilbert-ling|Gilbert Ling]]'s framework, which Peat absorbed and extended, ATP binds to cellular proteins and changes their conformation in ways that allow them to organize water and to selectively bind potassium and exclude sodium. "Two kinds of experiment show that the standard ideas about ATP and pumps have to be reconsidered," Peat wrote in [[energy-structure-and-carbon-dioxide-a-realistic-view-of-the-organism|*Energy, Structure, and Carbon Dioxide*]]. "When muscles are stretched, they synthesize ATP; this strongly suggests that its synthesis is a physical process, occurring in an environment in which water is inactive, allowing the reaction to be close to equilibrium. (In the heart, stretching has an anabolic effect.) In another experimental setup, the temperature is measured near the surface of a nerve; when the nerve is stimulated, the temperature falls, suggesting again that energy is being absorbed to make ATP." The implication is that ATP production and the structural state of the cell are not separable. A cell with abundant ATP is not merely fueled; it is *organized* — its proteins held in the conformation that excludes sodium, retains potassium, binds magnesium, and orients structured water. A cell whose ATP is falling is, simultaneously, a cell whose structure is collapsing.

This is also why ATP cannot be assessed simply by counting molecules. The cell maintains its ATP not at a fixed concentration but in a high ratio relative to ADP and AMP. The classical formulation is the **adenylate energy charge** — defined by Daniel Atkinson in 1968 as ([ATP] + ½[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]) — which ranges from 0 (all AMP, no ATP, cellular death) to 1 (all ATP, no phosphate available for further synthesis). Healthy resting cells hold the energy charge near 0.85–0.90, and the regulatory enzymes of glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain are tuned to respond to small deviations from this set point. "The usual definition of a cell's 'energy charge' is given in terms of ATP, ADP, and AMP, with a high energy charge — an abundance of ATP — corresponding to what I am calling the resting state of 'readiness,'" Peat wrote in [[a-biophysical-approach-to-altered-consciousness|*A Biophysical Approach to Altered Consciousness*]]. The energy charge is the cell's master metabolic signal, and the kinases and phosphatases that switch pathways on and off (PDH kinase, AMPK, the Krebs-cycle dehydrogenases, glycogen phosphorylase) all read it directly. A high energy charge says "build, store, repair." A falling energy charge says "burn whatever is available, fast." The substrate the cell turns to when the charge falls — glucose, fatty acid, ketone, amino acid — determines whether the recovery rebuilds structure or erodes it.

## The four major ATP-producing paths

Four pathways do almost all of the metabolic work that matters to Peat's framework. They differ in ATP yield, in CO2 output, in the waste products they leave behind, and in the structural conditions they create in the cell. The classifications below are useful but not exclusive — most cells run several pathways simultaneously, and substrate selection at the cellular level depends on hormonal signaling, oxygen availability, and the energy charge.

### Path 1: glycolysis to lactate

The first and oldest path is fermentation. Glucose enters the cytoplasm, is phosphorylated by hexokinase, isomerized to fructose-6-phosphate, phosphorylated again by phosphofructokinase-1 (one of the [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|three pivotal regulatory points]] Peat tracked), split into two three-carbon fragments, and reduced to two molecules of pyruvate. The reduction step generates NADH from NAD+, and the chain of investment-payoff steps yields a net of two ATP per glucose. If the pyruvate is then reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase — which simultaneously regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue — the cell has its two ATP and produces lactic acid as the only waste. No carbon dioxide is generated. The cytoplasmic redox state stays roughly balanced. The pathway runs in seconds and can sustain high flux as long as the cell can export lactate and import more glucose.

This is the path that Otto Warburg identified as the metabolic signature of cancer, that hypoxic muscle uses during sprint exertion, and that hypothyroid or PUFA-loaded tissue runs as its standing mode rather than its emergency mode. The full mechanism, with its experimental lineage from Warburg and Koch and Szent-Györgyi, is at [[glycolysis]] and [[oxidative-metabolism]]. The output — lactate — is not a neutral waste product. It is, in Peat's framing, the polar opposite of CO2. "Lactate should stand out as a polar opposite of carbon dioxide. I think a basic medical test should be the level of lactate in your blood versus total carbon dioxide. The higher lactate gets, the closer you are to being dead," he told Danny Roddy in *Generative Energy* episode 35. Lactate damages the mitochondria that would otherwise be repairing the respiratory defect; it acidifies the cytoplasm; it carries to the liver, where its conversion back to glucose (the Cori cycle) costs more energy than the glycolysis produced. A cell stuck on this path is producing ATP at a small fraction of theoretical yield and forcing the rest of the body to spend energy cleaning up after it.

### Path 2: glycolysis → PDH → Krebs → electron transport chain

The second path is the one Peat treated as the path of health. Glycolysis still runs in the cytoplasm, generating pyruvate. But instead of being reduced to lactate, the pyruvate is transported into the [[mitochondria]] and handed to [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|pyruvate dehydrogenase]] — the gateway enzyme that decarboxylates pyruvate (releasing one molecule of CO2) and conjugates the remaining two-carbon fragment to coenzyme A as acetyl-CoA. The acetyl-CoA enters the Krebs cycle, where it is oxidized through eight enzymatic steps that strip electrons off the carbon skeleton, hand them to NAD+ and FAD as NADH and FADH2, and release two more molecules of CO2 per acetyl group. The reduced cofactors shuttle the electrons to the inner-membrane respiratory chain (Complexes I–IV), where they flow through ubiquinone, cytochrome c, and finally cytochrome c oxidase, which combines them with molecular oxygen to produce water. The energy released along the electron transport chain pumps protons across the inner membrane, building the gradient that the F1F0 ATP synthase uses to phosphorylate ADP back to ATP.

The full yield per fully oxidized glucose molecule is approximately thirty-six ATP (the exact number depends on the shuttle systems that move cytoplasmic NADH into the matrix and the proton:ATP stoichiometry, but the textbook figure is good enough for the framework). The waste products are water and CO2. The respiratory quotient — moles of CO2 produced per mole of O2 consumed — is approximately 1.0, the highest of any substrate. The full anatomy of this path is at [[mitochondria]] and [[oxidative-metabolism]]; the gateway enzyme is at [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]]; the cofactors (riboflavin in FAD, niacinamide in NAD+, thiamine in TPP, pantothenic acid in CoA, lipoic acid on E2, magnesium throughout) are at their individual entries. Peat's name for this path is "the way the body was designed to produce energy" — the path that yields the most ATP per glucose, leaves the most CO2 behind to protect the proteins it just oxidized, runs the electron transport chain fast enough to minimize the radical leak that PUFA peroxidation feeds on, and produces the metabolic water that hydrogen-bonds into the structured layers around the cellular macromolecules.

### Path 3: beta-oxidation of fatty acids

The third path is the burning of fat. Triglycerides released from adipose stores or arriving from the diet are hydrolyzed to glycerol and free fatty acids; the fatty acids are activated by acyl-CoA synthetase, conjugated to carnitine for transport across the mitochondrial membranes (the rate-limiting step for long-chain fats), and re-formed as acyl-CoA inside the matrix. Beta-oxidation then strips two-carbon acetyl-CoA units off the long carbon chain, one cycle at a time, with each cycle generating one NADH, one FADH2, and one acetyl-CoA. The acetyl-CoA enters the Krebs cycle on the same terms as the acetyl-CoA from pyruvate; the NADH and FADH2 feed the electron transport chain. The ATP yield is high in absolute terms — roughly 106 ATP per 16-carbon palmitate molecule — but the per-oxygen yield is lower than for glucose because fat has fewer oxygen atoms in its starting structure and therefore requires more oxygen to oxidize.

This is where the metabolic accounting diverges from the metabolic *quality* accounting. The respiratory quotient for palmitate is approximately 0.7, well below glucose's 1.0. The cell oxidizing fat consumes more oxygen per mole of CO2 produced; it produces less CO2 per oxygen consumed. In a framework that treats CO2 as the protective Lewis acid that stabilizes the mitochondrial phase and occupies the protein amine groups that aldehydes would otherwise attack, this is a structural defect, not just an accounting variation. "When you oxidize carbohydrate, you get extra carbon dioxide as a byproduct. It's like insurance that you're going to reinforce the oxidizing process. But oxidizing fat — even if it's a safe saturated fat — you don't get such a return of carbon dioxide, and that increases the risk of falling over into the lactate, glycolytic slime metabolism," Peat said in episode 35.

A further wrinkle is that the fatty acid being burned is not metabolically neutral. The cell that has been loaded with [[pufa|polyunsaturated fats]] across its adipose stores will release those PUFA into circulation under stress (adrenaline, cortisol, fasting). Those PUFA do not just contribute their carbons to acetyl-CoA — they peroxidize en route, generate the aldehyde adducts that poison cytochrome oxidase, mobilize arachidonic acid for prostaglandin synthesis, and block glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]. The path is one of degraded structure even when it yields its ATP. Saturated-fat oxidation is closer to neutral — it does not generate the peroxidation products, it substitutes into [[cardiolipin]] in place of PUFA, and Peat treated coconut oil and butter as the safer fuel choices when fat oxidation is required at all. But even saturated fat oxidation does not generate the CO2 yield that glucose oxidation does, and the system Peat treated as optimal was one running predominantly on glucose with fat as a buffer.

### Path 4: ketogenesis (the backup path)

The fourth path is ketone-body production. Under conditions of low glucose availability — extended fasting, very-low-carbohydrate dieting, untreated type 1 diabetes — the liver converts acetyl-CoA from beta-oxidation into the three ketone bodies (acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone) and releases them into the blood. Other tissues, particularly the brain, can take up beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate, convert them back to acetyl-CoA, and oxidize them in the Krebs cycle. The ATP yield per ketone is reasonable (roughly 22 ATP per acetoacetate), and ketogenic-diet advocates have argued that the per-oxygen efficiency at Complex I exceeds glucose's. The path keeps the brain functioning during starvation and has therapeutic application in pediatric epilepsy, certain glycogen-storage disorders, and possibly mitochondrial disease.

Peat treated ketogenesis as a survival path, not a metabolic destination. The state that produces ketones is the same state that produces lactate, free fatty acids, ammonia, and cortisol — the stress metabolism. The body switches to ketones when it cannot get glucose, and the switch is appropriate in genuine starvation; the switch is inappropriate as a lifestyle choice in a person with abundant food. "When you're starving, diabetic, or on a ketogenic diet, you're lowering the oxidative state of the cell. By making you less stable, it's opening up the opportunities for all of the processes of aging: creating collagen, free radical activities such as nitric oxide which can degrade your DNA," he said in episode 35. The CO2 output is even lower than for fatty acid oxidation (the respiratory quotient drops further); the cortisol required to mobilize the fat in the first place is catabolic; the urinary loss of magnesium and calcium that accompanies the obligate ketone-acid excretion erodes mineral status. Ketogenesis-as-rescue and ketogenesis-as-lifestyle are different propositions; the framework's quarrel is with the second, not the first. The full counter-argument from the ketogenic-diet literature is in the [[mitochondria|mitochondria]] page's counter-arguments section.

## Peat's hierarchy: which path produces the right kind of energy

The cells of a healthy young animal can run all four paths and select between them in seconds. The cells of a chronically stressed, PUFA-loaded, hypothyroid adult often cannot. Peat's hierarchy is not a moral ranking of fuels but a structural prediction about what each path produces *besides* ATP, and how those byproducts feed back into the apparatus that produced them.

**Glucose oxidation is the metabolically privileged path** because it produces the most ATP per glucose, the most CO2 per oxygen consumed, the most metabolic water, and the least reactive-oxygen-species leak when the chain is running fast. The CO2 it produces stabilizes the mitochondrial phase that produced the CO2; the water it produces hydrogen-bonds into the structured layers around the macromolecules; the heat it produces (especially when [[mitochondria|uncoupling]] is partial) warms the cell that needs to be warm to run the next cycle. The pathway is autocatalytic in Peat's sense — running it reinforces the conditions under which it can keep running. "Glycolysis is very inefficient for producing usable energy compared to the respiratory metabolism of the mitochondria," Peat wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2 in Wounds, Sickness, and Aging*]], "and when lactate is carried to the liver, its conversion to glucose adds to the energy drain on the organism." A glucose-oxidizing cell is producing more ATP than a glucose-fermenting cell *and* relieving the rest of the body of the cleanup burden the fermenter would impose.

**Fat oxidation is the secondary path**, appropriate as a buffer and necessary in long-duration low-intensity work, but suboptimal as the standing mode. Saturated fat is preferable to polyunsaturated fat because it does not generate the peroxidation products. Medium-chain triglycerides (coconut oil) burn cleanly and partly uncouple the mitochondria. Long-chain saturated fats (palmitate, stearate, butter) substitute into cardiolipin and protect the respiratory chain. Polyunsaturated fats are the path Peat treated as actively pathological — not just lower in CO2 yield but generators of aldehyde adducts on cytochrome oxidase, mobilizers of prostaglandin precursors, blockers of glucose oxidation through the Randle effect, suppressors of thyroid hormone delivery.

**Lactate fermentation is the emergency path**, appropriate in brief exertion or acute hypoxia, pathological as a standing mode. Tumor cells run it; hypothyroid tissue runs it; PUFA-loaded muscle under stress runs it; the [[warburg-effect|Warburg state]] is its terminus. Lactate accumulation damages mitochondria, suppresses CO2 production, acidifies the cytoplasm, and forces the liver into the catabolic Cori cycle. The state is reversible — and most of the [[anti-stress-diet|anti-stress diet]] is aimed at the reversal — but the longer it has run, the more cardiolipin substitution, [[lipid-peroxidation]], and mitochondrial attrition have accumulated, and the slower the recovery.

**Ketogenesis is the survival path**, appropriate in starvation, inappropriate as a lifestyle. The clinical exceptions Peat acknowledged — pediatric epilepsy, certain mitochondrial diseases, refractory neurological conditions — were edge cases where the metabolic cost of running on ketones was less than the cost of seizures or brain injury. The mainstream framing of ketosis as the natural metabolic state of humans rests, in his reading, on a historical fiction. Hunter-gatherer diets included substantial carbohydrate where it was available (honey, fruit, tubers, milk in pastoral cultures); the ketogenic-diet narrative selects evolutionary precedent retroactively.

The hierarchy is not absolute — a healthy person eating mixed meals runs all four paths in proportions that vary by tissue, time of day, and activity. But the *standing mode* in a healthy adult, in Peat's framework, is predominantly glucose oxidation with fat as buffer, with brief glycolytic episodes during exertion that resolve back to oxidation when the demand passes. The standing mode in chronic disease is the opposite: predominantly glycolytic with fat and ketone substitution, with brief glucose-oxidative episodes during the post-meal rest that fail to fully reverse the lactate accumulation that the rest of the day produces.

## Energy charge: the AMP:ADP:ATP ratio as the master signal

The cell does not measure its own ATP concentration directly. It measures the ratio of phosphorylated to dephosphorylated adenine nucleotides — the energy charge — through the catalytic activities of the regulatory enzymes that read it. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), the most-studied of these sensors, is activated by binding AMP in its regulatory subunit; the more AMP there is relative to ATP, the more AMPK phosphorylates its downstream targets, switching off anabolic pathways (fatty acid synthesis, cholesterol synthesis, ribosomal protein synthesis) and switching on catabolic pathways (fatty acid oxidation, glucose uptake, autophagy). The cell is, in effect, sensing how short of ATP it is, and responding by mobilizing every alternative substrate it has access to.

The energy-charge framework predates AMPK. Atkinson's 1968 formulation captured the regulatory logic in a single number: when the charge is high, the cell builds; when the charge falls, the cell burns; when the charge collapses, the cell dies. Peat tracked the framework through the Szent-Györgyi tradition, where the "energy charge" extended beyond adenylate nucleotides to the broader electron-pool of cellular proteins. "According to Szent-Gyorgyi there is probably another sense in which cell proteins can be charged, namely, by addition to their 'electron pool,' which is a capacity of washed proteins to reduce large amounts of glutathione," he wrote in [[a-biophysical-approach-to-altered-consciousness|*A Biophysical Approach to Altered Consciousness*]]. The adenylate energy charge tracks one slice of the cell's reductive capacity; the broader electron-pool concept (the NAD+/NADH ratio in the cytoplasm, the GSH/GSSG ratio for glutathione, the reduced/oxidized state of the cytochromes) captures the full state of metabolic readiness.

The clinically relevant point is that a falling energy charge is the operational meaning of "low energy" in a person's experience. Fatigue, brain fog, anxiety, irritability, the cold extremities of late afternoon, the difficulty initiating tasks that should be easy — all of these are downstream of a cellular adenylate state in which AMP and ADP are rising relative to ATP. The cell is signaling, through every regulatory channel it has, that the substrate is insufficient to maintain its set point. Peat treated this signal as the foreground clinical phenomenon and the laboratory thyroid panel as the background distraction. The intervention is restoring the conditions under which the cell can replenish its ATP from the favored path — adequate sugar, adequate protein, [[thyroid]] supplementation if necessary, [[niacinamide]] to support the NAD+/NADH ratio, [[magnesium]] to stabilize ATP itself, warmth and light to support the respiratory chain. The full intervention list follows.

## What raises energy production

The conditions Peat treated as supporting ATP production share a single operational signature: they activate the path from glucose to CO2, remove the obstacles blocking it, or supply the cofactors the path requires. The one-line versions, with their primary mechanism, are:

- **[[thyroid|Thyroid hormone]] (T3).** The master regulator. T3 increases cytochrome oxidase activity, drives mitochondrial biogenesis, raises the NAD+/NADH ratio, activates [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|pyruvate dehydrogenase]], and partly uncouples the respiratory chain so it runs fast enough to minimize radical leak. Of every intervention in the vault, this is the load-bearing one. Detail at [[thyroid]] and [[mitochondria]].
- **Adequate sugar.** Glucose is the substrate the favored path was built for. Restricting it forces the body into the stress metabolism, mobilizes fatty acids, and slips the system toward lactate. Fruit, juice, honey, milk, and ripe fruit are the canonical sources. Detail at [[glucose]].
- **Adequate protein.** Roughly 80–100g per day in an adult. Ideally with gelatin (for glycine, which is anti-stress) and dairy (for the thyroid-supportive amino acid ratio). Protein deficiency stalls thyroid hormone synthesis and forces tissue catabolism.
- **Magnesium.** ATP is, structurally, a magnesium-binding molecule. The active form of ATP in nearly every cellular reaction is Mg-ATP, not free ATP. Magnesium also stabilizes the respiratory enzymes, is a cofactor in the Krebs-cycle dehydrogenases, and is depleted by chronic stress, by adrenaline, and by the early phase of thyroid restoration. Detail at [[magnesium]].
- **B-vitamins.** Thiamine (B1) as TPP for pyruvate dehydrogenase and the Krebs-cycle decarboxylations. Riboflavin (B2) as FAD for Complex II and beta-oxidation. Niacinamide (B3) as NAD+ for every dehydrogenase. Pantothenic acid (B5) as coenzyme A. Pyridoxine (B6) for transamination. Cobalamin (B12) for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. The respiratory chain runs on B-vitamin cofactors; depletion at any step bottlenecks the whole. Detail at [[niacinamide]] and the individual B-vitamin entries.
- **[[carbon-dioxide|CO2]].** The product that stabilizes the system. Bag-breathing, altitude exposure, sodium bicarbonate, mineral water with bicarbonate. CO2 raises tissue oxygen delivery (the Bohr effect), inhibits lactate formation, occupies the protein amine groups that aldehydes would otherwise attack, and is autocatalytic at the mitochondrial level — a cell producing CO2 produces the substance that maintains the apparatus producing CO2. Detail at [[carbon-dioxide]].
- **Sun and red light.** Red and near-infrared light penetrate tissue and activate cytochrome c oxidase directly. UV exposure raises vitamin D, lowers cortisol, and improves dopamine. Chronic light deficiency is, in Peat's framing, a metabolism-suppressing condition in its own right. Detail at [[red-light]] and [[vitamin-d]].
- **Warmth.** Cold drives stress hormones and shifts fuel selection toward fat and lactate. Warmth allows the system to run on its preferred substrate without the catabolic overhead.
- **Saturated fats.** Coconut oil, butter, beef tallow. Replace PUFA in cardiolipin, do not generate the peroxidation products that poison cytochrome oxidase, partly uncouple the mitochondria when oxidized. Detail at [[saturated-fat]] and [[coconut-oil]].
- **[[aspirin|Aspirin]].** Mild uncoupler. Lowers inflammatory prostaglandins, suppresses serotonin, protects mitochondrial respiration. Detail at [[aspirin]].
- **[[methylene-blue|Methylene blue]].** Substitutes for damaged electron carriers, can accept electrons that would otherwise leak into superoxide production, and bypasses the cytochrome oxidase bottleneck when PUFA peroxidation has poisoned the enzyme. The narrowest mitochondrial intervention in the framework. Detail at [[methylene-blue]].

Peat's compact synthesis from [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]]: "The 'treatment' for intracellular fatigue consists of normalizing thyroid and steroid metabolism, and eating a diet including fruit juice, milk, some eggs or liver, and gelatin, assuring adequate calcium, potassium sodium, and magnesium, and using supplements of niacin-amide, aspirin, and carbon dioxide when necessary." Not a list of boosters but the conditions under which a cell preferentially completes oxidation.

## What lowers energy production

The mirror list matters more in practice because most of these factors are running by default in modern environments. They are detailed in their own vault entries; the compressed cross-references are below.

- Chronic [[stress]] — adrenaline → free fatty acids → Randle block → lactate → cortisol cascade.
- [[pufa|PUFA accumulation]] — cardiolipin substitution at cytochrome oxidase, aldehyde adducts from peroxidation, arachidonic-acid mobilization under stress.
- [[endotoxin|Endotoxin]] — bacterial LPS suppresses mitochondrial respiration, raises cortisol, activates inflammation.
- [[iron]] overload — iron-catalyzed Fenton reactions generate hydroxyl radicals, accelerate [[lipid-peroxidation]].
- [[estrogen]] excess — blocks T4→T3 conversion, raises serotonin, mobilizes fatty acids, raises lactate.
- Undereating — caloric restriction lowers T3 within days, raises cortisol, mobilizes fatty acids.
- Cold — drives adrenaline, suppresses thyroid, shifts fuel selection toward fat and lactate.
- Phosphate excess — slows mitochondrial respiration directly. Detail at [[phosphate]].
- Calcium overload — at the mitochondrial level, calcium absorption is initially protective but becomes inactivating beyond threshold.
- Exercise done badly — eccentric loading or endurance work on top of an already-stressed system.

The pattern is dense link-compression on purpose. Each of these has a canonical entry developing the mechanism; the value of this article is in showing how they all converge on the same lever — the cell's ability to keep its ATP synthesis path running on glucose to CO2, fast enough and clean enough to maintain the energy charge that organizes everything else.

## Tracking signal

The cellular energy state is not directly measurable outside a laboratory. The externally visible proxies Peat treated as reliable were:

- **Pulse.** Resting in the mid-80s, rising appropriately after meals (to ~100 within 30 minutes), returning to baseline within an hour. A pulse stuck in the 60s in an adult is a sign of suppressed oxidation. A pulse spiked into the 90s without exertion and unresponsive to food is a sign of adrenaline holding the system up. Detail at [[temperature-tracking]] and [[metabolic-rate]].
- **Temperature.** Mid-morning oral temperature at or near 98.6°F, having risen from the sleeping low (~97.8°F) after breakfast. A temperature that *falls* after breakfast rather than rising is the [[broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]] / Peat diagnostic for low thyroid held up overnight by cortisol — food removes the need for stress hormones, they drop, and the true low metabolism is revealed.
- **Mood and cognition.** The brain is the most oxidatively demanding tissue in the body. Depressed metabolism shows up in cognition before nearly anywhere else: brain fog, anxiety, the difficulty initiating easy tasks, depression that lifts with sugar and worsens with caffeine on an empty stomach, sleep that does not restore.
- **Stamina.** Not the maximum work the body can do (which depends on stress-hormone reserves) but the work it can do *without* triggering the stress response — the difference between a person who feels good after a walk and a person who needs a nap. Endurance with calm, not with adrenaline.
- **Hair, skin, nails, libido, healing speed, cold tolerance, Achilles reflex relaxation time.** All downstream of the same variable. Detail at [[metabolism]] and [[temperature-tracking]].

The signal is dense. A person with cold extremities, a 70-bpm pulse, a 97.4°F morning temperature, brittle hair, dry skin, low libido, anxious sleep, and the need for caffeine to function in the morning is, in Peat's framing, running on stress hormones because the substrate is insufficient to meet the demand. The intervention is restoring substrate (sugar, protein, salt, thyroid, light, warmth), not adding stressors (cold plunges, fasting, exercise, meditation alone) on top of an already-failing system.

## Counter-arguments

Several mainstream positions push back against this framework directly. They deserve to be named.

**The mitohormesis camp.** Michael Ristow and collaborators have argued that mild mitochondrial stress — transient reactive oxygen species production, brief glycogen depletion, intermittent fasting, cold exposure — induces adaptive responses (antioxidant upregulation, biogenesis, improved insulin sensitivity) that result in net metabolic benefit. The framework is broadly compatible with metabolic flexibility but contradicts the Peatian case against PUFA in detail (the same logic would predict mild peroxidative stress should be beneficial) and against fasting (mild caloric stress should induce protective adaptations). The reconciliation, if there is one, would distinguish transient adaptive ROS from chronic substrate-driven peroxidation — but the distinction is not developed cleanly in either literature, and a substantial fraction of the mainstream metabolism field has accepted the mitohormesis framing without examining it.

**The ketogenic-superior camp.** Veech, D'Agostino, Cunnane, and the broader low-carb research community have argued that ketones are a superior fuel — that they generate more ATP per oxygen at Complex I, reduce ROS production, cross the blood-brain barrier when glucose transport is impaired, spare protein during fasting, and underlie therapeutic responses in epilepsy, type 2 diabetes, and possibly Alzheimer's. Peat addressed the claim dismissively, primarily on the CO2-yield argument (the respiratory quotient of fat oxidation is 0.7 against glucose's 1.0) and the stress-substrate argument (ketosis requires cortisol mobilization of fat, which is catabolic). The ketogenic literature's strongest case — the pediatric epilepsy data, where ketones provide unambiguous seizure control — is real and is conceded as a clinical-rescue use. The disagreement is over whether ketosis-as-lifestyle is the same proposition as ketosis-as-rescue. The mainstream case for keto as a longevity intervention rests on indirect biomarkers (insulin sensitivity, weight loss, lipid panels) rather than direct longevity evidence; the Peatian counter is that the biomarkers are improving on a stress substrate.

**The AMPK/mTOR longevity camp.** A large research tradition — Guarente, Sinclair, Longo, Mattison — has argued that caloric restriction and the AMPK-activating, mTOR-inhibiting interventions that mimic it (rapamycin, metformin, intermittent fasting, resveratrol, NAD-precursor supplementation) extend lifespan in laboratory species. The Peatian framework predicts the opposite — that *high* oxidative throughput, not low, is protective; that AMPK activation is the cell's emergency signal, not its rest signal; that high mTOR with adequate substrate is what builds tissue and what aging adults need more of, not less. The evidence on each side is selective. Calorie-restricted lab rodents do live longer, but they live at cooler housing temperatures (a stress that drives uncoupling and may extend lifespan independently of caloric restriction), eat purified diets free of the inflammatory components, and are selected for survival in environments their wild conspecifics would die in. Calorie restriction in humans (CALERIE) produces some biomarker improvements and no demonstrated longevity benefit. Long-lived populations (Okinawans pre-Westernization, some Mediterranean and Latin American cohorts) do not run particularly low metabolic rates and do consume substantial carbohydrate. The dispute is unresolved at the human level, and both sides cite evidence the other does not engage.

**The "ATP is just ATP" position.** Mainstream bioenergetics treats the four ATP-producing paths as roughly equivalent in their cellular consequences — ATP is the universal currency, the cell selects substrates by availability, and the difference between glucose-derived ATP and fat-derived ATP is one of accounting rather than structure. The Peatian framework treats the paths as producing different *kinds* of cellular states (different CO2:O2 ratios, different lactate accumulation, different ROS leak, different cofactor demand) and therefore different consequences for the protein-water phase that constitutes the cell. The mainstream position is the simpler one and the one the textbook teaches; the Peatian position requires accepting the [[gilbert-ling|Ling]]-derived framework in which the cellular state is not separable from the energy-production path that maintains it.

**The "respiratory quotient doesn't matter" position.** Some metabolic-physiology literature treats RQ as a research curiosity — useful for calorimetric calculations but not biologically load-bearing. The Peatian framework treats it as central, because CO2 is doing work the mainstream framework does not credit. If CO2 is just exhaust, RQ is just an accounting variable. If CO2 is the Lewis acid that stabilizes the mitochondrial phase, then the RQ is reading the protective output of the metabolism directly. The disagreement is upstream of the empirical literature on RQ itself, and is really about what CO2 does in the cell.

## Data gaps

- **Quantitative path-selection in vivo.** Most measurements of substrate utilization are indirect (RQ, isotope tracers, indirect calorimetry) and time-averaged over many minutes. The dynamic, second-by-second selection between glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, beta-oxidation, and ketogenesis in individual cell populations is not well characterized in living humans.
- **The metabolic-water claim.** Peat's emphasis on the structural significance of mitochondrially-produced water (versus imported bulk water) rests on the [[gilbert-ling|Ling]] framework and on selective experimental evidence. Whether this water is materially distinguishable from imported water in its hydration-shell chemistry, on a timescale relevant to cellular function, is unresolved.
- **The energy-charge set point in different tissues.** The 0.85–0.90 figure from Atkinson is a generalization. Different tissues (heart, brain, kidney, liver, muscle) run at different set points and respond differently to perturbation, and the Peat framework does not specify tissue-by-tissue what the "right" energy charge is.
- **Quantitative thresholds for the path hierarchy.** When does fat oxidation transition from acceptable buffer to net-pathological? At what plasma lactate level does the Cori cycle drain become significant? At what ketone level does the cortisol-mobilization cost outweigh the substrate benefit? None are quantified.
- **The cardiolipin turnover question.** How fast does dietary PUFA reduction reverse the substitution of polyunsaturated fatty acids into the mitochondrial cardiolipin pool? Animal data suggest weeks to months for serum and adipose, years for brain. Human kinetics are not well mapped and probably vary substantially with thyroid status and metabolic rate.
- **The methylene-blue dose-response.** The clinical pharmacology of low-dose methylene blue for non-pathological mitochondrial support is not well characterized. Most published data come from psychiatric and infectious-disease contexts at much higher doses.
- **Mitochondrial heteroplasmy and ATP synthesis.** Whether the mtDNA mutation burden that accumulates with aging is the primary driver of energy-production decline, or whether it is downstream of the cardiolipin/PUFA lever, is the same question that surfaces at [[mitochondria|mitochondria]]. The experimental design that would separate the two has not been performed.
- **The supercomplex question.** Contemporary mitochondrial biology emphasizes that Complexes I, III, and IV assemble into supercomplexes that channel electrons more efficiently than free complexes. The Peat framework predates much of this literature; the integration is not worked out.
- **Brown vs white adipose contribution.** The brown-adipose-tissue revival and the discovery of "beige" adipocytes in adult humans has reframed thermogenesis since the bulk of Peat's writing. Whether UCP1-driven uncoupling in brown fat is the major contributor to thyroid-driven heat production, or whether tissue-wide partial uncoupling matters more, is unsettled.
- **Substrate-cycle and futile-cycle energetics.** The fat-synthesis/fatty-acid-oxidation futile cycle Peat invoked for heat production under stress is not the only futile cycle in the cell; the broader contribution of substrate cycling to whole-body metabolic rate is debated and not quantified in the Peat framework.
