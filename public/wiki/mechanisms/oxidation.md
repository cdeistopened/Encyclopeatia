# Oxidation

In Ray Peat's framework, oxidative metabolism is not merely one way cells produce energy—it is the defining characteristic of health. The fundamental distinction in his biology is between oxidative metabolism (efficient, producing carbon dioxide and water) and fermentative metabolism (inefficient, producing [[lactic acid]]). Every condition Peat identified as degenerative—[[cancer]], [[aging]], [[diabetes]], [[inflammation]]—involves a shift away from oxidation. Every protective intervention he recommended—[[thyroid]], [[progesterone]], [[sugar]], [[aspirin]]—supports it.

## Carbon Dioxide: The Marker of Health

Carbon dioxide is the primary product of efficient oxidation, and Peat treated it as a protective substance in its own right. As his newsletter on homeostasis and aging described, "CO2 increases blood perfusion of brain, heart, and muscles"—meaning that the product of healthy metabolism directly supports the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to the organs that need them most.

When oxidation fails, carbon dioxide production drops and [[lactic acid]] rises. This shift is measurable: the respiratory quotient (ratio of CO2 produced to O2 consumed) declines, blood and tissue pH change, and lactic acid accumulates. As Peat discussed in a [[2012-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-acidity-x-alkalinity|2012 episode on acid-base balance]], CO2 has been wrongly cast as a waste product to be eliminated, when it actually performs essential functions: stabilizing cell pH, improving oxygen delivery to tissues (the Bohr effect), and directly opposing excitatory neurotransmitters.

The altitude effect illustrates this. Populations living at moderate altitude—where lower oxygen pressure allows CO2 to be retained rather than lost through hyperventilation—consistently show lower rates of cancer, heart disease, and degenerative disease. As Peat explained in a [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 episode on altitude]], "Thyroid lowers estrogen; estrogen causes hyperventilation and alkalosis." The loss of CO2 through estrogen-driven hyperventilation is itself a cause of disease, not a response to it.

## The Newborn Standard

Peat frequently pointed to newborn babies as the metabolic ideal: they have extremely high oxidative metabolism, minimal stored [[PUFA]], and virtually no lactic acid production. As he noted in a [[2022-11-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2022-kmud|2022 episode]], "Newborn babies have extremely high oxidative metabolism due to the absence of PUFA." This is not a coincidence—the placenta actively filters out polyunsaturated fats, protecting the fetus's oxidative capacity.

The decline from this newborn state of efficient oxidation is what Peat called aging. As PUFA accumulates in tissues, as estrogen rises relative to progesterone, as thyroid function declines—oxidative metabolism erodes, lactic acid increases, and the degenerative diseases of aging appear. The direction of this decline is not inevitable; it reflects the accumulation of specific suppressors of oxidation that can, in principle, be removed.

## What Suppresses Oxidation

**[[PUFA]]** is the primary dietary suppressor. Polyunsaturated fats block oxidative metabolism at multiple points: inhibiting thyroid function, displacing glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]], and generating lipid peroxidation products that damage mitochondrial enzymes.

**[[Estrogen]]** impairs cytochrome oxidase—the terminal enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. As Peat described, estrogen's effects on cellular respiration are "indistinguishable from the effects of X-rays, aging, and oxygen deprivation."

**[[Lactic acid]]** itself, when chronically elevated, suppresses oxidation further. The accumulation becomes self-reinforcing: cells producing lactic acid become increasingly locked into fermentative metabolism.

**Nitric oxide** blocks oxidative enzymes and shifts cells toward reductive metabolism. Despite its promotion as a vasodilator, Peat viewed chronic nitric oxide elevation as toxic to the respiratory machinery.

**Iron excess** catalyzes the oxidation of PUFA, generating free radicals that damage the mitochondrial enzymes required for respiration.

## What Supports Oxidation

**[[Thyroid]] hormone** is the master driver. It activates cytochrome oxidase, increases glucose uptake, supports mitochondrial biogenesis, and elevates metabolic rate. Everything else in Peat's framework builds on adequate thyroid function.

**[[Progesterone]]** supports mitochondrial respiration directly and opposes estrogen's inhibition of cytochrome oxidase.

**[[Sugar]]** (particularly as sucrose or fruit) provides glucose for direct oxidation while preventing the stress-hormone mobilization of stored PUFA. Fructose specifically catalyzes glucose oxidation.

**Carbon dioxide** retention—through adequate oxidative metabolism, avoiding hyperventilation, and moderate altitude—maintains the conditions that support further oxidation.

**[[Aspirin]]** blocks the inflammatory prostaglandins produced from PUFA oxidation, removing one of the signals that suppresses oxidative metabolism.

**Red and near-infrared light** supports cytochrome oxidase activity directly. Peat emphasized that adequate light exposure is not merely cosmetic but metabolically essential.

## Oxidation Versus "Oxidative Stress"

The mainstream concept of "oxidative stress"—where free radical production overwhelms antioxidant defenses—partly captures reality but misses the deeper point. As Peat's newsletter on cancer metabolism explained, the issue is not too much oxidation but too little of the right kind. Healthy oxidative metabolism in mitochondria produces controlled electron flow, generating ATP and CO2. Pathological oxidation occurs when PUFA in cell membranes undergoes uncontrolled lipid peroxidation—a process that has nothing to do with mitochondrial respiration and everything to do with tissue PUFA content.

The antioxidant supplement industry's approach—taking large doses of vitamins C and E to "neutralize free radicals"—misses the point. The solution is not to fight oxidative damage after it occurs but to reduce the substrate (PUFA) that generates it and to support the oxidative metabolism (through thyroid, sugar, and protective steroids) that represents health rather than disease.

Aspirin, flavonoids, and ascorbic acid actually work partly as pro-oxidants in the context of cancer—supporting the oxidative metabolism that cancer cells lack. As the newsletter on cancer metabolism noted, "pro-oxidant effects of ascorbic acid, flavonoids, and aspirin" contribute to their anti-cancer properties.
