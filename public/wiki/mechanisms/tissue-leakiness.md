# Tissue Leakiness

The standard medical picture of a leaky blood vessel is local and acute: a wasp sting, a bee bite, the swelling around a sprained ankle. Histamine is released by mast cells, the endothelial cells of the post-capillary venules contract and pull apart at their junctions, plasma proteins escape into the tissue, swelling and redness follow, the inflammation resolves over hours or days, and the textbooks move on. Ray Peat treated the same physiology as one of the most consequential and most generalized in biology — the mechanism by which acute inflammation becomes chronic disease, by which chronic disease becomes aging, by which aging becomes the cancer field, and by which all four converge on a single therapeutic target. "The prevention of inappropriate leakiness can protect against the degenerative processes, and against aging itself, which is, among other things, a state of generalized leakiness," he wrote in *[[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer]]* (2006). The sentence is the thesis of this page.

The unifying claim has three parts. First, the barrier between blood and tissue, between cell and cell, and between cytoplasm and extracellular space is held in place by *energy*. Endothelial cells with abundant ATP and intracellular CO2 retain their cargo and stay sealed to their neighbors; endothelial cells whose respiration has been knocked down by hypoxia, lactate accumulation, prostaglandin signaling, or estrogen contract, retract, and leak. Second, the mediators that produce acute leakiness — histamine, serotonin, bradykinin, prostaglandins, platelet-activating factor, endotoxin — are the same mediators that produce chronic leakiness when their levels stay elevated for years rather than minutes. Third, the same anti-permeability interventions that work on the acute event work on the chronic state: aspirin, thyroid, progesterone, antiserotonin drugs, vitamin K, low-PUFA diet, CO2 elevation. The pharmacology is unified because the mechanism is unified.

This page assumes the inflammatory mediator cascade covered at [[inflammation]], the fibrotic terminus at [[fibrosis]], the cancer field at [[cancer]], and the aging integration at [[aging]]. What follows is the leakiness-specific layer: what the barrier is and how it is held, what causes acute and chronic leakiness, how leakiness underwrites metastasis and edema and the visible features of vascular aging, and what Peat's anti-permeability toolkit does at each node.

## What "leakiness" means physiologically

The functional barrier between blood and tissue is layered. The luminal surface of the endothelial cell carries the glycocalyx, a fuzzy proteoglycan coat that excludes large proteins by charge and size. The endothelial cells themselves are held to each other by tight junctions (zonula occludens), adherens junctions (VE-cadherin), and gap junctions, and they sit on a basement membrane composed of laminin, collagen IV, and heparan sulfate proteoglycans. Pericytes wrap the capillary at intervals; smooth muscle cells take over at the arteriole and venule level. The cells also actively pump water and solutes through transcellular routes — caveolae, fenestrae in some organs, vesicular shuttles — that are normally tightly regulated.

Peat's preferred handle on this entire apparatus was the fibrin layer that lines the luminal surface. "A thin layer of fibrin lining blood vessels provides a filtering barrier that helps to strengthen the wall and prevent other proteins from leaking out of the vessels, and it participates in repair processes when the blood vessel is broken." Fibrin is the polymerized form of fibrinogen; the same protein that closes a wound is, in its surface-coating form, part of the everyday filtration apparatus. Its turnover is rapid — fibrinogen synthesis is up-regulated by every inflammatory stress — and its breakdown by plasmin generates the fibrin degradation products that themselves stimulate inflammatory mediator release. "Fibrin is an essential structural and functional part of the organism, but when it escapes from the bloodstream it participates in the degenerative processes of inflammation, fibrosis, and tumor formation. (Its fragments stimulate secretion of inflammatory mediators: Hamaguchi et al., 1991.)"

What "leaks" when the barrier fails ranges from water (the easiest to lose) to small solutes (sodium, potassium, glucose) to plasma proteins (albumin first, then larger globulins, eventually fibrinogen) to whole blood cells (in late-stage capillary failure or hemorrhage). The clinical signal of each escalation differs. Light scattered by escaped fibrin produces the "flare" visible in the aqueous compartment of the eye when stress raises ocular permeability — a test Peat noted as one of the simplest demonstrations of the phenomenon. Albumin in the urine (microalbuminuria) is the early signal of glomerular leakiness in diabetes and hypertension; serum enzymes in the bloodstream — troponin from heart muscle, transaminases from liver, CK from skeletal muscle, PSA from prostate — are the signals that organs are leaking their cellular contents into the circulation. "The leakage of enzymes out of cells and into the blood stream is recognized medically as evidence of damage to the organ that is losing them," Peat wrote. "But often the reason for the leakage isn't understood."

The chemistry of barrier failure operates at two scales. At the cell-junction scale, contraction of the endothelial cytoskeleton (driven by myosin light-chain phosphorylation in response to histamine, thrombin, bradykinin, and prostaglandins) physically pulls the junctional complexes apart and opens transient gaps of 0.1-1 micrometer. At the membrane-and-cytoplasm scale, energetic depletion shifts the structure of cell water itself: "When cells' energy is depleted, water and various dissolved molecules are allowed to move into the cells, out of the cells, and through or around cells inappropriately. The weakened cells can even permit whole bacteria and similar particles to pass into and out of the blood stream more easily." The acute mechanism is junctional retraction; the chronic mechanism is bulk cell-water disorganization. The two compound when both run together.

## What causes acute leakiness

The acute permeability response is mediated by a handful of signaling molecules with overlapping receptors and a common downstream actuator — endothelial myosin light-chain contraction. Each mediator has a textbook role; the Peat-frame reading is that each gets activated together by stress and that the combined signal is what produces what looks clinically like discrete diseases.

**Histamine** is the prototype. Mast cells in connective tissue store histamine in granules and release it in seconds in response to IgE-allergen crosslinking, complement fragments, substance P, mechanical injury, and — important to Peat's frame — alkaline pH. "In the case of mast cells, increasing alkalinity causes them to release histamine (Alfonso, et al., 2005), but similar 'alkaline-induced exocytosis' seems to occur in all stressed tissues." Histamine binds H1 receptors on post-capillary venules, raises intracellular calcium, and produces immediate endothelial contraction. The wheal-and-flare response of allergy is the visible cellular event of histamine permeability. See [[histamine]].

**Serotonin** runs the parallel platelet pathway. The 5-HT released from activated platelets at the site of mild vascular injury produces the same junctional contraction as histamine, plus vasoconstriction in the arterioles. Hyperventilation — the loss of CO2 from blood — destabilizes platelets and causes them to release serotonin without an injury signal. "Simply hyperventilating for a couple of minutes will increase leakage from the blood into the lungs; hyperventilation decreases carbon dioxide, and increases serotonin and histamine." The connection between low CO2 and high permeability is the reason mechanical ventilators, which still tend to hyperventilate their patients, contribute to lung injury rather than relieve it. See [[serotonin]].

**Bradykinin** is the kinin pathway. Plasma kallikrein cleaves high-molecular-weight kininogen to release bradykinin during contact activation of the clotting cascade and during inflammation. Bradykinin binds B2 receptors, induces endothelial nitric oxide synthase, and produces leakiness through a NO-and-prostaglandin amplification loop. ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) degrades bradykinin; the dry-cough side effect of ACE inhibitors is the bradykinin accumulation their off-target inhibition produces.

**Prostaglandins** — especially PGE2 from arachidonic acid — are the substrate-derived amplifiers. They sensitize the endothelium to histamine and bradykinin, lower the threshold for myosin light-chain contraction, and dilate arterioles to increase the hydrostatic pressure feeding the leaky venules. The full mechanism is at [[prostaglandins]].

**Endotoxin** (lipopolysaccharide from gram-negative gut bacteria) is the systemic-stress trigger. Even small spikes in portal endotoxin — produced by stress, exertion, restless sleep, certain meals — activate Kupffer cells in the liver and circulating monocytes, which release TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6; these cytokines induce iNOS in the endothelium, generate massive NO, and produce the diffuse vascular leak of sepsis at the extreme and a low-grade permeability state in the chronic case. "In the hormonal environment dominated by estrogen, mild stresses such as exertion, or even restless sleep, allow toxins (and sometimes bacteria) from the intestine to enter the bloodstream, triggering a complex chain of events that create a systemic inflammatory state." See [[endotoxin]].

**Platelet-activating factor (PAF)** and the **complement anaphylatoxins** (C3a, C5a) round out the acute mediator list. PAF is itself a phospholipid signal whose synthesis depends on the same PUFA substrate that feeds the prostaglandin pathway.

**Hypoxia and mechanical stretch** are the non-mediator inputs. A capillary deprived of oxygen for minutes begins to leak because its ATP-dependent junction maintenance fails; a capillary subjected to high hydrostatic pressure (as in pulmonary capillaries during left-heart failure, or systemic capillaries during volume overload) leaks because the mechanical stress on the junction exceeds its tensile capacity. Both eventually run through the same myosin-contraction actuator.

The signature finding across all these acute mediators is that they share an amplification step. Histamine release activates platelets, which release serotonin; serotonin activates more mast cells; bradykinin and PAF further amplify mast-cell degranulation; the prostaglandins generated locally sensitize everything to everything else. The acute leakiness response is, in Peat's frame, a chain reaction with multiple ignition points and a single endothelial actuator.

## What causes chronic leakiness

The chronic permeability state is the same physiology with the time constant stretched. The same mediators stay elevated, the same endothelial contraction signaling stays active, but the inputs have shifted from acute insults to constitutive metabolic conditions. Five drivers organize the chronic state.

**Chronic inflammation** is the obvious one. Whatever the upstream trigger — autoimmunity, persistent infection, atherosclerosis, fatty liver, obesity — the tissue carries continuous histamine, serotonin, bradykinin, and prostaglandin signaling. The endothelium stays in a low-grade contracted state. Plasma protein leakage is no longer the dramatic edema of acute inflammation but the slow extravasation that produces the interstitial fibrin deposition characteristic of aging tissue. See [[inflammation]].

**Aging itself** is the second driver. Capillary density falls with age; the surviving capillaries are spaced further apart, so the diffusion distance from blood to cell rises and tissue hypoxia becomes constant; the chronic hypoxia drives chronic ATP depletion in the endothelium, which drives chronic junctional failure. The basement membrane stiffens with PUFA-peroxidation-derived aldehyde cross-links (malondialdehyde, 4-hydroxynonenal); the glycocalyx erodes; the pericyte coverage thins. Each step lowers the barrier's energy budget. See [[aging]].

**PUFA-driven prostaglandin imbalance** is the substrate driver. "Incorporation of certain polyunsaturated fats into the tissues increases the leakiness of blood vessels, and amplifies the reactions to stresses and inflammatory stimuli." Linoleic and arachidonic acid in the endothelial phospholipid pool means that any activation of phospholipase A2 — by stress, by estrogen, by mechanical stretch — liberates arachidonic acid for prostaglandin synthesis. A PUFA-depleted membrane cannot mount the same response. See [[pufa]].

**Estrogen** runs its own chronic-permeability axis. "When estrogen is given to an animal, it almost instantly causes capillaries to become leaky, allowing water to move out of the blood stream, and at the same time, estrogen causes cells to take up water. Both of these processes are the same as the early effects of oxygen deprivation." The acute effect — the surge of estrogen at ovulation that produces brief tissue water uptake — is normally terminated within hours by the rising progesterone of the luteal phase. The chronic state of unopposed estrogen (postmenopause, anovulatory cycles, exogenous estrogen, peripheral aromatase upregulation) holds the leakiness on. Estrogen acts at every node: it increases lipid peroxidation, holds free fatty acids elevated, activates phospholipase A2, induces NF-κB and the inflammatory acute-phase response, and directly lowers endothelial junction maintenance. See [[estrogen]].

**Hypothyroidism** is the metabolic driver. A thyroid-insufficient cell cannot fully oxidize glucose to CO2, runs in a partial Warburg state with elevated lactate, has chronically low ATP relative to its workload, and cannot maintain its junctional and pumping machinery. "Hypothyroidism, for example, causes leakage of enzymes, possibly mainly from the liver, but also from other organs." The hypothyroid patient runs constantly elevated CK, transaminases, LDH — not because of organ damage in the conventional sense but because hypothyroid organs leak their contents. See [[thyroid]].

The chronic state is what mainstream medicine calls "endothelial dysfunction" and treats as a vague risk marker. Peat's frame is more specific: endothelial dysfunction is the chronic state of leakiness produced by the convergence of the five drivers above, and it predicts the diseases — cardiovascular, renal, neurodegenerative — that mainstream cardiology has assembled around it.

## Leakiness and metastasis

The mechanism by which cancer kills is metastasis, and the mechanism of metastasis is leakiness twice over: leakiness of the primary tumor's vasculature (which lets tumor cells enter the circulation), and leakiness of the distal capillary bed (which lets them exit and seed). Peat's framework reads cancer not as the disease of a rogue clone but as a disease of the *field* — the tissue environment whose permeability, prostaglandin signaling, and fibrin deposition allow tumors to start, persist, and spread. The full account is at [[cancer]]; the leakiness-specific layer follows.

Tumor vasculature is the canonical example of pathological leakiness. The vessels that grow into a tumor are constitutively leaky — they lack the normal endothelial junctions, have fenestrations of variable size, miss most of their pericyte coverage, and sit on a malformed basement membrane. The "enhanced permeability and retention" effect that drug-delivery research exploits to get chemotherapy into tumors is the same leakiness that lets tumor cells escape into the circulation. Tumor-released VEGF and prostaglandins (PGE2 most prominently) drive the leaky phenotype; tumor-released cytokines (TGF-β, IL-6) reinforce it.

Circulating tumor cells that escape the primary lesion must then exit at a distal site. The exit step ("extravasation") requires the distal capillary bed to be leaky too, which is why metastatic seeding clusters in tissues with high constitutive permeability: liver sinusoids (fenestrated by design), lung capillaries (large surface area, mechanical stress), bone marrow (slow flow, high permeability), brain (when the blood-brain barrier has been pre-broken by inflammation). The same permeability mediators run the extravasation: platelet activation, serotonin release, PAF and PGE2 generation, histamine release from local mast cells, and the bradykinin-and-NO axis. Aspirin and anti-permeability interventions reduce metastasis in animal models precisely because they interrupt this axis.

The fibrin layer is the central player. Fibrin deposits at the tumor site stabilize the tumor and provide the matrix on which it grows; fibrin deposits at the metastatic site provide the substrate for the seeded tumor cell to adhere and survive. "Fibrin promotes tumor growth, partly by serving as a matrix, partly by releasing stimulatory peptides." The aspirin-and-anticoagulant case against metastasis is the case against the fibrin-leakiness chain: every step that suppresses fibrinogen synthesis (aspirin's hepatic effect), every step that prevents fibrin polymerization (warfarin, heparin, direct anticoagulants), and every step that activates fibrinolysis (vitamin E, plasmin) reduces metastatic seeding in animal data. The mainstream oncology literature has converged on the aspirin signal — the long-term reduction in colorectal-cancer metastasis with low-dose aspirin is now well-replicated — but reads it as an "anti-platelet" effect rather than as an anti-permeability one. Peat's frame is that both readings point to the same chain.

The capillary-rebleeding-and-metastasis literature is the most pointed convergence. Surgical resection of a primary tumor produces a wound, the wound produces fibrin and inflammatory mediators, the inflammatory mediators produce systemic vascular leakiness, and the leakiness allows any circulating tumor cells to seed. "Looking at the events in a failing heart, we can see that the potential repair cells recruited by the stressed heart are diverted by the conditions that they encounter there," Peat wrote in *The Cancer Matrix*. The same logic applies: the post-surgical wound becomes the metastatic field. The clinical fact that recurrence often appears at distant sites within months of a "successful" resection is the readout. Anti-permeability intervention through the surgical period — aspirin, progesterone, low-PUFA preoperative loading, postoperative CO2 elevation — is the framework's recommendation and the published case-series evidence supports a real effect, though the controlled trial has not been done at scale.

## Leakiness and edema

Visible swelling — peripheral edema, ascites, pulmonary edema, cerebral edema — is the late stage of barrier failure when the lymphatic drainage can no longer keep up with the rate of plasma extravasation. The hydrostatic-and-oncotic Starling equilibrium that textbooks present assumes a stable barrier; in chronic leakiness, the barrier is no longer fixed and the equilibrium shifts toward bulk fluid accumulation in the interstitial space.

The everyday clinical edemas track the leakiness mechanism cleanly. **Pregnancy-induced edema** is the estrogen signal in its acute form. **Premenstrual swelling** is the late-luteal-phase estrogen-and-progesterone-withdrawal signal. **Postmenopausal lower-extremity edema** is the chronic-unopposed-estrogen signal compounded by aging vasculature. **Heart-failure edema** is the elevated hydrostatic pressure compounded by neurohumoral activation (angiotensin, aldosterone, vasopressin) that itself raises permeability. **Pulmonary edema in respiratory distress** — what Peat called "shock lung" — is the diffuse capillary leakiness of systemic inflammatory response: "shock of any sort causes the lung to get 'wet,' a waterlogged condition called 'shock lung.'" **Cerebral edema** is the blood-brain-barrier failure of stroke, head injury, hyponatremia, or high-altitude illness; the brain is unusually vulnerable because its capillaries normally have the tightest junctions in the body and any disruption produces dramatic consequences.

The osmotic logic of leakiness is the third Peat-frame angle on edema. "Although water loss causes the blood to become more viscous under the influence of estrogen, the plasma becomes hypotonic, meaning that it contains fewer osmotically active solutes than normal." The hypotonic plasma pulls more water into cells; the swelling cells lose their normal junction architecture; the cycle compounds. "The osmotic imbalance of the blood causes tissue cells to take up more water, contributing to their increased excitability. In many cases, the vascular leakage of inflammation and shock can be corrected by using osmotically active substances, such as starch solutions, gelatin, or concentrated sodium chloride." The clinical practice of using salt and gelatin (or albumin, in critical-care settings) to restore plasma osmotic pressure follows directly.

A second consequence of plasma hypotonicity is that the cells themselves swell. "The tissue water retention caused by estrogen, hypoxia, and stress is analogous to the swelling of gels and colloids, that is, it's governed by the state of the electrons and counterions in the system. Excitation, fatigue, or injury can cause a shift of pH toward alkalinity, causing water uptake and swelling." Cellular edema is its own pathology, distinct from interstitial edema: it raises intracellular sodium and calcium, lowers intracellular potassium and magnesium, raises intracellular pH (which itself stimulates further histamine and serotonin release), and degrades mitochondrial function. The visible peripheral swelling is the readout of a cellular state that has propagated throughout the organism.

## Leakiness and aging

The integration of leakiness into the aging picture is the single most important Peat-frame claim on this page. Aging, in his view, is not a clock running down. It is the cumulative path-integral of every previous episode in which the cellular and endothelial barriers were forced into leakiness and not fully recovered. "The prevention of inappropriate leakiness can protect against the degenerative processes, and against aging itself, which is, among other things, a state of generalized leakiness."

The mechanism runs through three layers. First, *capillary spacing*. With age, capillary density falls and the diffusion distance from blood to cell rises. The increased distance creates chronic mild tissue hypoxia, which produces the chronic endothelial energy depletion that holds leakiness on. The visible manifestations — declining exercise capacity, slow wound healing, cognitive slowing in middle age — track this single variable more reliably than they track any single hormone. Second, *junction integrity*. The basement membrane stiffens with PUFA-peroxidation aldehyde cross-links; the glycocalyx erodes; the tight-junction proteins (claudin-5, occludin, ZO-1) drop in expression and gain abnormal post-translational modifications. The barrier no longer holds against the same hydrostatic and osmotic stresses it held in youth. Third, *energy budget*. The aged cell has fewer mitochondria, lower cytochrome oxidase activity, and a phospholipid composition (PUFA-loaded cardiolipin) that further degrades respiration. The ATP available for junction maintenance falls; the leakiness rises.

The clinical signal is visible across systems. Aged kidneys spill albumin (microalbuminuria precedes overt diabetic or hypertensive nephropathy by years). Aged brains show blood-brain-barrier breakdown on contrast MRI long before cognitive decline is measurable; the breakdown localizes initially to the hippocampus and predicts subsequent dementia. Aged hearts leak troponin at low levels during exertion that would not have raised it in youth. Aged livers leak transaminases. Aged lungs become hyperpermeable to inhaled allergens and irritants. The aged eye develops the fibrin "flare" of the anterior chamber that Peat described as the simplest demonstration of the phenomenon. Each is the same physiology read at a different organ.

The convergence with the cancer field is the third element. The leaky aging tissue accumulates extravasated fibrinogen-fibrin deposits in the interstitium; these deposits provide the matrix on which dormant micrometastatic tumor cells can adhere and grow; the leaky aging vasculature provides the route by which circulating tumor cells reach the interstitium in the first place. "Kidney disease, diabetes, pregnancy toxemia and retinal degeneration are probably the best known problems involving vascular leakage, but increasingly, cancer and heart disease are being recognized as consequences of prolonged permeability defects." The age-related rise in cancer incidence is, in the Peat frame, the readout of the aging-leakiness-fibrin-cancer chain.

## Peat's interventions

Peat's anti-permeability toolkit is not aimed at sealing the barrier pharmacologically (the way a corticosteroid does, with broad side effects). It restores the metabolic environment in which the endothelium and the cellular barriers maintain themselves. The toolkit overlaps almost completely with the anti-inflammatory toolkit at [[inflammation]] and the anti-fibrotic toolkit at [[fibrosis]], with several leakiness-specific points of emphasis.

**[[Aspirin]]** is the central anti-permeability drug. It acetylates COX irreversibly, suppressing the PGE2 amplification that lowers the threshold for histamine, bradykinin, and serotonin permeability. It also inhibits phospholipase A2 in some contexts, blocks NF-κB activation in hepatocytes (lowering acute-phase fibrinogen production), suppresses platelet activation (cutting off the serotonin and PAF release that comes with it), and has direct anti-glycation and anti-cross-linking effects on the basement membrane. The colorectal and breast cancer mortality reductions in long-term low-dose aspirin trials are, on the Peat frame, the anti-permeability effect reading through to cancer mortality. The dose-response — even 81 mg/day produces measurable effects on tumor incidence and metastasis — is consistent with prostaglandin suppression rather than with the platelet-aggregation story alone.

**[[Thyroid]] optimization** restores the oxidative metabolism on which barrier maintenance depends. A thyroid-sufficient endothelial cell has the ATP to maintain its junctional and pumping machinery; a thyroid-insufficient one does not. The leakage of enzymes that mainstream medicine reads as organ damage in hypothyroidism is the barrier-failure readout of the same metabolic state. T3 supplementation, often in modest doses (1-3 mcg of T3 every few hours, or a small dose of natural desiccated thyroid), reverses the leakage signal alongside the metabolic markers.

**[[PUFA]] depletion** removes the substrate that feeds the prostaglandin-driven amplification of acute permeability and the aldehyde-driven cross-linking of chronic basement-membrane stiffening. The 2-4 year tissue half-life of linoleic acid means that the depletion is slow, but the trajectory is durable once started. A diet that derives its fat from butter, coconut oil, beef fat, and dairy rather than from seed oils, fish oils, and nuts produces a phospholipid composition that does not feed the cascade.

**[[Cyproheptadine]] and antiserotonin drugs** suppress the serotonin and (in cyproheptadine's case) histamine release that drives both acute and chronic leakiness. "The antiserotonin drugs are being increasingly used to treat fibrotic diseases, and other problems related to vascular leakage." Cyproheptadine in particular is on the framework's intermediate-line list for permeability-related conditions (asthma, urticaria, dumping syndrome, carcinoid, post-surgical edema) — see [[cyproheptadine]].

**Histamine reduction** runs in parallel — through avoidance of high-histamine foods (aged cheese, fermented foods, processed meats, alcohol), antihistamine drugs (the older sedating H1 blockers cross the BBB and have broader effects than the newer non-sedating ones), and the indirect reduction of mast-cell activation that aspirin, vitamin E, and progesterone produce. See [[histamine]].

**[[Vitamin-k|Vitamin K]] and flavonoids.** Vitamin K is essential for the carboxylation of the proteins that hold endothelial junctions and prevent ectopic calcification of leaky vessel walls; K2 (MK-4, MK-7) is the soft-tissue active form. Bioflavonoids — quercetin, hesperidin, rutin, diosmin — strengthen capillary walls and reduce permeability; "venotonic" pharmaceutical preparations (diosmin/hesperidin for chronic venous insufficiency, in particular) work through this pathway. The mechanism is partly mast-cell stabilization, partly direct membrane interaction.

**Magnesium** stabilizes endothelial calcium signaling. Mast cells, platelets, and endothelial cells all release their permeability mediators when intracellular calcium rises; magnesium opposes calcium at every step. Chronic magnesium deficiency raises baseline mediator release; supplementation lowers it.

**CO2 elevation** is the integrative intervention. Hyperventilation depletes blood CO2 and produces immediate histamine and serotonin release with measurable rises in lung and gut permeability; CO2 restoration (bag breathing, paced respiration, altitude descent, sodium bicarbonate) reverses the signal. "The release of serotonin during hyperventilation isn't the only cause of vascular leakage; the carbon dioxide itself is an essential factor in regulating the state of cellular electrons and in maintaining cellular integrity." Aspirin, niacinamide, and T3 all raise CO2 production indirectly by restoring the oxidative pathway that produces it. See [[carbon-dioxide]].

**[[Progesterone]]** is the steroid antagonist of the estrogen permeability axis. It opposes estrogen at the receptor (destroying the receptor protein rather than blocking it), suppresses aromatase, inhibits the phospholipase that liberates arachidonic acid, and directly lowers endothelial NF-κB activation. In conditions where estrogen excess is driving leakiness (pregnancy toxemia, postmenopausal cardiovascular events, premenstrual edema), progesterone is the lever Peat used most consistently.

The non-drug practices round out the toolkit. **Gelatin and glycine** support liver albumin synthesis (the protein that holds plasma oncotic pressure and prevents interstitial edema) and oppose the tryptophan-serotonin axis. **Salt** suppresses serotonin and aldosterone, supports plasma volume, and improves the osmotic regulation that prevents cellular edema. **Sugar** supports glucose oxidation, suppresses the free-fatty-acid mobilization that feeds the prostaglandin cascade, and provides the substrate for CO2 production. **Vitamin E** (mixed tocopherols) opposes lipid peroxidation, reduces the aldehyde load on the basement membrane, and has direct anti-estrogenic and pro-fibrinolytic effects.

The crucial framing point — the same point that organizes the anti-fibrotic toolkit at [[fibrosis]] — is that no single agent in the toolkit "treats leakiness" the way a corticosteroid or a vasoconstrictor would. Each acts at a different node of the metabolic environment, and the combined effect is to allow the endothelium and the cellular barriers to maintain themselves at the energy budget that respiration, properly supported, makes available.

## Tracking signal

The clinical signals of leakiness are simple and available without specialized testing.

**Visible swelling.** Peripheral edema (ankles, hands, periorbital), abdominal bloating, premenstrual swelling, postprandial swelling. Each is a tissue-fluid signal that the barrier is not holding. Trajectory matters more than absolute level; a young person who has begun to notice that their rings get tight or that their ankles indent is reading the early signal of the chronic state.

**Blood pressure variability.** Wide swings in measured blood pressure across the day are signs that the autoregulatory mechanisms — and the underlying vascular tone — are working harder than they should be to keep perfusion stable across a leaky bed. The early prehypertensive phase often shows this signal before the average rises into the hypertensive range.

**Microalbuminuria.** The single most useful laboratory signal, and one that mainstream nephrology has already accepted as a marker of vascular health. A urine microalbumin-to-creatinine ratio above 30 mg/g is the conventional threshold, but the trajectory below that threshold is informative: a ratio that has doubled across years, even within the "normal" range, is the leakiness chain making itself visible. The signal predates overt diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy, and cardiovascular events by years.

**Fluid retention patterns.** Weight that rises and falls by 2-3 pounds across days — without corresponding caloric changes — is the interstitial-fluid swing of a permeability state. The premenstrual and postmenopausal patterns are the gendered version; the heart-failure and renal-failure patterns are the disease versions.

**Enzyme leakage on routine panels.** Mildly elevated CK, AST, ALT, LDH in the absence of obvious organ pathology is the chronic-leakiness signal. Conventional medicine reads these as "subclinical organ damage" and waits for them to rise into clearly pathological ranges; the Peat-frame reading is that they are the early signal of the energy-failure state that produces the damage.

**The eye flare.** Slit-lamp examination of the anterior chamber for flare (light-scattering by escaped fibrin) is the cleanest demonstration of the principle and is sometimes used by ophthalmologists to assess inflammatory eye disease. The technique is generalizable; the principle (light scattered by extravasated plasma protein) holds throughout the body.

**Resting pulse and temperature.** The metabolic-state signals at [[aging]] (low temperature, low pulse, low CO2) predict where on the chronic-leakiness trajectory the patient sits, because they index the same underlying respiratory state.

## Counter-arguments

**Modern endothelial biology splits the mechanisms.** Mainstream vascular medicine treats endothelial dysfunction, vascular permeability, capillary leak syndrome, atherosclerosis, and metastasis as distinct phenomena with overlapping but distinct molecular mechanisms — VE-cadherin disruption in one, glycocalyx shedding in another, pericyte dropout in a third, basement-membrane proteolysis in a fourth. The Peat-frame unification under "leakiness" is, on this view, a useful clinical heuristic but not an accurate molecular picture. The response is that the molecular heterogeneity is real but downstream of a common metabolic disorder; each of the splintered mechanisms shares the energetic and substrate dependencies the framework names, and the therapeutic convergence (aspirin, thyroid, progesterone, PUFA depletion working across all of them) is consistent with the unified reading.

**Vascular medicine sees leakiness as a symptom, not a cause.** The mainstream framing is that vascular permeability is a downstream consequence of inflammation or atherosclerosis or hypertension or cancer, not a unifying cause of any of them. The Peat-frame response is that the directionality is not either-or — leakiness participates in feedback loops with inflammation, atherosclerosis, and tumor growth — and that the clinical evidence of intervention efficacy (aspirin reducing cancer mortality, ACE inhibitors reducing heart-failure mortality, low-PUFA diets reducing inflammatory disease) tracks the leakiness chain even when the trials were not designed to test it.

**The corticosteroid critique.** If leakiness were the unifying mechanism, corticosteroids (which are the most powerful anti-permeability drugs in clinical use) should be broadly anti-aging, anti-cancer, and anti-fibrotic. They are not — chronic steroid use produces accelerated aging, immunosuppression, muscle wasting, and metabolic disease. The response is that corticosteroids suppress permeability by suppressing the underlying metabolism (they are catabolic, they raise cortisol's chronic effects), and the framework treats them as a confirmation of the principle rather than a refutation: they buy short-term barrier integrity at the cost of long-term tissue damage. The Peat-frame interventions raise the energy budget so the barrier maintains itself.

**Aspirin's anti-platelet effect is the simpler explanation.** Mainstream readings of the aspirin-and-cancer-and-cardiovascular literature emphasize the platelet pathway: aspirin inhibits COX-1 in platelets, reduces TXA2, reduces platelet aggregation, reduces atherothrombosis, reduces metastatic seeding via platelet-tumor-cell interaction. The Peat-frame response is that the platelet effect is one node of a broader anti-permeability action; the long-term benefits in cancer and cardiovascular disease are larger than the platelet-only mechanism would predict, and the anti-glycation, anti-cross-linking, and CO2-raising effects of aspirin add real components.

**The metastasis-and-permeability literature is animal-model heavy.** The bulk of the experimental evidence linking vascular permeability to metastatic seeding comes from rodent models (B16 melanoma, 4T1 breast carcinoma) whose translation to human cancer is imperfect. The Peat-frame response is that the human aspirin-and-anticoagulant epidemiological data points in the same direction, and that the mechanistic case is robust enough to act on clinically even before the gold-standard human trials are run.

## Data gaps

**No quantitative permeability-aging model.** The framework's claim that aging is integrated leakiness has not been formalized into a model that predicts, from a patient's history, an expected permeability trajectory and an expected effect-size for each intervention. The biomarkers (microalbumin, transaminases, troponin sensitivity, hsCRP) exist; the integration into a single index does not.

**The CO2-and-permeability relationship is under-characterized.** Peat's claim that hyperventilation directly increases lung and gut permeability through CO2 depletion is supported by short-term experimental work but has not been worked out in the chronic-hypocapnia case (chronic anxiety states, sleep-disordered breathing, high-altitude residence). The clinical implication — that CO2-elevation interventions (bag breathing, paced respiration, supplemental CO2, sodium bicarbonate) should reduce permeability-driven disease — is not well-tested.

**The progesterone-and-permeability dose-response.** Peat's clinical claim that progesterone reverses estrogen-driven permeability across multiple contexts is supported by mechanistic and case-series data; the dose-response (5-10 mg topical for women, 10-20 mg for severe cases, lower doses or pregnenolone for men) is approximate and has not been formalized for permeability-specific endpoints.

**Mast cell biology.** The framework grants mast cells a central role in permeability but does not develop the mast-cell-activation-syndrome literature that has emerged in the 2010s and 2020s. The cromolyn-and-ketotifen mast-cell-stabilizer class would be predicted to be anti-leakiness; the prediction has not been worked out in detail.

**The glycocalyx layer.** Modern endothelial biology has put major weight on the glycocalyx as the first-pass permeability barrier. The Peat corpus predates the glycocalyx literature and does not engage it directly; whether the framework's interventions act on glycocalyx integrity (predicted yes — aspirin, vitamin K, low-PUFA, antiserotonin) is not formalized.

**Lymphatic drainage.** The framework treats leakiness as a barrier problem and gives less attention to the lymphatic drainage that determines whether extravasated fluid produces clinical edema. Conditions of lymphatic insufficiency (post-mastectomy lymphedema, congenital lymphedema, filarial elephantiasis) sit awkwardly in the framework and deserve their own treatment.

**The cancer-metastasis-aspirin trial data is observational.** The strongest evidence that anti-permeability intervention reduces cancer mortality comes from long-term cohort and case-control studies of regular aspirin use; the randomized-trial evidence is mixed and confounded by GI-bleeding harms in some populations. The framework predicts that the trial signal should be stronger if the comparator regimen included PUFA depletion, T3, progesterone, and vitamin K alongside aspirin — but this combined regimen has not been tested.

**Sex- and age-specific dosing for the toolkit.** The general guidance (aspirin 81-325 mg/day in middle-aged adults; progesterone 5-15 mg/day in adult women) is approximate. The titration logic for premenopausal vs postmenopausal women, for men of different ages, for patients with comorbid conditions, has not been formalized.
