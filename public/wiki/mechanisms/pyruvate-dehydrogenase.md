# Pyruvate Dehydrogenase

The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is the gate. Glycolysis runs in the cytoplasm and turns one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvic acid; the Krebs cycle runs inside the mitochondrion and oxidizes the carbon skeleton all the way to carbon dioxide and water. Between them, on the inner mitochondrial membrane, sits a multienzyme complex roughly the size of a ribosome — pyruvate dehydrogenase, often abbreviated PDH or PDC — whose only job is to decarboxylate pyruvate, attach what remains to coenzyme A as acetyl-CoA, and pass that fuel into the citric acid cycle. When the gate is open, the cell oxidizes sugar completely and produces roughly thirty-six ATP per glucose along with the [[carbon-dioxide]] that stabilizes its protein-water phase. When the gate is closed, pyruvate cannot enter the [[mitochondria]] for oxidation, backs up in the cytoplasm, and is reduced to [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] instead — the same metabolic profile Otto Warburg described in 1924 as the signature of cancer.

This is why Ray Peat returned to PDH so often. The enzyme is small enough to name and big enough to matter. He treated it as one of the "pivotal landmarks in cellular metabolism," alongside phosphofructokinase-1 and cytochrome c oxidase, in his *Generative Energy* #35 episode with Danny Roddy and Georgi Dinkov. Free fatty acids inhibit it. Insulin activates it. Thyroid hormone activates it. Fructose reactivates it by absorbing the excess phosphate that keeps it switched off. Cancer cells suppress it; dichloroacetate (DCA) is being studied because it reactivates it. Alzheimer's disease, in the Ishiguro 1998 paper Peat cited in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues*]], involves a specific PDH inactivation process. The Randle cycle — the substrate competition that makes diabetes a fat disease rather than a sugar disease — converges on PDH. The narrative this article tells is the molecular biography of one enzyme through whose hinge most of the diseases of civilization pass.

The article that comes before this one, [[randle-cycle]], frames PDH as a node in a larger substrate-competition story. The article that comes after, [[oxidative-metabolism]], treats the enzyme as one component of the system through which the cell maintains its differentiation. This page is about the enzyme itself: its three catalytic subunits, its five vitamin cofactors, the kinase that switches it off and the phosphatase that switches it back on, the regulatory logic that lets a cell choose between oxidation and fermentation in seconds, and the pathological states in which the choice is no longer free.

## Structure: Three Enzymes, Five Cofactors, One Reaction

The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is not a single protein. It is a multienzyme assembly built from three distinct catalytic activities working in tandem, organized around a central scaffold and decorated with five different cofactor molecules. In mammalian cells the assembled complex contains roughly thirty copies of each subunit and weighs about nine megadaltons — large enough to be visible by electron microscopy as a discrete particle on the inner mitochondrial membrane.

The three catalytic activities are conventionally labeled E1, E2, and E3.

**E1 — pyruvate dehydrogenase (decarboxylating).** This is the enzyme that gives the whole complex its name. E1 binds pyruvate, decarboxylates it (releasing one molecule of carbon dioxide), and transfers the remaining two-carbon fragment to a [[thiamine|thiamine pyrophosphate]] (TPP) molecule held in its active site. Thiamine pyrophosphate is the activated form of vitamin B1, and the chemistry it performs here — stabilizing the negatively charged carbanion intermediate that forms when carbon dioxide is released from pyruvate — is the canonical use case for the vitamin in mammalian biochemistry. A cell deprived of thiamine cannot run E1; pyruvate accumulates; lactate rises. Wernicke's encephalopathy, beriberi heart failure, and the lactic acidosis that follows refeeding malnourished patients are all manifestations of the same enzymatic bottleneck. The fact that thiamine deficiency mimics the metabolic profile of cancer and diabetes — high lactate, low CO2, energy failure in the brain — was, in Peat's framework, not a coincidence but a confirmation that PDH is the right place to look.

**E2 — dihydrolipoamide acetyltransferase.** E2 carries a covalently attached molecule of lipoic acid (also called lipoamide or alpha-lipoic acid), which acts as a swinging arm transferring the two-carbon fragment from E1 to coenzyme A. The lipoyl group has two sulfur atoms that cycle between an oxidized disulfide form and a reduced sulfhydryl form, and the swinging arm physically moves between the three catalytic sites of the complex. The acetyl group is attached to one of the sulfurs, transported to the second active site, and there transferred to the thiol group of coenzyme A. The product — acetyl-CoA — diffuses out of the complex and enters the Krebs cycle. Coenzyme A is built from pantothenic acid (vitamin B5), the second of the five cofactors. A cell deprived of B5 cannot synthesize coenzyme A, and acetyl groups have nowhere to go.

**E3 — dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase.** E3 regenerates the oxidized form of lipoamide, which is required to start the next cycle. It does this by transferring two electrons from the reduced lipoyl group first to FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide, made from vitamin B2, riboflavin) and then from FADH2 to NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, made from vitamin B3, niacin or niacinamide). The reduced NADH then diffuses to Complex I of the electron transport chain, where its electrons enter the respiratory cascade. The reaction E3 catalyzes is conceptually small but mechanistically critical: without it, the lipoyl arm cannot reset and the whole complex stalls within a single turnover.

The five-vitamin cofactor stack — B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin/niacinamide), B5 (pantothenate via coenzyme A), and lipoic acid — makes PDH the most B-vitamin-dependent enzyme in the cell. Peat returned to this dependency repeatedly. Niacinamide had a particular position in his framework not just for its NAD+ contribution but because, as he wrote in [[regeneration-and-degeneration-types-of-inflammation-change-with-aging|*Regeneration and Degeneration* (2012)]], "[[niacinamide]], by lowering free fatty acids and regulating the redox system, supporting sugar oxidation, is useful in the whole spectrum of metabolic degenerative diseases." The vitamin acts on PDH from two directions at once: it supplies the NAD+ that E3 needs, and it lowers the circulating free fatty acids that drive the kinase-mediated inhibition described in the next section.

The full reaction the complex catalyzes can be written in a single line:

> pyruvate + CoA + NAD+ → acetyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+

Three things leave the gate: a molecule of carbon dioxide (the diagnostic product of complete oxidation), a molecule of NADH (electrons heading down the chain), and a molecule of acetyl-CoA (carbon heading into the Krebs cycle). The reaction is irreversible under physiological conditions. Once pyruvate has passed through PDH and become acetyl-CoA, the cell has committed to oxidizing the carbon. The decision to enter the mitochondrion happens here.

## Regulation: PDK Off, PDP On

The PDH complex is regulated by reversible phosphorylation of three serine residues on its E1 subunit. When those serines are phosphorylated the complex is inactive; when they are dephosphorylated, it is active. The kinase that adds the phosphates is pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase, [[pdk|PDK]], and the phosphatase that removes them is pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase, PDP. The balance between PDK and PDP determines, moment to moment, how much of the cell's PDH pool is switched on.

Four signals push PDK toward more phosphorylation — toward shutting PDH off:

**High acetyl-CoA-to-CoA ratio.** When acetyl-CoA accumulates downstream of PDH (because the Krebs cycle is saturated, or because the cell is burning a lot of fat and generating acetyl-CoA from beta-oxidation), the ratio of acetyl-CoA to free CoA rises and PDK is activated. The product of the reaction inhibits the enzyme that produces it — a classic feedback loop.

**High NADH-to-NAD+ ratio.** When the electron transport chain is saturated or impaired and reduced NADH accumulates, PDK is activated. Again, the product inhibits the enzyme. This is the signal Georgi Dinkov highlighted in the *Generative Energy* #35 discussion: "Higher NADH is one of the signals to turn off pyruvate dehydrogenase. Some ketosis advocates, like Jeff Volek, talk about how that's a consequence of ketosis. What's the problem if PDH is turned off?" Peat's answer — "that makes the whole cell shift in that direction. Once it starts with the excess reducing environment, it tends to reinforce it and make it get worse" — is the substrate-level account of why the metabolic states that turn PDH off (fasting, ketosis, diabetes) are self-amplifying. The reduced state activates the kinase that locks the cell into the reduced state.

**High ATP-to-ADP ratio.** When ATP is plentiful, PDK is activated. The cell does not need more energy and stops feeding the Krebs cycle. This is the most innocent of the four signals — the cell at rest, satisfied, throttling its own respiration.

**Free fatty acids.** This is the regulatory node Peat developed most thoroughly. Beta-oxidation of fatty acids generates acetyl-CoA and NADH directly inside the mitochondrion, which raises both ratios above and activates PDK that way. But fatty acids also activate PDK more directly: through accumulation of intracellular phosphate, through generation of fatty-acyl-CoA intermediates that bind PDK, through induction of PDK isoform expression at the transcriptional level. The result is that a cell with elevated circulating free fatty acids has PDK chronically activated and PDH chronically off — the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] in molecular detail. Peat described the phosphate mechanism with particular care in the [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on diabetes and nerves]]: "They block pyruvate dehydrogenase by making more phosphate ions available. Where sugars bind them and lower free phosphates, fatty acids increase them and tend to poison the crucial enzyme."

Three signals push PDP toward more dephosphorylation — toward turning PDH back on:

**Calcium.** Intracellular calcium activates PDP. This is the link between contraction and respiration in muscle: when calcium is released into the cytoplasm to trigger contraction, the same calcium activates PDP in mitochondria nearby, switches PDH on, and accelerates glucose oxidation to meet the demand. The coupling is direct and elegant.

**Insulin.** Insulin activates PDP through a signaling cascade involving phosphatidylinositol kinases and direct effects on PDP transcription. The result is that an insulin-stimulated cell has PDH switched on and glucose flowing into the mitochondria. Peat noted this in [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|*Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes* (2012)]]: "Lactic acid itself, and the longer chain fatty acids, inhibit the regulatory enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase (which is activated by insulin), reducing the oxidative production of energy."

**Magnesium.** PDP is a magnesium-dependent phosphatase. Cells depleted of magnesium — under stress, under excess sodium-pump activity, under cortisol-driven mineral loss — cannot dephosphorylate PDH efficiently. The magnesium dependency is one of the underdeveloped substrates underneath the Peat observation that magnesium replenishment restores energy in fatigued patients. Some of the effect runs through PDP.

The kinase-phosphatase pair is itself a target of intervention. Dichloroacetate inhibits PDK; thyroid hormone induces both PDH transcription and (over longer timescales) reduces PDK isoform expression; fructose, by absorbing intracellular phosphate, shifts the equilibrium toward dephosphorylation. The pharmacology of the cancer metabolic axis is largely the pharmacology of this kinase-phosphatase balance.

## The Randle Inhibition

The Randle cycle — the substrate competition through which free fatty acids suppress glucose oxidation — operates at multiple enzymatic levels, but PDH is its central target. The full account of how fatty acids drive glucose into fermentation through this enzymatic chokepoint lives at [[randle-cycle]]. The unique signal here is the molecular sequence by which a long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid in the bloodstream ends up phosphorylating the E1 subunit and shutting the gate.

A fatty acid enters the cell, is activated to its CoA derivative, and crosses the outer mitochondrial membrane on the carnitine shuttle. Inside the matrix, it undergoes beta-oxidation: each cycle removes two carbons as acetyl-CoA and generates one FADH2 and one NADH. The acetyl-CoA-to-CoA ratio rises; the NADH-to-NAD+ ratio rises; both signals activate PDK; PDK phosphorylates E1; PDH is switched off. The cell, even if it still has glucose available, cannot run that glucose all the way to carbon dioxide. Pyruvate accumulates, gets reduced to lactate, and is exported. The Randle effect, at the molecular level, is the conversion of beta-oxidation products into PDK activation.

Peat described the consequence in [[2021-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-managing-hormones-and-cancer-treatment-with-nutrition|the 2021 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on cancer treatment]]: "Many years ago, a researcher named Randle noticed that if you increase the fat in your diet and it turns into free fatty acids, those fatty acids block the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase, which lets us oxidize glucose all the way to carbon dioxide. This is the Randle Cycle." The chain that follows — "Once you get ketogenic from starvation, you are poisoning your ability to fully oxidize glucose. That leaves you producing lactic acid. When pyruvate dehydrogenase is poisoned — especially by free fatty acids — you shift over to the cancer metabolism" — places PDH as the hinge between metabolic states the rest of medicine treats as discrete diseases. Diabetes, cancer, [[heart-failure|heart failure]], [[aging]], and neurodegeneration all share the same proximate substrate failure at this enzyme.

The PUFA-vs-saturated distinction Peat developed runs through the same node. Saturated fats, even when they elevate circulating free fatty acids, do not generate the lipid peroxidation products that further inhibit cytochrome c oxidase and the Krebs cycle. The Randle inhibition they impose is mild and self-correcting. Polyunsaturated fats, in contrast, generate the aldehyde adducts (4-HNE, MDA, acrolein) that poison the electron transport chain downstream of PDH, raising the NADH-to-NAD+ ratio still further and reinforcing the kinase-mediated PDH inhibition from a second direction. The full chain is at [[lipid-peroxidation]]; the unique point here is that PUFA shuts PDH off through two compounding pathways — direct substrate competition and downstream electron-chain damage — while saturated fat only operates through the first.

## Thyroid and PDH

Thyroid hormone is the principal upstream regulator that keeps PDH active. T3 acts at three levels: it increases the transcription of PDH subunits themselves, it suppresses the transcription of PDK isoforms (particularly PDK4, the isoform most responsive to fatty acid availability), and it amplifies the cellular conditions — high oxygen consumption, low NADH-to-NAD+ ratio, brisk Krebs cycle turnover — that keep PDK from being activated downstream. Peat's framework treats this as the molecular substrate underneath the hypothyroid-diabetes connection that runs through his entire corpus.

He wrote it most directly in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues* (2012)]]: "Cancer researchers have become interested in this enzyme system that controls the oxidation of pyruvic acid (and thus sugar) by the mitochondria, since these enzymes are crucially defective in cancer cells (and also in diabetes). The chemical DCA, dichloroacetate, is effective against a variety of cancers, and it acts by reactivating the enzymes that oxidize pyruvic acid. Thyroid hormone, insulin, and fructose also activate these enzymes." The grouping is diagnostic: thyroid, insulin, fructose, and DCA all converge on PDH activation through different mechanisms — T3 at the transcriptional level, insulin through PDP signaling, fructose through phosphate absorption, DCA through PDK inhibition.

The reverse is the failure mode Peat saw everywhere. A hypothyroid cell has fewer PDH complexes per mitochondrion to begin with, expresses more PDK4 to phosphorylate the complexes it has, runs a slower Krebs cycle that elevates the NADH-to-NAD+ ratio, and is more susceptible to fatty-acid-driven Randle inhibition because its baseline glucose oxidation is already compromised. The cell cannot escape the reduced state on its own. As Peat said in the *Generative Energy* #35 discussion: "That makes the whole cell shift in that direction. Once it starts with the excess reducing environment, it tends to reinforce it and make it get worse." Thyroid is what reverses it from above; fructose, insulin, niacinamide, aspirin, and (pharmacologically) DCA are what reverse it from below.

The temperature signature Peat treated as the most reliable thyroid diagnostic — mid-morning oral temperature near 98.6°F — is, at the level of mitochondrial chemistry, the visible product of T3-stimulated PDH activity. The CO2 in the breath, the warmth in the hands and feet, the basal metabolic rate measurable in a Benedict apparatus — all of these are downstream of PDH running at full capacity, oxidizing glucose completely to carbon dioxide rather than dumping pyruvate into the lactate pool. The full thyroid-mitochondria story is at [[thyroid]] and [[mitochondria]]; the unique node here is that PDH is the enzyme on which the T3 signal most visibly acts.

## Cancer: The Warburg Shift at PDH

Otto Warburg observed in 1924 that tumor cells consume glucose at enormous rates and ferment most of it to lactic acid even in the presence of abundant oxygen. The phenomenon — aerobic glycolysis, the Warburg effect — has been confirmed in essentially every solid tumor studied since. The mechanism, as the literature converged through the 2000s, runs through PDH. Cancer cells overexpress PDK (particularly PDK1 and PDK3), keep PDH chronically phosphorylated, and divert pyruvate to lactate through lactate dehydrogenase rather than to acetyl-CoA through PDH. The metabolic profile mainstream oncology has spent a century cataloguing is, at the level of one enzyme, a story about kinase-mediated PDH suppression.

Peat connected this to the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] with the simplest possible bridge: the same free fatty acids that suppress PDH in a diabetic's muscle cell suppress it in a tumor. The metabolic state of cancer and the metabolic state of insulin-resistant diabetes are, at the PDH node, the same biochemical lesion expressed in different tissues. As he wrote in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues*]]: "These are the enzymes that are inactivated by excessive exposure to fatty acids, and that are involved in the progressive replacement of sugar oxidation by fat oxidation, during stress and aging, and in degenerative diseases; for example, a process that inactivates the energy-producing pyruvate dehydrogenase in [[alzheimers|Alzheimer's disease]] has been identified (Ishiguro, 1998)." The Ishiguro reference is to a paper showing tau kinase-mediated PDH phosphorylation in Alzheimer's brain tissue — the same molecular lesion appearing in a neurodegenerative context.

The interventions that reverse the Warburg shift converge on PDH reactivation. DCA does it pharmacologically. Thyroid does it transcriptionally. Fructose does it through phosphate absorption. Insulin does it through PDP. The dietary corollary Peat developed — the high-sugar, low-fat anti-cancer protocol with [[fructose]], [[orange-juice|orange juice]], [[gelatin]], [[aspirin]], and [[niacinamide]] — is the substrate-level expression of the same molecular target. The full Warburg case is at [[warburg-effect]]; the unique point here is that the historical mystery of why tumors ferment glucose in the presence of oxygen has, in the past two decades, narrowed to a story about PDK overexpression, and the Peat framework anticipated this convergence by decades.

## Pathology: Congenital PDH Deficiency, Thiamine Failure, Alzheimer's

Three pathologies illustrate the enzyme's centrality through what happens when it fails.

**Congenital PDH deficiency.** Mutations in the E1-alpha subunit (encoded on the X chromosome, the gene PDHA1) produce a spectrum of clinical syndromes ranging from neonatal lactic acidosis with early death to milder forms presenting in childhood with episodic ataxia, dystonia, and developmental delay. The biochemistry is consistent across the spectrum: pyruvate cannot enter the mitochondrion as acetyl-CoA, accumulates in the cytoplasm, is reduced to lactate, and produces a chronic lactic acidosis that the brain in particular cannot tolerate. The ketogenic diet has been used as a clinical intervention because it bypasses PDH entirely — beta-hydroxybutyrate enters the mitochondrion and is converted to acetyl-CoA without going through pyruvate. This is the one clinical context in which Peat's general critique of the ketogenic diet does not apply: when PDH itself is genetically broken, the only fuel that can reach the Krebs cycle is one that bypasses it.

**Thiamine deficiency (beriberi, Wernicke-Korsakoff).** Thiamine pyrophosphate is the cofactor at the E1 active site; without it, pyruvate cannot be decarboxylated and the rest of the complex idles. Thiamine deficiency produces lactic acidosis, peripheral neuropathy, cardiac failure (wet beriberi), and the characteristic encephalopathy of Wernicke's syndrome. The neurological pattern is diagnostic — the brain regions most affected (mammillary bodies, thalamus, periaqueductal gray) are those with the highest baseline glucose oxidation rates, which makes them the most vulnerable to PDH failure. Refeeding syndrome — the lactic acidosis that develops when malnourished patients are given carbohydrate without thiamine — is the iatrogenic version of the same lesion. The clinical lesson is that high-carbohydrate intake without adequate B1 produces the lactic acidosis the carbohydrate is supposed to prevent, because the gate is closed at PDH.

**Alzheimer's disease.** Peat cited Ishiguro 1998 in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues*]] as evidence that "a process that inactivates the energy-producing pyruvate dehydrogenase in Alzheimer's disease has been identified." The subsequent two decades of literature have confirmed the finding repeatedly. Alzheimer's brain tissue shows reduced PDH activity, reduced cerebral glucose oxidation on FDG-PET imaging, and elevated lactate-to-pyruvate ratios in cerebrospinal fluid. The pattern matches the Warburg shift in tumors and the Randle shift in diabetic muscle — three different clinical contexts converging on the same enzymatic lesion. The therapeutic implication that Peat developed — that anti-PUFA, anti-stress, thyroid-supportive, sugar-supplied protocols should slow Alzheimer's progression — is testable, and the small clinical literature on niacinamide and methylene blue in cognitive impairment is consistent with the prediction.

## Dichloroacetate (DCA): The Cancer Angle

Dichloroacetate is the pharmacological intervention that put PDH on the oncology map. Structurally it is a tiny molecule — chloride-substituted acetic acid, two chlorines on the methyl carbon — and mechanistically it inhibits all four PDK isoforms by binding to the kinase's pyruvate-binding pocket. The result, in a tumor cell with chronically phosphorylated PDH, is rapid dephosphorylation, restored PDH activity, restored glucose oxidation, restored mitochondrial CO2 production, and (in the experiments that drew attention to the drug) tumor regression in glioblastoma, lung cancer, and breast cancer cell lines.

The Bonnet et al. paper in *Cancer Cell* in 2007 was the inflection point. Evangelos Michelakis and colleagues at the University of Alberta showed that DCA shifted glioblastoma cells from glycolytic to oxidative metabolism, reactivated apoptosis through mitochondrial pathways, and slowed tumor growth in animal models without obvious toxicity to normal tissue. The cellular mechanism was straightforward: tumor mitochondria are not absent (a misconception widespread in the older literature) but suppressed, and reactivating PDH allowed the suppressed mitochondria to resume function. Tumor cells, like all cells, undergo apoptosis when their mitochondria signal it; mitochondria that have been functionally silenced for many cell divisions had lost the apoptotic signal, and reactivating them restored it.

Peat tracked the DCA literature with interest. He mentioned the drug in [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|the 2014 nerve-damage episode]]: "The pyruvate dehydrogenase enzyme is suppressed in cancer — that's why dichloroacetate is gaining interest, as it reactivates that enzyme. But simply lowering the free phosphate in the cell tends to reactivate this crucial enzyme at the top of the energy-producing chain." The framing is characteristic — DCA is an interesting pharmacological proof of concept, but the same effect can be achieved with the simpler interventions (fructose, thyroid, niacinamide) that target the underlying substrate. He returned to the point in [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues*]]: "The chemical DCA, dichloroacetate, is effective against a variety of cancers, and it acts by reactivating the enzymes that oxidize pyruvic acid. Thyroid hormone, insulin, and fructose also activate these enzymes."

The clinical fate of DCA is the cautionary tale that ends this section. The drug was off-patent (it had been used for decades in mitochondrial encephalopathy and congenital lactic acidosis), which meant no pharmaceutical company stood to recover the cost of phase III trials. A small clinic in Alberta ran limited compassionate-use trials with mixed results. Off-label use proliferated on the internet. Peripheral neuropathy emerged as the dose-limiting toxicity in chronic administration. Major oncology organizations distanced themselves from the drug. As of 2026 DCA remains an off-label intervention with intriguing mechanistic credentials and no completed phase III evidence — a story that says more about pharmaceutical economics than about whether reactivating PDH is a sound oncological strategy.

## Reversing the Block

The Peat protocol for PDH reactivation parallels his protocol for [[randle-cycle|Randle effect]] reversal, with several enzyme-specific refinements:

**Fructose** is the most direct intervention. By absorbing intracellular phosphate, it shifts the PDK-PDP equilibrium toward dephosphorylation. As Peat wrote in the [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 diabetes-and-nerves episode]]: "Fructose absorbs excess phosphate ions. The absorption of phosphate ions by fructose is a direct defensive system. The pyruvate dehydrogenase enzyme is suppressed in cancer — that's why dichloroacetate is gaining interest, as it reactivates that enzyme. But simply lowering the free phosphate in the cell tends to reactivate this crucial enzyme at the top of the energy-producing chain." A quart of [[orange-juice|orange juice]] per day was the baseline dietary application.

**Thyroid (T3)** activates PDH transcriptionally and suppresses PDK4 expression. Whether through dietary support of thyroid function (sufficient calories, sufficient carbohydrate, low PUFA) or through direct supplementation (Cynomel, Cynoplus, Armour Thyroid), the goal is to keep the cell's baseline PDH pool large enough that the kinase-phosphatase balance can favor activity.

**Insulin** activates PDP through phosphatidylinositol kinase signaling and downstream phosphatase activation. The dietary corollary — sufficient carbohydrate intake to keep insulin secretion brisk — is the same intervention Peat recommended for reversing the [[randle-cycle|Randle effect]] generally.

**Niacinamide** acts on PDH from two directions: it lowers circulating free fatty acids (reducing the substrate that drives PDK activation), and it supplies the NAD+ that E3 needs to regenerate the lipoyl arm. The vitamin's role in PDH reactivation is one of the substrates underneath Peat's broader claim that niacinamide is "useful in the whole spectrum of metabolic degenerative diseases" — [[regeneration-and-degeneration-types-of-inflammation-change-with-aging|*Regeneration and Degeneration*]].

**Thiamine (vitamin B1)** is the cofactor without which E1 cannot decarboxylate pyruvate. In refeeding contexts, in alcohol-dependent populations, in malnourished states, and in the elderly with reduced absorption, B1 deficiency is the proximate cause of PDH failure. Allithiamine and benfotiamine — fat-soluble derivatives that cross membranes more readily than the hydrochloride salt — are the supplemental forms with the best clinical evidence in diabetic neuropathy. The thiamine-derivative [[ttfd|TTFD (thiamine tetrahydrofurfuryl disulfide)]] has emerged in the past decade as an aggressive form for cases where ordinary thiamine supplementation is insufficient.

**PUFA avoidance** is the long-term substrate-level intervention. Lipid peroxidation products generated from membrane PUFA poison the electron transport chain downstream of PDH, raising the NADH-to-NAD+ ratio and activating PDK through that route in addition to the direct substrate-competition route. Reducing dietary PUFA over years allows the body's adipose stores to gradually become more saturated, and the steady-state PDK activation falls with the change in substrate.

**Aspirin** inhibits lipolysis (reducing the free fatty acid load that activates PDK) and shifts mitochondrial metabolism toward glucose oxidation through multiple downstream mechanisms. Peat recommended evening dosing specifically to counteract the rise in free fatty acids during overnight fasting.

**Carbon dioxide.** A cell producing CO2 from full glucose oxidation is producing the substance that stabilizes its own protein-water phase, binds amine groups on respiratory enzymes (the Lewis-acid chemistry developed at [[carbon-dioxide]]), and inhibits the lactate dehydrogenase that diverts pyruvate to lactate. The CO2 production rate and the PDH activity rate are tightly coupled — each reinforces the other. High-altitude adaptation, bag-breathing, and the metabolic warmth of a thyroid-sufficient body all run through the same coupling.

## Counter-arguments

Three lines of pushback against the framework above deserve explicit treatment.

**The mainstream oncology view of cancer metabolism.** The hypothesis that the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]] is causal in cancer (rather than a consequence of upstream oncogenic mutations) remains contested. The dominant view in molecular oncology since the 1990s has been that cancer is fundamentally a disease of genetic mutations affecting growth-control pathways, and that the metabolic shift Warburg observed is a downstream consequence of those mutations — particularly mutations in PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, in p53, in MYC. From this perspective, reactivating PDH might slow individual tumors but cannot cure cancer because the underlying genetic damage remains. The Peat framework inverts the causation: metabolic dysfunction (chronic Randle-style PDH suppression) is the substrate condition that makes oncogenic mutations clinically relevant; tissues with sufficient oxidative metabolism resist transformation even when mutations accumulate. Which framework is right is, in 2026, still empirically open. The recent resurgence of interest in metabolic interventions (ketogenic diets, fasting protocols, mitochondrial-targeted drugs) reflects the partial vindication of the metabolic view, but mainstream oncology still allocates the vast majority of research funding to genetic and immunologic approaches. The DCA story illustrates the funding dynamic — a cheap, off-patent metabolic intervention has not attracted phase III investment from companies whose business models depend on patented targeted therapies.

**DCA toxicity in chronic administration.** The clinical experience with DCA in patients with congenital lactic acidosis and in compassionate-use cancer trials has consistently identified peripheral neuropathy as the dose-limiting toxicity. The mechanism appears to involve depletion of glutathione conjugates and disruption of myelin synthesis through interference with normal lipoamide handling. Patients on chronic DCA at oncological doses (10-25 mg/kg/day) commonly develop a stocking-glove sensory neuropathy that progresses with continued dosing and resolves slowly after discontinuation. The drug also has variable pharmacokinetics — it inhibits its own metabolism by inhibiting glutathione-S-transferase zeta, so blood levels rise unpredictably during chronic dosing. These limitations have made it difficult to design large clinical trials with consistent dosing protocols, and they constrain the off-label use that has proliferated in the absence of FDA approval. The Peat framing — that simpler substrate-level interventions can achieve the same PDH reactivation without the toxicity — is consistent with what is known about the drug, but it has not been formally compared in head-to-head trials.

**The B-vitamin maximalism critique.** Some commentators in the broader nutrition space have argued that the role of B-vitamin cofactors in PDH activity is overstated, that mild deficiencies in well-nourished populations do not produce measurable changes in enzyme activity, and that high-dose supplementation (niacinamide at multi-gram daily doses, thiamine derivatives at 600+ mg, riboflavin at 400 mg) may have effects unrelated to enzyme cofactor saturation. The counter-counter is that the populations Peat described — under stress, hypothyroid, eating modern Western diets — are not "well-nourished" by the relevant biochemical criteria, and that the supplemental doses act on PDH through both cofactor saturation and downstream effects (niacinamide lowering free fatty acids, thiamine derivatives suppressing branched-chain amino acid catabolism, riboflavin protecting the flavoprotein subunits of E3 from oxidative damage). The clinical evidence is uneven and the optimal dosing strategies remain undersettled — but the mechanistic case for PDH dependence on the B-vitamin cofactor stack is biochemically secure.

## Data gaps

Several questions in the PDH literature remain open and deserve future research attention:

- **PDK isoform-specific interventions.** Mammalian cells express four PDK isoforms (PDK1-4) with different tissue distributions, substrate preferences, and regulatory inputs. PDK2 is widely expressed and constitutively active; PDK4 is highly responsive to fatty acid availability and dramatically upregulated in starvation, diabetes, and hypothyroidism. Whether interventions targeting specific PDK isoforms would be more effective than the broad-spectrum DCA approach is undersettled in the clinical literature.

- **The phosphate-fructose mechanism in detail.** Peat described fructose's PDH-reactivating effect as mediated by intracellular phosphate absorption. The biochemistry of how fructose-1-phosphate formation acts as a phosphate sink is well-characterized in liver hepatocytes, but the extension to muscle, brain, and tumor tissue (which have different fructose-metabolizing enzymes) is less clear. Whether the phosphate mechanism Peat invoked is the dominant pathway through which fructose activates PDH in non-hepatic tissues remains an open question.

- **The thyroid-PDK4 connection.** T3 suppresses PDK4 transcription, but the magnitude and time course of this suppression in human tissues are poorly quantified. Whether the PDH reactivation seen clinically with thyroid supplementation reflects PDK4 suppression specifically, or broader transcriptional effects on PDH subunits and mitochondrial biogenesis, has not been carefully dissected.

- **Methylene blue and PDH.** The mechanism of [[methylene-blue|methylene blue]]'s pro-oxidative effect on cellular metabolism is conventionally framed in terms of electron transport chain bypass, but the molecule also affects NAD+/NADH ratios in ways that should affect PDK activation through the standard regulatory pathway. The interaction between methylene blue and PDH activity has not been directly studied.

- **The Alzheimer's PDH inactivation mechanism.** The Ishiguro 1998 paper identified a kinase-mediated PDH inactivation process in Alzheimer's brain tissue, and subsequent work has implicated tau kinases and amyloid-beta-induced oxidative stress. The pharmacological reversal of this process — through DCA, through high-dose thiamine, through methylene blue, through niacinamide — has been tried in small clinical trials with inconsistent results. Whether large-scale, properly powered trials of PDH-targeted interventions would slow Alzheimer's progression remains an open question.

- **PDH in fertility and steroidogenesis.** Pregnenolone is synthesized from cholesterol in the mitochondrion by P450scc, and the same mitochondrial environment that supports robust PDH activity should also support robust pregnenolone synthesis. The connection between PDH activity, mitochondrial health, and downstream steroid hormone production is mechanistically suggestive but undersettled. Peat developed the connection narratively but the formal quantitative work is missing.
