# Detoxification

The word "detoxification" arrives in the wellness lexicon attached to juice fasts, infrared saunas, "liver cleanses," powdered binders, and chelation protocols, and on every one of these Ray Peat was either skeptical or outright opposed. His framework starts from a different premise. Detoxification is not a periodic event. It is the work an adequately fueled liver does every minute of every day — conjugating circulating estrogens, bile acids, drugs, and the bacterial metabolites continuously absorbed from the gut, attaching glucuronic or sulfuric acid to make them water-soluble, and dumping them into bile to be carried out in the stool. Peat's clearest statement of the position came in a 2011 *EastWest Healing* episode: "Experiments on cattle showed that while 30-35% had toxins in muscle and fat, only 6-7% had toxins in the liver. The liver has the equipment for excreting toxins. If you slow down the liver's activity — which happens in a fast — your brain and organs are exposed to chemicals diffusing out of fat and muscles. The best cleansing diet is one that increases your liver's action. Tasty food, sugar, and thyroid activate the liver." High [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]] *is* detoxification. The practices that follow from this premise are about lowering the toxin load the liver has to process and keeping the liver in the fed, [[thyroid|T3]]-supplied, sugar-supplied state it needs to do the conjugation work.

This article is the anchor that Peat's other detox-relevant pages assume. [[endotoxin]] explains where most of the daily toxin load originates (the gut, not the environment). [[liver]] catalogues the dietary inputs the conjugation enzymes need. [[estrogen-detox]] applies the framework to a single hormone class. This page describes the system: what "detoxification" actually means as a hepatic process, why the gut-liver axis is upstream of all of it, what the daily practices accomplish at the level of mechanism, and where Peat broke with the functional-medicine detox industry.

## What "Detoxification" Means in This Framework

The biochemistry textbook divides hepatic detoxification into three phases. Phase I, carried out by the [[cytochrome-p450|cytochrome P450]] enzyme family, oxidizes lipid-soluble compounds — drugs, alcohols, hormones, environmental contaminants — to give them a chemical handle. Phase II conjugation enzymes (glucuronyltransferases, sulfotransferases, glutathione-S-transferases) attach a polar molecule to that handle, converting the compound into a water-soluble metabolite that the liver can no longer reabsorb. Phase III transporters move the conjugated metabolite into bile or back into blood for renal excretion. The body has multiple parallel pathways for every category of compound; the kidney handles small water-soluble residues, the liver-bile-gut axis handles fat-soluble and hormonal residues, the lung exhales volatile metabolites including the [[carbon-dioxide]] produced by complete oxidation, and the skin handles a small fraction through sweat and sebum.

Peat used this conventional scaffolding but added an emphasis the textbook usually omits: every step is energy-dependent. Phase I oxidation consumes oxygen and reducing equivalents. Phase II conjugation consumes ATP (to activate glucuronic acid as UDP-glucuronate, to activate sulfate as PAPS, to maintain reduced glutathione). Phase III transport consumes ATP directly at the canalicular membrane. A liver running on inadequate glucose, low [[thyroid]], and a mitochondrial chain poisoned by stored [[PUFA]] cannot keep up with the load. The "toxin" problem is, at root, an energy problem.

This reframing has practical consequences. It explains why fasting produces a feeling of "toxin release" without actually clearing toxins (mobilizing fat-stored compounds into a liver that has slowed down). It explains why heavy alcohol drinkers with fatty livers cannot detoxify estrogen, leading to gynecomastia. It explains why hypothyroid women have heavy painful periods (a slow liver fails to clear estrogen between cycles). It explains why coffee, which the textbook treats as a stimulant, lowers liver enzymes and protects against liver disease in dozens of epidemiological studies — coffee supports the energy state of the hepatocyte. The chain that links every Peat detox recommendation is whether the intervention raises or lowers the liver's metabolic capacity.

What Peat explicitly did *not* mean by detoxification was the pop-wellness inventory: pulling heavy metals out with EDTA, sweating perfluorocarbons through the skin, drinking lemon water with cayenne to "flush" the liver, taking activated charcoal as a daily supplement, fasting to "give the body a rest," or running coffee enemas to scour the colon. He held varying positions on these from "mostly useless" to "actively harmful," developed below.

## The Hepatic Pathways: Phase I, II, III in Brief

**Phase I — oxidation by cytochrome P450.** The P450 enzyme family is a set of heme-containing, oxygen-using catalysts that introduce hydroxyl groups onto otherwise inert lipid-soluble molecules. The same enzyme family that activates [[pregnenolone]] from [[cholesterol]] also detoxifies caffeine, alcohol, prescription drugs, and environmental contaminants. Peat noted the dual role in his 1993 newsletter on multiple sclerosis: "Cytochrome P450scc... refers to the removal of the 6 carbon atoms that distinguish cholesterol from pregnenolone. Other Cyt P450 enzymes are important for their detoxifying oxidizing action, and some of these are involved in brain metabolism." The same heme machinery is doing steroidogenesis and toxin-clearance, which is why anything that suppresses heme synthesis (estrogen excess, iron overload, lead exposure) simultaneously impairs hormone production and detoxification.

The unintuitive feature of Phase I is that its products are usually *more* reactive than the parent compound. Hydroxylation produces an intermediate that is chemically primed for Phase II conjugation but, if Phase II is bottlenecked, can do oxidative damage on its way through. This is the basis for the "Phase II must outrun Phase I" claim that floats through functional-medicine literature. Peat did not frame the problem in those exact terms, but his recommendation set converges on the same conclusion: feed the conjugation enzymes adequately and the Phase I intermediates do not accumulate.

**Phase II — conjugation.** The four practically important conjugations are glucuronidation (estrogens, bilirubin, many drugs), sulfation (steroid hormones, phenols, neurotransmitters), glutathione conjugation (reactive electrophiles, heavy metals via glutathione-bound transport), and glycine conjugation (benzoate residues and salicylate from [[aspirin]]). Glucuronidation is the workhorse for hormone clearance and the pathway most disrupted by hepatic energy deficits. The required cofactor is UDP-glucuronate, which is built from UDP-glucose, which requires adequate dietary carbohydrate. A hypoglycemic or fasting liver cannot generate enough UDP-glucuronate to conjugate the estrogen reaching it from the periphery, which is why Peat insisted that low-carb diets produce an estrogen-recirculation problem regardless of total estrogen production.

Sulfation depends on inorganic sulfate, which the body builds from sulfur-containing amino acids (methionine and cysteine). Adequate animal protein, especially whey and well-cooked meat, supplies the substrate. Glutathione conjugation depends on glutathione, which is built from cysteine, glycine, and glutamate. The glycine fraction is the limiting one for most people on a meat-heavy modern diet — muscle meat is rich in tryptophan and methionine and poor in glycine, while [[gelatin]] (collagen) is roughly one-third glycine by amino acid weight. This is the unspoken biochemistry behind Peat's bone-broth and gelatin emphasis: the liver runs out of glycine before it runs out of cysteine, and glycine-poor diets bottleneck glutathione conjugation.

**Phase III — biliary excretion and the gut-liver hand-off.** Once a compound is conjugated, the canalicular transporters (MRP2, BSEP, others) push it into bile against a concentration gradient. Bile is produced in the hepatocyte, stored in the gallbladder, and dumped into the small intestine when fat enters the duodenum. The conjugated compounds ride the bile down the intestinal tract and are excreted in the stool — *if* the gut behaves. This is where the system most often fails, and where most of Peat's actual interventions land.

## The Gut-Liver Axis Is Upstream of Everything

The mainstream model treats "toxins" as external invaders to be flushed out. Peat's model treats most of the daily toxin load as endogenous — produced inside the body by the bacteria in the lower intestine and continuously absorbed back into portal blood. This is the [[endotoxin]] thesis applied to detoxification. The full mechanism lives at the [[endotoxin]] hub; the relevant compressed version here is that gram-negative bacteria in the gut produce lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as part of their cell wall, that LPS leaks across the intestinal barrier in proportion to inflammation and slow transit, and that the liver spends a substantial fraction of its detoxification capacity neutralizing this internally generated load before it has any capacity left for external compounds.

Two specific gut-liver loops matter. The first is the enterohepatic circulation of bile acids. The liver dumps bile into the small intestine; the small intestine reabsorbs roughly 95% of those bile acids in the ileum and returns them to the liver via portal blood; the liver reconjugates and re-secretes them. A healthy loop is fine. A dysbiotic loop is not — bacterial enzymes deconjugate the bile acids in the lower intestine, producing secondary bile acids (deoxycholate, lithocholate) that are inflammatory and potentially carcinogenic. Peat positioned [[activated-charcoal|activated charcoal]] as an emergency intervention here: it binds bile acids in the gut, interrupts the loop, and forces the liver to synthesize new bile from cholesterol rather than recycling the deconjugated stock.

The second loop is the enterohepatic circulation of estrogen. The liver conjugates estradiol and estrone, dumps them into bile, and the small intestine excretes them — *unless* bacterial beta-glucuronidase, produced in excess under dysbiotic conditions, cleaves the glucuronic acid back off in the lower intestine. The free estrogen is then reabsorbed through the intestinal wall and goes back into circulation. Peat described the consequence directly: "The estrogen that goes into the bile tends to be reabsorbed if there's not enough fiber in the diet. So, keeping a steady supply of fiber running through the intestine allows the liver to get rid of the bile-excreted estrogen." The fiber that does this work is specifically the indigestible fiber of raw root vegetables — most prominently the [[carrot-salad|daily raw carrot]] — not the fermentable fiber of bran, psyllium, or "prebiotic" supplements, which feed the bacteria producing the deconjugating enzymes in the first place.

This is the reason Peat treated bowel cleanliness as the priority: most "toxin load" is endogenous, and the practical lever is whether the small intestine is sterile and the colon transits quickly. Sweating in saunas, chelating heavy metals, and "supporting Phase II" with NAC and milk thistle address peripheral issues while the dominant input continues unimpeded. Fix the gut and the liver's job gets dramatically easier.

## Peat's Preferred Detox Practices

The interventions Peat recommended come in two tiers: the daily baseline that everyone above his metabolic-foundation line should be doing, and targeted additions for specific situations. The baseline is the [[anti-stress-diet]] applied with detoxification in mind.

**Raw carrot salad, daily.** Peat's signature intervention, eaten every day for over twenty years. A medium raw carrot, scraped or julienned lengthwise, tossed with a small amount of vinegar, coconut or olive oil, and salt, eaten once a day. The non-fermentable fiber in raw carrot binds conjugated estrogen in the bile and physically drags it out before bacterial beta-glucuronidase can deconjugate it. The carrot also has mild antimicrobial action in the small intestine — root vegetables resist mold and bacterial decay underground, and the same compounds disinfect the gut on the way through. Peat: "The fiber binds to the estrogen which your liver is producing and excreting in the bile, preventing it from being reabsorbed. You can see a decrease in your estrogen level a day or two after eating a raw carrot." If the carrot is cooked, the fiber structure breaks down and the binding effect is lost — it must be raw. See [[carrot-salad]] for the full preparation rationale.

**Adequate sugar — specifically ripe fruit, fruit juices, milk, and honey.** Sugar is the substrate for UDP-glucuronate synthesis, the cofactor for glucuronidation. It is also the substrate for fructose-driven liver detoxification of alcohol and other compounds. Peat returned to this point repeatedly: "Many people have been showing that fructose detoxifies many things that enter the liver, including alcohol. It can almost double the rate of destruction of alcohol and prevent liver damage in the process. Many other toxins are detoxified in the presence of fructose." The conventional advice to "rest the liver" by reducing sugar is the exact opposite of what a stressed liver needs. The hepatocyte runs Phase II on glucose-derived cofactors; deprive it of glucose and the conjugation enzymes stall. See [[fructose]] and [[orange-juice]].

**Adequate protein — with glycine emphasis.** Roughly 100 grams of high-quality protein daily for most adults, with at least a third of it from glycine-rich sources ([[gelatin]], bone broth, slow-cooked cuts with skin and connective tissue, [[cheese]]) to feed the glutathione conjugation pathway. Muscle meat alone, while rich in tryptophan and methionine, leaves the liver glycine-limited. Peat's standard recipe was 100 grams of mixed protein with substantial gelatin daily, plus a weekly serving of liver for the B-vitamins (especially B1 and B2) that drive Phase I and the vitamin A required for thyroid function.

**Coffee.** Peat positioned coffee as a unique liver-supportive substance: it lowers ALT and AST in epidemiological data, reduces liver cancer incidence, supports hepatic clearance of heavy metals. From a 2021 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode: "Coffee is the only one I feel safe with. It has been shown to reduce the bound heavy metals in kidneys and other crucial organs, especially the liver. Many studies show that coffee drinkers have low ALT, which is an indicator of liver stress. Heavy coffee drinkers have an extremely low incidence of all kinds of liver diseases, including liver cancer." The mechanism is multiple — caffeine raises metabolic rate, coffee polyphenols induce Phase II conjugation enzymes, the bitter compounds stimulate bile flow. Drink it with cream and sugar to avoid the cortisol bump that black coffee on an empty stomach can produce. See [[coffee]].

**Daily walking and warmth.** Walking is the metabolic activity Peat consistently endorsed — long enough to raise body temperature without raising cortisol. Warmth itself supports detoxification: cold suppresses thyroid, slows the liver, and chronically activates the stress hormones that drive estrogen production. The mechanism is not sweat-as-toxin-elimination (Peat dismissed the volume of toxins exiting through sweat as trivially small). The mechanism is that a warm body has higher mitochondrial activity, which produces the ATP that Phase II conjugation requires.

**Salt and sugar to lower cortisol.** Cortisol catabolizes tissue, blocks T4-to-T3 conversion in the liver, and degrades the very conjugation enzymes detoxification depends on. Peat: "Although a physiologically balanced amount of cortisol induces enzymes of detoxification, for example in the intestine, an unopposed excess causes destruction of these enzymes, eliminating much of the intestine's barrier function, and leading to allergies." Adequate dietary salt prevents the aldosterone-cortisol surge that low-salt diets produce. Adequate dietary sugar between meals prevents the hypoglycemia-driven adrenaline-cortisol cycle. The result is a hepatocyte that retains its enzyme inventory.

**Adequate [[progesterone]] (especially in women).** The liver cannot clear estrogen at the rate it needs to without thyroid hormone, and progesterone is the hormone that opposes estrogen at the cellular level so that whatever does recirculate produces less damage. Progesterone is built from cholesterol, which requires saturated fat in the diet and adequate thyroid function. Low-fat dieting kneecaps endogenous progesterone production. For women with documented estrogen dominance, topical progesterone (typically 30 to 100 mg daily, often via a vitamin-E base) addresses the cellular response while the liver works on clearance. See [[progesterone]] and [[estrogen-detox]].

**Occasional cooked vegetables — bamboo shoots, well-cooked mushrooms, peeled potatoes.** When carrot fatigue sets in (an issue after years of daily eating), [[bamboo-shoots]] and well-cooked [[mushrooms]] supply similar non-fermentable, antiseptic fiber. Mushrooms must be cooked at least an hour to destroy agaritine; raw or lightly cooked mushrooms are not safe. Cooked greens (kale, spinach, chard) are acceptable for occasional variety but not central — their oxalate content and fermentable fractions make them lower-leverage than the carrot.

**[[Cascara-sagrada|Cascara sagrada]] or magnesium when constipated.** Constipation is detoxification's most common failure mode — it lengthens the contact time between conjugated estrogen and bacterial beta-glucuronidase, lengthens exposure to deconjugated bile acids, and increases endotoxin absorption. Peat endorsed cascara sagrada as a gentle herbal laxative and reversed the conventional warning that long-term use causes "melanosis coli" (he attributed the pigmentation to chronic constipation itself, not the herb that relieves it). Magnesium supplementation in adequate doses (300 to 600 mg daily as magnesium glycinate or chloride) also normalizes transit. Target at least one complete bowel movement daily, preferably two.

## What Sabotages Detoxification

The corollary list is the set of conditions that overwhelm hepatic capacity and let the toxin load accumulate.

**Tissue [[PUFA]] loading.** Polyunsaturated fatty acids stored in adipose tissue from decades of seed oil consumption do two things to detoxification. First, when mobilized (during stress, fasting, weight loss), they flood the liver with reactive substrates the [[cytochrome-c-oxidase|respiratory chain]] cannot process cleanly, producing [[lipid-peroxidation]] products that damage the conjugation enzymes themselves. Second, the same PUFA in cell membranes including the hepatocyte's own mitochondrial membranes degrades the ATP-producing capacity that conjugation depends on. The tissue half-life of stored linoleic acid is roughly two years; full clearance takes about four. Detoxification capacity rises slowly through that window as the cell substitutes saturated and monounsaturated fats. See [[pufa-depletion]].

**Undereating.** Skipping meals, low-calorie dieting, and intermittent fasting all reduce the substrate available for Phase II conjugation. The most measurable consequence is estrogen recirculation: dieting women routinely report worsening PMS, breast tenderness, and weight rebound, all consistent with a liver that has slowed below the rate of estrogen production. Peat: "Dieters used to go on very low-calorie diets or fasts and find they could lose 10 or 12 pounds quickly. Someone did a study analyzing this and found that on an extremely low-calorie diet, the weight loss was about 80% protein and only 15% or 20% fat. But if they took a moderate amount of nutrition — enough to reduce the stress hormones — they could lose 80% fat and only 15% or 20% protein tissue."

**Hypothyroidism.** Roughly two-thirds of the body's active T3 is produced not in the thyroid gland but in the liver via T4-to-T3 conversion, which requires adequate glucose and selenium. A liver short on either produces less T3, which means lower metabolic rate, which means slower Phase II conjugation, which means more endogenous toxin accumulation, which further burdens the liver. The loop is self-reinforcing. Restoring thyroid function (warm hands, pulse 75 to 85, T3 in the upper third of the reference range) is often enough to restart detoxification on its own. See [[hypothyroid-recovery]].

**Regular alcohol.** Ethanol consumes Phase I and Phase II capacity preferentially over other compounds, displaces estrogen clearance, and inflames the gut wall, all of which raise the endogenous toxin load. The detoxifying capacity of fructose (above) partly compensates for occasional moderate drinking, but daily alcohol overwhelms the system at any meaningful dose. [[Beer]] is the worst case because brewer's yeast produces actual estradiol that adds directly to the estrogen burden.

**Constipation.** Per above — every additional hour of contact between bile and bacterial enzymes worsens estrogen recirculation, bile acid deconjugation, and endotoxin absorption.

**High [[estrogen]] (in women, especially perimenopausal).** Estrogen itself slows hepatic clearance by binding albumin and competing with other substrates for conjugation enzymes. The excess that the liver cannot clear recirculates and worsens the next cycle. The lever is upstream — restore [[progesterone]], reduce aromatase via aspirin and coffee, eliminate [[xenoestrogens]], and the load drops.

**Chronic stress.** Sustained cortisol does what unopposed cortisol always does: catabolizes the conjugation enzymes themselves, weakens the intestinal barrier (raising endotoxin absorption), and suppresses T4-to-T3 conversion. The intervention is the metabolic baseline — adequate salt, sugar, protein, sleep — rather than direct cortisol manipulation.

**Polyphenolic antioxidant supplements at supplement doses.** Green tea extract, resveratrol, quercetin, milk thistle silybin and the like are frequently estrogenic and frequently inhibit P450 enzymes at concentrations that occur with supplementation but not with whole-food consumption. The mainstream functional-medicine "Phase II support" stack often slows the very pathways it claims to support. Peat was consistent on this: the compounds are tolerable as foods, problematic as isolates.

## Heavy Metals, Environmental Toxins, and the "Detox Protocol" Industry

This is the section where Peat broke decisively with functional medicine. The standard functional-medicine detox model identifies external toxin classes — mercury, lead, glyphosate, mycotoxins, microplastics — and proposes mobilizing them with chelators, binders, and "drainage support" while sweating, fasting, or running coffee enemas. Peat treated almost all of this as either useless or harmful, for reasons that follow from the energy framework.

**Heavy metals.** Peat acknowledged that lead, mercury, cadmium, and aluminum accumulate in tissues. He treated chelation therapy as a last resort for documented poisoning. The risk is that mobilizing metals from bone or fat faster than the liver can clear them simply redistributes the burden — often to the kidney, sometimes to the brain. The protective approach is twofold: prevent further accumulation (filtered water, no leaching cookware, no contaminated supplements), and support the endogenous chelators already running. Calcium suppresses lead absorption from the gut. Coffee reduces bound heavy metals in liver and kidney. Adequate glycine drives glutathione conjugation. Magnesium displaces aluminum from binding sites.

**Glyphosate and environmental chemicals.** Real but downstream of metabolic state. A liver running well, with adequate glycine and cysteine for Phase II, clears low-level pesticide exposure without difficulty. The detox protocol Peat never endorsed is one that adds binders (chlorella, modified citrus pectin, zeolite) to a metabolically suppressed person on a low-calorie diet — the protocol mobilizes more than it clears.

**Microplastics.** The lipid-soluble plasticizers (BPA, phthalates) get cleared through hepatic glucuronidation, so the same interventions apply. Plastic particles themselves are not detoxified; they are either excreted in stool if swallowed or deposited in tissue if absorbed. Reducing exposure (no plastic water bottles, no microwaving in plastic, no plastic-lined cans) is the only meaningful intervention. Sweating them out does not work.

**Fasting as detox.** Peat treated this as the most pernicious wellness-detox claim. The "toxin release" people experience during fasts is real — but what is being released is the contents of adipose tissue (PUFA, fat-soluble pesticides, lipophilic drugs and metabolites, fat-stored hormones) into a liver that has simultaneously slowed because it lacks the glucose to run conjugation enzymes at capacity. The result is symptomatic — headaches, brain fog, fatigue, body odor — but the toxin load reaches the circulation faster than the kidney and bile can handle it. Peat: "If you slow down the liver's activity — which happens in a fast — your brain and organs are exposed to chemicals diffusing out of fat and muscles." See [[intermittent-fasting]].

**Saunas and sweat.** Peat acknowledged sweat as a minor route of clearance but treated the sauna-as-detox claim as marketing rather than physiology. The volume of toxin clearance through sweat is dwarfed by the volume through bile and urine. Sauna's real benefits — circulation, hormetic heat stress, mild metabolic boost — are unrelated to detoxification per se.

**Coffee enemas.** Mixed Peat treatment. He acknowledged the early twentieth-century Gerson-era evidence that enemas relieved cancer symptoms. He did not recommend them as routine practice — the risk of disrupting colonic flora and creating dependence on mechanical evacuation outweighs the benefit for most people who simply need to fix transit time and reduce endotoxin load.

## Tracking Signal: What "Working Detoxification" Looks Like

Peat consistently directed people to body signals rather than blood panels for tracking metabolic state, and detoxification is no exception. The signals to watch:

- **Clear, pale urine without strong odor.** Concentrated dark urine indicates dehydration or kidney stress; strongly malodorous urine indicates either dietary mismatch (asparagus, coffee) or metabolic backup. The baseline target is pale yellow, near-odorless urine throughout the day.
- **Daily, complete bowel movements.** One to two formed, easy bowel movements per day, no straining, no urgency, no incomplete evacuation. Stool color brown but not dark (very dark stool indicates excess bile reabsorption; very pale stool indicates inadequate bile flow). Stool odor minimal — strong odor indicates bacterial fermentation in the lower bowel, which is the endotoxin problem.
- **No persistent body odor.** Sour-smelling sweat, strong armpit odor, foot odor, or strong vaginal odor all indicate either dietary mismatch or bacterial overgrowth — both correctable by the gut-axis interventions above. People doing the protocol correctly report odor reduction within weeks.
- **Calm mood, stable energy.** The neurological signs of detoxification working are subtle but consistent: less brain fog after meals, more even energy across the day, less reactivity to stressors. The mechanism is that a liver clearing estrogen and endotoxin is also lowering the cortisol-adrenaline drive that produces irritability.
- **Warm hands and feet.** The metabolic-rate marker that crosses every Peat protocol. A liver running well on adequate thyroid, glucose, and protein is paired with a body that holds temperature at the extremities. Cold hands at the start of a day on adequate calories suggest the liver is still under-fueled.
- **For women, lighter and shorter periods.** Estrogen-recirculation problems show up as heavy, prolonged, painful menstruation. The protocol's effect on flow is often the first measurable signal — frequently visible within one to two cycles.

Lab markers, when they are available, are secondary. Lower ALT and AST (indicating less hepatocyte stress), lower estradiol with higher progesterone in women, lower prolactin, normalized TSH with T3 in the upper third of the reference range, and lower LDL with normalized HDL are all consistent with the protocol working. None of them is a substitute for the body signals.

## Counter-arguments

The functional-medicine detox industry, the sauna-and-binders camp, and the fasting-as-detox camp all run protocols that Peat's framework predicts will produce short-term symptom shifts and long-term metabolic deterioration. Each deserves explicit attention because each has substantial popular followings and credentialed proponents.

**The functional-medicine model (Pizzorno, Hyman, Bland).** Frames detoxification around isolated Phase I/II cofactors — NAC, milk thistle, broccoli sprouts, sulforaphane, methylated B-vitamins — combined with elimination diets and periodic fasting. The strength is the accurate identification of conjugation enzymes as the bottleneck. The weakness is that it ignores the energy state of the hepatocyte. Adding NAC to a hypothyroid, low-calorie, high-PUFA patient supplies a Phase II cofactor to a cell that cannot generate the ATP to use it. Where Peat agreed: glycine and sulfur are limiting, fiber matters. Where he pushed back: the energy state is upstream of all of it.

**The sauna-and-binders camp (Klinghardt, Shoemaker, mold-illness literature).** Treats environmental toxin exposure — mold mycotoxins, plastics, "biotoxins" — as the dominant driver of chronic illness and prescribes aggressive sweating, cholestyramine sequestration, and infrared sauna programs. The merit is taking environmental toxicity seriously; the deficit is under-weighting the endogenous gut-derived load. A patient who removes themselves from mold, fixes the gut with carrot and adequate sugar, and restores thyroid function recovers with or without the sauna program.

**Fasting-as-detox (autophagy literature, Fung, Longo).** The autophagy mechanism is real; the inference that fasting is therefore optimal detox is the leap Peat rejected. Autophagy is a stress response — the cell cannibalizes healthy components alongside damaged ones. Mobilizing stored PUFA and fat-soluble compounds into a liver running on depleted UDP-glucuronate produces the symptomatic "detox crisis" practitioners interpret as therapeutic. For a typical modern person with decades of seed oil in adipose tissue, repeated fasting redistributes toxins faster than it clears them.

**The chelation industry (IV EDTA, DMSA, DMPS).** Appropriate for documented poisoning under medical supervision; not supported as routine wellness intervention diagnosed by hair analysis or provocation testing. Real risks of kidney injury and redistribution to the brain. Peat's position: treat documented poisoning, do not chelate speculatively, focus on preventing further accumulation.

The pattern across all four camps is the same: correctly identifies a real piece of physiology, then under-weights the metabolic foundation. The Peat correction is not to reject the pieces but to subordinate them to the energy state.

## Data Gaps

- **PUFA clearance kinetics in individuals.** The two-year tissue half-life of linoleic acid is an average; individual variation is large and depends on metabolic rate, total PUFA load, and rate of new exposure. There is no easy clinical test for current adipose PUFA composition, which makes it hard to track depletion progress in individual patients.
- **Phase II enzyme polymorphisms.** Substantial genetic variation in UGT, SULT, and GST families produces real differences in conjugation capacity between individuals. Peat's framework treats the variation as one more reason to keep the metabolic state high enough to compensate, but the individualization implications are underexplored.
- **Microbiome composition and beta-glucuronidase activity.** Beta-glucuronidase is produced by specific gram-negative species (notably *Clostridium* and *Bacteroides* lineages), and the population dynamics depend on diet, antibiotics, and host immune state. The intervention literature is dominated by probiotic supplementation, which Peat was skeptical of; the prebiotic literature (which he opposed) is even more dominant. The actual mechanism by which the carrot-salad-plus-fermentable-fiber-elimination diet shifts the microbiome over months is not characterized in detail.
- **Phase I induction by coffee polyphenols.** Coffee's protective effect against liver disease is empirically robust but mechanistically multiple. The relative contributions of caffeine, chlorogenic acid, melanoidins, and the still-unidentified compounds in unfiltered coffee are not fully separated.
- **Heavy metal redistribution risk during chelation.** The clinical literature on chelation is dominated by case series rather than controlled trials, and the redistribution-to-brain concern is plausible mechanistically but poorly quantified.
