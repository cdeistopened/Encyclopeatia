# Immune System

The mainstream view of immunity centers on specificity—antibodies recognizing particular invaders, vaccines training the system to remember specific threats. Ray Peat's framework, drawing on Metchnikoff's original research, treats immunity as fundamentally a metabolic function: the body's ability to identify, engulf, and eliminate foreign or damaged material depends on the same oxidative [[metabolism]] that defines health in every other context. When metabolism is adequate, immunity is robust. When metabolism fails—from [[hypothyroidism]], [[PUFA]] accumulation, or [[estrogen]] excess—immunity fails with it, producing both susceptibility to infection and the autoimmune conditions that arise from disordered immune regulation.

## Metchnikoff's Phagocytes

Peat consistently credited Elie Metchnikoff, not Paul Ehrlich, as the researcher who understood immunity correctly. In a [[2011-12-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-questions-and-answers-ii|2011 episode]], he described how "Metchnikoff discovered phagocytes over 100 years ago—white blood cells that engulf and eat foreign material." This cellular immunity—where active cells physically consume pathogens and debris—is the older, more fundamental immune mechanism.

Ehrlich's competing framework—specific antibodies binding to specific antigens like a lock and key—became dominant because it was more amenable to pharmaceutical intervention (vaccines, immunoglobulins, monoclonal antibodies). But as Peat's newsletter on the implications of inflammation argued, the reductionist "lock-and-key" model misses the metabolic context that determines whether immune cells function properly.

In a [[2011-01-18-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-inflammation|2011 episode on inflammation]], Peat emphasized that the phagocytic system does not require prior exposure to function—it recognizes damaged and foreign material on energetic and structural grounds. This innate immunity is the body's primary defense, and it depends entirely on the metabolic competence of the white blood cells performing it.

## The Thymus: Metabolic Organ

The thymus gland—which trains T-lymphocytes and regulates immune tolerance—is exquisitely sensitive to metabolic status. As Peat's newsletter on autoimmunity described, the thymus "shrinks with aging, and this shrinkage is associated with autoimmune diseases." The causes of thymic atrophy are the same factors that suppress metabolism generally: radiation, PUFA, estrogen, and environmental toxins.

In a [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 episode on endocrinology]], Peat explained that "estrogen shrinks the thymus via cortisol." The mechanism is indirect but powerful: estrogen elevates cortisol, and cortisol is directly toxic to thymic tissue. This explains why immune function declines with aging in parallel with rising estrogen and cortisol—the thymus, the organ responsible for immune education, is being progressively destroyed by the stress hormones that characterize metabolic decline.

## PUFA and Immunosuppression

[[PUFA]]'s immunosuppressive effects were documented before their current promotion as "health foods." As Peat noted, researchers in the 1960s found that polyunsaturated fats prolonged graft survival—meaning they suppressed the immune system powerfully enough to prevent organ rejection. This is the same immune system responsible for eliminating cancer cells, clearing infections, and preventing autoimmune reactions through proper regulation.

"The polyunsaturated fats work by exactly the same mechanism that X-rays do: creating an immune deficiency and an inability to produce inflammation," Peat observed. The appearance of "anti-inflammatory" effects from PUFA is actually immunosuppression—the body loses its ability to mount the inflammatory response that is the first stage of healing.

## Inflammation: Necessary But Dangerous

Peat distinguished sharply between acute inflammation (a necessary healing response) and chronic inflammation (a pathological state). In a [[2011-12-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-questions-and-answers-ii|2011 episode]], he noted that "prenatal repair occurs without inflammation"—the fetus heals tissues through regeneration rather than inflammation because its metabolic environment (low PUFA, high progesterone, efficient oxidation) does not require the inflammatory intermediate.

After birth, as PUFA accumulates and metabolism declines, the body increasingly relies on inflammation as part of its repair process. The swelling, redness, and pain of inflammation represent a less efficient healing mode—one that can become self-perpetuating when the metabolic conditions that should resolve it (adequate thyroid, low PUFA, protective steroids) are absent.

As the newsletter on immunity, hormones, and yeast infections described, "Free radical oxidation is central to immunity"—referencing Koch, Szent-Gyorgyi, and Warburg's understanding that immune cells kill pathogens through oxidative bursts. The immune system's killing mechanism depends on the same oxidative metabolism that defines cellular health. Without adequate oxidative capacity, immune cells cannot function.

## Autoimmunity: Metabolic Disorder

Autoimmune diseases—where the immune system attacks the body's own tissues—represent disordered immune regulation rather than an overactive immune system. In Peat's framework, autoimmunity results from the same metabolic dysfunction that causes immunodeficiency: thymic atrophy (reducing the T-regulatory cells that prevent self-attack), endotoxin exposure (creating chronic immune activation), and estrogen excess (stimulating inappropriate immune responses).

As Peat discussed in his [[2021-12-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-hormones-behind-inflammation|2021 episode on inflammation]], "Endotoxin plus estrogen equals autoimmunity." The combination of intestinal endotoxin absorption (from poor [[digestion]]) and estrogen dominance creates an immune environment primed for self-attack. Addressing the metabolic basis—improving gut function, reducing estrogen, supporting thyroid—often improves autoimmune conditions more effectively than the immunosuppressive drugs used conventionally.

## Vaccines in Context

Peat's newsletter on vaccination contexts raised concerns about adjuvants—the immune-stimulating substances added to vaccines. "Adjuvants produce allergies," he noted, pointing to the phenomenon of "polio provocation syndrome" where vaccination during an acute illness could worsen outcomes.

His concern was not opposition to the concept of immunity but skepticism toward interventions that stimulate the immune system artificially while ignoring the metabolic conditions that determine whether immunity functions properly. A well-nourished organism with adequate thyroid, low PUFA, and functional thymic tissue responds effectively to infections through its innate immune capacity. An organism whose metabolism is suppressed and whose thymus is atrophied responds poorly regardless of how many antibodies it has been trained to produce.

## Supporting Immune Function

**[[Thyroid]] support** is foundational—it drives the oxidative metabolism that immune cells depend on and protects the thymus from cortisol-induced atrophy.

**PUFA elimination** removes the immunosuppressive dietary factor and allows immune cells to produce the oxidative bursts needed to kill pathogens.

**[[Vitamin A]]** supports epithelial barriers (skin, mucous membranes) and immune cell differentiation.

**[[Vitamin D]]** (from sunlight and diet) supports immune regulation, particularly the T-regulatory cells that prevent autoimmunity.

**Adequate protein** provides the amino acids needed for antibody production and immune cell proliferation.

**Intestinal hygiene** (raw carrot, reducing bacterial overgrowth) limits the endotoxin that creates chronic immune activation.

**[[Progesterone]]** opposes the estrogen that drives thymic atrophy and autoimmune activation.
