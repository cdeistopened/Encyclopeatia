# Semiconduction in Biology

Proteins, in twentieth-century biochemistry's settled picture, are catalysts — accelerators of specific reactions at specific active sites, the rest of the molecule treated as inert scaffolding. Long-range electronic effects, when admitted at all, were exotic curiosities of photosynthesis.

Albert Szent-Györgyi proposed in a 1941 *Nature* note, expanded at the Koranyi Memorial Lecture and published in *Science* the same year, that this picture missed the point. Proteins, he argued, have an energy-band structure analogous to crystalline semiconductors — a filled valence band, a forbidden gap, and a conduction band into which electrons can be excited and through which they can move over distances much greater than any single active site. The biological function of a protein depends on its electronic structure the way a transistor's function depends on its band gap and doping. The active site is a doping defect in an electronic medium that does the heavy lifting.

The proposal arrived a decade before the transistor and the first crystallographic protein structures. It was received as speculation, ignored, and survived as a fringe interest among theoretical biophysicists for thirty years. By the 1970s experimental work had vindicated the framework in pieces — solid-state conductivity on dry and hydrated proteins, EPR signals from delocalized unpaired electrons, aromatic charge-transfer complexes, and long-range electron-tunneling at the photosynthetic reaction center and the respiratory chain. The strong claim — that the cytoplasm as a whole functions as a doped semiconductor — was never fully confirmed. But the framework persisted as the substrate Peat built his clinical medicine on top of. What [[albert-szent-gyorgyi]] gave Peat at the biographical level, this article gives at the mechanistic: how electrons move in living material, the evidence for and against it, and the practical consequences that follow if it is roughly correct.

## The 1941 Proposal

Szent-Györgyi's 1941 reasoning was driven by an arithmetic problem in respiration. Hopkins's dehydrogenation chains implied electrons traveling long distances inside the cell; the known enzyme-substrate complexes could not account for the speed and coordination observed. Szent-Györgyi argued that the regular spacing of conjugated double bonds in the peptide backbone, the inductive effects of bound substrates, and the speed of nervous conduction together suggested that proteins were solid-state electronic devices — the backbone's alternating single and double bonds forming a delocalized π-electron system analogous to a one-dimensional crystal, with valence and conduction bands separated by a gap small enough that thermal or metabolic excitation could push electrons into the conduction band.

Peat compressed the argument in his article [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]]: "Albert Szent-Györgyi was probably the first person to seriously investigate the semiconducting properties of living material. Since he was aware of W.F. Koch's idea of a free radical catalyst to support oxidative metabolism, his suggestion in 1941 that cellular proteins could function as electrical conductors (or semiconductors) was very likely based on his research in cellular respiration, as well as on his work with muscle proteins." The proposal joined two claims incompatible with the biochemistry of the day: that the unpaired electrons Koch's clinical work had implicated in normal respiration were load-bearing rather than toxic, and that they moved through the protein matrix the way charge carriers move through doped germanium — by delocalized propagation through a band of accessible states, not by hopping between localized orbitals.

The reception was polite dismissal. Solid-state physics in 1941 was not yet a discipline — the Bardeen-Brattain-Shockley transistor was six years away — and few biochemists had the training to evaluate the claim. The Hopkins-style dehydrogenation chain became the textbook picture, refined by Krebs and Lipmann into the citric acid cycle and the chemiosmotic theory of ATP synthesis. The semiconductor picture was filed under "interesting but unsupported."

## Experimental Vindication

The first measurements supporting Szent-Györgyi's claim came from solid-state physics rather than biology. Dry crystalline proteins, measured with the same techniques applied to inorganic semiconductors, conducted electricity weakly but measurably — conductivities increasing on light excitation and varying with temperature in the way doped semiconductors do. Eley's measurements on dry hemoglobin and cytochrome c in the 1950s established conductivities in the 10⁻¹² to 10⁻⁹ siemens per centimeter range, with band gaps of 2 to 3 electron volts — values consistent with intrinsic semiconductors. Hydration lowered the effective band gap and increased carrier mobility, suggesting the living protein-water system was substantially better than the dry crystal.

Robert Mulliken's charge-transfer-complex work in the 1950s and 1960s gave Szent-Györgyi's "D-A pair" framework experimental footing. Aromatic electron donors (indoles, phenols, anilines) form measurable complexes with aromatic acceptors (quinones, dicarbonyls, certain dyes) in which an electron is shared between the two molecules in a delocalized excited state, detectable by characteristic absorption spectra and by electron paramagnetic resonance. Szent-Györgyi's *Electronic Biology and Cancer* (1976) built on this framework, arguing that ATP, NADH, ascorbate, and methylglyoxal — the small-molecule cofactors of metabolism — were the dopants determining the electronic state of the cellular protein lattice.

EPR applied to biological samples in the 1960s and 1970s detected long-lived unpaired electrons in respiring tissue — stable paramagnetic species localized to specific protein components. The mitochondrial electron transport chain proved to be the cleanest case. The iron-sulfur clusters of Complex I, the semiquinone intermediates of ubiquinone, the copper centers of cytochrome c oxidase — all produced characteristic EPR signals reporting the presence and state of bound electrons traveling through the respiratory apparatus. Hopkins's "electron pool" had become measurable, and Peat referred to it as the "Hopkins–Szent-Györgyi–Racker electron pool" — the inventory of labile electrons held in protein structures, ready to flow to acceptors when the cell's metabolic state called for it.

The most decisive vindication came from photosynthesis research. Reaction centers of green plants and photosynthetic bacteria were shown to move electrons over 20 to 30 angstroms in picoseconds, through chains of bound cofactors embedded in protein. The mechanism — quantum-mechanical tunneling between fixed donor and acceptor sites — was not exactly Szent-Györgyi's band-theory proposal, but it confirmed the more general claim that proteins are active electronic media, not inert scaffolds. The further claim that this happened throughout the cytoplasm rather than only in specialized photosynthetic and respiratory complexes remained contested, but the existence of the phenomenon in any biological context closed the door on the strict "catalysts only" position.

## What Conducts and What Doesn't

The modern reading distinguishes three kinds of biological electron transfer, only one of which is genuinely semiconductive in Szent-Györgyi's sense.

**Enzyme-mediated point-to-point transfer** is the textbook case: substrate binds the active site, electrons transfer to a bound cofactor (NAD+, FAD, a cytochrome), cofactor diffuses or is replaced. Distances are short, chemistry is local, the band-theory framework adds nothing. Most of cellular biochemistry works this way.

**Bound-cofactor tunneling** in multi-subunit complexes — the respiratory chain, the photosynthetic reaction center, certain dehydrogenases — moves electrons 5 to 30 angstroms through the protein interior between fixed cofactor sites, by quantum tunneling whose rate depends on distance, electronic coupling, and energy difference. The Marcus theory predicts these rates accurately. This is the mechanism most clearly vindicated by modern biophysics.

**Band conduction proper** — that the protein backbone and its associated water form an electronic medium through which carriers move over long distances by propagation through delocalized states rather than by tunneling between localized sites — is the strong form of the proposal and the one that remains partly speculative. The conduction in dry proteins is real but slow; the question is whether the hydrated, doped, living system performs significantly better. The candidates for the conducting medium are specific:

- **Aromatic amino acid side chains** — tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine, histidine — whose π-electron systems are the most easily excited and the most likely to support delocalized states. [[tryptophan]] in particular has been shown in proteins to participate in long-range electron-transfer chains, with characteristic UV absorption and EPR signatures.
- **The peptide backbone**, whose alternating amide groups have partial π-character through resonance and whose hydrogen-bonded α-helix and β-sheet structures can support coherent electronic states.
- **π-stacked nucleobases in DNA**, whose aromatic rings overlap closely in the double helix and which support electron and hole transfer over tens of angstroms — the "DNA wire" effect demonstrated by Barton and others in the 1990s and 2000s.
- **Heme and other porphyrin pigments**, whose extended aromatic systems function as natural antennas and electron carriers in cytochromes, hemoglobin, chlorophyll, and the methylene-blue-class of synthetic dyes.

The hydration shell of [[structured-water|structured water]] that [[gilbert-ling|Ling]] described — its rotational mobility constrained, its capacity to stabilize solvated electrons and excited electronic states markedly higher than bulk water's — is the medium in which these π-systems sit. The Peat-Ling-Szent-Györgyi convergence on the protein-water gel as an electronic medium is anchored here.

## The Methylene-Blue Cycle as a Demonstration

The cleanest confirmation of the framework — confirmation in the sense of measurable consequences in patients — is the [[methylene-blue|methylene blue]] bypass effect. The thiazine dye Paul Ehrlich introduced into medicine in 1891 has a three-ring aromatic structure with a redox couple at a reduction potential of approximately +11 millivolts, between NADH and cytochrome c. Dissolved into cytoplasm and mitochondrial matrix, it accepts electrons in its blue oxidized form and donates them in its colorless reduced form (leucomethylene blue), shuttling carriers around damaged steps of the respiratory chain and delivering them to cytochrome c oxidase or to molecular oxygen directly.

What makes this a demonstration of the framework rather than a pharmacological curiosity is the dose. At one milligram a day sublingually, the molecule produces measurable antidepressant, anti-fatigue, and cognitive effects in patients with mitochondrial dysfunction. The tissue concentration is micromolar — far below what a conventional enzyme inhibitor would require, and far below what would let the molecule engage every defective respiratory complex through stoichiometric binding. The effect at this concentration only makes sense if methylene blue is acting as a delocalized electron carrier in a medium where the bottleneck is not enzyme abundance but flow rate through the conducting network. Peat's framing on the [2014 nitric oxide interview](../../app/public/transcripts/ask-the-herb-doctor/polished/2014-11-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-nitric-oxide.md) was explicit: methylene blue is an "artificial electron transporter" that "can pick up electrons from nutrients such as glucose and pass them into the mitochondrion. Even if the first two units of the mitochondrion are damaged, methylene blue and vitamin C — and probably many other natural pigments — can deliver energy electrons down to the third complex of the mitochondrion and allow it to keep functioning."

The dual nature of the effect — antioxidant at low concentration, pro-oxidant at high — is the other signature of a band-conducting medium. At micromolar concentrations the dye cycles smoothly, accepting electrons that would otherwise leak from damaged Complex I or III; the leak is prevented and steady-state reactive oxygen species fall even as oxygen consumption rises. Above millimolar concentrations the cycling overwhelms available downstream acceptors, electrons leak back to molecular oxygen at the dye itself, and the net effect inverts. In a system governed by point-to-point enzyme kinetics this dose-dependent inversion would be hard to explain; in a system whose key parameter is flow rate through a partly damaged conducting network, it is what the framework predicts. ## Mitochondrial Electron Transport Reread

The textbook respiratory chain — four protein complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane, ubiquinone and cytochrome c diffusing between them, electrons traveling NADH → I → III → IV → oxygen with proton pumping at three sites — is correct as far as it goes. What the semiconduction framework adds is a different reading of what each complex actually is.

Each of the four major complexes is itself a long-range electron-transfer device. Complex I moves electrons through seven or eight iron-sulfur clusters over approximately 90 angstroms before delivering them to ubiquinone. Complex III moves electrons through a Rieske iron-sulfur cluster, two b-type hemes, and a c-type heme — roughly 30 angstroms. Complex IV moves electrons through copper A, heme a, and the copper B / heme a3 binuclear center — about 20 angstroms — before reducing molecular oxygen to water. None of these transfers is enzymatic in the lock-and-key sense; all are quantum tunneling events between bound cofactors embedded in protein, rates predicted by Marcus theory.

The cardiolipin lipid that anchors cytochrome c and stabilizes the inner-membrane organization of the respiratory complexes is, in Peat's reading, the structural component most vulnerable to PUFA-driven peroxidation — and the one whose damage most directly disrupts the long-range electron flow the framework treats as central. A cardiolipin loaded with arachidonic and linoleic side chains, oxidized to aldehyde-modified species by iron-catalyzed peroxidation, no longer holds the respiratory complexes in their high-conductivity arrangement. The bottleneck the modern literature attributes to "membrane fluidity" or "supercomplex assembly" is, on this reading, disruption of the protein-lipid electronic medium through which the band-conduction picture says electrons are supposed to flow. The downstream chain — fewer ATP per glucose, more electrons leaking to superoxide, lower heat output, lower body temperature — is at [[mitochondria]] and [[polyunsaturated-fats]] and is not re-walked here.

The point for this article: mitochondrial electron transport, reread through the framework, is not a sequence of enzyme-catalyzed reactions punctuated by diffusing carriers. It is a set of long-range electron-transfer devices embedded in a lipid-protein medium whose geometric and electronic state determines the flow rate.

## Peat's Clinical Extensions

Three practical implications follow if the framework is roughly correct.

**Red light therapy** ([[red-light]]) drives the cytochrome c oxidase copper centers through their conducting transitions. Wavelengths in the 600-700 nm and 800-900 nm ranges correspond to electronic transitions at the terminal step of the respiratory chain; absorbed photons accelerate the enzyme's catalytic cycle by direct electronic excitation. This is what Szent-Györgyi's framework predicts for any properly tuned weak photon input to a biological semiconductor: absorbed energy populates conducting states and increases carrier flow. The therapy is the framework operationalized.

**Thyroid hormone** ([[thyroid]]) builds more electron-transport apparatus per cell — more cardiolipin in the correct saturated composition, more cytochromes per mitochondrion, more mitochondria per cell. The thyroid-sufficient cell has a denser conducting network with fewer broken steps. The temperature signature Peat treated as the most reliable thyroid diagnostic is, at this level, heat dissipation from uncoupled electron flow through many cristae of many mitochondria.

**Carbon dioxide** ([[carbon-dioxide]]) is the cardinal adsorbant that holds the protein-water lattice in its structured electronic state. CO2 binding to protein amine groups acts as a Lewis-acid dopant — withdrawing electron density from the backbone, shifting the electronic state of distant sites, and stabilizing the cooperative arrangement of bound water and ions that constitutes Ling's structured cytoplasm. A cell producing CO2 from complete oxidation is producing the substance that maintains the conducting state that allowed CO2 production in the first place.

## Counter-arguments

**The strong claim has limited modern instantiation.** Solid-state physics has confirmed semiconducting properties in dry proteins and the hydrated state conducts better; mitochondrial and photosynthetic complexes move electrons through proteins over short distances. But the strong Szent-Györgyi claim — that the cytoplasm as a whole functions as a doped semiconductor through which electrons flow over cellular distances — has not been confirmed and is not part of any modern consensus. The gap between validated short-range cases and the proposed long-range case has not been closed.

**Marcus theory is sufficient for the validated cases.** The respiratory chain and the photosynthetic reaction center are well-described by tunneling between fixed cofactor sites, with rates determined by distance, electronic coupling, and reorganization energy. The band-theory framework Szent-Györgyi proposed is not required to explain these transfers, and most pharmacologists treat the electronic-state framing as redundant to the receptor-and-enzyme model.

**The aromatic-side-chain conduction story is partial.** Long-range electron transfer through tryptophan and tyrosine residues has been demonstrated in specific proteins (DNA photolyase, ribonucleotide reductase, the BLUF photoreceptors). Whether this is a general feature of the proteome or a specialized adaptation in particular enzymes is not resolved.

**DNA conductance has been overstated.** Barton's group demonstrated electron and hole transfer through π-stacked nucleobases over tens of angstroms, but the conductance is highly sensitive to sequence, hydration, and conformation. Whether DNA functions as a "molecular wire" in vivo — as opposed to under specialized in vitro conditions — remains contested.

**Methylene blue's clinical effects can be partly explained without the framework.** The MAO-inhibitor effect, the nitric oxide synthase inhibition, the guanylate cyclase block, the NMDA receptor sulfur oxidation, the microtubule stabilization — each is a conventional pharmacological mechanism with its own receptor or enzyme target. The electron-shuttle interpretation is consistent with the data but not strictly required by it.

**Reductionist molecular biology has produced cures the framework has not.** Imatinib, the HIV protease inhibitors, the GLP-1 agonists, the CFTR modulators in cystic fibrosis — these are lock-and-key drugs whose development required exactly the point-to-point thinking the framework treats as superficial. The track record of the receptor model since 1976 is far better than any submolecular-biology research program.

## Data gaps

- **Direct measurement of long-range electron transport in living tissue.** The framework predicts the protein-water-lipid system of intact cells conducts electrons over micron distances. This has not been tested with modern instrumentation; scanning tunneling microscopy and conducting AFM have measured isolated membrane proteins but not intact cytoplasm.

- **Band-gap structure of the actual cellular medium.** Eley's 1950s measurements established band gaps for specific dry proteins. The effective band structure of the heterogeneous protein-water-lipid mixture of cytoplasm has not been characterized.

- **Quantitative integration of methylene blue's mechanisms.** Of the six mechanisms Peat cited, the electron-shuttle effect is most consistent with the framework. The relative contribution of each mechanism to clinical outcomes is not resolved, and the dose-response curves are not fully mapped.

- **Aromatic-amino-acid conduction at proteome scale.** Specific proteins use tryptophan and tyrosine as electron-relay residues. Whether this is a general feature of large protein networks or a specialized adaptation has not been mapped at proteome scale.

- **Hydration-shell contribution to conductivity.** Hydrated proteins conduct better than dry ones, and structured water differs measurably from bulk. Whether the structured-water phase functions specifically as a low-loss conducting medium for solvated electrons and excited states, as Peat's framing suggests, has not been measured at the rigor that would resolve the question.

- **The PUFA-cardiolipin-conductivity bridge.** The framework predicts that PUFA-peroxidized cardiolipin reduces the long-range electron-flow capacity of the inner mitochondrial membrane. The biochemistry of cardiolipin peroxidation is well-mapped; the electronic consequence in measurable conductivity is not.
