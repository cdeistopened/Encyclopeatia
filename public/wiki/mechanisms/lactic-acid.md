# Lactic Acid

Mainstream exercise physiology treats lactic acid as a harmless byproduct of intense exercise -- a temporary inconvenience that causes muscle soreness and then clears. Ray Peat saw it as something far more significant: a metabolic distress signal, a cancer promoter, and a marker of the fundamental failure that defines disease and [[aging]]. When cells produce lactic acid, they are confessing that they cannot complete the oxidation of [[glucose]] to [[carbon-dioxide]]. That failure -- not genetic mutation, not bad luck -- is the common thread connecting exercise fatigue, chronic disease, and tumor growth.

## The Metabolic Dead End

When glucose enters a cell, it is first broken down through [[glycolysis]] into pyruvic acid. In a healthy, well-oxygenated cell with functioning mitochondria, pyruvic acid enters the mitochondria and is completely oxidized to carbon dioxide and water, yielding approximately 36 ATP per glucose molecule. But when oxygen is insufficient, when mitochondria are damaged, or when the cell's respiratory enzymes are inhibited, pyruvic acid is instead converted to lactic acid. This reaction yields only 2 ATP -- a 95% loss of available energy.

As Peat explained in a [[2015-03-11-politics-and-science-politics-science-biochemical-health|2015 Politics and Science interview]], "When you are unable to oxidize the sugar all the way down to carbon dioxide and produce lactic acid instead, you lose the greatest part of the energy stored in the glucose molecule." The cell producing lactic acid is an energy-starved cell, burning through glucose at high speed but capturing almost none of its potential.

This is why cancer cells consume enormous quantities of glucose while remaining energetically impoverished -- a paradox that becomes no paradox at all once the distinction between oxidation and fermentation is understood.

## Lactic Acid as a Stress Signal

Peat consistently grouped lactic acid with the body's stress mediators. It is not a neutral waste product but an active signaling molecule that promotes further stress and degeneration. As he described in his newsletter on bioelectric fields and lactic acid, the molecule "is involved in stress, aging, edema, inflammation, rheumatic diseases, bioelectricity, nerve-muscle interactions, cancer, and the anti-inflammatory actions" of various protective substances.

The production of lactic acid triggers a cascade of harmful responses. In a [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-acidity-x-alkalinity|discussion of acidity and alkalinity]], Peat explained that lactic acid "acidifies the environment, which in itself could actually be protective if it was acidified by carbon dioxide. But it happens that lactic acid acts as a signal to do all of the things associated with cancer, such as stimulating the growth of new blood vessels" -- the angiogenesis that feeds tumor expansion.

This distinction between CO2 acidity and lactic acid acidity is crucial to Peat's framework. Carbon dioxide, despite being acidic, stabilizes cells and promotes efficient function. Lactic acid, also acidic, destabilizes cells and promotes degeneration. The type of acid matters as much as the pH.

## The Cancer Connection

[[Otto Warburg]]'s 1924 discovery that cancer cells ferment glucose to lactic acid even in the presence of oxygen -- the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]] -- was one of the most important findings in the history of cancer research. Peat considered it the central insight into what cancer actually is: not a genetic disease but a metabolic one.

In a [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|2016 episode on cancer metabolism]], Peat described the detailed mechanism: "One way the cancer cell tries to return to normal is to use those excess electrons -- that can't be consumed by converting pyruvic acid to lactic acid -- to synthesize fat. Interestingly, the cancer cell turns on the enzymes that synthesize fat as a way to drain away more electrons." The cancer cell, trapped in fermentative metabolism, desperately attempts to manage its electron surplus through fat synthesis.

Peat's newsletter on lactate, regression, and political-medical implications detailed how lactic acid drives the dedifferentiation that characterizes cancer. In areas of chronic stress where lactic acid accumulates, cells lose their specialized identity and revert to a more primitive, stem-cell-like state. This process of dedifferentiation is not merely associated with cancer -- it is the process by which normal cells become cancerous.

The practical diagnostic implication was not lost on Peat. In a [[2014-09-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-field-biology|2014 episode on field biology]], he noted that "it's increasingly being used to diagnose cancer and monitor the progress. When the cancer is being regressed, the lactic acid in the serum goes down." He later elaborated in a conversation with Georgi Dinkov: "You can diagnose cancer even if you can't find a tumor. If you have a serious cancer developing, your blood lactate is going to be increasing, and the lactate becomes carcinogenic to other tissues."

This last point is critical: lactic acid is not just a consequence of cancer but a cause of its spread. The lactate produced by one stressed tissue creates the conditions for neighboring tissues to undergo the same metabolic shift. This is the mechanism behind what Peat called the "cancer field" -- a zone of metabolic degeneration that progressively expands unless conditions change.

## Lactic Acid and the Heart

The heart is exquisitely sensitive to the choice between oxidative and fermentative metabolism. As Peat discussed in a [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|2013 East West Healing interview]], "A very similar thing happens in cancer cells. They begin turning glucose into lactic acid or fat, and then they oxidize the fat. In treating heart disease over the last five years, there's been a movement towards thinking of ways to stop the oxidation of fatty acids, allowing the heart to avoid the Randall Cycle and get back to oxidizing glucose instead of turning it into lactic acid."

When the heart shifts to fat oxidation, it produces lactic acid from the glucose it cannot burn -- the [[Randle cycle]] in action. This metabolic shift reduces cardiac efficiency, promotes fibrosis, and sets the stage for heart failure. The therapeutic insight is that restoring glucose oxidation -- by reducing PUFA and supporting thyroid function -- can reverse the heart's decline.

## Exercise and Overtraining

The conventional view of exercise-induced lactic acid as benign misses Peat's deeper point about metabolic stress. While brief, moderate exercise that raises temperature and produces more carbon dioxide than lactic acid is beneficial, overtraining crosses the threshold into harmful fermentative metabolism.

As Peat explained in a [[2020-01-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-oxygen-saturation-lactic-acid-thyroid-vaccines-pufas|2020 One Radio Network interview]], "Just the right amount of exercise" is key. "You don't want to overtrain; that is where the lactic acid starts melting you down, increasing cortisol, and lowering the constructive progesterone, DHEA, and testosterone. The right amount of exercise increases those constructive steroid hormones and produces more carbon dioxide than at rest."

The dividing line is clear: exercise that produces carbon dioxide is constructive; exercise that produces lactic acid is destructive. This explains why moderate walking, brief weight training, and activities that maintain aerobic metabolism support health, while marathon running, intense cycling, and other endurance activities that push beyond the oxidative threshold produce the stress hormones and tissue catabolism associated with aging.

## The pH Paradox

One of Peat's more subtle insights concerned the relationship between lactic acid and intracellular pH. In a [[2015-03-11-politics-and-science-politics-science-biochemical-health|2015 interview]], he explained an apparent paradox: "In stress and cancer, a tumor will become very acidic. Inflammation produces high concentrations of lactic acid and a very low pH in that area. But when lactic acid is formed, the conversion from pyruvic acid to lactic acid draws an extra proton out of the NADH catalyst. In taking away this extra proton, it raises the pH inside the cell."

So while the environment around a cancer cell is acidic, the interior of the cell becomes alkaline -- a state Peat termed "reductive stress." This intracellular alkalinity promotes the conditions for continued fermentation and cell proliferation. The alkaline interior and acidic exterior together create the characteristic metabolic environment of cancer.

## Reducing Lactic Acid Production

Peat's approach to reducing lactic acid focused on restoring the conditions for complete glucose oxidation:

- **[[Thyroid]] support** activates the mitochondrial enzymes that oxidize glucose completely
- **Adequate [[glucose]]** supply through frequent meals prevents the fat mobilization that triggers the [[Randle cycle]]
- **PUFA elimination** removes the fatty acids that block glucose oxidation and damage mitochondria
- **[[Carbon-dioxide]]** retention (through proper breathing, avoiding [[hyperventilation]]) supports oxidative metabolism
- **[[Aspirin]]** blocks the inflammatory [[prostaglandins]] that promote the shift to fermentation
- **[[Progesterone]]** stabilizes cells against the excitatory processes that drive lactic acid production
- **[[Vitamin B1]]** (thiamine) supports the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex that converts pyruvic acid into acetyl-CoA for mitochondrial oxidation rather than into lactate

The goal, consistent across Peat's entire framework, is to keep the cell in its efficient oxidative state -- burning glucose to carbon dioxide, maintaining high energy, and resisting the fermentative shift that leads to [[inflammation]], dedifferentiation, and disease.

## Tracking Lactate Without a Blood Draw

Serum lactate is now a standard cancer-monitoring marker, but Peat's practical framework predates the lab work: the body broadcasts its lactate state through four free, continuously available signals. The point of tracking these is not biohacker theater -- it is to keep the [[Randle cycle]] and the fermentative shift visible in daily life, before they show up as disease.

**Resting pulse rate.** In the [[2020-01-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-oxygen-saturation-lactic-acid-thyroid-vaccines-pufas|2020 One Radio Network interview]] Peat returned to the [[Broda Barnes]] benchmarks: a resting pulse around 85 beats per minute with a basal temperature around 37 degrees Celsius (98.6 F) is the signature of a cell oxidizing glucose to carbon dioxide. A pulse in the 60s with cool extremities, by contrast, is the signature of [[adrenaline]] holding the system together over a hypothyroid baseline -- which is also the signature of chronic lactate production. The hypothyroid system runs on fatty acid oxidation and dumps lactate quietly into circulation; the stress hormones tighten the peripheral vessels, slow the pulse, and create the deceptive appearance of "athletic" bradycardia.

**Basal temperature.** Carbon dioxide is the byproduct of oxidative metabolism, and the heat that warms a 37-degree body is the same heat released when pyruvate finishes its journey through the [[mitochondria]]. A waking oral temperature under 36.7 C (98 F) -- especially when paired with cold hands and feet -- is one of the surest signs that glycolysis is terminating in lactate rather than continuing into the [[Krebs cycle]]. Peat repeatedly noted that temperature, not weight, is the readout that matters: a thin person with a cold body is producing more lactate than a heavier person whose hands are warm.

**Breathing rate and CO2 retention.** The [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]] story and the lactate story are the same story told from opposite sides. Chronic [[hyperventilation]] blows off CO2, alkalinizes the blood, and (through the [[Bohr effect]]) prevents oxygen from reaching the tissues -- which then produce lactate because they cannot finish oxidation. Peat told listeners in the [[2014-09-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-field-biology|2014 Field Biology episode]] that the simplest intervention is a paper bag over the nose and mouth for a few minutes: rebreathing CO2 immediately restores oxygen delivery and shuts down the lactate cascade. The corollary diagnostic is to count breaths at rest. A respiratory rate above 16 per minute at rest, especially with sighing or chest-dominant breathing, is a lactate signal.

**Perceived exertion and recovery time.** The subjective signal that matters is not whether a workout was "hard" but how the body feels two and twenty-four hours later. Lactate-producing exercise leaves a characteristic combination of late-day exhaustion, poor sleep, low morning temperature, and irritable mood -- the cortisol tail of the stress response. Exercise that stayed in the oxidative range leaves a person warm, hungry, and rested. Peat treated this subjective gradient as more reliable than heart-rate-zone training, which tends to push people into the fermentative range under the cover of a number.

Taken together, these four markers form a daily lactate dashboard. None requires a finger-stick or a lab. A person whose pulse is in the 80s, temperature is 37, breathing is slow and nasal, and mood is steady after activity is almost certainly oxidizing glucose. A person with a 65 pulse, cold hands, sighing breath, and post-workout exhaustion is almost certainly fermenting -- regardless of what a wearable says.

## Endotoxin and the Gut-Lactate Axis

One of the most under-appreciated sources of chronic lactate is the gut. Peat returned to this pathway constantly because it explains why so many people produce stress-pattern lactate without exercising, without being diagnosed with cancer, and often without obvious symptoms beyond fatigue. The mechanism connects to the canonical [[endotoxin]] story: bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine releases [[lipopolysaccharide]] (LPS), which crosses into the portal circulation, reaches the liver, and triggers [[Kupffer cells]] to release [[inflammatory-cytokines|inflammatory cytokines]] -- TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6. Each of these cytokines inhibits the same mitochondrial enzymes ([[pyruvate dehydrogenase]] in particular) that lactate elimination depends on.

The downstream signature is a liver that cannot keep up with the lactate load delivered to it. Under normal conditions the liver performs the [[Cori cycle]] -- absorbing circulating lactate, converting it back to [[glucose]], and returning that glucose to the bloodstream. An endotoxin-burdened liver is too inflamed to run this cycle efficiently, so circulating lactate rises even at rest. The person reports brain fog, hypoglycemia between meals, and the characteristic late-morning crash that Peat associated with a "stagnant" digestive system.

In a [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|2016 KMUD episode on cancer metabolism]] he tied this loop directly to the carrot-salad and bamboo-shoot recommendations: the raw fiber binds endotoxin and estrogen in the gut, reduces the cytokine load on the liver, and within hours allows lactate clearance to resume. The same logic explains why occasional well-cooked button mushrooms, low-dose [[antibiotics]] in stubborn cases, and avoidance of fermentable starches at night all reduce serum lactate measured the next morning. The gut is not metaphorically "stressful" -- it is literally feeding the same biochemical cascade that drives the [[Warburg effect]] in tumor tissue.

The practical implication is that no lactate-reduction strategy works if the gut is producing endotoxin faster than the liver can clear it. [[Thyroid]] supplementation, [[aspirin]], and [[progesterone]] all become more effective once the endotoxin load drops. Conversely, a person who exercises moderately, eats sugar appropriately, and still cannot warm their hands is almost always running a quiet endotoxin pattern that is keeping lactate elevated regardless of what they do upstream.

## The Dietary Lactate Question

A recurring source of confusion among new readers of Peat's work is whether dietary lactate -- in [[yogurt]], aged cheese, sauerkraut, sourdough, kefir, and some [[milk|cultured milk products]] -- is metabolically harmful in the same way that endogenous lactate is. The question matters because traditional diets are full of lactate-containing foods and because Peat himself recommended cheese, gelatin-rich broths, and small amounts of well-strained yogurt as protein sources.

His answer, repeated in several email exchanges and on radio, was that the lactate in fermented dairy is quantitatively trivial and metabolically distinct from the lactate produced by stressed tissue. A serving of yogurt contains perhaps a gram of lactic acid; a single intense workout produces tens of grams of endogenous lactate; a cancer patient can dump hundreds of grams per day into circulation. The dietary contribution disappears into the [[Cori cycle]] within minutes and never reaches concentrations that signal stress.

The more important distinction, which Peat made carefully, is between lactate as a *molecule* and lactate as a *signal*. Endogenous lactate is a signal because it is released by stressed tissue into the local microenvironment, where it changes the behavior of neighboring cells -- promoting angiogenesis, dedifferentiation, and immune suppression in real time. Dietary lactate enters the portal circulation, mixes with the much larger pool of glucose and other substrates, and is metabolized as fuel rather than read as a signal. The receptors that respond to lactate as a stress signal (described in the next section) are gated by local concentration gradients that food cannot reproduce.

The problematic lactate from the gut, by contrast, is not the lactate in the food but the lactate produced by gut bacteria fermenting undigested starches and fibers in the small intestine. This bacterial lactate -- particularly D-lactate, which mammals metabolize poorly -- can accumulate and cause genuine systemic effects, including the "D-lactic acidosis" seen in short-bowel syndrome. Peat's preference for ripe fruit, well-cooked starches, and avoidance of resistant starch and FODMAP-heavy foods comes from this distinction. The fermented foods themselves are fine; what matters is whether the gut is doing the fermenting.

This is also why Peat was generally permissive about hard cheeses, traditional yogurt, and lacto-fermented vegetables in moderate quantities while being skeptical of probiotic supplements, kombucha consumed in large volumes, and any food that arrived at the table still actively fermenting. The lactate in a finished food is metabolic fuel; the lactate produced by ongoing fermentation in the gut is metabolic noise.

## The Sodium Lactate Paradox

Hospital critical care presents a striking paradox from a Peat perspective: the most widely used intravenous resuscitation fluid in the world -- Ringer's lactate, sometimes called lactated Ringer's or Hartmann's solution -- is essentially salt water buffered with sodium lactate. Patients in shock, with severe burns, with sepsis, with elevated serum lactate from tissue hypoperfusion, are routinely given more lactate intravenously. How does Peat's framework, which identifies lactate as the chemical signature of metabolic failure, reconcile with the clinical observation that infusing lactate often helps these patients?

The answer Peat sketched in correspondence is that the sodium lactate molecule has two faces. As a *buffer*, it provides bicarbonate after hepatic conversion -- the liver oxidizes lactate to CO2 and water, consuming a proton in the process and raising blood pH. This is genuinely useful in the acidotic patient. As a *signal*, however, exogenous lactate at the doses delivered in Ringer's lactate (about 28 mEq per liter) does not reach the local microenvironmental concentrations that drive the autocrine and paracrine effects discussed earlier. A liter of Ringer's distributed across forty liters of total body water raises lactate by less than one millimolar -- well below the local concentrations seen in tumor tissue or ischemic muscle.

The deeper paradox is that critical care medicine has gradually moved away from lactated Ringer's in some indications precisely because the lactate, while not signaling locally, does add to the metabolic burden of an already-stressed liver. In septic patients with hepatic dysfunction, the lactate cannot be cleared and accumulates. Modern protocols increasingly favor balanced crystalloids like Plasma-Lyte that buffer with acetate or gluconate instead -- both of which the body converts more directly to bicarbonate without passing through the lactate pathway. Peat's framework predicts exactly this trajectory: the more impaired the patient's oxidative metabolism, the worse exogenous lactate performs as a buffer.

The clinical lesson generalizes. Sodium lactate is not categorically bad -- the [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-acidity-x-alkalinity|2025 acidity-and-alkalinity episode]] noted that any sodium salt of an organic acid behaves as a mild alkalinizer in a healthy liver. But in any system where mitochondrial function is compromised -- which includes most chronic illness, all cancer, and much of aging -- adding lactate to the circulation is asking the most impaired organ to do additional work. The Peat-consistent IV would be something closer to salt water buffered with bicarbonate or acetate, with sugar and magnesium added to support the very oxidative pathway that lactate clearance depends on.

## Lactate as Autocrine Signal and Driver of Dedifferentiation

Recent research has confirmed something Peat had argued for decades: lactate is not merely a metabolite but a hormone-like signaling molecule with its own dedicated receptors. The most studied of these is [[HCAR1]] (hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 1), also called GPR81, a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed on adipocytes, immune cells, neurons, and -- crucially -- on most solid tumors. When local lactate concentrations rise into the millimolar range, HCAR1 activates and triggers a cascade of effects that read as a near-perfect description of Peat's "cancer field": suppression of antitumor immunity, recruitment of angiogenic factors, inhibition of [[cyclic AMP]], and induction of the stem-cell-like transcription programs that define dedifferentiated tissue.

Peat anticipated this entire mechanism without naming the receptor. His newsletter on lactate and metabolic regression argued that lactate behaves "as a signal to do all of the things associated with cancer" -- and listed specifically the angiogenesis, the immune suppression, and the loss of cellular identity that HCAR1 research has now mapped molecularly. The newsletter on bioelectric fields went further, proposing that lactate disrupts the resting electrical state of cells in a way that locks them into a more primitive developmental program. The contemporary research on lactylation -- the discovery that lactate can attach directly to histone proteins and rewrite gene expression -- gives a molecular vocabulary for what Peat described as cellular regression.

The autocrine and paracrine character of the lactate signal explains several observations that the "lactate as waste product" model cannot. It explains why elevated tumor lactate is not just a marker of poor prognosis but a *cause* of progression -- the tumor's own waste is its growth signal. It explains why aerobic exercise that briefly raises systemic lactate is harmless (the local concentrations never reach the signaling threshold) while chronic low-grade lactate elevation from a stressed thyroid or an inflamed gut is dangerous (the local concentrations are sustained at the threshold). And it explains why the same molecule can be a fuel for the heart (via the [[MCT1]] transporter, which moves lactate down a concentration gradient into cells with healthy mitochondria) and a poison to surrounding tissue (via HCAR1, which reads the same molecule as a stress alarm).

The practical implication closes the loop with the rest of Peat's framework. The interventions that reduce lactate -- [[thyroid]], [[aspirin]], [[progesterone]], [[carbon-dioxide]] retention, gut decontamination, PUFA elimination, adequate sugar -- all work by collapsing the local lactate gradient below the HCAR1 signaling threshold. They do not have to eliminate lactate; they only have to keep it from acting as a signal. This is why a person on a Peat-consistent diet who happens to do moderate exercise and produces a transient spike of muscle lactate does not develop the chronic conditions associated with lactate signaling, while a sedentary person with a quiet endotoxin pattern can develop them at far lower absolute concentrations. Concentration matters; context matters; duration matters. The molecule itself is merely the messenger -- and the message, in chronic disease, is that the cell has given up on oxygen.

## Counter-arguments

Mainstream exercise physiology now distinguishes "lactate" (the conjugate base) from "lactic acid" and argues that lactate is a fuel rather than a cause of muscle fatigue or soreness. The 2004 [George Brooks lactate shuttle work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell-to-cell_lactate_shuttle) showed lactate moving between fast-twitch fibers, slow-twitch fibers, the heart, and the brain via [[MCT1]] transporters -- a useful fuel exchange, not a toxin. Critics of Peat's framing argue that the *Warburg* lactate (cancer) and *Brooks* lactate (exercise) are the same molecule and that vilifying one vilifies both. Peat's reply is the autocrine-signaling distinction: identical molecule, different concentration profile, different effect.

Oncology orthodoxy still treats elevated tumor lactate as a *marker* of metabolic reprogramming rather than a *cause* of tumor progression. The genetic-mutation-first model of cancer (Vogelstein, Weinberg) sees the metabolic shift as downstream of oncogene activation, the reverse of Peat's metabolic-first framing. The lactylation and HCAR1 literature is starting to reverse this consensus, but mainstream cancer biology is not there yet.

Critical care medicine continues to administer Ringer's lactate by the billions of liters annually. The position taken in the sodium-lactate section above -- that it is a compromise fluid, useful as a buffer but not ideal in hepatic dysfunction -- is consistent with current evidence but not with bedside default practice in much of the world.

The dietary-lactate position will look strange to readers of Sally Fallon's *Nourishing Traditions* or to lacto-fermentation enthusiasts who treat probiotic intake as therapeutic. Peat's tolerance for traditional fermented foods coexisted with skepticism about probiotic supplementation; the line he drew was concentration and continued fermentation, not the molecule itself.

## Data gaps

- Quantitative thresholds for HCAR1 activation in vivo. Cell-culture work pins the EC50 around 1-5 mM, but tissue-microenvironment concentrations during chronic stress are poorly mapped and the threshold for chronic dedifferentiation may be much lower than the threshold for acute signaling.
- The role of D-lactate vs. L-lactate in human disease outside short-bowel syndrome. Mammalian metabolism handles L-lactate well; the D-isomer accumulates more easily but is rarely measured.
- Whether routine perioperative or septic patients do meaningfully worse on Ringer's lactate vs. balanced crystalloids. The clinical trials (SMART, BaSICS) show small effects in mixed populations; subgroup data on hepatic dysfunction is thin.
- The lactylation field is young. How much of the chronic-disease lactate signal is mediated by histone lactylation vs. classical HCAR1 signaling vs. simple pH effects remains open.
- Daily lactate clearance varies massively with body composition, gender, and thyroid status. Peat's pulse-temperature-breathing rubric is robust but does not give a quantitative serum number.
