# Aromatase

Aromatase is the enzyme that converts androgens, principally [[testosterone]] and androstenedione, into [[estrogen|estrogens]]. Mainstream endocrinology has historically treated it as an ovarian enzyme whose relevance declines after menopause. Ray Peat's framework treats it as something far more consequential: a stress-responsive enzyme expressed throughout the entire body, activated by injury, [[cortisol]], [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]], [[serotonin]], hypoxia, and radiation, and suppressed by [[progesterone]], [[aspirin]], [[thyroid]] hormone, and a surprisingly accessible set of dietary compounds. "Any injury to any tissue, anywhere in the body, happens to activate the enzyme which creates estrogen," Peat explained in a [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on dementia]]. "It's like when you injure a tree, it causes sprouting by turning on hormones in response to the injury. That's how estrogen works." Understanding aromatase as a ubiquitous stress response, rather than a specialized reproductive enzyme, reorganizes the entire picture of aging, cancer, and degenerative disease.

## The Stress-Responsive Enzyme

The conventional view confines aromatase to the ovaries and, secondarily, to fat tissue. Peat spent decades arguing that this picture is radically incomplete. "They used to think aromatase existed only in the ovary," he told listeners on a [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|2013 *EastWest Healing* episode on energy and metabolism]], "but it turned out to exist in fat cells, breast cells, skin cells, bone, and muscle. Everywhere that is under stress will begin to produce estrogen molecules." Andrew Murray, Peat's frequent interviewer on KMUD, summarized the anatomy in a [[2022-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-february-2022-kmud|2022 episode]]: "The enzyme aromatase is expressed in many different cells in the body, liver, placenta, bones, and breast tissue."

The clinical implications of this ubiquity are profound. After menopause, fat becomes the dominant source of estrogen production, a finding first demonstrated by P.K. Siiteri and discussed by Peat in a [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 *One Radio Network* episode on natural healing]]: "P.K. Siiteri was the first to demonstrate that fat becomes a major source of estrogen production after menopause. With stress, diabetes, and inflammation, the aromatase enzyme increases, producing more estrogen." Women who have their ovaries removed are told they need supplemental estrogen, but as Peat wrote in his [[tissue-bound-estrogen-in-aging|"Tissue-Bound Estrogen in Aging" (2008)]] newsletter, "animal experiments consistently show that removal of the gonads causes the tissue aromatases to increase. The loss of progesterone and ovarian androgens is probably responsible for this generalized increase in the formation of estrogen." The body compensates for the missing ovaries by ramping up local estrogen production in every tissue that has aromatase, which, under stress, means nearly every tissue.

This is why aging itself becomes estrogenic. In his [[hot-flashes-energy-and-aging|"Hot Flashes, Energy, and Aging" (2013)]] newsletter, Peat wrote that "with aging, estrogen begins to be produced throughout the body, in fat, muscles, skin, bones, brain, liver, breast, uterus, etc." The result is that old men often have more estrogen than young women. As Peat put it in that 2022 KMUD episode: "Old men typically have more estrogen than women. It's not just the ratio; their testosterone is being massively converted to estrogen by the time they're 65 or 70."

## The PUFA-Prostaglandin-Aromatase Cascade

The single most important activator of aromatase in Peat's framework is the cascade that begins with [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]]. The chain runs: PUFA breaks down under stress into [[arachidonic-acid|arachidonic acid]]; [[COX-2]] converts arachidonic acid into [[prostaglandins]], especially [[PGE2|prostaglandin E]]; prostaglandin E activates aromatase; aromatase converts androgens into estrogen; estrogen activates more COX-2, producing more prostaglandins. The result is a self-amplifying loop.

Peat described this cascade with particular clarity in his [[preventing-and-treating-cancer-with-progesterone|"Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone" (2007)]] newsletter: "In the breast, COX-2 converts arachidonic acid into prostaglandins, which activate the enzyme aromatase, that forms estrogen from androgens." In the same newsletter, he widened the picture to include inflammatory signaling: "Both the excitatory amino acids and a peptide that promotes inflammation, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), activate the enzyme which makes estrogen, aromatase. Estrogen, by activating NF kappaB, increases the formation of TNF, which in itself can promote the growth and metastasis of cancer."

The critical insight is that even people who have stopped eating PUFA remain vulnerable. As Peat explained on *EastWest Healing*: "Even if you aren't eating it currently, the polyunsaturated fat that was incorporated into your tissues is going to break down under stress, flow into the bloodstream, and feed the production of estrogen." The body stores years of accumulated PUFA in its fat tissue, and stress liberates it. This is why aromatase inhibition and PUFA elimination work on different timescales: you can block the enzyme today with aspirin, but the substrate for the cascade persists in your tissues for years.

Shifting the balance toward [[saturated-fats|saturated fats]] reduces aromatase activation at the substrate level. Peat confirmed this directly: "The estrogen aromatase activating effect of PUFA is antagonized when you shift the balance to saturated fats."

## The Serotonin-Cortisol Vicious Circle

Aromatase sits at the center of a feedback loop involving [[serotonin]], cortisol, and estrogen that Peat identified as one of the core engines of stress physiology. In a [[2012-08-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hot-flashes-night-sweats-the-relationship-to-stress-aging-pm|2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hot flashes]], Peat laid out the circuit: "Serotonin activates stress hormones, specifically the cortisol system, and activates aromatase, the enzyme that makes estrogen. Estrogen, in turn, releases and activates the production of serotonin. It becomes a vicious circle of stress, flushing, and tissue breakdown."

Cortisol's role is especially significant. On a [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on aging]], Peat stated plainly: "Cortisol is a major activator of aromatase." This means that any condition elevating cortisol, whether psychological stress, [[hypoglycemia]], sleep deprivation, or hypothyroidism, simultaneously increases local estrogen production. The function of [[pregnenolone]], as Peat explained, "is to lower the excess cortisol and prevent the overstimulation of aromatase."

[[Endorphins]] add another amplifying layer. In a [[2015-03-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-breast-cancer|2015 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on breast cancer]], Peat described the circuit: "Even though estrogen turns on lactic acid, endorphins, and nitric oxide, the endorphins in turn activate estrogen receptors, aromatase, and prolactin, which acts as an amplifier of estrogen's effect. You get this back-and-forth action increasing inflammation, estrogenicity, and lowering energy production."

## The "Holistic Switch"

When Georgi Dinkov asked Peat what drives the synthesis of the aromatase protein itself, the answer was characteristically integrative. In [[#67-weaponized-art-and-language-iron-overload-ant-physiology-pth-lactic-acid-with-ray-peat|episode 67 of *Generative Energy*]], Peat replied: "Radiation, hypoxia, vitamin E deficiency, progesterone deficiency, all of these have been known to activate the formation of more aromatase. Each one of those factors is also activating many other parts of the cell. It's like a holistic switch mechanism in which everything gathers up a summation of forces to either turn aromatase on or off."

This "holistic switch" concept is central to understanding why single-lever interventions often fail. In the same episode, Peat cautioned: "Working on one part of it, like taking an aromatase inhibitor, isn't going to solve very much. At the same time, improving one thing is likely to be making something else worse. You have to look at the whole process as far as you can and think of how you can remedy things with the least risk of doing more damage."

The practical consequence is that effective aromatase management requires addressing the entire metabolic context: reducing PUFA intake, supporting thyroid function, maintaining adequate progesterone, keeping cortisol low through blood sugar stability, and providing the dietary factors that directly inhibit the enzyme. No pharmaceutical aromatase inhibitor can substitute for this systemic correction.

## Natural Inhibitors

One of the most practically useful dimensions of Peat's aromatase work is his identification of safe, accessible inhibitors. In a [[2022-02-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-february-2022-kmud|2022 KMUD episode]], he offered what may be the single best summary: "Just about everything that's good for you tends to inhibit aromatase because it's activated by stress, cortisol, and prostaglandins, and inhibited by progesterone."

### Aspirin

Aspirin was Peat's most frequently recommended aromatase inhibitor. In his [[aspirin-brain-and-cancer|"Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer" (2006)]] newsletter, he wrote: "Since the polyunsaturated fats and prostaglandins stimulate the expression of aromatase, the enzyme that synthesizes estrogen, aspirin decreases the production of estrogen." On *EastWest Healing*, he was more pointed: "Aspirin is a very effective aromatase inhibitor, but the drug companies don't want people to know that." Aspirin works at multiple points in the cascade: it blocks [[COX-2]], preventing prostaglandin synthesis from arachidonic acid, and it also appears to act directly on aromatase expression.

### Progesterone

Progesterone opposes aromatase through several overlapping mechanisms. Peat explained in *Generative Energy* episode 67 that "one of the powerful promoters of aromatase is prostaglandin E. Progesterone is known to, in multiple overlapping ways, block the formation of prostaglandin E." Beyond blocking the promoter, progesterone simultaneously eliminates the target: Peat noted that it "inhibits aromatase and knocks out the estrogen receptors," a dual action that no pharmaceutical aromatase inhibitor replicates. In a [[#61-bodybuilding-and-steroids-progesterone-for-men-basic-bioenergetic-therapies-with-ray-peat|*Generative Energy* episode on bodybuilding and steroids]], Peat reported receiving an email from someone whose "chest fat and gynecomastia regressed thoroughly from taking progesterone."

### Thyroid

Thyroid hormone inhibits aromatase primarily by lowering cortisol. As Peat stated in that same episode: "Thyroid is our basic anabolic hormone. It directly activates protein synthesis, lowers cortisol, and inhibits the effect of cortisol on aromatase." The triad of aspirin, progesterone, and thyroid was Peat's standard recommendation for restoring the testosterone-to-estrogen ratio: "Just with aspirin, progesterone, and thyroid, you can get your ratio back, testosterone and pregnenolone up, and estrogen down."

### Dietary Inhibitors

Peat identified several foods containing direct aromatase-inhibiting compounds. [[oranges|Orange juice]] contains naringenin, and guavas contain [[apigenin]], both effective aromatase inhibitors. "Two fruits that contain effective aromatase inhibitors are oranges (naringenin) and guavas (apigenin)," he said in a [[2013-12-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-aging-and-energy-reversal|2013 KMUD episode]]. On breast cancer, he added: "The foods that naturally contain anti-aromatases are very important. I always mention orange juice and guavas, but there are lots of fruits and vegetables that contain similar chemicals." Celery and parsley are among the highest sources of apigenin.

White button [[mushrooms]] earned special mention. Peat cited a Chinese study showing that "the women who had at least 10 grams a day on average had extremely low cancer mortality, like 88% lower, especially if they had green tea too." The mushrooms contain anti-aromatase compounds alongside anti-[[nitric-oxide|nitric oxide]] agents, a parallel Peat found significant. The carcinogenic hydrazines in raw mushrooms are heat-sensitive and break down with cooking; Peat described the balance as "between the pro-carcinogenic hydrazines and the anti-cancer agents, such as the aromatase inhibitors."

[[Coffee]] and [[caffeine]] round out the dietary picture. Peat listed "coffee, progesterone, flavonoids, and aspirin" as aromatase inhibitors in the 2022 KMUD episode. Caffeine also stimulates progesterone production, providing indirect aromatase inhibition through that pathway.

Other substances Peat mentioned include nettle root, which Sarah Murray noted "blocks the aromatase enzyme," and nicotine, which Peat said "is even being studied because it lowers aromatase and estrogen." Lobelia contains lobeline, which occupies the nicotinic receptor and may have a similar protective effect.

### Calcium, Vitamin D, and Parathyroid Hormone

An often-overlooked pathway runs through [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]]. Peat explained that "getting your calcium, vitamin D, and magnesium high enough that you turn off your parathyroid hormone" would "necessarily reduce the local stress and all of the signals that increase aromatase." This connects mineral metabolism directly to estrogen regulation through the stress-aromatase axis.

## Pharmaceutical Aromatase Inhibitors

Peat's position on pharmaceutical aromatase inhibitors like Exemestane (Aromasin) was consistently skeptical. When asked directly about them on [[2019-11-12-jodellefit-jodellefit-listener-q-a-calories-cortisol-cellulite-exercise-ephedra-more|a 2019 *Jodellefit* episode]], he replied: "Those generally have fairly serious side effects. The steroid-based one is the least toxic of the aromatase inhibitors, but there are safe aromatase inhibitors. Aspirin and progesterone, for example, do a very good job."

His objection was not that pharmaceutical inhibitors fail to block aromatase; they do. The problem is that blocking aromatase without addressing the conditions that activate it leaves the underlying metabolic disturbance intact. The holistic switch remains thrown. The same stress, cortisol, PUFA, and prostaglandin environment that activated aromatase is also activating dozens of other harmful processes, none of which the pharmaceutical inhibitor touches. Meanwhile, the drug itself introduces its own side effects, which may worsen other aspects of the metabolic situation.

By contrast, aspirin, progesterone, and thyroid address the upstream conditions. They lower cortisol, block prostaglandin synthesis, reduce PUFA-derived inflammation, and support oxidative metabolism. Aromatase inhibition is one consequence among many, and all the consequences point in the same direction.

## Clinical Contexts

### Dementia

The connection between aromatase and [[dementia]] is among the most striking in Peat's framework. In the [[2012-12-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-dementia-and-progesterone|2012 episode on dementia and progesterone]], Peat described a knockout mouse study: "Other mice are similarly altered genetically so that they have no aromatase enzyme and can't produce estrogen. When they're crossed with a demented strain, they can age normally without dementia, and they happen to have twice as much testosterone in their brain as the normal mice that can produce aromatase. So the estrogen is being produced at the expense of the testosterone." The implication is that local estrogen production in the aging brain, driven by aromatase, contributes directly to neurodegeneration. The testosterone that would otherwise support brain function is being consumed as raw material for estrogen synthesis.

### Breast Cancer

Aromatase is central to Peat's understanding of breast cancer. The PUFA-prostaglandin-aromatase-estrogen cascade operates locally in breast tissue, creating what amounts to an autonomous estrogen-producing circuit within the tumor environment. In a [[2019-04-29-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-natural-healing|2019 *One Radio Network* episode]], Peat explained: "Cutting out polyunsaturated fats is key because they produce prostaglandins, which are the main force activating aromatase and estrogen production. Aspirin is a natural substance that blocks aromatase and is very safe to use in breast cancer. Progesterone is another." Various anti-inflammatory agents, he wrote, "including aspirin, progesterone, testosterone, saturated fats, and glycine, can inhibit the production of NF kappaB," breaking the TNF-estrogen amplification loop.

### Bone Loss

Osteoporotic bone presents a paradox that aromatase helps resolve. As Peat noted in *Generative Energy* episode 67: "One of the interesting things is that the bone with aging and osteoporosis forms lots of aromatase. It becomes a powerful source of estrogen at the same time that it's degenerating." The conventional assumption that estrogen protects bone obscures the fact that degenerating bone is producing estrogen locally through aromatase, and this local estrogen is part of the degenerative process, not a defense against it. Progesterone "strengthens your bones and turns off aromatase. Once you recognize that stressed bones produce estrogen by their local aromatase, then you see one of the reasons that progesterone is going to strengthen the bones."

### Gynecomastia and Male Estrogen Excess

In aging men, aromatase converts testosterone to estrogen at accelerating rates. Peat recommended a combination approach: "It's essential to use Vitamin D, thyroid, and anti-aromatase substances like aspirin to keep it under control." He also cautioned about supplemental [[DHEA]] in younger men: "A 30-year-old taking 10mg of DHEA is likely to produce too much estrogen if they are slightly hypothyroid. Progesterone helps activate the thyroid and reduce aromatase."

### Endometriosis

Peat connected [[endometriosis]] to the same mechanism operating in displaced tissue. As he explained on *EastWest Healing*: "The cells begin producing estrogen; they express the enzyme aromatase, which manufactures estrogen." Stressed cells anywhere in the body can become autonomous estrogen producers, and in endometriosis, misplaced endometrial cells do exactly this, fueling their own growth through local aromatase activity.

## The Reductive State

One of the more subtle dimensions of aromatase biology in Peat's framework involves the cellular redox environment. In his "Preventing and Treating Cancer with Progesterone" newsletter, he described how injury shifts cells from oxidative to glycolytic metabolism: "This shift in itself increases the formation of active estradiol from estrone." The reductive state, characterized by abundant sulfhydryl groups and reduced glutathione, favors the conversion of the less active estrone into the more active estradiol. This means that any condition that impairs [[mitochondria|mitochondrial]] respiration, whether hypothyroidism, PUFA accumulation, or iron overload, tips the redox balance toward greater estrogen activation, compounding the direct effects of aromatase induction.

"Those processes, initiated by excitation or injury, increase the amount of estrogen in the cell, which intensifies the excitation," Peat wrote. The cell's own metabolic decline feeds the very hormone that accelerates that decline.
