# Lipid Peroxidation

The modern obsession with "antioxidants" has it almost exactly backward. In Ray Peat's framework, the real antioxidant is efficient oxidative [[metabolism]] itself—the focused burning of fuel in [[mitochondria]] that keeps electrons moving in orderly paths. Lipid peroxidation is what happens when that order breaks down: [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]] in cell membranes undergo uncontrolled chain reactions with oxygen, producing a cascade of toxic breakdown products that damage proteins, DNA, and the very mitochondria the body depends on for energy.

The vulnerability is built into the molecule. Polyunsaturated fats contain multiple double bonds that make them chemically unstable—prone to reacting with oxygen in ways that saturated fats simply cannot. As Peat explained, good oxidation is "very focused" and actually functions as an antioxidant by "preventing things like chain reactions of polyunsaturated fat breakdown." The distinction is crucial: mitochondrial respiration *prevents* lipid peroxidation, while anything that impairs respiration *promotes* it.

## The Chain Reaction

Once initiated, lipid peroxidation propagates through a self-amplifying chain reaction. A single free radical attacking a polyunsaturated fatty acid generates products that attack neighboring PUFAs, each reaction producing more reactive intermediates. The process generates malondialdehyde, 4-hydroxynonenal, and other aldehydes that cross-link proteins and damage cellular structures far from the original site of attack.

This is not a minor biochemical footnote. The brown "age spots" visible on aging skin are lipofuscin—the indigestible residue of oxidized polyunsaturated lipids that accumulates in cells over a lifetime. As Peat noted in his newsletters, PUFA are "the main source of this material," a fact established in the scientific literature for roughly eighty years but largely ignored by mainstream nutrition. The age pigment accumulates not just in skin but in the brain, heart, and every tissue where PUFA have been deposited.

## Why PUFA Accumulation Matters

The body cannot quickly eliminate stored polyunsaturated fats. Once incorporated into cell membranes and adipose tissue, PUFA remain available for peroxidation during any period of stress. [[Cortisol]], [[adrenaline]], and [[estrogen]] all promote lipolysis—the release of stored fatty acids into circulation—and when those fatty acids are polyunsaturated, they become substrates for peroxidation.

This creates a progressive vulnerability with age. As Peat described, the accumulation of PUFA in tissues means that "activation of cyclooxygenase by [[endotoxin]] produces progressively larger inflammatory effects" as a person gets older. A young person with low tissue PUFA can tolerate stresses that become increasingly dangerous in someone whose tissues have accumulated decades of dietary linoleic and linolenic acids.

The brain is particularly vulnerable. [[DHA]], the omega-3 fatty acid concentrated in neural tissue, increases in the brain with age, making it what Peat described as "more susceptible to lipid peroxidation." This progressive accumulation of peroxidation-prone fats in the brain connects lipid peroxidation to the degenerative neurological conditions—[[Alzheimer's disease]], Parkinson's, dementia—that increase with aging.

## The Estrogen-Peroxidation Connection

Peat's doctoral research at the University of Oregon directly linked [[estrogen]] to lipid peroxidation. He demonstrated that estrogen induces an NADH oxidase involved in the "peroxidative conversion of polyunsaturated fatty acids" in microsomal membranes. Estrogen does not merely coincide with peroxidation—it actively drives the enzymatic machinery that breaks down PUFA into toxic products.

This connection extends to [[nitric oxide]], which Peat identified as another central mediator. Nitric oxide "stimulates the peroxidation of the unsaturated fats" while simultaneously inhibiting mitochondrial respiration—a double hit that shifts cells away from efficient energy production and toward the very conditions that generate peroxidation products.

The practical consequence is that estrogen dominance, [[hypothyroidism]], and high PUFA intake compound each other. Each condition worsens the others through lipid peroxidation as the common mechanism. Estrogen promotes the release and breakdown of PUFA; the peroxidation products impair mitochondrial function; impaired mitochondria fail to produce the [[progesterone]] and [[thyroid]] hormones that would otherwise keep estrogen and inflammation in check.

## Peroxidation and Disease

Lipid peroxidation products do not merely correlate with disease—Peat argued they actively drive it. In [[diabetes]], PUFA continuously damage pancreatic beta cells, contributing to the insulin dysfunction that medicine then treats as a glucose problem. The [[Randle cycle]]—fatty acids blocking glucose oxidation—operates in parallel with peroxidation, both mechanisms stemming from the same root cause of excessive circulating PUFA.

In [[cancer]], the relationship is equally direct. Peat described cancer as involving "a shift in the direction of reduction"—an excess of reduced molecules relative to oxidized ones, the metabolic opposite of healthy respiration. Lipid peroxidation products promote this shift by poisoning the mitochondrial enzymes that would otherwise maintain oxidative metabolism. Studies Peat cited showed that fish oil diets increased metastasis, consistent with the understanding that PUFA-derived peroxidation products promote rather than prevent cancer progression.

Research by Fu, which Peat cited in discussions of the [[Randle cycle]], demonstrated that the glycated proteins typically blamed on glucose in diabetics "are actually caused more powerfully by polyunsaturated fatty acids." The tissue damage attributed to high blood sugar may originate primarily from peroxidation of PUFA rather than from glucose itself.

## Breaking the Cycle

The therapeutic implications follow directly from the mechanism. Reducing dietary PUFA limits the substrate available for peroxidation. [[Coconut oil]] and other saturated fats are, as Peat noted, "relatively exempt" from these problems because their saturated bonds resist chain-reaction oxidation. [[Vitamin E]] directly interrupts peroxidation chain reactions by donating electrons to lipid radicals without becoming reactive itself. [[Aspirin]] blocks the cyclooxygenase enzymes that convert PUFA into inflammatory [[prostaglandins]]. And adequate [[thyroid]] function maintains the efficient mitochondrial respiration that Peat identified as the body's primary defense against uncontrolled lipid oxidation.

The deepest protection comes from supporting the metabolic conditions under which peroxidation cannot propagate: high metabolic rate, adequate thyroid and progesterone, low tissue PUFA, and the stable, focused oxidation that characterizes healthy youth.
