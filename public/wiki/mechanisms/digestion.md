# Digestion

In Ray Peat's framework, digestion is not simply the breakdown of food but the body's first line of defense against [[endotoxin]]—the lipopolysaccharide fragments from gram-negative bacteria that, when absorbed, trigger cascading [[inflammation]], [[estrogen]] production, and [[serotonin]] release. A well-functioning digestive system moves food through quickly, limits bacterial overgrowth, and maintains the intestinal barrier that prevents endotoxin from reaching the liver and systemic circulation. When this system fails—from slow transit, bacterial imbalance, or mucosal damage—the consequences extend far beyond the gut.

## The Endotoxin Problem

The intestine always contains bacteria, and those bacteria always produce endotoxin. The question is how much reaches the bloodstream. As Peat explained in a [[2010-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endotoxins|2010 episode on endotoxins]], bacterial growth accelerates when intestinal transit slows—particularly when raw vegetables or poorly digested foods provide substrate for fermentation.

A healthy liver clears portal-blood endotoxin before it reaches systemic circulation. But when liver function is compromised—by [[hypothyroidism]], alcohol, [[PUFA]] accumulation, or the endotoxin itself—the clearance fails and endotoxin enters general circulation. This triggers a cascade: estrogen production increases through aromatase activation, serotonin rises (the gut produces 95% of the body's serotonin), inflammatory prostaglandins are generated, and the liver's already-compromised detoxification slows further.

As Peat noted in a [[2021-12-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-hormones-behind-inflammation|2021 episode on hormones and inflammation]], "Endotoxin plus estrogen equals autoimmunity. PUFA and prostaglandins amplify inflammation and cause leaky vessels." The gut is not merely absorbing nutrients—it is the site where the body's inflammatory balance is determined.

## Transit Time and Bacterial Control

Speed of intestinal transit is critical. Slow transit allows bacteria to multiply, producing more endotoxin and more serotonin (which itself slows transit further—a vicious cycle). As Peat discussed in a [[2010-04-26-politics-and-science-politics-and-science-digestion|2010 episode dedicated to digestion]], the body's primary bacterial control mechanisms are hormonal: "Thyroid and steroid hormones control intestinal flora."

Adequate [[thyroid]] function promotes intestinal motility directly. Many cases of chronic constipation resolve with thyroid supplementation alone—not because thyroid is a laxative but because adequate metabolic rate maintains the peristaltic activity that moves food through before bacteria can overgrow.

[[Progesterone]] and other protective steroids also influence intestinal flora. The hormonal environment of the gut determines which bacterial species thrive—a finding that preceded the modern "microbiome" research by decades in Peat's framework.

## The Carrot Salad

Peat's famous raw carrot salad serves multiple digestive functions. As he explained, "raw carrots have a germicidal effect" on intestinal bacteria. The carrot fiber, when raw and shredded (not juiced or cooked), passes through the intestine relatively undigested, physically sweeping the intestinal lining and binding bile acids that contain excreted [[estrogen]] and [[cortisol]]—preventing their reabsorption.

"When it's raw carrot and moderately shredded, we absorb very little of the irritating chemicals, but the fiber helps prevent the reabsorption of estrogen from the bowel," Peat explained. Cooked bamboo shoots and cooked mushrooms serve similar functions, providing indigestible fiber that supports transit without the irritating effects of most raw vegetables.

The raw carrot is not a nutrient source—it is a tool for intestinal hygiene. Peat recommended it daily, typically with a small amount of vinegar and coconut oil.

## Raw Vegetables: The Counterintuitive Problem

While raw vegetable consumption is promoted as universally healthy, Peat identified it as a common cause of intestinal irritation and bacterial overgrowth. Raw vegetables contain cellulose and other compounds that human enzymes cannot break down. When these reach the colon, bacteria ferment them, producing endotoxin, serotonin, and gas.

As Peat noted in his endotoxin discussion, "Raw vegetables cause constipation; bacterial growth from vegetable irritation" creates a stimulant-laxative dynamic where the bowel alternates between stasis and irritation-driven motility. Overcooking food is actually preferable: "Overcooking food is better for digestion" because it breaks down the compounds that feed harmful bacteria.

The exception is the raw carrot specifically, whose unique fiber structure resists bacterial fermentation while providing mechanical cleansing.

## Germ-Free Evidence

Perhaps the most striking evidence for Peat's view comes from germ-free animal research. In a [[2015-01-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-digestion-and-emotion|2015 episode on digestion and emotion]], he cited studies showing that "germ-free animals live longer, resist obesity and diabetes." Animals raised without intestinal bacteria develop normally, live longer, and are resistant to the metabolic diseases that afflict conventionally raised animals.

This does not mean bacteria are purely harmful—but it undermines the modern "microbiome" narrative that intestinal bacteria are essential for health. In Peat's view, the relationship is more adversarial than symbiotic: the body tolerates certain bacteria while actively defending against their products. Health depends more on limiting bacterial effects than on cultivating the "right" bacterial species.

## Serotonin and the Gut-Brain Axis

The gut's production of 95% of the body's [[serotonin]] connects digestion directly to mood, sleep, and systemic inflammation. Intestinal irritation increases serotonin production, and that serotonin acts both locally (slowing motility, increasing secretion, promoting inflammation) and systemically (constricting blood vessels, promoting platelet aggregation, contributing to depression and anxiety).

As Peat noted in his emails, "The gut makes 95% of serotonin, which is the main promoter of stress hormones, inflammation, pain, and anxiety. Serum serotonin fluctuates with intestinal irritation." This means that digestive dysfunction does not merely cause gastrointestinal symptoms—it drives the systemic inflammatory state that Peat identified as the basis of degenerative disease.

[[Cyproheptadine]]—the antihistamine with potent anti-serotonin effects—was one of the few pharmaceuticals Peat regularly recommended, partly because it addresses the gut serotonin that drives IBS and other functional digestive disorders.

## Practical Digestive Support

**[[Thyroid]] support** maintains the motility that prevents bacterial overgrowth and the liver function that clears endotoxin.

**Raw carrot salad** daily to bind estrogen and cortisol, reduce bacterial overgrowth, and support transit.

**Cooked food** preferred over raw vegetables to reduce substrate for bacterial fermentation.

**Adequate sugar and frequent meals** prevent the stress hormones that slow digestion and increase intestinal permeability.

**Antibiotics** (specifically rifaximin, or Peat's frequent mention of tetracycline at low doses) can reduce intestinal bacterial overgrowth when dietary measures prove insufficient.

**Avoiding problematic foods**: raw starch, beans, large amounts of raw vegetables, and under-cooked grains provide the most substrate for harmful bacterial fermentation.
