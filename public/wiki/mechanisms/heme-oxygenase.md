# Heme Oxygenase

A cell in trouble does not have many ways to signal what is wrong. It can leak its contents, release cytokines, shift from oxidation to fermentation and pour out [[lactic-acid|lactate]], or begin to disassemble its own most expensive components in a regulated way. Heme oxygenase is the enzyme of the last category. It binds heme — the iron-porphyrin cofactor on which hemoglobin, myoglobin, [[cytochrome-c-oxidase|the cytochromes]], and catalase depend — and cleaves the porphyrin ring with an oxygen- and NADPH-consuming reaction that releases free iron, biliverdin (rapidly reduced to bilirubin), and a single molecule of carbon monoxide for every heme destroyed. The mammalian body produces, by this route, roughly half a gram of CO every day. In a healthy young person almost all of it comes from the routine recycling of senescent red blood cells in the spleen. In an aged, stressed, or cancerous person, the rest of the body's tissues take over an increasing share of the work, and the meaning of the molecule changes from a disposal byproduct into something Ray Peat treated, in [[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy in its Origin and Treatment* (2013)]] and [[the-cancer-matrix|*The Cancer Matrix* (2015)]], as one of the more under-recognized stressed-cell signals in mammalian biology.

## What heme oxygenase does

The reaction is elegant and expensive. The enzyme binds a single heme b — the molecule that gives blood its red colour — and, using three molecules of oxygen and seven electrons donated by NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase, opens the porphyrin ring at the alpha-methine bridge. The carbon at that bridge departs as carbon monoxide. The four pyrrole rings unfold into linear biliverdin. The iron at the porphyrin centre is released in the ferrous (Fe²⁺) state, where in healthy tissue it is rapidly handed to ferritin for storage; in stressed tissue a significant fraction escapes the handoff and becomes the catalytic [[iron|iron]] pool that drives Fenton-reaction generation of hydroxyl radicals from hydrogen peroxide. Biliverdin is then reduced to bilirubin and excreted in bile.

The three products have separate fates. Bilirubin is the mainstream success story: a potent lipid-soluble antioxidant that defends membranes against [[lipid-peroxidation|peroxidation]] at submicromolar concentrations. Carbon monoxide is the mainstream surprise: reclassified since the late 1990s as a gasotransmitter that activates soluble guanylate cyclase, dilates vessels, and dampens inflammatory cytokine release. Iron is the part mainstream cell biology has the most trouble with — necessary for life, toxic when released without a chaperone, with the answer depending on the tissue. In Peat's reading the iron is load-bearing: "the high mortality of old age is associated with a high concentration of iron in the tissues" ([[mortality-again|*Mortality Again* (1994)]]), and HO is the major regulated route by which iron is released from one of its safest storage forms into the catalytic pool where it drives oxidative damage.

## HO-1 vs HO-2

Mammals have two functional isoforms encoded by separate genes. The distinction matters because the literature that mainstream pharmacology cites for "heme oxygenase cytoprotection" is almost entirely about HO-1, while the routine physiological role of heme catabolism is largely about HO-2.

**HO-2 is the constitutive housekeeper.** It is expressed at high levels in the brain (cerebellum, hippocampus, olfactory neurons), the testis, the vascular endothelium, and the spleen. Its expression is flat across the lifespan — the gene is on, it stays on, it makes a steady stream of CO and biliverdin from routine heme turnover. HO-2-generated CO has been implicated in cerebellar coordination, odor adaptation, and penile erection (alongside nitric oxide). HO-2 is not, in any reading, a stress signal. It is a quiet metabolic feature.

**HO-1 is the stress-responsive cousin.** Its baseline expression in most tissues is near zero. Under oxidative stress, hypoxia, heat shock, heavy metal exposure, hemin loading, inflammatory cytokines, ultraviolet light, and the influence of estrogen, the gene comes on hard — sometimes 50- to 100-fold above baseline within hours — and stays on as long as the stress is present. HO-1 (originally called HSP32) is on the short list of the most strongly stress-induced genes in mammalian biology, alongside the heat-shock proteins, the metallothioneins, and the Nrf2-driven antioxidant enzymes. The question Peat's framework presses is whether a stress-response gene that is on chronically is doing the cell any good.

The two enzymes do the same chemistry. The biological meaning differs because of the company they keep. HO-2 in the cerebellum is operating in a tissue with healthy mitochondria, intact iron handling, and abundant bilirubin clearance. HO-1 in a hypoxic tumor, an aged liver, or a chronically inflamed joint is operating in a tissue with damaged mitochondria, broken iron handling, and saturated clearance — and the same chemistry, in that context, produces a very different result.

## What induces HO-1

The list of HO-1 inducers reads as a directory of the cellular stresses Peat treated as the core of chronic disease.

**Oxidative stress.** Hydrogen peroxide, peroxynitrite, 4-hydroxynonenal and the other lipid-peroxidation aldehydes that [[pufa|PUFA]] generates, and the reactive oxygen species from mitochondrial dysfunction all activate the Nrf2 transcription factor, which binds the antioxidant response element in the HO-1 promoter.

**Hypoxia.** Low tissue oxygen activates HIF-1α, which (alongside Nrf2) drives HO-1 transcription. This is the link to the [[warburg-effect|Warburg state]]: a tissue that has lost respiratory capacity becomes functionally hypoxic even in well-oxygenated blood, and HIF-driven HO-1 induction is part of the same program that drives the lactate phenotype.

**Heavy metals.** Cadmium, lead, arsenic, mercury, and hemin all induce HO-1 strongly. The original demonstration of HO-1 as a heat-shock-like protein came from heavy-metal experiments in the 1980s.

**Hormones.** Estrogen induces HO-1 in liver, breast, and uterine tissue — a finding deployed both ways. Mainstream framings cite it as evidence estrogen is "cytoprotective." Peat's framing is that estrogen induces HO-1 because [[estrogen|estrogen]] is itself a chronic stressor driving oxidative load, and the resulting HO-1 induction is part of the estrogen-driven cancer-field phenotype rather than a separate protective mechanism.

**Nitric oxide.** NO induces HO-1 strongly, bridging the iNOS-driven inflammatory phenotype to the heme oxygenase response.

**Hemin and heme-loaded states.** Sickle cell disease, malaria, hemolytic anemias, rhabdomyolysis, and hemochromatosis all run with chronically high HO-1 — a feedback loop where heme is toxic, HO-1 degrades it, more heme arrives, more HO-1 is made.

**Prostaglandins.** PGE₂ and PGJ₂ — products of the [[prostaglandins|prostaglandin]] pathway PUFA feeds — induce HO-1 in macrophages, bridging to the aspirin-and-cancer story.

**Ultraviolet light.** UV-A and UV-B induce HO-1 in skin keratinocytes through ROS generation — bridging to Peat's contrarian position on melanoma as a PUFA-and-iron disease rather than a UV-mutation disease.

The pattern is consistent: every classical Peat-frame cause of chronic disease is on the HO-1 inducer list. The enzyme is, as its discoverers Schmid and Tenhunen noted in the 1960s, a sensor of cellular distress.

## The double-edged sword framing

The phrase appears repeatedly in the mainstream HO-1 literature, and it is the only part of the mainstream framing that maps cleanly onto Peat's reading. Acute HO-1 induction — the kind that happens during a single oxidative episode, a single bout of exercise, a single dose of a hormetic stressor — is unambiguously protective. The bilirubin is antioxidant. The iron is rapidly sequestered by the ferritin that the same induction event turns on. The CO suppresses inflammation and dilates capillaries. The damaged heme is removed. The tissue is left in better shape than it would have been without the induction.

Chronic HO-1 induction is not so clean. The bilirubin generation outruns conjugation and excretion. The iron release outruns ferritin sequestration, and the catalytic iron pool grows. The CO accumulates to concentrations that begin to inhibit [[cytochrome-c-oxidase|cytochrome c oxidase]] competitively with oxygen. The tissue, instead of recovering, sinks deeper into the metabolic state that drove the induction. The cancer literature is the clearest demonstration: HO-1 is highly elevated in most solid tumors, correlates with poor prognosis, and contributes to chemoresistance. The neurodegeneration literature shows the same: HO-1 chronically elevated in Parkinson's substantia nigra, in Alzheimer's hippocampus, in the amyloid plaques themselves. The cardiovascular literature shows HO-1 elevation in atherosclerotic plaques tracks plaque instability. The pattern is uniform: acute HO-1 is good, chronic HO-1 is bad, the line between them set by the rest of the tissue's metabolic state.

Peat's reading adds one element the mainstream framing typically misses: chronic HO-1 is not just a marker of trouble, it is a contributor to the trouble through the iron-and-CO byproducts that the protective short-term response normally clears. In the chronic case the enzyme is part of the disease.

## Why Peat called it a stressed-cell signal

Three legs hold the argument.

**The carbon monoxide leg.** CO binds the same heme iron in [[cytochrome-c-oxidase]] that oxygen does, with an affinity roughly 250-fold higher than oxygen's affinity for hemoglobin. In normal tissue, steady-state CO is far too low to compete with oxygen at the cytochrome oxidase site. In tissue with chronically elevated HO-1 and reduced perfusion — exactly the tissue that develops cancer, ages fastest, fails first — CO can rise into the range where it begins to compete. The result is respiratory inhibition added on top of whatever damage drove the HO-1 induction in the first place. "Carbon monoxide... at moderate concentrations behaves a great deal like estrogen," Peat noted in [[2013-01-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-carbon-monoxide|the 2013 KMUD episode on carbon monoxide]] — shifting metabolism toward fermentation, suppressing oxidative phosphorylation, producing lactate. Mainstream gasotransmitter literature acknowledges the cytochrome oxidase binding as "well below the threshold of physiological significance" at endogenous concentrations; Peat's reading is that the threshold is reached in the chronic state.

**The iron leg.** Every cycle of HO-1 releases a molecule of iron from one of the body's most stable storage forms. In young, healthy tissue the iron is captured by ferritin (whose synthesis is coordinately induced) and held safely. In aged, stressed, or chronically inflamed tissue the ferritin capture is saturated, the catalytic iron pool grows, and the Fenton-reaction generation of hydroxyl radicals accelerates. The hydroxyl radicals attack the membrane PUFA that started the trouble, generating more lipid peroxidation products, generating more HO-1 induction, releasing more iron. The loop is one of the slower-running but most consequential of the chronic-disease feedbacks, and it is the part of the HO-1 story mainstream "cytoprotection" framings have the most trouble integrating.

**The chronic-stress-signal leg.** Most genes that come on in acute stress turn off when the stress resolves. HO-1 does too, in a tissue that recovers. In a tissue that does not, HO-1 stays on for years, and its sustained activity is itself one of the signals that the cell is in a chronic state. The signature is the same shape as the [[lactic-acid|lactate]] signal, the [[serotonin|serotonin]] signal, and the chronic cortisol signal — an acute response appropriate at one timescale and pathogenic at another, with no clean break point in between. Peat treated HO-1 induction as one item on the list of chronic-stress signals, alongside lactate, serotonin, cortisol, prolactin, estrogen, and the inflammatory cytokines. The cell broadcasts the signal not because anything good comes of it but because the signaling apparatus is what it has.

## HO-1 in cancer

HO-1 is elevated 5- to 50-fold over surrounding normal tissue in most solid tumors — pancreatic, prostate, colon, lung, melanoma, glioma, hepatocellular, renal — from early stage through metastatic progression. The mechanism is convergent: tumor hypoxia, oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines, and heme from intratumoral hemorrhage all drive HO-1 transcription, and the enzyme activity helps the tumor survive the metabolically hostile environment its own dysregulated growth has produced.

The elevation tracks poor prognosis and contributes to therapy resistance. Mainstream cancer biology reads HO-1 as protecting the tumor. Genetic deletion or pharmacologic inhibition with tin protoporphyrin slows tumor growth in preclinical models. Chemotherapeutic agents that generate oxidative stress (doxorubicin, cisplatin, alkylators) induce HO-1, and the induction is part of how tumor cells survive treatment; radiation does the same. The clinical phenomenon — the first round of treatment shrinks the tumor and the second round shrinks it less — is mediated in part by rising HO-1 in the surviving cells, selected for the highest induction capacity. The framework treats this as a special case of "the treatment increases HIF, which produces the resistant phenotype the treatment was supposed to kill" ([[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]]).

The Peat-frame implication is that the right intervention against HO-1 in cancer is not direct enzyme inhibition but reduction of the upstream signals: lower tissue iron, reduce PUFA load, restore thyroid, suppress the prostaglandin cascade with aspirin, oppose estrogen with progesterone.

## HO-1 in aging and regeneration

The aging literature documents a steady rise in tissue HO-1 across the lifespan in essentially every tissue — liver, brain (substantia nigra, hippocampus), heart, kidney, skeletal muscle, skin. The rise is not from more heme being available; circulating heme is roughly stable with age. It is from the chronic stress signals an aged tissue broadcasts: lipid peroxidation products, free iron, low-grade inflammation, mild chronic hypoxia from declining capillary density, the cortisol-and-estrogen drift of the [[aging|aging cascade]]. Tissue HO-1, tissue ferritin, tissue lipofuscin, tissue calcium, and the decline of cytochrome oxidase copper all rise together — not five independent processes but one process (the slow collapse of the oxidative-respiratory phenotype) measured at five observation points.

Mainstream gerontology reads the elevation as protective; the Peat-frame reading is that it is a confession that the tissue can no longer clear the upstream signals. The two readings make different predictions. Mainstream predicts that increasing HO-1 activity in aged tissue (with hemin, cobalt protoporphyrin, or natural Nrf2 inducers — curcumin, resveratrol, sulforaphane) should slow aging. The Peat-frame reading predicts chronic induction will accelerate iron deposition and CO accumulation and produce no net benefit beyond a brief acute hormetic effect. The clinical data on chronic Nrf2 activators is mixed — bardoxolone methyl halted in phase 3 for cardiovascular harm, the trial data on curcumin and resveratrol less impressive than preclinical hype suggested.

The [[regeneration]] literature gives the clearest demonstration of the acute-vs-chronic distinction. In acute injury HO-1 induction is unambiguously protective: bilirubin defends membranes, CO suppresses inflammation, iron is captured by coordinately induced ferritin, heme from damaged red cells is cleared. Animals with genetic HO-1 deficiency heal poorly. In chronic injury the same tissue develops sustained HO-1 elevation that no longer protects and contributes to fibrosis, dedifferentiation, and tumor field formation. The wound that heals once in three weeks is doing one kind of HO-1 work; the wound chronically inflamed for three years is doing another. The Peat-frame distinction — that the difference is in the tissue's overall metabolic state, not in the enzyme — clarifies a picture the mainstream literature has documented but struggled to articulate.

## Inhibitors and modulators

The mainstream pharmacologic program around HO-1 has two branches: drugs that induce it (proposed for cytoprotection in cardiovascular disease, organ transplantation, acute injury) and drugs that inhibit it (proposed as chemotherapy adjuncts). Both branches have produced compounds that work in animal models and none that have produced approved drugs in their target indications.

**Inducers.** Hemin (used in acute porphyria), cobalt protoporphyrin, synthetic Nrf2 activators (bardoxolone methyl, omaveloxolone), and natural Nrf2 activators (curcumin, sulforaphane, resveratrol, green-tea catechins, cruciferous isothiocyanates) all elevate HO-1. Bardoxolone is instructive — encouraging short-term biomarker improvements in chronic kidney disease, halted in phase 3 for cardiovascular harm. The Peat-frame reading is consistent: chronic activation of the stress response, even one labelled "antioxidant," produces the same compounded damage as the stress it is responding to.

**Inhibitors.** Tin protoporphyrin, tin mesoporphyrin, zinc protoporphyrin, chromium mesoporphyrin all work in vitro and in animal models. Tin mesoporphyrin has been used clinically in neonatal hyperbilirubinemia. None has been approved as an adjunctive cancer therapeutic because systemic suppression of heme catabolism produces dose-limiting neurological and hepatic toxicity. Selective HO-1 inhibitors sparing HO-2 are still preclinical.

**Peat-aligned interventions** work upstream against the signals that drive HO-1 rather than against the enzyme itself: [[aspirin]] (suppressing prostaglandin-driven inflammatory induction), [[vitamin-e]] (interrupting the lipid-peroxidation chain that generates Nrf2-activating products), [[thyroid]] (improving perfusion and oxygen delivery to reduce hypoxia-driven HO-1), periodic blood donation in iron-loaded individuals (reducing the catalytic iron pool the enzyme adds to), [[copper]] sufficiency (supporting cytochrome oxidase and reducing CO-binding sensitivity), [[progesterone]] in women (opposing estrogen-driven induction), and the broader anti-PUFA program (reducing the substrate for lipid peroxidation). The framework predicts this set reduces chronic HO-1 induction by collapsing the signals that drive it.

## Counter-arguments

The dominant mainstream framework treats HO-1 induction as cytoprotective in most contexts, including chronic disease. The cardiovascular literature is the strongest single body of evidence: HO-1 elevation correlates with reduced atherosclerotic plaque burden in some cohorts, statins' partial cardiovascular benefit has been attributed to HO-1 induction, and patients with genetic HO-1 deficiency have an extreme inflammatory phenotype that is unambiguously bad. The transplantation literature also fits: HO-1 induction in the donor organ before transplant reduces rejection. The sirtuin / NAD / Nrf2 program — the post-2010 anti-aging consensus from Sinclair, Pearson, and others — treats HO-1 as a longevity-associated gene whose chronic activation slows aging, and the lifespan-extension data for natural Nrf2 inducers in mice and worms has been read as evidence for this position.

The Peat-frame response is that the mainstream conflates acute and chronic induction; that the acute protective effect is real and the chronic damaging effect is also real; that the difference is in the rest of the tissue's metabolic state; and that the clinical trials of chronic Nrf2 activators (bardoxolone) have produced the cardiovascular and hepatic harm the framework predicts. The framework does not deny that acute HO-1 induction protects against acute injury; it denies that chronic HO-1 elevation is therapeutic in chronic disease, and it denies that the right intervention is more HO-1 rather than less of the upstream stress that drives the elevation. The gasotransmitter literature's CORM (CO-releasing molecule) program may produce useful drugs in acute indications but is unlikely, on the framework's reading, to produce useful drugs in chronic disease. The sirtuin-NAD camp's reading of HO-1 as a longevity gene mistakes the downstream stress-response activation for the upstream signal reduction that actually drives the caloric-restriction and exercise effects: animals on caloric restriction live longer not because their HO-1 is on more often but because the substrates driving HO-1 induction sit at lower steady-state concentrations.

## Data gaps

The threshold for "acute beneficial" vs "chronic harmful" HO-1 induction is not quantitatively defined. The qualitative difference is clear in animal and cell-culture work; the duration-and-magnitude boundary is empirical.

The CO accumulation argument depends on tissue perfusion. In a well-perfused tissue, CO produced by even chronically elevated HO-1 is rapidly carried away to the lungs and exhaled; in poorly perfused tissue (tumor cores, fibrotic regions, aged tissues with declining capillary density) it accumulates locally. This is consistent with the cancer literature but has not been measured directly in human tissue across the relevant range.

The interaction with bilirubin is not fully worked out. Moderate bilirubin elevation is one of the body's better lipid-soluble antioxidants, and the bilirubin generated by chronic HO-1 induction might offset some of the iron-and-CO damage. The clinical correlation of moderate hyperbilirubinemia with reduced cardiovascular disease and cancer suggests bilirubin is helping even in chronic states, but the data are correlational.

The estrogen–HO-1 axis is where the framework's predictions diverge most sharply from the mainstream cytoprotection reading. Mainstream literature treats estrogen-driven HO-1 induction as part of estrogen's "cardiovascular protective" effect; the Peat-frame reading treats it as part of the estrogen-driven cancer-field phenotype. Resolving this would require population-level data on HO-1 expression in tissues of women on hormone replacement, with breast and endometrial cancer outcomes as the endpoint.
