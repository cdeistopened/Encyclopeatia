# Metabolism

In Ray Peat's framework, metabolism is not merely a background process of burning calories—it is the foundation of health itself. The rate at which cells oxidize fuel to produce carbon dioxide determines everything from mental clarity to cancer resistance, from longevity to mood. Where mainstream medicine often treats metabolic rate as something fixed by genetics or age, Peat sees it as the central variable that can be optimized through diet, hormones, and environment.

"Energy is the thing that protects and guides the proper expansion of our structures," Peat explained in a [[2021-04-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-april-2021-kmud|2021 interview]]. "The energy increase under the influence of thyroid, oxidative metabolism, and carbon dioxide is a constant flow tending to complexify and build structure. If your energy fails, it goes the other way; structure deteriorates."

This insight—that energy and structure are interdependent at every level—forms the philosophical core of Peat's bioenergetics.

## The Two Metabolic States

At the cellular level, there are fundamentally two ways to produce energy from glucose: oxidative metabolism (efficient, health-promoting) and glycolytic metabolism (inefficient, stress-associated). Understanding the difference is essential to grasping Peat's entire framework.

In oxidative metabolism, glucose is fully burned in the [[mitochondrial-respiration|mitochondria]] using oxygen, producing carbon dioxide and water as end products. This process extracts maximum energy from fuel while generating substances that are actively protective. As Peat noted, "A high metabolic rate produces carbon dioxide, which has an antioxidant, anti-stress effect throughout the organism."

Glycolytic metabolism, by contrast, converts glucose only partially—producing [[lactic-acid-production|lactic acid]] instead of carbon dioxide. This happens when cells are stressed, oxygen-deprived, or metabolically impaired. While it keeps cells alive in emergencies, chronic glycolysis is the metabolic signature of aging, cancer, and degenerative disease.

"When your thyroid is low, you don't have efficiency with oxidative metabolism, so you turn sugar into lactic acid," Peat explained in a [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 discussion on thyroid]]. "Your liver spends energy converting lactic acid back to sugar, so low blood sugar is a constant problem."

This creates a vicious cycle: glycolysis produces lactic acid, which burdens the liver, which depletes blood sugar, which triggers stress hormones, which further suppresses oxidative metabolism.

## The Cell as an Electronic System

Peat's understanding of metabolism draws heavily on the work of [[Gilbert Ling]], who demonstrated that cells are not simply bags of water with floating molecules but highly structured systems governed by electronic principles. In this view, metabolism is fundamentally about the flow of electrons from fuel to oxygen.

"The actual process is a flow—a streaming process," Peat explained in a [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|2016 episode on cancer metabolism]]. "There is no standing still for the electron. The cell dies if it isn't constantly moving the electrons from fuel to oxygen."

This electronic perspective explains why ATP works differently than textbooks suggest. Rather than being "burned" like fuel, ATP acts by changing the structure of proteins through its mere presence. Gilbert Ling showed that ATP's adsorption energy—the energy released when it binds to protein—is twice as powerful as the hypothetical bond energy that conventional biochemistry focuses on. "Simply the relationship between the molecules shifts the whole structure," Peat noted.

The implications are profound: cellular energy is not about consuming fuel packets but about maintaining the continuous flow of electrons that keeps proteins in their proper structural state. When that flow is interrupted, cellular structure degrades.

## Thyroid: The Master Regulator

[[Thyroid]] hormone is the primary driver of oxidative metabolism. It activates the mitochondria to consume oxygen and produce carbon dioxide. Without adequate thyroid function, cells cannot maintain the oxidative state that characterizes health.

"Thyroid is the main hormone that activates the oxidative metabolism of the mitochondrion," Peat stated in a [[2016-04-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-mitochondria-gaba-herbs-and-more|2016 discussion on mitochondria]]. "If your thyroid is low, the mitochondria don't pull in the cholesterol and turn it into pregnenolone."

This explains the cascade of problems in [[hypothyroidism]]: without adequate thyroid, cells shift toward glycolysis, producing lactic acid instead of carbon dioxide. Cholesterol accumulates because mitochondria cannot convert it to protective steroids. Body temperature falls. Mental function deteriorates.

The relationship between thyroid and metabolic rate was so well established by the 1930s that doctors could diagnose hypothyroidism simply by measuring cholesterol. As Peat recounted, "When a person's thyroid gland was removed, as their metabolic rate declined, the blood cholesterol increased. When they were given a supplement of thyroid, it was a mirror image."

For practical assessment, Peat recommends tracking body temperature and pulse rate rather than relying solely on blood tests. A temperature reaching 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit by mid-afternoon, combined with a resting pulse around 75-85 beats per minute, indicates adequate metabolic function.

## Carbon Dioxide: The Forgotten Product

Mainstream physiology treats carbon dioxide as a waste product to be eliminated. Peat inverts this completely: CO2 is a protective substance that the body works to retain. The ratio of carbon dioxide production to oxygen consumption—the respiratory quotient—indicates metabolic health.

Carbon dioxide does at least three things that maintain proper cellular function. First, it acts as a calming agent, stabilizing proteins and reducing excitation. Second, it is a Lewis acid that modifies proteins to favor the oxidative state over lactic acid production. Third, it replenishes the catalytic intermediates of the Krebs cycle, keeping oxidative metabolism running smoothly.

"Carbon dioxide keeps the serotonin bound and out of trouble," Peat explained in a [[2011-06-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-serotonin-endotoxins-stress|2011 episode on serotonin]]. "When you're stressed and make lactic acid, that displaces the carbon dioxide and activates the release of serotonin."

This explains why altitude—which increases carbon dioxide retention through the Bohr effect—has documented health benefits. Studies have shown cancer mortality about 10% lower in high-altitude cities, and Russian researchers found that taking carcinogen-treated rats to 17,000 feet allowed 50% of them to spontaneously reject their tumors.

## The Warburg Effect and Cancer

Otto Warburg won the Nobel Prize in 1931 for demonstrating that cancer cells produce lactic acid even in the presence of oxygen—a phenomenon now called the Warburg effect. This insight was largely ignored for decades as genetics dominated cancer research, but it places metabolism at the center of understanding malignancy.

"In the absence of oxygen, life can't do anything but grow and divide," Peat noted in a [[2015-10-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-current-trends-on-nitric-oxide|2015 interview]]. "Anything that blocks cytochrome c oxidase—blocking oxidative metabolism—tends to stimulate cell division because that's all life can do on the lactic acid-producing cycle."

Cancer, in this view, is not primarily a genetic disease but a metabolic one. Something has damaged the cell's ability to oxidize fuel fully, forcing it into the primitive glycolytic mode that supports only growth and division, never differentiation or specialized function.

The cancer cell's lactic acid production creates a gradient that irritates neighboring cells, recruiting them into the same dysfunctional state. "A cell that is approaching or has turned into cancer will chronically produce an excess of lactic acid, even when it gets oxygen," Peat explained. "There will be a gradient of lactic acid surrounding that cell that irritates nearby cells, which then begin producing lactic acid."

## What Suppresses Metabolism

Several factors shift cells away from oxidative metabolism toward the stress-associated glycolytic state:

**Polyunsaturated fats** are the most important long-term suppressors. They accumulate in tissues over years and directly inhibit oxidative enzymes. "Every function of the cell can be disrupted by too much of the polyunsaturated fats," Peat stated. "One of the worst things they do is interrupt oxidative metabolism." Remarkably, newborn babies—who have minimal [[PUFA]] accumulation—have metabolic rates far higher than they will ever have again in life. For details on PUFA's mechanisms, see [[PUFA]].

**Estrogen** promotes the glycolytic shift by increasing cell excitation, water uptake, and lactic acid production. It opposes [[thyroid]] function at multiple levels. "Estrogen is essentially anti-thyroid," Peat explained. "Thyroid is our basic oxygen-using hormone. Estrogen changes the way oxygen is used, diverting it from making carbon dioxide to fat metabolism."

**Endotoxin** from gut bacteria triggers inflammation and shifts metabolism toward the stress state. It increases [[serotonin]], which further suppresses oxidative function.

**Stress hormones** like [[cortisol]] and adrenaline, when chronically elevated, promote the breakdown of tissue to provide fuel—but the resulting free fatty acids block glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]], perpetuating the stress state.

## What Supports Metabolism

The therapeutic implications of Peat's metabolic framework point toward several interventions:

**Thyroid optimization** is primary. This may require supplementation if endogenous production is inadequate. Temperature and pulse monitoring provides ongoing feedback.

**Sugar and fruit** supply the glucose that cells need for efficient oxidation. "A 20% increase in your metabolic rate makes a tremendous difference in how much you can eat without getting fat," Peat noted in a [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|2010 discussion on sugar]].

**Saturated fats** like [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]] and butter do not suppress metabolism the way polyunsaturated fats do. "For a couple of hours after you eat a tablespoon of coconut oil, your metabolic rate is increased, producing more carbon dioxide and accelerating cell repair processes."

**Calcium** from dairy powerfully stimulates metabolic rate. "Calcium, especially from low-fat milk supported by vitamin D, is very good for weight loss," Peat explained, "because they keep your metabolic rate higher, the way thyroid does."

**Salt** works synergistically with calcium. "Salt has direct effects on the mitochondria, increasing energy production and shifting the balance to produce more carbon dioxide."

**Light exposure**—particularly red and orange wavelengths—supports mitochondrial function directly. Bright daylight, up to 15 hours per day, has been associated with improved recovery from degenerative conditions.

## Measuring Metabolic Health

Peat's practical approach to assessing metabolism relies on simple measurements that anyone can perform at home:

**Body temperature** should reach approximately 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit (37 degrees Celsius) by mid-afternoon. Morning temperature before rising is less reliable because stress hormones naturally elevate it regardless of true metabolic status. A pattern where temperature drops after eating suggests the metabolism is being run on stress hormones rather than thyroid.

**Pulse rate** at rest should be around 75-85 beats per minute in someone with adequate thyroid function. A chronically low pulse (below 65) often indicates hypothyroidism, while a very high pulse may indicate stress hormone excess.

**Response to eating** is telling. "If your temperature hasn't stabilized by 10:00 or 11:00 AM, a low thyroid person—especially a woman with high estrogen—might see temperature and pulse fall before lunch," Peat explained in a [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|2013 interview on temperature and pulse]]. Healthy metabolism shows temperature rising after meals as thyroid activates, not falling.

**Cholesterol levels** serve as an indirect marker. High cholesterol often indicates that the mitochondria are not pulling in cholesterol fast enough to convert it to steroids—a sign of low thyroid function. "The metabolic rate is a mirror image of the blood cholesterol level. As you increase your metabolic rate, your cholesterol will come down."

## The Hierarchy of Energy

Peat places metabolism within a larger biological hierarchy. Energy creates and maintains structure at every level—from protein folding to organ function to consciousness itself.

"We're always having a flow of energy," Peat explained in a [[generative-energy/polished/Optimizing The Environment with Ray Peat [Generative Energy #33]|discussion on environment]]. "That flow itself is disturbing the equilibrium, and it's tending to absorb energy and turn it into structure. Anytime you flow energy through a system, it is creative and creates structure."

This principle—that energy spontaneously generates complex organization rather than degrading toward chaos—connects Peat's bioenergetics to the work of Ilya Prigogine on dissipative structures and to the broader tradition of vitalist biology that mainstream science largely abandoned.

In this framework, degenerative disease represents the failure of energy production, which allows structure to deteriorate. Aging is not programmed obsolescence but accumulated metabolic damage. And health is the state where adequate energy maintains the complex organization that distinguishes living systems from mere chemistry.
