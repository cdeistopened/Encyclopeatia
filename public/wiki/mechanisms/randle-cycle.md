# Randle Cycle

In 1963, P.J. Randle demonstrated that increasing the concentration of [[free-fatty-acids]] in the bloodstream rapidly suppresses the oxidation of [[glucose]]. Cells flooded with fatty acids lose the ability to burn sugar completely; instead of oxidizing glucose all the way to [[carbon-dioxide]] and water in the [[mitochondria]], they ferment it into [[lactic-acid]]. The discovery provided, in Ray Peat's view, the biochemical explanation for [[diabetes]]--an explanation that was promptly ignored by an industry committed to blaming sugar for the disease that fat causes.

Peat returned to this mechanism in hundreds of interviews, newsletters, and emails across three decades. He considered it the single most important concept for understanding the diseases of modern civilization: diabetes, [[cancer]], [[heart-failure|heart failure]], [[aging]], and neurodegeneration all converge at this point where fatty acids hijack the cell's energy system. "Diabetes was basically explained biochemically in the 1960s," Peat told listeners in a [[2008-12-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cholesterol-is-an-important-molecule|2008 episode on cholesterol]]: "if you feed more fats, you block the response to insulin and create insulin resistance. This is now called Type 2 diabetes."

Peat also corrected the name. In his newsletter *Energy, Structure, and Carbon Dioxide*, he wrote: "In the Randle effect (it's called the 'Randle cycle,' but there is no cycle), increasing the amount of fat in the bloodstream decreases the ability of cells to metabolize glucose." What Randle described is not a cycle in the thermodynamic sense but a competition--one in which the wrong fuel wins and the cell pays with its life.

## The Effect: What Happens

The Randle effect operates instantaneously. When a soy oil emulsion is injected intravenously into a hospitalized patient, blood sugar rises within about fifteen minutes. As Peat described in a [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 episode on sugar]]: "This was demonstrated in hospitals when giving nutritional support to patients in the form of a soy oil emulsion. About 15 minutes after injecting this emulsified soy oil, people would get hyperglycemic." The fatty acids in the emulsion immediately suppressed glucose oxidation, so blood sugar accumulated with nowhere to go.

The same effect happens endogenously. Any stress--physical trauma, emotional strain, fasting, infection--triggers the release of [[adrenaline]], which activates lipase enzymes in fat cells, flooding the bloodstream with free fatty acids. These fatty acids immediately begin competing with glucose for the cell's oxidative machinery. When they win, glucose cannot be fully oxidized, and the cell shifts from its efficient aerobic mode (producing approximately 36 ATP per glucose molecule) to fermentation (producing only 2 ATP), with [[lactic-acid]] as the waste product.

Peat framed this as more than a biochemical curiosity. In a [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|2013 discussion of energy and metabolism]], he traced the full cascade: "This process of substituting fat burning for sugar burning was called a cycle by P.J. Randle. It isn't really a cycle, but now it's called the Randall Cycle. This means that if you burn fatty acids, that stops you from oxidizing glucose; instead, you'll turn it into lactic acid." The cell still takes up glucose--which is why the old idea that diabetic cells cannot absorb glucose is wrong. "If you're making lactic acid, the sugar is getting into the cell. Something goes wrong in the oxidation process and the cell begins to favor the oxidation of fat rather than glucose."

## The Enzyme: Pyruvate Dehydrogenase

The specific molecular target through which fatty acids block glucose oxidation is the enzyme [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]] (PDH). This enzyme sits at the gateway between glycolysis and the mitochondrial [[Krebs-cycle]]: it converts pyruvic acid into acetyl-CoA, the fuel the mitochondria actually burn. When PDH is active, glucose flows smoothly through glycolysis, into the mitochondria, and out as carbon dioxide, water, and abundant energy. When PDH is blocked, pyruvic acid backs up and is diverted to lactic acid instead.

Peat identified PDH as one of the "pivotal landmarks in cellular metabolism," alongside phosphofructokinase-1 and cytochrome c oxidase. In a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 episode on diabetes and saturated fats]], he described two distinct points of inhibition: "There are two clear points where free fatty acids inhibit the use of glucose: pyruvate dehydrogenase (which you need to burn glucose) and they stimulate [[glucagon]]. Glucagon turns on the synthesis of glucose at the expense of protein. Glucagon then stimulates the release of *more* fatty acids."

The mechanism of PDH inhibition involves intracellular phosphate. As Peat explained in a [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 episode on diabetes and nerve damage]]: "They block pyruvate dehydrogenase by making more phosphate ions available. Where sugars bind them and lower free phosphates, fatty acids increase them and tend to poison the crucial enzyme." This phosphate-mediated mechanism is critical because it explains how [[fructose]] can reactivate PDH: fructose absorbs excess phosphate ions, directly reversing the inhibition that fatty acids impose.

Several substances reactivate PDH, and Peat noted their convergence: "Thyroid hormone, insulin, and fructose also activate these enzymes," he wrote in *Sugar Issues*. The pharmaceutical drug dichloroacetate (DCA) accomplishes the same thing chemically, and Peat noted its effectiveness "against a variety of cancers" precisely because cancer cells have characteristically suppressed PDH. But the simpler interventions--[[fructose]], [[insulin]], [[thyroid]]--do the same work without the side effects, by lowering the intracellular conditions that keep PDH inactive.

The downstream consequences of PDH suppression extend beyond energy production. When PDH is blocked and the cell shifts to lactic acid production, the reducing environment inside the cell changes. As Georgi Dinkov observed in a discussion of CO2 and ketosis: "If you turn off PDH, you're starting to immediately build up lactate because pyruvate will be used as the emergency oxidant." Peat added that "to keep the production of lactate going, you need something to get rid of the buildup of higher levels of NADH. The intrinsic alternative to oxygen is the synthesis of fats." The cell begins manufacturing fatty acids to consume excess electrons--a survival mechanism that reinforces the very metabolic shift that created the problem. [[Estrogen]] and other stressors "turn on this enzyme system to consume excess NADH in the absence of oxygen," running glycolysis and lactic acid production and "intrinsically changing the cell over to consuming energy at a high rate but in a destructive, primitivizing direction."

Saturated fatty acids are a notable exception. In *Regeneration and Degeneration*, Peat wrote that "saturated fats activate the pyruvate dehydrogenase enzyme that is essential for the efficient use of glucose, while PUFA block it." And in the *Mitochondria and Mortality* newsletter, he identified an even more specific distinction: "While most fatty acids inhibit the oxidation of glucose without immediately inhibiting glycolysis, palmitic acid is unusual, in its inhibition of glycolysis and lactate production without inhibiting oxidation." He attributed this to palmitic acid's role in [[cardiolipin]] and [[cytochrome-oxidase|cytochrome c oxidase]]--the respiratory enzyme at the end of the mitochondrial electron transport chain.

## The Critical Distinction: PUFA vs. Saturated Fat

The Randle effect, as originally described, applies to all fatty acids competing with glucose. But Peat argued that its consequences are radically different depending on the type of fatty acid involved. This distinction is the crux of his entire dietary framework.

When free fatty acids in the blood are predominantly saturated--as they would be in a person eating [[butter]], [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], and ruminant fat--the Randle effect is mild and self-correcting. Saturated free fatty acids provide clean fuel, inhibit the stress hormones that triggered their own release, and do not damage the mitochondria that burn them. As Peat told listeners in the [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 diabetes episode]]: "If you compare stearic acid (butter/beef fat) and linoleic acid (corn oil), the butter turns off adrenaline and cortisol, while the corn oil turns them on."

When free fatty acids are predominantly polyunsaturated--the condition of virtually everyone raised on a modern diet of [[seed-oils|seed oils]]--the Randle effect becomes catastrophic. Polyunsaturated fatty acids not only block glucose oxidation at PDH, they also destroy the mitochondria themselves. "The mitochondrial oxidative system itself is basically destroyed in proportion to the polyunsaturated fat exposure," Peat explained in the same episode. "The mitochondria produce something like 35 times more energy per molecule of sugar than the diabetic pathway (lactic acid) can produce."

Furthermore, PUFA amplifies the stress response that mobilized it in the first place. There are "several points where free fatty acids activate adrenaline, ACTH, cortisol, TSH, and glucagon--all of which increase the release of free fatty acids from your fat storage," Peat continued. "It seems illogical for the body to create those vicious circles where an energy failure turns on exactly what caused it. But it turns out that it's only the *polyunsaturated* fatty acids that have those terrible anti-energy effects." The saturated fatty acids shut the alarm off; the polyunsaturated ones make it louder.

[[Coconut-oil|Coconut oil]] occupies a unique position in this framework. When a questioner asked Peat directly whether coconut oil was "exempt from becoming free fatty acids and competing with glucose in the Randle cycle," he replied: "Relative to PUFA, yes. When the body contains a lot of PUFA, eating coconut oil increases oxidative metabolism, partly because of the shorter fatty acids that are more quickly oxidized, like sugar, and partly because of the antimetabolic effects of the PUFA that they displace." The short and medium-chain fatty acids in coconut oil behave metabolically more like carbohydrates than like the long-chain fats that drive the Randle effect. They burn rapidly without competing with glucose for the same enzymatic pathways, while simultaneously displacing the PUFA that does compete. (See [[coconut-oil]] for the full account of this mechanism.)

## The Stress Feedback Loop

The Randle effect does not operate in isolation. It is embedded in a feedback loop that Peat considered one of the most dangerous patterns in human physiology.

The sequence begins with any form of stress: an emotional shock, a skipped meal, an infection, overexertion, cold exposure. The adrenal glands release [[adrenaline]] and [[cortisol]]. Adrenaline activates lipase enzymes in fat tissue, breaking triglycerides apart and releasing free fatty acids into the blood. If those fatty acids are polyunsaturated--as they overwhelmingly are in a modern person--they immediately activate the Randle effect, blocking glucose oxidation. The cell, now energy-deprived, signals for more fuel. The liver makes more glucose from protein (under cortisol's direction), and the fat cells release more free fatty acids (under adrenaline and growth hormone). The fatty acids block the new glucose, too. The stress hormones escalate.

Peat traced this in his newsletter *Lactate vs. CO2 in Wounds, Sickness, and Aging*: "Excessive stimulation depletes glucose and produces lactic acid, and causes cortisol to increase, causing a shift to the consumption of fat and protein rather than glucose. Increased cortisol activates the Randle effect (the inhibition of glucose oxidation by free fatty acids), accelerates the breakdown of protein into amino acids, and activates the enzyme fatty acid synthase, which produces fatty acids from amino acids and pyruvate, to be oxidized in a 'futile cycle,' producing heat, and increasing the liberation of ammonia from the amino acids."

The loop has additional nodes. [[Estrogen]], which rises under stress through cortisol-induced activation of [[aromatase]], "increases the free fatty acids circulating in the blood, and this shifts metabolism away from oxidation of glucose to oxidation of fat," as Peat wrote in *Hot Flashes, Energy, and Aging*. Growth hormone, also elevated by stress, mobilizes fatty acids from storage. [[Lactic-acid|Lactic acid]], produced when glucose cannot be oxidized, stimulates the release of [[endorphins]], which are themselves lipolytic--releasing still more free fatty acids. Each node amplifies the others.

The practical consequence is that a person whose fat stores are loaded with PUFA enters a positive feedback loop where every stress event is worse than the last, every night's fasting releases a more toxic payload, and the metabolic capacity to recover from stress degrades progressively. The Randle effect is the mechanism that converts a momentary crisis into a chronic disease.

## The Mechanism of Diabetes

Peat considered diabetes to be fundamentally a Randle cycle disease--not a disease of sugar excess but of fatty acid excess blocking sugar oxidation. He stated this plainly in the [[2008-12-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-cholesterol-is-an-important-molecule|2008 cholesterol episode]]: "Diabetes was basically explained biochemically in the 1960s: if you feed more fats, you block the response to insulin and create insulin resistance."

The conventional view treats hyperglycemia as the primary problem and sugar restriction as the solution. Peat inverted this entirely. High blood sugar is the body's *compensation* for the Randle effect, not its cause. As he explained in the [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|2014 diabetes episode]]: "In hyperglycemia, what you have is the body's adjustment to the inability of cells to get enough energy because they can't oxidize glucose because the fats are blocking it. The body tries to overcome that poisonous effect of the free fatty acids by increasing glucose production. To a great extent, the rising blood sugar shouldn't be fought in itself. The cause that interferes with the use of glucose should be concentrated on."

The evidence that glucose enters diabetic cells was clear in the lactate data. "Diabetics typically have elevated lactate, which shows that glucose doesn't have a problem getting into their cells, just getting oxidized," Peat wrote in an email exchange on diabetes. The glucose gets in; it gets fermented to lactic acid because fatty acids have disabled PDH; the lactic acid goes to the liver, which converts it back to glucose (the Cori cycle); and blood sugar rises further. The Randle effect creates the appearance of a sugar disease from a fat disease.

The chronic dimension involves the pancreas itself. As Peat explained in a [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|2011 interview on glycemia]]: "The Randle Cycle is the instantaneous diabetogenic effect of fatty acids. Chronically, this poisons the beta cells in the pancreas." The same PUFA that block glucose oxidation in every cell also destroy the insulin-producing beta cells through lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage. "Free fatty acids are constantly killing the beta cells in the pancreas," Peat said in a [[2018-11-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-skin-cancer|2018 episode on skin cancer]]. "Every time they regenerate under the influence of glucose, if there isn't enough glucose to suppress the free fatty acids, the regenerated beta cells get killed again. Keeping the fatty acids down is the essence of curing diabetes."

The protein damage attributed to glucose is, in Peat's analysis, actually caused by PUFA. He explained in the [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 sugar episode]] that "a group led by Fu demonstrated that the glycated proteins seen in diabetic people--which are usually blamed on glucose--are actually caused more powerfully by polyunsaturated fatty acids. Spontaneously oxidized polyunsaturated fats stick to the proteins." The so-called advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) that drive diabetic complications are, more accurately, lipoxidation end-products. In his *Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes* newsletter, Peat added: "The reactive fragments of polyunsaturated fatty acids are much more often the source of the protein-damaging radicals than the carbohydrates are."

### The Argentine Researchers and Sugar's Protective History

The historical evidence for sugar's anti-diabetic effect was, in Peat's telling, suppressed by the insulin and seed-oil industries. "Argentine researchers showed sugar was protective against diabetes," he told Josh Rubin in the [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|2011 glycemia interview]]. The Nobel laureate Bernardo Houssay "found that coconut oil protected animals from poison-induced diabetes, while a lard-based diet failed to protect them," Peat wrote in *Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes*. "Later, glucose itself was found to protect the pancreatic beta-cells from poisons."

The protective mechanism runs through the suppression of lipolysis. When a person eats sugar, insulin is released; insulin inhibits the lipase enzymes in fat cells; free fatty acids in the blood drop; and the Randle effect diminishes. "When a normal person, or even a 'type 2 diabetic,' is given a large dose of sugar, there is a suppression of lipolysis, and the concentration of free fatty acids in the bloodstream decreases," Peat wrote in the diabetes newsletter.

## The Fructose Bypass

[[Fructose]] occupies a special position in relation to the Randle cycle because it enters glycolysis below the point at which fatty acids block glucose metabolism. As Peat wrote in *Sugar Issues*: "One of the points at which fatty acids suppress the use of glucose is at the point at which it is converted into fructose, in the process of glycolysis. When fructose is available, it can by-pass this barrier to the use of glucose, and continue to provide pyruvic acid for continuing oxidative metabolism."

The bypass operates at the level of PDH itself. Fructose activates pyruvate dehydrogenase the same way insulin does--by absorbing excess intracellular phosphate, reversing the phosphate-mediated inhibition that fatty acids impose. As Peat put it in the 2014 nerve damage episode: "When you are supplied with aspirin, caffeine, and fructose, you are not calling on free fatty acids. If you load up the cell with excess free fatty acids from stress, they reverse all of those processes." Fructose and fatty acids are, at this enzymatic chokepoint, exact antagonists.

This is why diabetics metabolize fructose better than glucose--a finding first reported by E. Kulz in Germany in 1874 and confirmed repeatedly since. The diabetic's glucose oxidation is blocked by the Randle effect, but fructose sidesteps the block entirely. (See [[fructose]] for the full account of the bypass mechanism, the historical evidence, and Peat's reframing of diabetes as a fructose deficiency.)

## The Cancer Connection

The link between the Randle cycle and cancer was, for Peat, a direct consequence of the shift from oxidative to fermentative metabolism. When fatty acids block PDH and the cell produces lactic acid instead of carbon dioxide, it has entered what Otto Warburg described in 1924 as cancer metabolism. Peat made this connection explicit in a [[2021-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-managing-hormones-and-cancer-treatment-with-nutrition|2021 episode on cancer and nutrition]]:

"When the pyruvate dehydrogenase is poisoned by various things, but especially free fatty acids, then you shift over to the cancer metabolism, producing lactic acid, which shifts the metabolism of other cells in the same direction."

The [[lactic-acid]] produced by this shift is not merely a waste product but an active cancer promoter. It stimulates angiogenesis (the growth of new blood vessels to feed tumors), creates an acidic extracellular environment that favors tumor invasion, and drives neighboring cells toward the same fermentative state--what Peat called the "cancer field." (See [[lactic-acid]] for the full treatment of this signaling cascade.)

### The Ketogenic Diet Criticism

Peat's Randle cycle analysis led him to a sharp critique of the ketogenic diet as a cancer therapy. The reasoning is direct: the ketogenic diet works by restricting carbohydrates so severely that the body shifts to fat oxidation and ketone production. But this is precisely the metabolic shift that activates the Randle effect. As Peat stated in the 2021 cancer episode: "Sugar starvation causes the Randle cycle to take effect, blocking your ability to oxidize sugar fully. So you ferment it, producing lactic acid, which promotes more of the cancer metabolism, creates inflammation, and brings up the cortisol stress hormones that break down proteins to make more glucose or amino acids which will feed the cancer."

The ketogenic claim that "cancer lives on sugar, therefore starve the cancer of sugar" was, in Peat's view, "absolutely 100% wrong." Cancer cells do consume enormous amounts of glucose--but they ferment it to lactic acid rather than oxidizing it. Restricting sugar does not starve the cancer; it forces the rest of the body into the same metabolic pattern the cancer is already using, while generating the free fatty acids and stress hormones that feed tumor growth. "All of the liberated free fatty acids that have been studied promote metastasis," he noted. "It isn't just the extremely toxic fish oil type, but just the fact of being a fatty acid rather than a sugar."

His dietary recommendation for cancer was the inverse of the ketogenic approach: high carbohydrate (especially [[sucrose]] and fruit), very low fat, and anti-inflammatory substances to prevent fatty acid liberation. He cited the "Grape Diet" of the 1940s and 1950s as a historical precedent--"a very high glucose and sucrose diet combined with a very high intake of anti-inflammatory flavonoids."

## Heart Disease: The Failing Heart on Fat

The failing heart provided Peat with one of his clearest clinical illustrations of the Randle effect. In the [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|2013 energy and metabolism interview]], he traced the progression from normal cardiac function to failure:

"When you're sitting at rest, the quiet muscle burns almost pure sugar and oxygen--pure glucose and oxygen. But as soon as it starts exercising, it shifts over to fat oxidation. At maximum exercise, it's burning mostly fat and very little oxygen. It produces quite a bit of lactic acid because it's wasting sugar."

The failing heart is in permanent maximum-exertion mode: burning fatty acids, producing lactic acid instead of carbon dioxide, and progressively losing the ability to contract efficiently. In his *Heart and Hormones* newsletter, Peat described the therapeutic principle: "This sequence, from reduced efficiency of energy production to heart failure, can be opposed by agents that reduce the availability of fatty acids and promote the oxidation of glucose. [[Niacinamide]] inhibits the release of free fatty acids from the tissues, and [[thyroid]] sustains the oxidation of glucose."

The cardiac Randle effect could be reversed with remarkably simple interventions. Peat cited an experiment in which "providing a small amount of sugar (0.8% sucrose in their drinking water) restored the calcium binding and heart function, without increasing either thyroid hormone or insulin." The sugar lowered free fatty acids, glucose oxidation resumed, carbon dioxide production increased, calcium regulation normalized, and the heart recovered.

Peat noted with interest that the pharmaceutical industry had begun to recognize this principle: "In treating heart disease over the last five years, there's been a movement towards thinking of ways to stop the oxidation of fatty acids, allowing the heart to avoid the Randall Cycle and get back to oxidizing glucose instead of turning it into lactic acid." The FDA had approved a drug to inhibit cardiac fatty acid oxidation, though it came with serious side effects that the natural approach--thyroid, niacinamide, sugar, and PUFA avoidance--did not.

## The Estrogen Connection

[[Estrogen]] drives the Randle effect through multiple simultaneous mechanisms. It increases [[free-fatty-acids]] in the circulation, preferentially liberating the most unsaturated (and most toxic) fatty acids from storage. It suppresses [[thyroid]] function at every level of the axis. It activates the fatty acid synthase that produces new fats from glucose. And it promotes the reductive, fermentative metabolic state that the Randle effect initiates.

In *Gelatin, Stress, Longevity*, Peat described the estrogen-Randle connection directly: "One of estrogen's 'excitatory' effects is to cause lipolysis, the release of fatty acids from storage fat; it directs the conversion of glucose into fat in the liver, so that the free fatty acids in the circulation remain chronically high under its influence. The free fatty acids inhibit the oxidation of glucose for energy, creating insulin resistance, the condition that normally increases with aging, and that can lead to hyperglycemia and 'diabetes.'"

Women's higher susceptibility to diabetes and Alzheimer's disease, in Peat's analysis, follows directly from estrogen's amplification of the Randle effect. As he noted in *Mitochondria and Mortality*: "Women, with higher estrogen and growth hormone, usually have more free fatty acids than men, and during exercise oxidize a higher proportion of fatty acids than men do. This fatty acid exposure 'decreases glucose tolerance,' and undoubtedly explains women's higher incidence of diabetes."

The connection to oral contraceptives was documented from the beginning. Peat cited Spellacy and Carlson in his *Diabetes, Scleroderma, Oils and Hormones* newsletter: when the oral contraceptive pill was new, "it was found to produce signs of diabetes, including decreased glucose tolerance." The researchers noted that "free fatty acids can block the Krebs cycle, with relative insulin action resistance resulting" and warned of "the potential danger of the oral contraceptives" through "prolonged pancreatic stimulation." (See [[free-fatty-acids]] for the full account of estrogen's role in driving lipolysis and PUFA release.)

## Carbon Dioxide: The Protective Product Lost

A dimension of the Randle effect that Peat emphasized beyond most biochemists is its impact on [[carbon-dioxide]] production. When glucose is oxidized completely in the mitochondria, the ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed (the respiratory quotient) is 1.0. When fat is oxidized, the ratio drops to approximately 0.7. The difference is not trivial.

As Peat explained in a Generative Energy episode on CO2 and ketosis: "When you oxidize carbohydrate, you get extra carbon dioxide as a byproduct. It's like insurance that you're going to reinforce the oxidizing process. But oxidizing fat--even if it's a safe saturated fat--you don't get such a return of carbon dioxide, and that increases the risk of falling over into the lactate, glycolytic slime metabolism."

The mechanism involves oxygen itself. Peat noted in a Generative Energy episode with Georgi Dinkov that "the carbohydrates carry their own oxygen. Each carbon already has part of the carbon dioxide. With fat, you have to start with molecular oxygen. There's no oxygen built into the fat molecule." Oxidizing glucose produces more carbon dioxide per unit of oxygen consumed, which means the Randle shift to fat oxidation simultaneously reduces carbon dioxide and increases the cell's demand for oxygen--a double penalty.

Carbon dioxide is not merely a waste gas. It stabilizes proteins, inhibits [[lactic-acid]] production, maintains calcium binding, and supports the organized water structure that Peat considered essential to cellular function. The loss of carbon dioxide through the Randle shift contributes to every downstream pathology: the calcium accumulation that destroys mitochondria, the lactic acid that promotes cancer, the protein glycation blamed on sugar, and the edema and inflammation that characterize degenerative disease.

At high altitude, where oxygen is scarce, the body spontaneously favors glucose oxidation to conserve oxygen--the opposite of the Randle shift. Peat noted in a [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 episode on altitude]] that "at high altitude, a person uses their oxygen more efficiently and they're more reluctant to burn their stored fats. Anything you do to increase the carbon dioxide will tend to protect you against mobilizing your fats and creating that diabetes-like state." The lower rates of cancer, heart disease, and diabetes at high altitude may reflect, in part, a natural suppression of the Randle effect.

## Reversing the Randle Effect

Peat's therapeutic logic followed directly from the mechanism. The goal is to suppress lipolysis, lower circulating free fatty acids (especially polyunsaturated ones), reactivate pyruvate dehydrogenase, and restore the conditions for complete glucose oxidation:

**Sugar and frequent meals** are the most immediate intervention. Adequate carbohydrate intake stimulates [[insulin]], which inhibits the lipase enzymes in fat cells and reduces free fatty acid release. "Stress tends to increase your circulating free fatty acids, activating the Randle effect, blocking the ability to use sugar for energy," Peat explained in a Generative Energy episode on bioenergetic nutrition basics. "Increasing the sugar in your diet tends to lower the lipolytic activity, keeps the fat where it belongs, and prevents the Randle shift to fat oxidation."

**[[Fructose]]** provides a specific enzymatic bypass, reactivating PDH by lowering intracellular phosphate and entering glycolysis below the fatty acid block. A quart of [[orange-juice]] daily was Peat's baseline recommendation.

**[[Coconut-oil|Coconut oil]]** displaces PUFA from oxidative pathways without itself driving the Randle effect. Its short-chain fatty acids are "more quickly oxidized, like sugar," and each tablespoon provides one to two hours of thyroid-supportive, anti-PUFA effect.

**[[Niacinamide]]** inhibits the lipase that releases free fatty acids from storage, reducing the substrate that drives the Randle shift. It also supports NAD+ levels, keeping the cell's redox state oxidative rather than reductive.

**[[Aspirin]]** inhibits lipolysis, blocks the conversion of released [[arachidonic-acid]] into inflammatory [[prostaglandins]], and shifts mitochondrial metabolism toward glucose oxidation. Peat recommended it in the evening specifically to counteract the rise in free fatty acids that occurs during the overnight fast.

**[[Thyroid]] support** activates the mitochondrial enzymes that burn glucose completely to carbon dioxide. When cells are hypothyroid, they cannot fully oxidize glucose regardless of how much is available, and the Randle shift to fat oxidation deepens. Peat cited the case of a friend whose diabetic gangrene healed completely on Armour Thyroid, then returned each time the doctor discontinued it.

**PUFA avoidance** is the most fundamental long-term intervention. Every gram of polyunsaturated fat consumed either gets burned (producing lipid peroxidation products), stored (increasing the toxicity of future stress responses), or must be detoxified by the liver. Reducing intake allows the body's stores to gradually become more saturated, progressively weakening the Randle effect with each passing year.

**Salt** prevents the sodium depletion that triggers adrenaline, which triggers lipolysis. As Peat noted in *When Energy Fails*: "The increase of adrenalin, caused by a deficiency of sodium, is one of the factors that can increase blood pressure; if the tissues' glycogen stores are depleted, the adrenalin will mobilize free fatty acids from the tissues, which tends to inhibit energy production from glucose."

## The Unified Picture

The Randle cycle is not one mechanism among many in Peat's framework. It is the central mechanism through which the modern diet, chronic stress, and hormonal disruption converge to produce the diseases of civilization. The path from seed oils to diabetes, from stress to cancer, from estrogen to heart failure--each runs through the same biochemical chokepoint where fatty acids overwhelm glucose at the entrance to the mitochondria.

As Peat wrote in *Energy, Structure, and Carbon Dioxide*: "Respiration decreases, mitochondria retain calcium, which tends to accumulate until it destroys the mitochondria. The calcium, when it is released from the mitochondria, causes excitation to increase. Stimulation without efficient energy production leads to proteolysis and apoptosis or other forms of cell death." The Randle effect, in this light, is the first step in a cascade that ends in cellular death--whether compressed into hours (as in [[shock]]), stretched across decades (as in [[aging]]), or channeled into uncontrolled proliferation (as in [[cancer]]).

The body was designed to use saturated free fatty acids as a self-correcting energy reserve during brief stress. In a person whose fat stores are predominantly saturated, the Randle effect is mild, temporary, and self-terminating. The modern epidemic of PUFA-loaded fat stores has transformed a protective physiological response into a self-amplifying engine of destruction. Peat's dietary program--the sugar, the coconut oil, the aspirin, the niacinamide, the thyroid, the avoidance of seed oils--can be understood as a systematic effort to reverse this transformation, one meal at a time.
