# Randle Cycle

The biochemistry of [[diabetes]] was essentially solved in 1963, but medicine chose to ignore it. That year, P.J. Randle demonstrated that elevated [[free fatty acids]] directly block cells from oxidizing [[glucose]]—a finding that should have revolutionized how we think about blood sugar. Instead, the focus remained on glucose itself, leading to decades of treatments that address symptoms while ignoring causes.

In Ray Peat's framework, the Randle cycle (sometimes called the Randle effect) is not just a metabolic curiosity but the key to understanding why modern diets produce epidemic levels of metabolic disease.

## How Fatty Acids Block Glucose Oxidation

The mechanism operates through direct enzymatic interference. When free fatty acids rise in the bloodstream, they inhibit pyruvate dehydrogenase—the enzyme cells need to fully oxidize glucose in the [[mitochondria]]. Unable to complete oxidation, cells shift to producing [[lactic acid]], an inefficient backup system that signals metabolic stress.

As Peat explained in a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 discussion on energy and diabetes]], the blockade operates at multiple points: "There are two clear points where free fatty acids inhibit the use of glucose: Pyruvate dehydrogenase, which you need to burn glucose, and they stimulate glucagon. Glucagon turns on the synthesis of glucose at the expense of protein. Glucagon then stimulates the release of *more* fatty acids."

This creates the characteristic vicious cycle. The body, sensing that cells cannot get adequate energy despite available glucose, responds by making even more glucose. The elevated blood sugar seen in diabetics is thus compensation, not cause.

Hospital medicine stumbled upon this dramatically when intravenous fat emulsions became popular for preventing weight loss in cancer patients. In a [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|2010 episode on sugar]], Peat described the discovery: "About fifteen minutes after injecting this nutritional dose of emulsified soy oil intravenously, people would get hyperglycemic." The effect was so rapid it couldn't be denied—fatty acids immediately interfered with glucose metabolism.

## Why Blood Sugar Goes Up in Diabetes

Conventional medicine treats elevated blood sugar as the problem to attack. Peat inverts this understanding entirely. The rising glucose represents the body's intelligent attempt to overcome fatty acid interference—flooding the system with glucose in hopes that some will get through to starving cells.

Evidence for this interpretation comes from measuring [[lactic acid]] in diabetics. As Peat noted in [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|Sugar I (2010)]], "Diabetics often have very high lactic acid concentrations, showing that the glucose *is* getting into the cell, but it can't be used oxidatively because the fatty acids are blocking the respiratory system. So then lactic acid goes to the liver, which turns it into more glucose."

The glucose gets in; it just cannot be properly burned.

> "To a great extent, the rising blood sugar shouldn't be fought in itself. The cause that interferes with the use of glucose should be concentrated on."
> — Ray Peat, [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|Diabetes I]] (2014)

This represents a fundamental inversion of standard treatment. Rather than restricting glucose or forcing it into cells with medications, the therapeutic target should be the fatty acids blocking oxidation.

## Why Polyunsaturated Fats Create Lasting Damage

While any fatty acid can trigger the Randle effect, [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fats]] inflict unique long-term harm that saturated fats do not. The distinction is crucial to Peat's framework.

In an [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|interview on saturated fats]], Peat contrasted the two directly: "If you compare stearic acid—butter, beef fat—and linoleic acid—corn oil—the butter turns off adrenaline and cortisol, while the corn oil turns them on." Saturated fats suppress stress hormones; polyunsaturated fats activate them. Since [[cortisol]], [[adrenaline]], and [[growth hormone]] all promote lipolysis—releasing more fatty acids from storage—PUFA creates self-perpetuating cycles that saturated fat does not.

The damage extends beyond hormones. Peat cites research by Fu demonstrating that the glycated proteins typically blamed on glucose in diabetics "are actually caused more powerfully by polyunsaturated fatty acids." The very tissue damage attributed to high blood sugar may originate primarily from PUFA exposure.

This explains what Peat describes as an apparent biological paradox: "It seems illogical for the body to create those vicious circles where an energy failure turns on exactly what caused it. But it turns out that it's only the *polyunsaturated* fatty acids that have those terrible anti-energy effects."

## The Ketogenic Paradox

The Randle cycle has direct implications for the popular [[ketogenic diet]], particularly its use in cancer treatment. The logic seems intuitive: since cancer cells rely heavily on glucose (the [[Warburg effect]]), starving them of glucose while providing ketones should be therapeutic.

Peat argues this reasoning ignores what actually happens metabolically. In a [[2021-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-managing-hormones-and-cancer-treatment-with-nutrition|2021 discussion on cancer and nutrition]], he explained: "Once anything interferes with your ability to oxidize glucose fully, that liberates any stored fatty acids you have in your tissues. In the ketogenic state, you are releasing free fatty acids into the bloodstream. All of the liberated free fatty acids that have been studied promote metastasis."

The ketogenic diet activates the very mechanism—elevated free fatty acids—that the Randle cycle identifies as problematic. Whatever benefit might come from glucose restriction could be overwhelmed by the metabolic consequences of chronic fatty acid elevation.

## Breaking the Cycle

Understanding the Randle cycle suggests interventions opposite to conventional wisdom.

**Sugar**: Dietary sugar suppresses lipolysis, keeping fatty acids stored rather than circulating. As Peat explained in a [[2021-01-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-january-2021-kmud|2021 interview]], "Increasing the sugar in your diet tends to lower the lipolytic activity, keeps the fat where it belongs, and prevents the Randle shift to fat oxidation." The feared dietary villain becomes protective when the real mechanism is understood.

**[[Thyroid]]**: Adequate thyroid hormone supports glucose oxidation directly. Peat has stated that "supplementing active T3 thyroid hormone will cure lots of the so-called effects of diabetes."

**[[Niacin]]**: This vitamin directly lowers free fatty acids. "It's been recognized for years that niacin is effective for not only heart disease but diabetes simply because it lowers the free fatty acids."

**Avoiding PUFA**: The most important intervention is preventing the accumulation of polyunsaturated fats in tissues, where they can be released during any stress to perpetuate metabolic dysfunction.

## Broader Implications

The Randle cycle connects to Peat's larger view of degenerative disease as fundamentally an energy problem. In [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|Energy Production, Diabetes and Saturated Fats]], he drew the connection explicitly: "Diabetes is a good model of the energy-deprived state. Several years ago, someone suggested that Alzheimer's disease was 'diabetes of the brain.' People are seeing the effects of inflammation in all degenerative diseases. Inflammation involves a failure of energy and a shift to the diabetic metabolism, in which all you can do with glucose is mainly make lactic acid."

Cancer, Alzheimer's, heart disease, aging itself—these conditions share the metabolic signature the Randle cycle describes: elevated fatty acids, impaired glucose oxidation, and compensatory stress responses that make everything worse.
