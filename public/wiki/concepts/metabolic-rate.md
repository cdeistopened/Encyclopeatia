# Metabolic Rate

Metabolic rate is the speed at which the body's cells produce ATP, carbon dioxide, and heat from the oxidation of glucose. In the broader [[metabolism]] umbrella, the rate is the operational handle — the variable a clinician can actually watch change as treatment proceeds. In Ray Peat's writing it occupied the place mainstream endocrinology gave to TSH, and it occupied that place because the temperature, pulse, reflex, and fluid turnover that together index the rate are downstream of the cell's actual use of [[thyroid]] hormone, while the blood test that replaced them in 1947 is not. This article is the clinical-measurement anchor. The mechanism is at [[oxidative-metabolism]] and the failure mode at [[glycolysis]]; the umbrella that ties these together is [[metabolism]]; the longer Peat-biographical case against TSH is at [[tsh]]; the step-by-step tracking protocol with the full decision matrix is at [[temperature-tracking]]. The job of this page is to say what the rate *is*, how Peat measured it in his clinic, what each instrument reveals that the others miss, where the targets fall, and why the lab tests that replaced this method are misleading.

## What metabolic rate is

In the textbook sense, metabolic rate is the quantity of energy a body produces per unit time, conventionally expressed in kilocalories per day or in liters of oxygen consumed per minute. The basal metabolic rate, or BMR, is this quantity measured in a fasted, supine, thermoneutral, awake subject — the energy a body spends keeping itself alive at idle. Indirect calorimetry estimates BMR from oxygen consumed and carbon dioxide produced; direct calorimetry, much rarer, measures the heat actually released. Either way, the physical quantity being indexed is the rate at which mitochondria are completing the oxidation of substrate to CO2 and water, releasing free energy that becomes ATP and heat.

Peat accepted this physical definition and inverted its clinical emphasis. He wrote, in his 2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on Hashimoto's and temperature, that "the best single test would be the Basal Metabolic Rate from the 1930s, measuring how much oxygen you breathe in and how much carbon dioxide you exhale." Calorimetric BMR was the gold standard — but he immediately added that "temperature is just a rough approximation of how much fuel you're burning." The rate of CO2 production is what he actually wanted to know; temperature is a proxy that works because heat is the inevitable byproduct of mitochondrial respiration. A cell oxidizing glucose to CO2 cannot help producing heat. A cell fermenting glucose to lactate produces very little. The temperature is the residue of the metabolism, expressed at the body's surface.

The crucial Peat-specific framing is that two adults burning the same total calories per day can be in radically different metabolic states. One may be producing ATP through full oxidation (~36 ATP per glucose, exhaust CO2 + water); the other may be running stress-driven [[glycolysis]] (~2 ATP per glucose, exhaust [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]) with the calorimeter reading propped up by [[adrenaline]] and [[cortisol]] driving fatty-acid mobilization and futile cycling. The *quantity* of calories burned tells you almost nothing about the *quality* of the metabolism producing them. The clinical instruments — pulse, temperature, reflexes, mood, fluid balance — are designed to read the quality, not just the quantity. The vault's umbrella treatment of why this distinction matters is at [[metabolism]]; the [[oxidative-metabolism]] page works through the mechanism in detail. What follows here is how the quality gets read at the bedside.

## How Peat measured it clinically

Peat learned the clinical method from [[broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]], whose four decades of practice in Fort Collins demonstrated that the most reliable diagnostic for thyroid function was the morning body temperature — not any blood test then or now in routine use. Peat adopted Barnes's protocol early, found it failed in warm humid climates (a hypothyroid patient in Eugene in August can hold 98.6 with the air doing the work), and extended it. The mature Peat method has four instruments. None requires a lab. None requires a doctor's order. The total equipment cost is under thirty dollars.

The four instruments are:

1. **Resting pulse**, taken at the radial artery, seated and still, for a full sixty seconds.
2. **Oral temperature**, taken with a digital thermometer accurate to 0.1°F, held under the tongue until it beeps. (Axillary readings work but read about 0.5°F lower than oral; rectal readings are most accurate but few people will track them daily.)
3. **Achilles tendon reflex relaxation rate**, observed by tapping the tendon with a reflex hammer or pencil while the foot hangs freely.
4. **Fluid turnover**, the difference between fluid intake and urinary output (the residue is evaporation, which scales with metabolic rate).

All four are taken multiple times per day, none interpreted in isolation. The relationship between morning and post-breakfast readings — the *pattern* — does most of the diagnostic work. As Peat put it: "the combination of pulse rate and temperature is much better than either one alone." A high pulse with low temperature means adrenaline is compensating for inadequate thyroid; an Achilles reflex that drifts slowly back to neutral confirms a hypothyroid pattern that pulse and temperature have only suggested. The instruments triangulate. What Peat refused to do is the load-bearing piece — he did not order TSH and accept its number. He read the case from the body, used blood tests as confirmatory or to flag unrelated conditions, and treated the laboratory's normal range as a statistical artifact of a population that is, by his estimate, roughly 40 percent hypothyroid. The full case is at [[tsh]] and [[hypothyroidism]].

## Pulse

Resting pulse, in Peat's framework, runs higher than the modern cardiology textbook expects. His target is **75 to 85 beats per minute, seated and relaxed**. A pulse in the sixties is not, in this framework, the sign of a fit cardiovascular system; it is the sign of a metabolism running cool. Peat cited studies of elderly pacemaker patients whose cognitive function, memory, and mood improved measurably when their paced rate was raised from around 70 to around 85. The brain — the most oxygen-demanding organ in the body — wants more blood than a hibernation-physiology heart rate provides.

Athletic bradycardia at 55 to 60 is, by Peat's reading, the same defect dressed in different clothes. Endurance training depresses thyroid function, raises [[cortisol]], shifts fuel selection toward fat oxidation, and produces a heart that beats less because it is running a lower-throughput metabolism. "Athletic bradycardia at 55 to 60 is hibernation physiology, not health," as the [[temperature-tracking]] protocol page puts it bluntly. Endurance athletes often have the thyroid profile of a cold-adapted rodent.

The post-meal response is at least as diagnostic as the resting number. A healthy pulse rises 5 to 10 beats per minute after a meal containing carbohydrate, peaks within 30 to 60 minutes, and returns toward baseline within an hour. The rise reflects the liver successfully converting some of the meal's glucose to T3 and the heart responding to the resulting hormonal signal. A pulse that *falls* after food is a stress-driven signature: cortisol was holding the rate up, the food triggered an insulin response, the insulin drove cortisol down, and the pulse fell with it. The food did not cause the problem; it exposed the problem. This is one of the cleanest single observations in the entire Peat method, and it cannot be obtained from any blood test at any cost.

The most diagnostically confusing pattern is a pulse persistently above 90 at rest combined with a temperature below 98.0. This is not high metabolism; this is the stress-driven pseudo-high metabolism that hypothyroid patients commonly present with. Adrenaline is racing the heart while the cells fail to produce enough heat to warm the body. In his emails on [[high-metabolism|high metabolism]] Peat described his own experience of exactly this pattern — a measured BMR roughly three times the population average that *normalized* (came down to roughly normal) within days of starting thyroid supplementation. "Thyroid is the basic regulator of blood glucose, and it causes it to be fully oxidized for energy, so that it produces ATP efficiently, on relatively few calories." The pulse fell because thyroid replaced adrenaline as the driver. The metabolism became efficient, and the body stopped needing to burn fuel wastefully to compensate. The high pulse before treatment was a sign of inefficiency, not vigor.

## Temperature

Oral temperature is the second instrument, and the targets are unforgiving. The Peat-canonical reference numbers, drawn from Barnes's protocol and refined across four decades of Peat's clinical correspondence:

- **Morning oral temperature on waking, before getting out of bed:** 97.8 to 98.2°F borderline; rising toward 98.6°F over the course of treatment. Below 97.8 is a low-thyroid signal.
- **Post-breakfast temperature, 30 to 60 minutes after eating:** should rise above the morning baseline. Target 98.6°F or slightly above.
- **Afternoon temperature, 3 to 4 PM:** the daily peak of the thyroid rhythm. Target 98.6 to 99.0°F. Below 98.4 in the afternoon is a strong hypothyroid signal even if the morning reading looked acceptable.
- **Axillary mid-morning:** approximately 0.5°F lower than oral, so 97.5°F under the arm corresponds roughly to 98.0°F oral. Track only one site consistently.

Seasonal variation is real. A cool dry climate (Fort Collins, where Barnes practiced) forces the body to make its own heat and produces clean readings. A hot humid climate (Eugene in August) lets the air do the warming and produces deceptively normal temperatures in obviously hypothyroid patients. Peat ignored temperature data taken in a hot bedroom, under an electric blanket, or during a heat wave, and substituted pulse and reflex testing for warm-weather months. Sex differences: women run about 0.5°F higher in the luteal phase due to [[progesterone]], so the follicular phase is the cleaner hypothyroid indicator; a flat temperature across the cycle suggests inadequate progesterone production. Children run substantially higher than adults; the adult 98.6 target is a *floor* for them.

The single most diagnostic temperature pattern, the one Peat treated as nearly pathognomonic, is **a morning temperature that *falls* after breakfast**. Stress hormones held the temperature up overnight; food removed the need for those stress hormones; when cortisol and adrenaline dropped, the true low metabolic rate was revealed. This is not an argument for skipping breakfast. It is an argument for a breakfast large enough — orange juice, milk, eggs, salt, fruit, coffee — to supply the liver with the glucose, protein, and minerals it needs to convert T4 to T3 and keep the temperature up *without* stress hormones. The food is doing the work the cortisol was doing, more efficiently. The full pattern matrix lives at [[temperature-tracking]]; the brief decision rule is: if the temperature falls after a proper breakfast, the diet is insufficient and supplementation may be necessary; if the temperature rises after breakfast, the system is responding and the diet is working.

Surface temperature — hands, feet, nose, fingertips — is a fifth diagnostic, taken by touch. Cold extremities with normal oral temperature signal high adrenaline cutting off peripheral circulation to preserve core flow. The nose and fingertips are the most sensitive readers; they are the first places the body sacrifices under metabolic stress. Especially in overweight patients, axillary readings lag because the insulation is thicker. Checking the mouth alongside the armpit, while noticing whether fingers, toes, and nose feel cold, gives a more complete picture than any single site.

## The Achilles tendon reflex

The fastest, cheapest, most underused diagnostic in Peat's toolkit is the Achilles tendon reflex, and the diagnostic signal is **the speed of relaxation, not the strength of the contraction**. Sit on the edge of a counter so the foot hangs freely. Tap the Achilles tendon firmly with a reflex hammer or a pencil. In a healthy euthyroid person, the foot swings down sharply and returns to neutral promptly, sometimes with a small overshoot. In a hypothyroid person, the foot swings down and then drifts back slowly, like a door-closer with worn fluid. The drift is the diagnostic.

Peat explained the mechanism in his 1993 newsletter "Thyroid: Misconceptions": "Most physicians know how to test the Achilles tendon reflex, but believe it is a poor indicator of thyroid function. Often, this is because they have observed the reflex contraction itself, rather than the relaxation rate, which gives a clear indication of the rate at which energy is regenerated in the muscle." The contraction is driven by calcium flooding the sarcoplasmic reticulum; the relaxation requires ATP to pump that calcium back out. A hypothyroid muscle relaxes slowly because it does not have enough ATP to pump calcium quickly. The same defect is visible on an electrocardiogram as a prolonged QT interval, because the T wave represents ventricular repolarization — the cardiac equivalent of the Achilles relaxation. As Peat described it in a 2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on heart disease: "If your thyroid function is good, the relaxation will be instantaneous — your foot will relax with a floppy, instantaneous, complete relaxation. If your thyroid is low, it'll come back slowly to exactly where it started. The heart is doing the same thing with a prolongation of the QT interval."

Barnes used the Achilles reflex as a quick office screen. Peat endorsed it as a free, fast, and surprisingly sensitive supplement to temperature and pulse, especially in patients whose temperature readings are confounded by climate, electric blankets, or stress-driven adrenaline. A reflex that snaps back is reassuring even if a single temperature reading looks low; a reflex that drifts back is alarming even if temperature and pulse look adequate.

## Fluid turnover

The fourth and least-used Peat instrument is the rate at which the body evaporates water. A healthy adult takes in roughly two quarts more fluid per day than they excrete in urine; the difference is evaporation through breath, skin, and stool. Hypothyroid patients evaporate less than a quart a day. They are, in Peat's phrase, waterlogged — the mucopolysaccharide accumulation that produced the puffy myxedematous face of nineteenth-century thyroid medicine is the visible expression of this fluid retention, and it clears quickly once metabolic rate rises. The puffy face is data.

## The TSH problem

TSH is a **trailing indicator measured at the wrong point in the loop**. It reflects the pituitary's opinion of thyroid sufficiency, not the cells' actual use of thyroid hormone. Stress lowers TSH directly: a patient under sufficient cortisol stress can have TSH near zero while their thyroid produces almost nothing, because the same cortisol that suppresses the thyroid suppresses the pituitary. The feedback loop disappears. The blood test reads normal. T4 in blood is the inactive storage form; conversion to active T3 happens in the liver and requires adequate glucose, protein, and [[selenium]]. T3 in blood has a half-life of about a day and fluctuates with every meal. A snapshot T3 measures that moment, not the cellular saturation that determines symptoms. Peat's summary across many interviews: the blood test measures the messenger, the thermometer measures the message received.

The historical case is the most damaging piece. As Peat documented in his 2007 newsletter "Thyroid Therapies, Confusion and Fraud," until WWII hypothyroidism was diagnosed on BMR plus clinical signs; roughly 40 percent of the population qualified. In the late 1940s the Protein Bound Iodine (PBI) test was introduced; it found high iodine-bound protein in ~95 percent of the population, which was reinterpreted to mean only 5 percent needed supplementation. The diagnosed rate collapsed from 40 percent to 5 percent overnight. By the 1960s the PBI test was retracted as biologically meaningless — but the 5 percent threshold it created persisted, and the TSH test that replaced PBI inherited that calibration. A test whose biological basis was retracted in the 1960s continues to define normal in 2026. Full case at [[tsh]] and [[broda-barnes]].

This is not a license to ignore blood tests. Cholesterol above ~200 on a low-PUFA diet is a useful hypothyroid signal because the liver requires T3 to process cholesterol. Free T3 in the upper quartile with TSH below 2.0 is at least consistent with adequate function. High reverse T3 with low free T3 suggests stressed conversion. But the order matters: read the body first, use blood tests to confirm or to flag unrelated conditions, and never let a "normal" TSH overrule a 97.2°F afternoon temperature and an Achilles reflex that takes two seconds to settle back.

## What raises metabolic rate

The substances and conditions that raise oxidative throughput compress into a short list. Each has its own vault entry; the brief versions, with their direction of action:

- **[[thyroid|Thyroid hormone]]** (T3, NDT, occasionally combinations). The master regulator. T3 increases cytochrome oxidase activity, drives mitochondrial biogenesis, raises the NAD+/NADH ratio, stimulates uncoupling. The single highest-leverage intervention when temperature and pulse fail to respond to diet within two weeks.
- **Adequate sugar** (fruit, juice, honey, milk). Glucose is the preferred fuel of the oxidative pathway. Restricting it forces the body into the [[stress|stress metabolism]]. Detail at [[glucose]].
- **Adequate protein**, ~80–100g/day, with [[gelatin]] for glycine balance and dairy for calcium and the thyroid-supportive amino-acid ratio. Protein deficiency stalls thyroid synthesis.
- **Sun and red light.** Cytochrome c oxidase absorbs red and near-infrared light directly. Detail at [[red-light]].
- **Warmth.** Cold drives stress hormones, suppresses thyroid, shifts fuel selection toward fat and lactate. Warm rooms, warm food, warm baths.
- **Calcium, magnesium, salt.** Calcium suppresses [[parathyroid-hormone|PTH]]; magnesium stabilizes ATP; salt lowers stress hormones at adequate intake.
- **[[niacinamide]]**, [[aspirin]], **[[carbon-dioxide]]**, **saturated fats**. The supporting cast. Each lowers a specific obstacle to oxidation.

The full mechanistic treatment is at [[metabolism]] under "What raises metabolism"; the dietary protocol is at [[anti-stress-diet]].

## What lowers it

The mirror list is more important than the raise list because in modern environments most of these are running by default:

- **Chronic [[stress]].** The adrenaline → free-fatty-acid → Randle block → lactate → cortisol cascade is the operational meaning of low metabolic rate in most adults. Detail at [[stress]] and [[cortisol]].
- **[[pufa|PUFA]] accumulation** in [[cardiolipin]]. Multi-year clearance. The chronic suppressor.
- **Undereating, fasting, low-carb.** Lowers T3 within days. The popular [[intermittent-fasting]] literature is a sales pitch for the metabolic state that defines starvation, in Peat's reading.
- **Cold exposure, cold plunges, endurance training.** Direct stressors.
- **[[estrogen]] excess, [[iron]] overload, [[endotoxin|endotoxin]].** Each suppresses oxidation through a distinct mechanism. Compressed in the [[metabolism]] umbrella; detailed in their own pages.

## Tracking discipline

Pick a digital thermometer accurate to 0.1°F. Cheap 0.2°F units do not have the resolution to track the changes that matter. Take readings at the same three times every day: on waking before getting out of bed, 30–60 minutes after breakfast, and between 3 and 4 PM. Record pulse for a full 60 seconds, seated, at each of those times. Log in a spreadsheet or notebook with columns for date, AM temp/pulse, post-breakfast temp/pulse, PM temp/pulse, sleep quality, mood, ambient temperature, menstrual phase (women), and unusual events (alcohol, illness, travel, hard workout). Track for **at least 14 days** before drawing conclusions; two weeks averages out the random daily noise and exposes the pattern.

Confounders to flag and exclude from interpretation: ambient temperature (hot rooms mask low core temp), illness and fever, menstrual luteal phase, pregnancy, alcohol the previous night, fasting, over-exercise, electric blankets, thyroid dose timing, and cold drinks or gum within fifteen minutes of an oral reading. The full confounder list and the decision matrix for acting on the pattern live at [[temperature-tracking]]. The shortest decision rule: temperature stable below 98.0°F across the day for two weeks on a proper diet is a signal that diet alone is insufficient; temperature rising week over week is a signal to keep going and add nothing.

## Counter-arguments

- **The calorimetry-only camp.** Indirect calorimetry is the closest modern physiology has to a direct measurement of metabolic rate, and the only one that distinguishes oxidative from glycolytic substrate use cleanly. It is expensive, not routinely available, and not feasible for daily tracking. The Peatian position accepts calorimetric BMR as the gold standard *in concept* (Peat called it "the best single test") but argues the bedside proxies are more useful in practice. The literature that treats calorimetric BMR as the only legitimate measurement is technically correct and clinically useless.
- **The TSH-is-sufficient endocrinology school.** Mainstream endocrinology treats TSH as the single best diagnostic, with free T4 and free T3 as confirmatory. The reference range is calibrated on a population that, by Peat's reading, is approximately 40 percent hypothyroid. The historical case — descent from the PBI test of 1947, persistence of the 5 percent threshold after the test was retracted — is largely unanswered in the endocrinology literature, which treats the range as if derived from healthy controls rather than from a biologically meaningless predecessor.
- **The "metabolic rate is fixed by genetics" position.** Twin studies show modest heritability of resting metabolic rate, but the *modifiable* variance — by diet, hormones, temperature, light, and PUFA load — dwarfs the heritable component. See [[genetic-determinism]]. The clinical observation that hypothyroid patients normalize their metabolic rate within weeks of adequate treatment is the strongest single piece of evidence against the fixed-genetics framing.
- **The CR-extends-lifespan literature.** Calorie restriction extends lifespan in most laboratory species; the Peatian position attributes most of the effect to artifacts (cooler housing, removal of inflammatory PUFA, cohort selection) rather than reduced rate per se. The mainstream interpretation — that *lower* rate is protective — is directly opposite to Peat's. Unresolved whether rodent CR data measures the same variable.
- **The "you can't measure metabolism with a thermometer" objection.** Strict physiology argues temperature is influenced by ambient conditions, vasomotor tone, and hydration. The Peatian rejoinder is empirical: the temperature *pattern* (morning baseline, post-breakfast response, afternoon peak) tracks the clinical course of hypothyroid treatment with high reliability across thousands of cases. The instrument is noisy on any single reading and reliable in pattern across two weeks. Robustness comes from triangulation across pulse, temperature, reflex, and fluid turnover.

## Data gaps

- **Individual optimal range.** The population target (~98.6°F mid-morning, pulse ~85, Achilles reflex snap-back within ~250ms) is a mean. Whether some healthy adults run cleanly at 98.0 / pulse 75, or whether such patients are subtly suppressed, is not resolved in Peat's writing.
- **Female menstrual cycle effects.** Luteal-phase elevation is real, but the magnitude of the effect on post-breakfast response, afternoon peak, and resting pulse is not characterized. Protocols recommend separating phases without specifying comparison thresholds.
- **Sleep-temperature dynamics.** The relationship between core temperature during sleep, mid-sleep awakenings, and morning waking temperature is qualitative in Peat's writing. Whether the morning low is the sleeping low, or whether temperature has already begun rising at waking, is unclear without continuous monitoring.
- **Pediatric thresholds.** Children run hotter than adults; the boundary between healthy high metabolism and stress-driven hyperthermia in a child is not laid out.
- **The hyperthyroid endpoint.** At very high thyroid doses, benefits invert: muscle wasting, bone loss, atrial fibrillation, anxiety. The inverted-U is qualitative; the temperature and pulse thresholds at which iatrogenic hyperthyroidism becomes a concern are not quantified beyond "the patient feels worse, not better."
- **Continuous monitoring.** Wearables now measure pulse continuously and skin temperature with reasonable accuracy. Whether continuous data refines the Peat instruments (catching mid-day stress dips) or merely produces noise is unresolved.
- **Lactate-to-CO2 ratio.** Combined breath CO2 + blood lactate would be a more direct read of the oxidative-vs-glycolytic split than temperature or pulse — closer to what Peat actually cared about — but is not yet a routine clinical tool.
