# Regeneration

Mainstream medicine inherited from the mid-twentieth century the working assumption that adult mammalian tissues are mostly post-mitotic — that hearts, brains, kidneys, and pancreases cannot meaningfully regenerate, and that what looks like regeneration is either fibrotic scarring or the privileged exception of a few stem-cell-rich compartments (gut, blood, skin, liver). The clinical corollary: lost tissue is lost, and the only path forward is technological — bone-marrow harvests, induced pluripotent stem cells, mesenchymal-stem-cell injections, the tissue-engineering pipelines that have been the announced future of medicine since the 1990s.

Ray Peat spent his career arguing that the framing has the polarity reversed. Regeneration in his account is the *default state* of every tissue when two conditions are met: adequate oxidative energy production, and the structure-supporting nutrients (protein with the right amino-acid profile, the fat-soluble vitamins, magnesium, copper, the B-vitamins) that the regenerating cells need as substrate. Degeneration is what happens when those conditions fail. "Regenerative healing also requires freedom from substances that inhibit the digestion of the debris. The great decline in proteolytic autophagy that occurs with aging can be reduced by inhibiting the release of fatty acids," Peat wrote in [[regeneration-and-degeneration|*Regeneration and Degeneration* (2010)]], the article that is the load-bearing source for this page. The single most important sentence: aging is, in this frame, just *cumulative loss of regenerative capacity*, and the cumulative loss tracks the cumulative substitution of [[pufa|PUFA]] into mitochondrial cardiolipin, the cumulative drift toward estrogen and cortisol dominance, and the cumulative failure of the phagocytic clearance that every regenerative process requires.

This page sits adjacent to [[aging]] (the chronic-degeneration story), [[fibrosis]] (what failed regeneration looks like at the tissue level), and [[inflammation]] (the mediator between scarring and restorative healing). The substrate biochemistry is at [[oxidative-metabolism]] and [[mitochondria]]; this page integrates the regeneration-specific arguments and stays compressed on the upstream chain via `[[hub-link]]` cross-references rather than re-deriving them.

## The regeneration-degeneration axis

The first conceptual move is to recognize that every tissue is constantly remodeling. The gut epithelium turns over in three to seven days. Skin replaces its outer layer every month. Blood is fully replenished every three to four months. The liver, even without traumatic insult, runs a "cell streaming" process that continuously replaces parenchyma. Bone is being resorbed and rebuilt at every moment. Even the tissues mainstream gerontology calls "post-mitotic" — neurons, cardiomyocytes — are now known to undergo turnover, slowly but measurably, throughout life. The question is *not* whether tissues remodel, but whether the remodeling improves or degrades function.

The improving end is the regeneration the embryo runs as standard equipment. Fetal wounds heal without scars; cleft palates can be surgically corrected in utero and leave no histological trace ([[regeneration-and-degeneration|*Regeneration and Degeneration*]] cites Weinzweig et al., 2002). Hyaluronic acid, not collagen, is the dominant extracellular material at this stage, and its decomposition products carry regulatory signals that guide redifferentiation. Cytokine concentrations are different — PDGF-A, the three forms of TGF-beta, IGF-1, and bFGF are all *lower* in fetal tissue than in adult tissue, and when TGF-beta1 is added experimentally to a healing fetal wound, the fetus loses its scarless capacity and starts running the fibrotic response (Lanning et al., 1999). The prostaglandins PGE2 and PGF2-alpha produce inflammation in adult rabbits but not in fetal rabbits (Morykwas et al., 1994). The fetus is not a different *kind* of animal from the adult — it is the same animal in a metabolic configuration the adult has lost, and the configuration is reproducible in principle by restoring the inputs (low PUFA, high CO2, adequate protein, thyroid sufficiency).

The degrading end is the fibrotic response that comes to dominate adult healing as the years accumulate. Collagen replaces parenchyma. The replacement is mechanically tough but functionally inert. "In adulthood as the amount of inflammation increases, healing fails to completely restore lost structures and functions, resulting in scarring, the replacement of functional tissue with fibrous tissue. Identifiable changes in the nature of inflammation under different conditions can explain some of these losses of healing capacity. Factors that limit inflammation and fibrosis, while permitting tissue remodeling, could facilitate regeneration and retard aging," Peat wrote in [[regeneration-and-degeneration|*Regeneration and Degeneration*]]. The unique signal of this page is the framing of the *axis itself*: every tissue at every moment is moving along it, and what sets the direction is the local energy state.

## Conditions for regeneration

The conditions that move a tissue along the regenerative end of the axis are the same conditions that support [[oxidative-metabolism]] generally, applied to the specific situation of damaged or remodeling tissue.

**High oxidative metabolism.** Phagocytosis — the clearance of damaged cells and debris that is the first step of any restorative healing — is one of the most energy-expensive processes a cell can run. "Degenerating tissue stimulates the formation of new tissue, but this requires adequate cellular energy for phagocytosis, which requires proper thyroid function," Peat wrote in [[regeneration-and-degeneration|*Regeneration and Degeneration*]]. Tissues with low oxidative capacity heal slowly and badly; tissues with high oxidative capacity heal fast and cleanly.

**Adequate [[progesterone|progesterone]]** in women, and smaller doses in men. Progesterone opposes estrogen at the tissue level, suppresses serotonin, blocks the inflammatory cytokines that drive fibrosis, and supports mitochondrial respiration directly. "Fibrosis of the heart and liver, which are often considered to be unavoidably progressive, can be regressed by thyroid hormone, and various fibroses, including breast, liver, and mesentery, have been regressed by progesterone treatment," Peat wrote in [[regeneration-and-degeneration|*Regeneration and Degeneration*]].

**Low [[pufa|PUFA]].** The single most important variable, and the one most likely to be invisible to mainstream wound-healing research because PUFA is so universally present in modern tissue that it is treated as a baseline rather than a variable. "When animals are made deficient in the polyunsaturated fatty acids, their wounds heal, with normal or accelerated collagen synthesis, and with more vigorous collagen breakdown" (Peat citing Parnham et al., 1977). PUFA-derived prostaglandins suppress local cell division and shift "responsibility for tissue repair to mobile cells, for example stem cells from the blood." They inhibit the proteolytic enzymes needed for tissue remodeling. They kill the monocytes essential for proper development and regeneration (Sweeney et al., 2001). They interfere with the migration signals that guide phagocyte recruitment (Ferrante et al., 1994). Full mechanism at [[pufa]] and [[lipid-peroxidation]].

**Low estrogen.** Estrogen "interferes with energy production, so if its stimulation is prolonged, cells are damaged or killed, rather than being stimulated to regenerate," Peat wrote in [[stem-cells-cell-culture-and-culture-issues-in-regeneration|*Stem Cells, Cell Culture, and Culture* (2008)]]. Estrogen activates collagen synthesis and aromatase, promotes fibrosis, and (through its interaction with serotonin and PUFA) drives the chronic-inflammatory signaling that converts a potentially regenerative wound into a scarring one.

**Low cortisol baseline.** Acute cortisol can be permissive for repair (it suppresses excessive inflammation), but chronically elevated cortisol is catabolic — it breaks down the protein the regenerating tissue needs as substrate, and suppresses the protective steroids whose synthesis the aged mitochondrion can no longer support without help. Full story at [[cortisol]].

**Magnesium, B-vitamins, copper.** The cofactors of the respiratory enzymes. Copper-deficient cytochrome oxidase cannot consume oxygen completely. Magnesium-depleted ATPases cannot maintain ionic gradients. These are the structural conditions regenerating tissue needs as substrate.

**[[thyroid|Thyroid hormone]].** "The thyroid hormone is necessary for liver regeneration, and the ability of the thyroid gland itself to regenerate might be related to the also great ability of the adrenal cortex to regenerate," Peat wrote in [[regeneration-and-degeneration|*Regeneration and Degeneration*]]. T3 stimulates the removal of inactive cells (Kurata et al., 1980); experimental hyperthyroidism *increases* the effectiveness of nerve graft implantation into the hippocampus (Lewin-Kowalik et al., 2002). Hypothyroidism is one of the most reliable suppressors of regenerative capacity, and the failure to recognize this is responsible for "many inappropriate medical treatments."

These conditions converge on a single physiological signature: high local production of CO2 relative to oxygen consumption. "The amount of disorganized fibrous material formed in injured tissue is variable, and it depends on the state of the individual, and on the particular situation of the tissue. For example, the membranes lining the mouth, and the bones and bone marrow, and the thymus gland are able to regenerate without scarring. What they have in common with each other is a relatively high ratio of carbon dioxide to oxygen," Peat wrote. The CO2 signature is what unifies the protective hormones, the substrate nutrients, and the avoidance of suppressors into a single working variable ([[carbon-dioxide]]).

## Tissues that regenerate well — when conditions are right

The tissues the conventional list calls "regenerative" — liver, gut epithelium, skin, blood, bone — are not biologically privileged. They are the tissues in which the conditions above are most often met. The liver runs hot, rich in mitochondria, with the highest local concentration of T3 of any organ (because the liver itself produces about 70% of the body's active T3 from T4). The gut epithelium is the most metabolically active tissue in the body. The skin has access to ample oxygen and constantly produces CO2 at its surface. The hematopoietic compartment of the bone marrow is one of the highest-CO2 environments in the body.

The implication: the *same* tissues, in the *same* organism, can lose their regenerative capacity when the conditions deteriorate, and other tissues — those on the "non-regenerative" list — can recover regenerative capacity when the conditions are restored. The liver in late cirrhosis stops regenerating not because the hepatocytes have lost their capacity but because the local energy environment has collapsed.

A specific and underappreciated example: fingertip regeneration. "The regeneration of finger tips, including a well-formed nail if some of the base remained, will occur if the wounded end of the finger is kept enclosed, for example by putting a metal or plastic tube over the finger. The humidity keeps the wound from forming a dry scab, and the cells near the surface will consume oxygen and produce carbon dioxide, keeping the ratio of carbon dioxide to oxygen much higher than in normal uninjured tissue" (from [[regeneration-and-degeneration|*Regeneration and Degeneration*]]). The intervention is mechanical — occlusive dressing — but the mechanism is metabolic.

## Tissues that resist regeneration — and where the resistance is conditional

The historical list of "non-regenerative" tissues is shorter than the textbooks claimed.

**Long-axon nervous tissue.** The CNS was for most of the twentieth century considered incapable of regeneration — Cajal's "no new neurons after birth" dictum was textbook into the 1990s. The position is now untenable. Adult hippocampal neurogenesis is well-documented; the subventricular zone produces new neurons throughout life; spinal-cord and peripheral-nerve regeneration occur to a meaningful degree when the local environment supports it. "Salamanders, which are able to regenerate legs, jaw, spinal cord, retina and parts of the brain (Winklemann & Winklemann, 1970), spend most of their time under cover in burrows, which besides preventing drying of their moist skin, keeps the ratio of carbon dioxide to oxygen fairly high," Peat noted. The salamander is not a different *kind* of vertebrate from a mammal at the level of regenerative competence; it lives in a CO2-rich micro-environment, has very little PUFA in its tissues, and runs the protective hormonal profile that mammals lose with maturation. [[niacinamide]] "protects against the development of scars in spinal cord injuries, facilitates recovery from traumatic brain injury, and accelerates healing generally."

**Cardiac muscle.** The classical position was that cardiomyocytes are post-mitotic and that post-MI fibrotic scarring is permanent. The position is partially wrong. Cardiac stem cells exist; cardiomyocyte turnover occurs throughout life (about 1% per year in young adults, declining with age). The fibrotic outcome that dominates clinical practice is, in Peat's frame, the predictable result of the metabolic state of the post-MI patient (elevated cortisol, PUFA mobilization, estrogen-driven aromatase in damaged cardiac tissue, low T3 from non-thyroidal-illness syndrome) — not a structural limitation of cardiac biology.

**Pancreatic beta cells, kidney parenchyma.** Same pattern: regeneration occurs when the local environment supports it, fails when it does not. The older theory was wrong about absolute inability; what remains is the empirical observation that *under modern metabolic conditions* these tissues regenerate poorly. Change the conditions and the empirical observation changes.

## The stem-cell question

The mainstream framing has consolidated around the stem-cell program: most adult regeneration is attributable to tissue-resident or bone-marrow-derived stem cells; the therapeutic future of regenerative medicine lies in identifying, expanding, and reintroducing these cells. Induced pluripotent stem cells, mesenchymal stem cell therapy, and the broader stem-cell-fetishism of the field have produced real research progress but, in Peat's reading, a misallocation of attention.

Peat's position in [[stem-cells-cell-culture-and-culture-issues-in-regeneration|*Stem Cells, Cell Culture, and Culture* (2008)]] is that most regeneration in adult mammals is not stem-cell-mediated in the canonical sense. It is dedifferentiation of parenchymal cells: a hepatocyte adjacent to a wound can dedifferentiate, divide, and redifferentiate; "connective tissue cells becoming nerve cells, nerve cells becoming muscle and fiber cells, fat, fiber, and muscle cells redifferentiating." The "stem cell" identified in tissue X after regeneration is, in many cases, not a developmentally privileged precursor cell that was waiting in a niche but a dedifferentiated parenchymal cell whose lineage is being read backward by an investigator who assumes the textbook model.

The deeper problem is what the stem-cell framing misses: the energy-substrate question. "When a hormone such as noradrenaline or insulin causes a stem cell to differentiate in vitro, it causes new mitochondria to form," Peat wrote. The mitochondrial biogenesis is the substantive event; the "stem cell" label is the proximate descriptor. A regenerating tissue is one in which cells are forming new mitochondria, restoring respiratory capacity, and re-expressing the differentiated phenotype that respiratory capacity supports. Stem-cell injection without restoring the local energy environment produces transient engraftment and poor functional integration.

The clinical implication is not that stem-cell therapy is fake — some MSC, hematopoietic, and limbal-stem-cell results are real. The implication is that the stem-cell modality is one of several ways to provide the local environment with what it needs, and that the more efficient interventions — thyroid sufficiency, PUFA depletion, CO2 elevation, progesterone, niacinamide, occlusive dressing for surface wounds — get less attention because they are not patentable and not high-technology. "The emphasis on stem cell technology has the function of directing attention away from traditional knowledge, the way allopathic medicine has de-emphasized the intrinsic ability of people to recover from disease."

## Peat's practical regeneration support

The clinical interventions Peat recommended for supporting regeneration are the same metabolic-substrate interventions that protect against [[aging]] and the chronic degenerative diseases.

**High-protein-with-[[gelatin|gelatin]] diet.** Adequate protein (about 100 g/day for an adult) provides the amino-acid substrate. The gelatin fraction (roughly one-third to one-half of total protein intake) shifts the amino-acid balance toward glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline — the amino acids that oppose the tryptophan-serotonin axis driving fibrosis, and that are the structural substrate for the new collagen healing tissue needs in measured amounts.

**[[vitamin-a|Vitamin A]].** A regenerating epithelium needs retinoic acid to express the differentiated phenotype properly.

**[[vitamin-k|Vitamin K]].** Both for calcium handling (K2 directs calcium to bone rather than soft tissue) and for mitochondrial-respiration support.

**[[vitamin-e|Vitamin E]].** Principal protection against the lipid peroxidation PUFA accumulation makes inevitable. "Aspirin and vitamin E are other natural substances that are therapeutic in unnaturally large amounts because of our continual exposure to the highly unsaturated plant-derived n-3 and n-6 fats," Peat wrote.

**[[niacinamide|Niacinamide]].** "Niacinamide is a nutrient that inhibits the release of fatty acids, and it also activates phagocytic activity and lowers phosphate. It protects against the development of scars in spinal cord injuries, facilitates recovery from traumatic brain injury, and accelerates healing generally," Peat wrote in [[regeneration-and-degeneration|*Regeneration and Degeneration*]]. Dose range: 50-100 mg several times per day.

**Sleep, sunlight, low-PUFA-cooking-fat substitution.** Sleep is when the heaviest regenerative work happens. Sunlight (particularly red and infrared wavelengths) supports cytochrome oxidase activity directly. Substituting saturated and monounsaturated cooking fats for PUFA-rich oils is the largest single dietary intervention for shifting the body away from the fibrotic-default state.

**Carbon dioxide.** Where local CO2 elevation is feasible (bag breathing for chronic conditions, CO2 baths for limb wounds, occlusive dressing for fingertip injuries), it directly shifts the local axis toward regeneration. Living at moderate altitude produces a chronic CO2-and-lactate-suppression effect that may contribute to the lower incidence of keloid formation at altitude noted by Ranganathan (1961).

**[[progesterone|Progesterone]] (women) and [[thyroid|thyroid]] (both sexes).** The hormonal levers. Progesterone has reversed fibrosis of breast, liver, and mesentery in clinical reports. Thyroid is the most direct support for the oxidative metabolism that all the rest depends on.

## Counter-arguments

**Modern regenerative-medicine field.** The mainstream stem-cell-and-tissue-engineering program has produced real successes — corneal-stem-cell transplantation for limbal-stem-cell deficiency, hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation for hematologic malignancies, iPSC-derived retinal pigment epithelium grafts showing efficacy in macular degeneration trials, engineered cartilage and skin grafts in clinical use. The Peat-frame critique is not that these therapies are fake but that they are technology-intensive workarounds for a metabolic environment that, if restored, would make most of them unnecessary. The honest counter-argument: some lesions — large traumatic tissue loss, congenital absence of an organ, complete hematopoietic failure — exceed what metabolic restoration alone can address.

**Induced pluripotent stem cells.** The Yamanaka-factor work (2012 Nobel) demonstrated that adult somatic cells can be reprogrammed to a pluripotent state. The Peat-frame reading: this is one *experimental* route to the dedifferentiation-redifferentiation cycle the embryo runs as standard equipment, and the *physiological* route is metabolic restoration. The iPSC counter-argument is that transcription-factor control gives a level of cell-fate precision the metabolic-restoration pathway cannot match — and for engineered-tissue applications that is true.

**Mesenchymal stem cell therapy.** MSC injections for osteoarthritis, ischemic cardiomyopathy, and inflammatory bowel disease have shown variable but real effects. The mechanism appears to be partly trophic-signaling and partly immunomodulatory rather than direct differentiation. The Peat-frame reading: MSCs are one delivery mechanism for the trophic signals any regenerating tissue needs, and endogenous production of those signals is the more direct route. The MSC industry's counter: injecting the source is faster and more reliable than restoring endogenous production. Honest answer: not mutually exclusive, and the more interesting question is whether MSC injection produces durable improvement *without* metabolic restoration (evidence so far: often transient).

**Cardiac regeneration after MI.** The Peat-frame argument that post-MI fibrosis is conditional on metabolic state has not been tested in a large trial. The honest version: the proposition is mechanistically plausible and supported by individual case reports, but the gold-standard intervention trial has not been done.

**The age-dependence of regenerative capacity.** Even granting Peat's frame, regenerative capacity falls with age. The counter-argument: this is the strongest evidence for an *intrinsic* (perhaps stem-cell-pool-depletion) mechanism rather than a purely environmental one. The Peat-frame response: the age-dependence tracks the metabolic-state changes catalogued at [[aging]] — accumulated PUFA, accumulated iron, falling thyroid, falling progesterone — and an old organism whose metabolic state was restored would, in principle, recover regenerative capacity in proportion. The proposition has not been falsified, but it has also not been demonstrated to the standard the counter-argument demands.

## Data gaps

**No quantitative regenerative-capacity model.** The framework predicts that regenerative capacity is a function of the local metabolic state — but does not formalize the prediction into an equation that would let a clinician calculate, from a patient's measurements, an expected regenerative rate or expected effect-size for each intervention.

**No large trial of the combined Peat-frame stack against regenerative endpoints.** Niacinamide, vitamin E, progesterone, thyroid, gelatin, and PUFA depletion have each been studied individually in trials targeting specific outcomes (mostly not regeneration), but the combined stack — and the dose ranges Peat recommended, which differ from typical clinical doses — has not been tested as a coherent intervention against regenerative endpoints.

**The salamander-mammal gap.** The Peat-frame claim that the difference is mostly metabolic (CO2-rich micro-environment, low tissue PUFA, retained embryonic-like signaling) is plausible but not demonstrated. The experimental tests — raising local CO2 to salamander-like levels in a mammalian limb, fully depleting PUFA before traumatic injury — have not been done at the scale that would settle the question.

**The dedifferentiation-vs-stem-cell controversy.** Peat's argument that most adult regeneration is dedifferentiated parenchymal cells, not true stem cells, is supported by individual experimental observations (Hendrix on tumor-cell redifferentiation, the cell-streaming observations of gut and liver, the Johnson 2003 finding that oocytes derive from somatic cells) but not by a comprehensive lineage-tracing program. Some regeneration events look stem-cell-mediated by modern lineage-tracing methods; some look dedifferentiation-mediated. Relative contribution in different tissues remains open.

**The CO2-and-fibrosis literature is thin.** Clinical use of CO2 baths, intra-abdominal CO2 insufflation, and bag-breathing has produced encouraging individual results but has not been formally tested in large randomized trials against fibrotic endpoints.

**The fetal-to-adult transition.** The biochemical changes that accompany the loss of scarless regeneration (rising cytokine concentrations, shifting prostaglandin sensitivity, hyaluronic-acid-to-collagen ratio shift, PUFA accumulation in the post-natal period) are documented in fragments but have not been integrated into a single model that would predict how much fetal-like regenerative capacity could be recovered by reversing each.
