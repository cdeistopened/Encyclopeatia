# Science as Ideology

The closing chapter of *Mind and Tissue*, Peat's 1975 book on Soviet brain physiology and the politics of Western psychology, is the densest single statement of his meta-position on the relationship between science and political economy. Chapter 20, "End of Science?", is the place where the political-economy axis that runs underneath the nutritional, endocrine, and developmental work he would do over the next four decades is set out explicitly. The other books, newsletters, and interviews treat its argument as background. The chapter itself is foreground, and reading it makes the rest of the corpus legible as a single project rather than a set of disparate critiques.

The argument has three moving parts. The descriptive claim: "the scientists of empire are announcing the end of science and art, and of all cultural progress" — a pattern that runs from the late nineteenth-century physicists who declared physics complete except for a few details through the late-twentieth-century molecular biologists who declared biology nearly finished pending "a few puzzles in genetics — and the general form of these solutions is already known." The philosophical claim: the recurring "end of science" announcement is the characteristic move of *idealism* — the metaphysical posture that treats established ideas as the primary reality and material change as a secondary phenomenon — and materialism, the opposite posture, is constitutionally incapable of producing such an announcement, because for the materialist new matter and new conditions continuously generate new theory. The political claim: idealism's stasis serves whichever class currently holds power, and materialism's change serves whichever class is trying to bring a new order into being. Mainstream biology in Peat's lifetime, on this account, took the form of idealism not by intellectual accident but because the funding structure of postwar Anglo-American science selected for it.

This page treats the philosophical and epistemological axis. The institutional plumbing — funding agencies, journals, regulatory bodies, the AHA-and-Procter-and-Gamble case histories — is covered separately in [[regulatory-capture]]. The two argue jointly: one diagnoses the structural mechanism, the other diagnoses the philosophical posture the structural mechanism produces.

## The "end of science" announcements

The catalog is striking once it is laid out. In the 1890s, on the eve of relativity and quantum mechanics, several leading physicists declared that the principal laws of physics had been discovered and that the remaining work was the determination of constants to additional decimal places. The next two decades demolished the claim more thoroughly than any prior overturning of a paradigm in the history of the field. By the 1920s the *foundations* of physics, not merely its boundary conditions, had been rewritten.

In the late 1960s and early 1970s a parallel set of announcements began to appear from molecular biology. *The Coming of the Golden Age* (1969), the book by the Berkeley molecular biologist Gunther Stent that Peat treats as exemplary in Chapter 20, argued that biology was approaching its terminal state: the central dogma had been worked out, the genetic code had been deciphered, the wiring diagram of the nervous system was a finite engineering problem whose general solution was known, and consciousness — by epistemological constraint, in Stent's argument — would never be available to scientific explanation at all. The conclusion was that the remaining work was incremental and that the social organization of the species should shift toward managing the surplus population that scientific labor would no longer require. Stent's specific suggestion, which Peat quotes at length, was that the genetic distribution of the "will to power" would sort future humanity into Huxley's Alpha-Beta-Gamma classes from *Brave New World* — a minority maintaining the technology, a middle class of unemployed beatniks, an underclass of unemployable hippies.

The pattern Peat names is not that any individual "end of science" announcement turned out to be wrong, though every one of them did. The pattern is that the announcements come in clusters, coincide with periods of consolidation in the political-economic order, and always license a corresponding social-political program: in the 1890s, the imperial confidence of European industrial capital; in the late 1960s, the postwar American consensus on technocratic management of a fixed-pie economy. Each announcement was a way of saying that the existing distribution of power was the terminal distribution. Each was wrong, and the wrongness was not random — it ran in a consistent direction, the direction that better served the funders of the science announcing the ending.

## Idealism's stasis vs. materialism's change

The deeper claim Chapter 20 makes is that the "end of science" gesture is not a contingent error but the characteristic output of a particular philosophical orientation. "The term 'materialism' describes the attitude that likes to begin with 'the matter at hand,'" Peat writes; "'idealism' describes an approach that emphasizes the importance of established ideas." From this distinction the rest follows. The idealist starts from the existing conceptual apparatus and asks how new phenomena can be fit into it; the materialist starts from new matter, new conditions, new tools, and asks how the conceptual apparatus must change to accommodate them. The idealist treats theory as primary and observation as confirmatory; the materialist treats observation as primary and theory as scaffolding. The idealist announces that science is ending because the conceptual apparatus is, on the idealist's own terms, nearly complete. The materialist could never announce that science is ending, because the supply of new matter is by hypothesis inexhaustible.

The lineage Peat traces for the idealist tendency in twentieth-century Anglo-American science runs through German philosophy. "German idealism has been influential in western science for most of the 20th century," he writes, "but now scientists in capitalist countries are letting it guide them into cultural fascism." The specific charge is that the descendants of Hegel arrived at the position that the conceptual apparatus of the dominant science was complete, that the political arrangements it underwrote were terminal, and that the role of new research was decorative rather than transformative. Stent's borrowing from Chomsky and his citation of Nazi-era genetics papers at his Berkeley lectures are placed in the same lineage: idealist science licensing political-genetic argumentation about which populations are permitted to reproduce.

The materialist counter-tradition Peat draws from is the Russian one — Pavlov, Anokhin, Ukhtomskii, Bernstein, the Soviet physiology that the postwar American synthesis treated as backward. Whatever the limitations of that tradition, its philosophical posture was the opposite of the postwar American one: change was the default, holism was the default, the assumption that the species was at the beginning rather than the end of its scientific work was the default. The Soviet theoretical physicist Stanyukovich's argument against the heat-death of the universe is cited at the close of Chapter 20 as the characteristic gesture of the other posture: "Man is still very young. His recorded history spans less than ten thousand years from the most ancient civilizations."

## Heraclitus, Marx, and Peat

Three figures anchor the philosophical genealogy Peat is working in. The first is Heraclitus: "Ever since Heraclitus, materialists have emphasized change, while idealists emphasize stasis." The pre-Socratic intuition that everything flows, that the river is never the same river twice, is the original statement of the materialist posture. It precedes the Platonic counter-move — the insistence on eternal Forms as the truly real, with material flux as a derivative shadow — by two generations, and the Western philosophical tradition has oscillated between these positions ever since. Peat's claim is that the choice is not a matter of taste but a matter of political consequence: the Heraclitean posture orients the scientist toward emergent phenomena and toward humility about what has been concluded; the Platonic posture orients the scientist toward classification, closure, and the defense of the existing taxonomy against revision.

The second figure is Marx. Peat quotes the line that "real history couldn't begin until capitalism has been overcome" and notes immediately that the line is "more tautology than perception" — that the materialist will, by the structure of the position, always see the present as preliminary, and the idealist will always see the present as terminal. The deeper Marxian point is the political-economic critique: that the dominant ideas of any era are the ideas of the dominant class, that scientific consensus is downstream of the institutional structures that produce and disseminate it, and that the appearance of disinterested objectivity is itself an ideological achievement. This is the point at which the Chapter 20 argument hands off to [[regulatory-capture]].

The third figure is Peat himself, whose contribution is to extend the materialist posture from political theory into cell physiology. The bioenergetic framework — the insistence that cellular metabolism is the load-bearing variable, that temperature and pulse and CO₂ are reliable indicators of organismic state, that the mitochondrion is more interesting than the genome — is the materialist science of biology. It refuses the "end of biology" gesture not on ideological grounds but because the underlying physiology is manifestly unfinished, and the materialist's response to that fact is to look for a different conceptual apparatus rather than to declare the work complete.

## How captured science announces its endings

The mechanism by which a captured science arrives at an "end of science" announcement is, on the joint Chapter-20-plus-[[regulatory-capture]] account, neither conspiracy nor coincidence. It is an equilibrium effect of three pressures running in the same direction. Funding selection: research programs that promise to close out a field with the existing tools attract concentrated capital, because they are the programs that promise the dominant industries continued protection from inconvenient discoveries. A genetics that announces it is nearly finished is a genetics that will not, in its remaining lifespan, discover that polyunsaturated fats are mitochondrial poisons or that aluminum-smelter waste is a developmental neurotoxin. Editorial selection: journals that publish "end of science" claims attract advertisers and prestige, while the editors who publish materialist counter-evidence are not promoted into the senior editorial positions that determine which manuscripts are published in the next generation. Curricular selection: textbooks adopt the synthesis of the captured prestige journals, medical schools train physicians on the textbooks, and the physicians twenty years later are the senior clinicians whose intuitions about what is reasonable are calibrated to the captured synthesis.

The "end of science" announcement is the rhetorical capstone of this equilibrium. It does not cause the equilibrium; it is what an equilibrium of this kind eventually says about itself.

## Practical protocol — epistemological hygiene

The corrective practice the chapter implies, and that Peat applied across forty years, is a set of compensating habits for working in a captured information environment.

**Read primary sources, not abstracts.** The abstract is where the author tells the reader what conclusion to draw; the methods and raw data are where the reader can decide what conclusion the experiment actually supports. The two diverge often enough that the abstract is no longer a reliable summary, particularly in the captured prestige journals where framing is selected for compatibility with the dominant synthesis.

**Track the funding source as one piece of the evidence.** A finding from an industry-funded study can be correct, and a finding from an independent study can be wrong, but the funding source raises the prior for independent replication. A research program funded entirely by an industry that profits from the conclusion is evidence that the result needs cross-checking, not evidence that it is false.

**Weight older, Russian, and obscure literature more heavily than recent prestige-journal Anglophone literature.** The pre-1950s American work on PUFA toxicity, the Soviet work on radiation biology and CO₂, the German cellular-respiration tradition through Warburg, and the East European endocrinology Peat cited throughout his career sat outside the funding structure that captured the postwar American synthesis. The work is not necessarily better; it is less likely to have been shaped by the specific industrial interests that shaped the synthesis. Triangulating across funding ecosystems is a way of partially de-capturing the evidence base.

**Apply the Peat skepticism heuristics.** When an institutional consensus is loud and the underlying evidence is thin, the prior should be neutral rather than deferential. When a finding contradicts the framework the dominant industries prefer, expect it to be ignored rather than refuted. When the framework that explains a finding is older and outside the prestige literature, that is not on its own a reason to discount it.

**Watch for "end of" rhetoric as a diagnostic signal.** Any announcement that a field is essentially complete, that the remaining work is the determination of constants, that consciousness or biological complexity is beyond scientific reach, or that the existing taxonomy is exhaustive should be treated as evidence about the institutional state of the field rather than as evidence about the underlying reality. The rhetoric is the characteristic output of idealism doing the work of empire.

## Pairs with regulatory-capture

The relationship between this page and [[regulatory-capture]] is the relationship between a philosophical critique and a structural-institutional one. [[regulatory-capture]] is the *institutional mechanism*: which agencies funded which research, which journals published which manuscripts, which professional societies were captured by which industrial interests, and which canonical cases (PUFA, fluoride, ECT, soy formula, synthetic estrogen) the mechanism produced. *Science as Ideology* is the *epistemological frame*: what philosophical posture a captured science takes, why "end of science" announcements recur in captured fields, and what materialist posture is required to resist the capture.

Either account on its own is incomplete. A purely institutional critique reduces to "follow the money" and misses the philosophical work the captured science is doing. A purely epistemological critique reduces to "be more skeptical" and misses the structural reasons skepticism alone is insufficient. The two together produce a meta-frame for the entire encyclopedia: each substantive entry (on PUFA, on aspirin, on thyroid, on estrogen, on aluminum, on fluoride) is at once an institutional case and a philosophical case, and the materialist physiology Peat developed in response is the constructive output of the combined critique.

## Counter-arguments

**The scientific-realist position.** The strongest defense of the postwar synthesis is that the methods are self-correcting: errors get caught, revisions get made, and the long-run trajectory of the literature converges on truth even if individual periods are biased. The replication crisis of the 2010s, the partial reversal of the saturated-fat consensus, and the continuing reevaluation of hormone replacement therapy can be cited as evidence that the apparatus is working. The Peat-side response is that these corrections came forty to sixty years after the relevant findings were available, that the human cost of the lag is enormous, and that the corrections are still partial. Both sides agree that the apparatus eventually corrects; they disagree on the timescale and on whether the lag is acceptable.

**The "consensus IS correct" position.** A more pragmatic defense is that, in expectation, the institutional consensus is better than any individual dissident — that a clinician deferring to the consensus will, across cases, make fewer errors than one deferring to Peat. This is probably true across the full distribution of clinical questions. The Peat-side response is that the distribution of errors is not uniform: on the questions where capture is strongest (PUFA, hormone replacement, ECT, fluoride), the consensus is systematically wrong in a specific direction, and the dissident who can identify which questions those are will do better than the consensus precisely on those questions. The framework is most useful as a question-selection heuristic.

**The post-modernism overcorrection.** A symmetric critique is that Peat-style skepticism, taken to its logical conclusion, dissolves into a generalized anti-science posture that delegitimizes all institutional knowledge claims. The risk is real and shows up in adjacent popular literatures. Peat himself was careful about this: his complaint was not that science was illegitimate but that the *captured* portion of postwar Anglo-American medical science was, and his framework was an explicit attempt to recover the institutional achievements (Soviet physiology, German cellular respiration, pre-1950s American endocrinology) that the captured synthesis had buried. The framework only works when the critique of capture is paired with substantive engagement with primary data and with the older un-captured literature; without that pairing it does collapse into anti-science, and the collapse is the failure mode the careful reader has to guard against.

## Data gaps

The historical evidence for the "end of science" pattern is solid for the 1890s physics case and the 1969 Stent case, but the cross-disciplinary catalog has not been compiled in a single accessible source. A systematic survey — which fields, in which decades, produced which "we are nearly done" claims, and which fields produced no such claims — would let the reader assess how strong the pattern is. The chapter implies such a survey rather than producing one.

The relationship between idealist philosophy and captured funding is asserted in Chapter 20 but is not quantitatively demonstrated. A formal analysis — funding source by funding source, journal by journal, decade by decade — does not exist in a single place. The case-by-case histories exist (PUFA, fluoride, estrogen); the aggregated picture is missing.

The contemporary application of the frame is harder than its application to the postwar decades. The replication crisis, the rise of preprint servers, and the partial discrediting of the captured prestige journals have shifted the institutional landscape since the 2010s. The frame is not obsolete — the financial entanglements remain — but its application to current literature requires more case-by-case work than its application to 1940-1990 does.
