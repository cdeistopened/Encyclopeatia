# Submolecular Biology

Submolecular biology is the name [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Albert Szent-Györgyi]] gave, in the title of his 1960 monograph *Introduction to a Submolecular Biology* (Academic Press), to a research program that the molecular biology consensus of the same decade was actively burying. Where Watson and Crick had announced in 1953 the DNA double helix and a generation of American biology was setting itself the task of reading the gene as the elemental unit of life, Szent-Györgyi proposed — to a discipline that was no longer listening — that the elemental unit was something smaller. Not the gene, not the protein, not even the molecule, but the electron. A cell is not a sack of macromolecules running on a genetic program; it is an electronic device built out of a structured protein-water-ion lattice through which electrons flow, and the *behavior of those electrons* is what the cell's life consists of.

The name is a deliberate provocation. "Molecular biology" had become the prestige term of post-war American biology — Cold-Spring-Harbor genetics, the Watson-Crick framework, the Central Dogma of DNA → RNA → protein. By 1960 the term had also become a programmatic claim: that the right level to do biology was the level of macromolecules, that the rules at that level (sequence, folding, binding) explained the rules above it (cells, tissues, organisms), and that going below the molecule into physics was a category mistake. *Sub*molecular biology says the category mistake runs the other way. The molecular level is not the floor of biology. It is one floor in a building whose foundation is electronic, and a biology that refuses to look below the molecule misses what the molecules are doing there in the first place. Peat read the book early, cited it for the rest of his life, and built his clinical framework on the program it announced. This page is about submolecular biology *as a framework* — its claims, its empirical basis, its quarrel with mainstream molecular biology, and how Peat operationalized it. For Szent-Györgyi the man and the broader catalogue of his contributions, see [[albert-szent-gyorgyi]]. For the umbrella under which Peat used these ideas clinically, see [[bioenergetics]].

## The 1960 Book and Its Claims

*Introduction to a Submolecular Biology* was a thin book — the printed text runs about 150 pages — but its scope was deliberately enormous. It opened with the observation that the great strides of twentieth-century biology had come from the chemist's analytical method: take the cell apart, isolate the molecules, identify their structures, work out their reactions. By 1960 most of biochemistry's central facts had been established by this method, and the remaining problems were of a different sort. How does a muscle contract? How does a nerve conduct? How does a cell decide to divide? How does an embryo organize itself? These were no longer questions about which molecules were present. The molecules had been catalogued. The questions were about how the molecules interacted, in real time, in a living matrix, to produce coordinated function. And to answer those questions, Szent-Györgyi argued, biology had to go below the molecule, to the level at which molecules actually communicate with each other — the level of electronic interactions.

The book's central claims, distilled, are three. First, **proteins conduct electrons.** Szent-Györgyi had proposed this in a 1941 *Science* paper based on the regular spacing of conjugated double bonds in protein backbones, the inductive effects of bound substrates, and the demonstrable speed of nervous conduction. By 1960 he treated it as the foundational empirical fact on which submolecular biology rested. The cytoplasmic proteins of a living cell, he argued, function as solid-state semiconductors — electronic conductors whose conductivity is governed not by the migration of ions through aqueous channels but by the delocalization of electrons across a structured molecular lattice. This was reasoned from first principles a decade before solid-state physics had built the transistor industry on the same kind of analysis.

Second, **the cell's water and small molecules dope the protein semiconductor.** In ordinary inorganic semiconductors, the conductivity is controlled by tiny concentrations of "dopants" — atoms with one more or one less valence electron than the host lattice — that donate or accept electrons and shift the conducting properties. Szent-Györgyi proposed that the cell's small molecules — sugars, hormones, metabolites, the dicarboxylic acids of the citric acid cycle, the redox couples like NAD+/NADH and ascorbate/dehydroascorbate — work the same way on the protein lattice. They are not "substrates" that get consumed by enzymes in isolation; they are dopants that shift the electronic state of the surrounding protein-water medium. A bound hormone is a dopant. A bound ATP is a dopant. A bound CO2 is a dopant. The macroscopic function the cell performs — contracting, secreting, dividing, signaling — is the electronic state the doping has produced.

Third, **the same logic governs disease.** Cancer, in Szent-Györgyi's reading, is not a genetic error in a particular protein. It is an electronic state in which the donor-acceptor balance has shifted toward the reduced, division-permissive end of the spectrum and the cell cannot return to the oxidized, differentiation-stable state. Aging is the long slide in the same direction. Stress, fatigue, the rigor-mortis state of an ATP-depleted muscle, the swollen mitochondria of a hypoxic tissue — all are electronic states that the protein-water lattice has fallen into. A submolecular medicine, by this account, would intervene not by killing specific cells with cytotoxic drugs (the cancer-as-parasite picture) but by adjusting the dopant balance of the lattice. Methylglyoxal at homeostatic concentration. CO2. Thyroid hormone. Vitamin C in its dehydroascorbate form. Light at specific wavelengths. These act, in the Szent-Györgyi framework, the way a doping current acts in a semiconductor — by shifting the electronic state of the whole system.

The follow-up books — *Bioenergetics* (1957, which the 1960 monograph extends), *The Living State, with Observations on Cancer* (1972), and *Electronic Biology and Cancer* (1976) — develop the program into specific predictions about [[cancer]], [[muscle-contraction]], and the electron donor-acceptor (D-A) pair framework. The clinical content of [[albert-szent-gyorgyi]]'s late career is the working-out of the framework the 1960 book announced.

## The Empirical Basis

Submolecular biology was not pure theory. Szent-Györgyi grounded the framework in a series of experimental demonstrations that, in his reading, made the standard membrane-and-pump cell biology of the 1950s untenable.

**Semiconduction in proteins.** Szent-Györgyi and his collaborators measured the electrical conductivity of dried protein preparations under various conditions and found them to behave as wide-band-gap semiconductors. The numbers were tiny — orders of magnitude below copper, and below the silicon and germanium of the emerging solid-state industry — but they were measurable, reproducible, and they responded to dopants in the way semiconductor theory predicted. Subsequent work in the 1960s and 1970s by other laboratories confirmed that proteins, especially hydrated proteins, do conduct electrons, that the conductivity depends on water content (more water, more conductivity, up to a point), and that the mechanism is the delocalization of electrons across the conjugated backbone rather than the migration of ions. This is not contested by modern solid-state biophysics. What remains contested is whether the conductivity is high enough, and the distances long enough, for it to be biologically load-bearing at the scales Szent-Györgyi proposed.

**Electron donor-acceptor pairs in muscle contraction.** The 1949 *Biological Bulletin* paper "Free-energy relations and contraction of actomyosin" demonstrated that muscle could contract without splitting ATP — that the contractile event was not the hydrolysis of a "high-energy bond" but the binding of ATP to the protein lattice. Szent-Györgyi extended the work in the 1950s by showing that certain combinations of electron donors and electron acceptors, when added together to a muscle preparation, would cause contraction without ATP — and that the same molecules alone, or in pairs that were not electronically tuned to each other, failed to cause contraction. "In one of his demonstrations, Szent-Gyorgyi showed that muscles could be caused to contract when they were exposed to two substances which, when combined, partially exchange an electron, causing an intense color reaction, but without breaking any of the molecules' bonds," Peat wrote in [[cascara-energy-cancer-and-the-fda-s-laxative-abuse|*Cascara, Energy, Cancer, and the FDA's Laxative Abuse*]]. "[T]he same molecules, combined in pairs that weren't exactly tuned to each other, failed to cause contraction. Szent-Gyorgyi believed that biological signal substances operated in a similar way, by adjusting the electronic balance of cellular proteins." A muscle contraction is, in this framework, a state transition of the protein lattice driven by the electronic perturbation of a tuned D-A pair. The molecules involved are not "consumed" in the textbook stoichiometric sense; they donate or accept electrons, and the macroscopic motion follows.

**The methylene blue cycle.** [[methylene-blue]] was, in Szent-Györgyi's framework, an exemplary submolecular intervention. The dye is electron-active: it cycles between oxidized (blue) and reduced (colorless) forms, accepting electrons from biological reductants and donating them back to oxygen. In a cell with a slowed respiratory chain — a Warburg-state cell, a cancer cell, a hypoxic cell — methylene blue can substitute for the missing electron acceptors and re-establish electron flow toward oxygen. It is not a "drug" in the receptor-and-ligand sense. It is a dopant that shifts the cell's redox state back toward the oxidized, differentiation-stable end of the spectrum. The same logic applies to [[vitamin-c]]: dehydroascorbate (the oxidized form) predominates in healthy cells running full oxidative metabolism; ascorbate (the reduced form) accumulates in cancer cells and stressed cells. The ratio is itself a submolecular state variable, reporting the redox condition of the cytoplasm. Peat developed this account at length in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]] (July 2016).

**The fluorescence-in-muscle experiment of 1947.** Working at Woods Hole after his emigration from Hungary, Szent-Györgyi demonstrated that fluorescent dyes dissolved in muscle cytoplasm behaved as if they were in ice — their rotational mobility was sharply constrained by the surrounding water structure — until the muscle was stimulated, at which point the fluorescence shifted toward what one would see in ordinary liquid water. The water inside a resting cell is structured. The structure melts during a normal physiological event. This single demonstration, Peat argued in [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]], "should have caused biologists to abandon the membrane theory of cellular excitation, and to return to basic physics for their understanding of cell behavior." It became, instead, the foundation for [[gilbert-ling]]'s Association-Induction Hypothesis — a full extension of submolecular biology into a thermodynamically consistent picture of the whole cytoplasm. See [[structured-water]] and [[gilbert-ling]] for the development.

## Where It Diverges from Mainstream Molecular Biology

Submolecular biology and molecular biology are not rival theories about the same thing. They are theories about different levels of organization, and the disagreement is about which level is foundational. The mainstream sequence runs upward: atoms → small molecules → macromolecules → organelles → cells → tissues → organisms. Each level is built out of the level below it, and explanations move upward — the structure of hemoglobin explains its oxygen-carrying behavior, the sequence of DNA explains the structure of the protein, the regulation of the gene explains the behavior of the cell. Cause flows from the molecular level up.

The submolecular sequence inverts the foundation. Below the molecule, Szent-Györgyi argued, is the electron — and the electronic state of the protein-water lattice is what determines how the macromolecules above it behave. A protein in one electronic state is, functionally, a different protein in a different electronic state, even though no covalent bond has changed and no sequence has been altered. The hormone receptor that binds the steroid the way a lock binds a key is, in the submolecular picture, a region of the lattice whose electronic state has shifted because of the hormone's dopant effect — and the "binding" is the persistence of that state under the appropriate dopant concentration. The Central Dogma of DNA → RNA → protein is, in this framework, a partial description of one direction of information flow. The reverse direction — the cell's electronic state regulating which genes are read and which proteins are expressed — is just as real, and just as causally important.

This is not a critique that mainstream biology has had no answer to. Modern epigenetics, signal transduction, and systems biology have all incorporated some version of the bottom-up-to-top-down feedback. What submolecular biology retains as a distinctive claim is that the relevant feedback level is *not* the molecular level (proteins regulating gene expression) but the *sub*molecular level (electronic states of the protein-water medium regulating both). The Watson-Crick gene-centric biology, in the submolecular reading, made an error of scope: it took a real and powerful description of inheritance (the sequence is conserved across generations) and mistook it for a description of cell function (the sequence determines what the cell does in real time). The cell does what the cell's electronic state lets it do, and the genome is one of the dopants shaping that state, not the controller above it.

The reductive atomism Peat criticized repeatedly is the next layer down of the same error. Take a cell apart, identify the molecules, study the molecules in isolation in a test tube, and assume that the sum of the isolated reactions reconstitutes the cell. Szent-Györgyi's objection — and Peat's after him — was that the cell's molecules behave differently *in vivo* than they do in dilution, because in the cell they are part of an electronically integrated structure, and the integration is what they are doing. "This violates the basic assumption of conventional (and stupid) 'test-tube biochemistry,' namely, that reactions in the test-tube meaningfully reflect what occurs in the cell," Peat wrote in [[szent-gyorgyis-energy|*Szent-Györgyi's Energy*]] (Townsend Letter, 1989). The cell, in the submolecular reading, is not a sum of its molecules. It is a phase — a structured electronic phase — and its molecules are the constituents of that phase, behaving in ways the dilute test tube cannot reproduce.

## Peat's Clinical Extension

If submolecular biology is the framework, Peat's bioenergetic medicine is the framework applied to a body that hurts. The translation is direct, but it is also the hardest move in the literature. Szent-Györgyi spent his American decades developing the physics. He named the categories (retine, promine, the methylglyoxal cancer hypothesis) but did not work out the dosing, the patient population, the route of administration, the interaction with diet, the way to monitor effect at the level of the whole organism. That working-out is what Peat did, and the body of clinical recommendation in his fifty-year corpus is what submolecular biology looks like when carried into the consulting room.

The translation runs through three commitments.

**The dopants are accessible at the level of food, light, temperature, and breath.** Submolecular biology is not, in Peat's clinical reading, a research program that requires synthesizing exotic compounds in a graduate-school laboratory. The dopants that shift the cell's electronic state are mostly things a person can adjust without a prescription — the sugar in fruit and juice (substrate for oxidative metabolism, see [[oxidative-metabolism]]), the [[thyroid|thyroid hormone]] that activates the respiratory chain, the [[carbon-dioxide]] produced by full oxidation and accumulated by [[bag-breathing]], the saturated fats that don't poison the mitochondrion the way [[pufa]] does, the light at red and infrared wavelengths that stimulates cytochrome c oxidase. The submolecular framework predicts which interventions matter and which don't, and what predicts it is whether the intervention is doping the protein-water lattice in the right direction.

**Mitochondria are the central submolecular organelle.** The respiratory chain is, in this framework, the canonical example of a structured-protein electron-flow device, and its impairment is the canonical submolecular pathology. [[otto-warburg|Warburg's]] respiratory defect — cancer cells producing lactate in the presence of oxygen — is what happens when the electron flow through the [[mitochondria|mitochondrial]] lattice fails. The cell falls back on glycolysis because the downstream acceptors are gone, the electronic state shifts toward the reduced and divisive end, and the cell loses its differentiation. This is one of the cleanest cases where submolecular biology and clinical observation meet: the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]] is the visible chemistry; the submolecular fact under it is the failure of electron transport, with all of the structured-protein, structured-water, semiconductor-doping framework as the explanation of *why* the chain has failed.

**The anti-stress diet is submolecular medicine.** The combination of practices Peat recommended across his career — adequate sugar, adequate calcium and salt, gelatin, milk, limited PUFA, supplemental thyroid for the hypothyroid, [[aspirin]] for the inflamed, [[methylene-blue]] for the failing respiratory chain, sunlight or red light for the under-illuminated, [[bag-breathing]] for the chronically hyperventilated — is, in the submolecular reading, a coherent program of dopant management. Each intervention shifts the electronic state of the protein-water lattice in the direction Szent-Györgyi's framework identified as health: oxidized, structured, full oxidative metabolism, dehydroascorbate dominant over ascorbate, CO2 dominant over lactate, [[progesterone]] dominant over [[estrogen]], saturation dominant over unsaturation. The [[anti-stress-diet]] is what submolecular biology prescribes when it is carried through to the kitchen.

The methylglyoxal case shows the translation in miniature. Szent-Györgyi proposed in his late work that methylglyoxal — a dicarbonyl side-product of glycolysis — was the candidate molecule for *retine*, the electron-acceptor that holds the cell in its non-dividing, oxidized state. The dose was unknown, the toxicity was real (methylglyoxal at high concentration is the glycation agent in diabetic damage), and the clinical application was speculative. Peat carried the hypothesis forward by attending to *homeostatic* methylglyoxal — the concentration the cell produces naturally when oxidative metabolism is running fully — and by treating elevated methylglyoxal damage as a secondary phenomenon of failing thyroid and PUFA-poisoned mitochondria, not the molecule itself. The submolecular reading and the clinical reading meet here. The fix is not to dose methylglyoxal; the fix is to fix the metabolism that lets the cell produce its own methylglyoxal at the right concentration. The page on [[methylglyoxal]] develops this in detail.

## Modern Parallels

Submolecular biology was a 1960 framework that did not name a discipline that existed by 1990. But several twenty-first-century programs cover overlapping territory, and the comparison helps locate the submolecular claim on the modern map.

**Systems biology** is the closest descendant in spirit. The recognition that cellular function is irreducible to any single molecule, that emergent network behavior governs phenotype, and that the cell must be modeled as an integrated system rather than dissected into parts — all of this is submolecular biology in modern dress. What systems biology has not absorbed is Szent-Györgyi's specific claim about *which* level the integration happens at. Systems biology integrates at the level of molecular networks (genes, transcripts, proteins, metabolites). Submolecular biology argued the integration happens one level deeper, at the electronic-state level of the protein-water lattice. The systems biology view is compatible with mainstream molecular reductionism in a way the submolecular view is not.

**Network biology** and the related body of work on metabolic networks, gene regulatory networks, and signaling networks supplies the mathematical formalism that submolecular biology lacked. The graph-theoretic methods, the differential-equation models of metabolic flux, the constraint-based analyses of stoichiometric networks — these give the systems-biology program the quantitative bite that submolecular biology was never able to develop. What network biology gives up, in the trade, is the deeper-than-molecule level of analysis. The networks are networks of molecules.

**Quantum biology** is the closest descendant in technical content. The recognition that quantum-coherent electron transfer governs photosynthesis (the photosystem II reaction center), that quantum tunneling participates in enzyme catalysis, that magnetoreception in birds may exploit radical-pair quantum mechanics — these are the kinds of phenomena Szent-Györgyi was pointing toward. Mae-Wan Ho's program on liquid-crystalline coherence in living tissue, which Peat cited repeatedly in his late interviews (he told the [[2015-07-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-on-the-back-of-a-tiger|July 2015 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] that "Mae-Wan Ho is, to a great degree, an extension of some of Szent-Györgyi's ideas"), is one bridge from submolecular biology into the quantum-biology mainstream. Luca Turin's vibrational theory of olfaction is another. These programs are still marginal within mainstream molecular biology, but they are no longer treated as the obvious crank work that submolecular biology was relegated to between 1960 and 2000.

**Structured-water research and water-as-a-fourth-phase claims.** The descendants of the 1947 fluorescence experiment have had a checkered career. The careful version — that water near protein surfaces has different rotational mobility, different dielectric constant, and different hydrogen-bonding lifetime than bulk water — is uncontroversial in modern biophysics. The strong version — Gerald Pollack's "fourth phase of water," the claim of macroscopic exclusion zones with distinct optical and electrical properties — is contested. The contest has, in some readings, damaged the credibility of the careful version by association. See [[structured-water]] for the longer treatment.

The honest summary is that submolecular biology has not been refuted, has not been confirmed at the framework level, and exists in 2026 as a partially-vindicated, partially-orphaned research program whose specific claims about semiconduction, dopant-controlled electronic states, and the protein-water lattice are mostly outside the textbook consensus, while overlapping pieces of the program have been taken up by various twenty-first-century specialties without the unifying framework that Szent-Györgyi tried to give it.

## Counter-arguments

- **The lack of a mathematical formalism.** Submolecular biology, as Szent-Györgyi developed it, was qualitative and analogical. The analogy to doped semiconductors is suggestive but was never developed into the kind of quantitative theory — band-structure calculations, conductivity tensors, predicted current-voltage curves — that would have let the framework compete on mainstream physics ground. Molecular biology, by contrast, produced quantitative tools: sequence alignment scores, binding constants, kinetic rate equations, statistical-mechanical models of folding. The framework that has the math wins more of the funding and more of the textbook real estate. Whether the qualitative submolecular claims could be operationalized into the quantitative form they would need to attract a generation of physicists remains an open question, and the absence of such a formalism is the cleanest mainstream objection.

- **The semiconduction values are too low to be biologically load-bearing at long distances.** The measured electrical conductivities of dehydrated proteins are real but small. In living tissue, with its high water content and ionic strength, the conductivities drop further (or the picture changes substantially, depending on the laboratory). Mainstream mitochondrial biology accepts short-range electron tunneling through the respiratory complex proteins — tens of ångströms. The broader claim that the entire cytoplasm functions as a semiconductor doped by small molecules, with electronic states propagating over micrometer scales through structured protein lattices, is not supported by the quantitative measurements as cleanly as Szent-Györgyi's framework would require. The framework remains plausible at short range and speculative at the cellular scale.

- **The molecular-biology mainstream produced cures.** Molecular biology in the post-1960 period did not just produce textbooks; it produced antibiotic resistance mechanisms, recombinant insulin, monoclonal antibodies, gene therapy, mRNA vaccines, CRISPR. The submolecular framework has not produced a clinical pipeline of comparable scale. Defenders of the framework argue this is a function of funding and consensus rather than truth, but the asymmetry of practical output is real, and an honest accounting cannot ignore it.

- **The retine-promine framework has not been confirmed.** Szent-Györgyi's specific cancer hypothesis — that a deficiency of growth-inhibitor electron-acceptors (retine, candidate molecule methylglyoxal) relative to growth-stimulator electron-donors (promine) is the submolecular signature of cancer — has not produced a clinical therapy in the sixty years since he proposed it. Modern oncology has confirmed pieces (the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]] is real, metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells is real, methylglyoxal has some anti-cancer effects in some preparations) but has not adopted the retine-promine framework as a working clinical model. The gap between Szent-Györgyi's framework and the molecular-genetic mainstream of oncology is wider now than it was at his death.

- **Submolecular biology was not refuted, but it was also not adopted.** A research program that does not refute and does not get adopted is in an uncomfortable middle state — neither vindicated by experimental closure nor falsified by experimental disproof. Mainstream biology has simply moved on to other questions. Whether this reflects the framework's premature timing (the instrumentation of the 1960s could not measure what the framework predicted) or its inadequacy (the predictions are not testable in the form the framework gives them) is itself disputed.

- **The Watson-Crick gene-centric biology has explanatory virtues the submolecular framework lacks.** The Central Dogma supplies a clean mechanism of inheritance, a basis for evolutionary theory at the molecular level, a framework for diagnosing and treating genetic disease, and a research program with a clear forward path. Submolecular biology offers a deeper-physics frame but does not, in its 1960 form, supply equivalent traction on any of those problems. A complete biology probably needs both — the molecular reductionists and the submolecular holists — but the asymmetry of explanatory power on the central problems of twentieth-century biology weighed against the submolecular program in the funding competitions of the post-war era, and continues to weigh against it.

- **The framework's clinical translation rests on a chain of inferences.** Peat's bioenergetic recommendations are derived from the submolecular framework rather than from direct measurement of cellular electronic states in patients. The framework predicts that thyroid hormone, sugar, CO2, and saturated fats will shift the cytoplasmic electronic state toward health. The predictions are plausible and the recommendations have a long anecdotal track record, but the chain "framework → predicted dopant → predicted electronic shift → predicted clinical improvement" has links that have not been measured in patients. The clinical case for [[anti-stress-diet]] and the rest of the Peat protocol is, in the strict-evidence sense, not the same as a randomized trial showing benefit. The framework's defenders argue that the right level of evidence is the long observational record across a fifty-year practice; its critics argue that the right level is the trial.

## Data gaps

- **The cellular electronic state has no routine clinical measurement.** Submolecular biology predicts that cellular function tracks cellular electronic state, but the framework gives no operational way to measure that state in a living patient. Modern proxies — NAD+/NADH ratio, redox couple measurements, mitochondrial membrane potential — touch pieces of the underlying claim but do not assemble into the integrated submolecular phase variable the framework requires. Until such a measurement exists, the framework's clinical predictions cannot be directly tested.

- **The hydrated-protein semiconductor parameters have not been worked out in vivo.** Most semiconduction-in-proteins measurements were done on dried, purified preparations. The behavior in the living cell — at full hydration, full ionic strength, body temperature, with the natural complement of dopants — has not been systematically characterized. Whether the framework's claims about long-range electron transport hold under physiological conditions is, in 2026, still unknown at the quantitative level the framework would need.

- **The methylglyoxal-as-retine hypothesis has no dose-finding clinical work.** Szent-Györgyi proposed the molecule; later work confirmed it has some anti-cancer activity in some contexts; but the dose-response, the route of administration, the patient selection, the duration of treatment, and the safety profile have never been worked out at clinical scale. Peat's reading — that endogenous methylglyoxal at homeostatic concentration is the relevant variable, and that the intervention is metabolic restoration rather than methylglyoxal dosing — is internally consistent with the framework but has not been tested as a treatment program.

- **The submolecular framework's claims about consciousness are largely undeveloped.** Szent-Györgyi gestured at consciousness as an emergent property of the electron-flow scanning through the structured cytoplasm; Peat picked up the thread in his 1976 *Mind and Tissue* and his 1975 *Biophysical Approach to Altered Consciousness*. The detailed working-out — what specific electronic states correspond to specific conscious states, how anesthetics and psychoactive drugs intervene at the submolecular level, how the framework relates to the molecular neuroscience of receptors and neurotransmitters — has not been developed in a form that could be tested.

- **No mainstream textbook treats submolecular biology as a named discipline.** The term itself is mostly absent from twenty-first-century biology curricula. Whether this is a problem of nomenclature (the same ideas under different names) or a real omission (the framework was buried and stays buried) is a question of historical reception rather than science, but it shapes what a new generation of researchers can build on.

- **The framework's intersection with epigenetics and the regulatory genome has not been developed.** The submolecular framing should, in principle, supply a deeper level of explanation for how environmental and metabolic signals reach the genome — the same protein-water lattice that mediates contraction and respiration also mediates chromatin state. The work has not been done. Connecting submolecular biology to the active research program of epigenetic regulation is a natural project that has not, to date, been pursued.

- **The framework's claims about pediatric and developmental phenomena.** Submolecular biology, like the rest of Peat's bioenergetic framework, is most persuasive when applied to adult chronic disease developing on a background of accumulated metabolic insult. Its application to pediatric cancer, congenital disease, and developmental disorders has been thinner. Whether the framework extends naturally to those domains or requires modification for them is open.
