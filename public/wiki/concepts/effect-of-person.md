# Effect of Person

Someone in a Soviet laboratory in the early 1950s noticed that when a researcher entered the room where a dog was hooked up to recording devices, the dog's pulse and respiration slowed and deepened. Not in fear, not in arousal — in a third state, which the Russians named *effect of person*. The same response was found in humans, and later in lower organisms, where it shows up as an electrophysiological shift well below the threshold of behavior. The bare observation is the load-bearing one. Two bodies in a room with each other are not in the same physiological state they would be in alone, and the difference is not subtle: heart rate, capillary tone, skin temperature, autonomic balance — all shift toward parasympathetic dominance. "Someone noticed that when a person enters a room where a dog is hooked up to recording devices, the dog's pulse and respiration become slower and deeper. People respond the same way to the approach of another person," Peat wrote in Chapter 15 of *Mind and Tissue* (1976/1994). "This response is called 'Effect of Person.' The changes are similar to some of those occurring in sexual excitement."

The chapter is the most distinctive in the book and, with the closely related Chapter 7 (*Group Energy*) and Chapter 16 (*Resolution and Transformation*), the load-bearing argument for the most political position in Peat's writing — that mass-mediated culture, screen saturation, antiseptic clinical encounters, and the systematic substitution of recorded for live human presence are not stylistic preferences of late modernity but a sustained, measurable experiment in suppressing the parasympathetic-expansive response healthy organisms run almost continuously in the company of other healthy organisms. The intervention is therefore not principally pharmacological. It is environmental. The patient with depression, the child with hyperactivity, the elder with dementia, the convalescent recovering from surgery — each is treated, in the Peat framework, partly by the metabolic interventions of [[learned-helplessness]] and [[stress]] and partly by the deliberate restoration of warm, in-person, mutually-affirming contact that the effect-of-person response evolved to register and amplify.

## The measurable physiological signature

What changes in a body that has just registered the presence of another body. The vector is consistent across the Soviet experimental record Peat indexed.

**Heart rate slows.** The signature finding — bradycardia of several beats per minute on the entry of a familiar person — is mediated by acetylcholine released by the vagus, and it follows the time-course of a reflex rather than a cognitive evaluation. "Slowing of the heart beat is produced by acetylcholine, which is released by the parasympathetic division of the nervous system," Peat wrote. The same shift that on the [[learned-helplessness|opposite]] pole of the [[stress]] cascade is the autonomic shutdown killing Richter's rats in five minutes is, under friendly presence, an *expansion* — the relaxed, slow-pulsing state of an organism that has registered safety.

**Respiration deepens.** Tidal volume rises. In a stress state the breath is shallow, fast, adrenaline-driven; in the effect-of-person state it is slow, full, CO2-conserving (see [[carbon-dioxide]] and [[consciousness]] for the cognitive load-bearing function of preserved arterial CO2).

**Capillary tone shifts toward dilation.** The skin warms. Lisina's 1957 work used vasodilation in finger capillaries as the autonomic endpoint. The same vasodilation Peat treated as a clinical marker of [[thyroid]] adequacy and as the inversion of the cold-extremities pattern of [[hypothyroidism]] is, under friendly presence, induced by the social signal alone. The clinical implication: warm hands in the company of a particular person are a measurement of the autonomic system saying *this person is safe*.

**Skin pinks.** From Chapter 16, quoting Proust on what it feels like from the inside: "As we feel that we are being affirmed and loved by another, we feel that the other person is expanding us, and filling us; our body shifts toward parasympathetic dominance, skin gets pink, eye-lids and gaze may drop." Pink skin is one of the few directly observable autonomic markers a clinician can use without instruments. A patient whose skin pinks in the presence of certain people and not others is mapping in real time which relationships are physiologically nourishing.

**Voluntary muscle tone falls.** Reich's "armoring" releases. The shoulders drop. The jaw unclenches. The breath reaches lower. The effect is asymmetric across relationships — with some people the armor stays up; with others it falls within minutes. Peat treated this as diagnostic of the relationship, not the patient's general stress level.

These are not separate findings. They are the convergent expression of one shift — autonomic balance moving from sympathetic-shrinking to parasympathetic-expansive — registered by every tissue with autonomic innervation.

## Lisina's biofeedback work

The empirical anchor is the 1957 work of M. I. Lisina, a Soviet psychologist working under the broad Pavlovian umbrella. Peat returned to her experiment because it accomplished two things at once. It established that an autonomic response (finger-capillary vasodilation) could be brought under voluntary control through awareness alone — biofeedback, decades before the term entered American psychology — and it established that the same autonomic response could be evoked through *imaginal* and *interpersonal* means as well as through direct effort. One mechanism, three handles: thought of warmth, presence of a warmed object, presence of a person.

The setup was simple. Subjects sat in a recording environment with sensors measuring blood-vessel dilation in the fingers. A light came on whenever the vessels dilated. With awareness of the light, subjects learned within a few sessions to dilate their finger capillaries at will. Without awareness — no feedback, no light — the same subjects could not produce the response even with extensive training. Awareness was the gating variable. Peat: "Lisina (in 1957, long before 'autonomic training' and biofeedback became respectable in the U.S.) found that biofeedback makes it possible to control a vascular response by acting through the orienting reflex, and that a response which couldn't be formed through 'ordinary' conditioning became possible when awareness was made part of the procedure."

The deeper finding came when Lisina varied the elicitor. Subjects could dilate their capillaries by thinking of warmth, by recalling a hot environment, or by attending to the presence of a warming object. The autonomic response was not bound to any single sensory input. It was bound to the *image* (the Soviet *obraz* — the perceptual representation that organizes orienting and learning). "Learning not only requires awareness, but images are directly able to evoke some of the autonomic responses that are appropriate for that situation. For example, thinking of a frightening event will make the heart beat harder; thinking of heat can make blood vessels in the skin dilate."

The connection to *effect of person* is direct. The presence of another body is, for the autonomic system, an *image* — a structured perceptual representation that evokes its appropriate response, here the parasympathetic-expansive pattern of bradycardia, vasodilation, and slow breath. The response is involuntary in the reflex sense and voluntary in Lisina's biofeedback sense — it can be cultivated by deliberate attention and degraded by deliberate avoidance. The clinical opportunity Peat saw: the response can be *trained* in patients whose chronic-stress physiology has suppressed it, by the deliberate cultivation of warm in-person contact under conditions of awareness.

The broader Soviet program (Pavlov's orienting reflex, Ukhtomskii's dominant theory, the "psychic self-regulation" research at Kazakh State University) converges on the same operational point: the autonomic system is a learning system, sensitive to images, trainable through awareness. Effect-of-person is one such trainable state, and the modern Western clinical environment systematically fails to evoke it.

## Reich's expansive vs shrinking model

Wilhelm Reich's framework — parasympathetic as *expansive*, sympathetic as *shrinking*, character as the chronic biasing of one or the other — supplied Peat with the conceptual scaffolding the effect-of-person finding required. The two halves of Reich's work Peat retained were the autonomic-balance picture and the diagnostic concept of *armoring*. From Chapter 15: "Reich and others have described parasympathetic function as being 'expansive,' in contrast to the 'shrinking' function of the sympathetic nerves, as in fear. Both of these functions are meaningfully related to the central nervous system, the cerebral cortex, and to the perception of outside events."

The expansive-shrinking distinction is not the anatomic sympathetic-parasympathetic split. It reads the same machinery phenomenologically — the experienced shape of the organism in each state. Sympathetic-shrinking is small: chest contracts, shoulders rise, pupils narrow, skin pales, eye field narrows. Parasympathetic-expansive is large: chest fills, shoulders drop, pupils settle, skin warms and pinks, eye field widens, body opens outward into the other.

The Reichian addition was that chronic biasing toward shrinking — the *armored* character — is a tissue condition, not an emotional disposition. The muscles bracing against expansion are doing metabolically costly work with structural consequences over decades. The armored organism cannot enter the effect-of-person state even in the presence of a person who would evoke it in a non-armored subject. Peat extended this to the cellular layer: "Many Reichians consider tension of muscles to be an active inhibition of natural energy flow; the problem might be a failure of brain activity to actively relax them, as in the 'rapid eye movement' phase of sleep" (*Mind and Tissue*, Chapter 6). The chronic-armor pattern is a failure of the cortical-trophic system that should be relaxing the musculature continuously — and that system fails when oxidative metabolism (see [[oxidative-metabolism]]) is suppressed by [[pufa]], [[estrogen]], hypothyroidism, or the [[learned-helplessness]] state.

The expansive-shrinking framework supplies the relational dimension Selye lacked. Selye described the cascade by which an organism degrades under chronic demand; Reich described the shape of the degraded organism in social space. Peat fused them. The chronic-stress patient is not just biochemically depleted; she is *small* in the Reichian sense — armored, contracted, unable to expand into the parasympathetic state that the company of another safe organism should induce. The clinical signature: the patient whose body does not register *you* in the room with her, whose heart rate does not slow on your entry, whose skin does not warm.

## The parasympathetic-expansion-in-love thread

Peat treated the effect-of-person response as the first stage of a continuous physiological sequence whose end-point was sexual orgasm. The intermediate stages — presence (mild bradycardia, mild vasodilation), rapport (deeper bradycardia, eyelid drop, skin pink), affection (vagal tone climbing, breath synchronizing), arousal (parasympathetic dominance with sympathetic tension building underneath), orgasm (sympathetic tension collapsing into terminal parasympathetic discharge) — are one phenomenon at different intensities, and the autonomic signature is continuous throughout.

Chapter 6 makes it explicit: "The 'Effect of Person,' which exists not only in people and dogs as a slowing of heart rate (bradycardia), but even in lower organisms as an electrophysiological response, is such a physiological change toward parasympathetic arousal, and during orgasm the bradycardia suddenly becomes extreme — the rhythm changes so suddenly that the heart seems to have stopped. The explanation for this continuity, from 'Effect of Person' to orgasm, seems to be that excitement and desire exist as a tension of approximated balance, and that with resolution, the 'expectancy' tension of the sympathetic system falls away, leaving parasympathetic dominance."

The hormonal substrate is the [[progesterone]]-and-[[thyroid]] pair Peat treated as the master neurotrophic stack. Progesterone raises cholinesterase, supports the reticular activating system, calms GABAergic tone, and protects against the sympathetic-shrinking cascade. Thyroid is the master oxidative-metabolism hormone, and oxidative metabolism funds the high-energy parasympathetic state. The hypothyroid, low-progesterone organism cannot sustain effect-of-person physiology even in ideal company. Patients in Peat's clinical reports who recovered the capacity for warm in-person contact were patients whose thyroid status and steroid pool had been restored.

Chapter 16 (*Resolution and Transformation*) describes the sleep that follows real interpersonal contact as the assimilation phase, during which "the old self, the old world are transformed by interaction with the loved person. The assimilation is probably completed and stabilized by sleep." The dream content of sleep is, on this reading, the brain re-organizing its perceptual world around the new dominant the other person has introduced — Ukhtomskii's *dominant* concept doing the work: every strong perception is a center of cortical excitation that recruits surrounding tissue. The same parasympathetic expansion that Peat tracked in [[sleep]], [[progesterone]], the [[orienting-reflex]], and the dreaming-versus-rumination distinction in [[consciousness]] is the expansion the effect-of-person response is a slice of.

## Why this is more than placebo

The standard mainstream framing absorbs social-physiology findings into the placebo-response literature — the patient feels better in the company of a warm clinician because of expectancy, attention, demand characteristics. Peat rejected this with unusual force on two empirical grounds.

First, the effect-of-person response is *autonomic* and *involuntary*. Finger-capillary vasodilation, deepened breath, slowed heart rate are measurable in real time in subjects who do not know the response is being measured, in dogs and lower organisms that cannot have expectancy effects, in electrophysiological recordings that bypass conscious report. The placebo-response literature is methodologically tied to subjective rating scales; the effect-of-person literature is tied to autonomic recording. They do not measure the same thing.

Second, the autonomic signature has a *consequence* — it shifts the patient's metabolic state in the direction of recovery. Parasympathetic dominance lowers free fatty acid mobilization, lowers cortisol, restores liver glycogen storage, supports thyroid conversion, raises CO2, and supports the cholinesterase-progesterone-thymus axis the chronic-stress state was depleting. A patient who spends thirty minutes in the company of someone who reliably evokes her parasympathetic-expansive response has shifted in the direction Peat's framework wants — not because of expectancy, but because of an autonomic state change with documented downstream metabolic consequences. The placebo framing has no story for the downstream metabolism.

A third anchor, more recent, is the convergence of heart-rate variability (HRV) research with the polyvagal-theory literature on roughly the same observation. HRV reliably rises in the presence of safe, familiar others and falls in the presence of unfamiliar or hostile ones. The mainstream HRV-and-social-engagement literature treats the finding as descriptive; Peat treated it as prescriptive.

The deeper mechanistic claim — see [[consciousness]] for the full account — is that subjective experience and autonomic state are not separable. The metabolic-structural state of the organism *is* the consciousness, not its cause. Under this identity, the question whether effect-of-person is "real" or "placebo" is the wrong question. The autonomic state is the experience. A patient whose body has entered the parasympathetic-expansive state is in the metabolic precondition for recovery, not a state that merely "feels like" recovery.

## Practical protocols

The implications are unusual in the Peat corpus because they are *environmental and relational* rather than dietary or pharmacological. The metabolic interventions Peat is best known for ([[thyroid]], [[progesterone]], sugar, salt, light, low-PUFA diet, suppression of serotonin and estrogen) are necessary but not sufficient. The patient who has done the metabolic work and is still failing often has an environment that fails to evoke the effect-of-person response.

**Limit screen time.** The screen — television, computer, smartphone, the recorded-voice-and-image substitute for in-person encounter — is the canonical low-energy person-effect medium. It produces a flat, attenuated autonomic signature, just enough to occupy the orienting reflex without delivering the parasympathetic-expansive payload. The brain registers a person-image (face on screen); the body does not register the autonomic signature (no shared CO2, no warmth, no synchronization of breath, no genuine bradycardia). The orienting system is engaged and the parasympathetic-expansive system is not. The protocol implication: substitute in-person for screen-mediated encounter wherever possible, especially for the depressed, the anxious, and the chronically lonely.

**Prefer in-person consultation.** The telemedicine encounter is in this framework an inferior clinical modality not because of any specific deficit in spoken content but because it fails to evoke the autonomic state an in-person encounter routinely produces. The patient sitting with a warm clinician whose presence slows her heart and warms her hands is in a different metabolic state than the same patient on a video call. The diagnostic information available to the clinician is also different — pink skin, warm hands, dropped shoulders, deepened breath are observable in person and effectively unobservable on a screen.

**Recognize the autonomic signature of being loved.** Warm hands and pink skin in the company of a particular person are clinical markers — perhaps the most reliable bedside markers — of the parasympathetic-expansive response. The clinician asking *which people in your life make your hands warm* is asking for a direct map of the patient's autonomic ecology. The cognitive version ("who do you feel close to?") produces verbal report mediated by self-narrative; the autonomic version produces a physiological measurement.

**Warm presence + affirming people for depressed patients.** The standard psychiatric protocol is pharmacological and secondarily cognitive. The Peat addition is the deliberate construction of an environment in which the patient is surrounded by people whose presence evokes her parasympathetic-expansive response. Family, old friends, a sympathetic priest or pastor, a small group of patients in similar circumstances. The autonomic state change induced by warm company is, by itself, a metabolic intervention on the cortisol-FFA-lactate cascade of [[stress]]. Not a substitute for [[thyroid]] and [[progesterone]] — a parallel input the framework requires to land.

**The case against the antiseptic clinical encounter.** The modern hospital — fluorescent-lit, sterile-surfaced, masked-and-gowned staff, the patient surrounded by strangers in identical uniforms — is an environment that *suppresses* the effect-of-person response. Masking, in particular, removes the human face from the autonomic-recognition system that the response depends on. The patient runs on chronic mild sympathetic activation, with corresponding elevation of cortisol and free fatty acids and corresponding impairment of healing. Convalescence at home with family is not a courtesy; it is a medical intervention.

**The household, the parish, the small community.** Peat's writing converges with Christian, Aristotelian, and communitarian-socialist accounts on the same operational point: the unit of human flourishing is the small face-to-face group, not the mass society and not the isolated individual. Chapter 7 (*Group Energy*) makes the argument explicit: "The sexual pair, and other personal groupings, are where culture and discovery really exist." Cultivation of small in-person community — family at table, parish at worship, the small work group with shared purpose — is therapeutic in its own right. The patient whose life lacks such a community has lost access to a major class of autonomic regulators.

## The social-political implications

The effect-of-person framework supplies the load-bearing argument for the most political position in Peat's writing — that mass-mediated culture is not a neutral technological condition of late modernity but an active suppressor of the parasympathetic-expansive response. The argument runs through Chapters 7, 15, 16, and 21 of *Mind and Tissue* and surfaces in the later *Generative Energy* podcast episodes and the *Newsletter* essays on authoritarianism and education.

The structural claim: mass media (television, radio, newspapers, the digital screen) function as *low-energy* person-effect substitutes. They occupy the orienting-reflex system with person-images — faces, voices, the perceptual surface of personhood — without delivering the autonomic payload (shared CO2, warmth, breath synchronization, vagal coupling). The result is a population whose orienting reflexes have been chronically engaged with person-shaped stimuli and whose parasympathetic-expansive responses have been chronically under-evoked: a society of armored individuals with chronically mild sympathetic activation, suppressed vagal tone, depleted progesterone-and-thyroid axis, narrowed perceptual fields. Peat closed Chapter 7 with: "At the present primitive-tyrannical state of mass communication, this emotional-energetic aspect of discovery would limit us to only a little progress in a lifetime, if we had only the large culture of civilization. The sexual pair, and other personal groupings, are where culture and discovery really exist."

The alternative he posited was deliberate cultivation of *high-energy* in-person environments — communities organized around shared purpose, parishes around shared worship, households around shared work, small groups around shared discovery. The political question Peat did not shrink from: a culture organized to suppress the parasympathetic-expansive response is a culture that produces a population easier to govern. The chronically armored, screen-saturated, mass-mediated individual is a less obstreperous citizen than the warm, present, integrated one. Peat's reading of the post-WWII rise of authoritarian-personality research, the CIA's MK-Ultra program, and the post-1968 turn in American behavioral psychology toward learned-helplessness conditioning is that the science of *suppressing the autonomic-expansive response* was developed deliberately, in service of social control, and that pervasive mass media is its applied form (see [[learned-helplessness]] for the parallel argument on the suppression of the escape response).

The corollary: the recovery interventions are *political* in the etymological sense. Cultivation of in-person community, deliberate avoidance of screen-mediated substitutes, restoration of household and parish and small-group life are interventions at the level of the *polis*. Peat was not a parishioner but wrote with unusual respect for the institutional forms of in-person community the religious tradition developed — the household, the parish, the small congregation, the shared meal — as therapeutic environments secular modernity has systematically dismantled.

## Counter-arguments

**Modern psychiatry's "interpersonal skills as soft science" framing.** The dominant academic-psychiatric position treats interpersonal-and-environmental factors as low-grade variance to be controlled for in the search for the *real* (pharmacological or neuroanatomical) cause. Peat's framework inverts the priority: the autonomic state induced by interpersonal factors is the proximate cause of the metabolic state, which is the proximate cause of the condition. The mainstream framing produces studies that control for the load-bearing variables and miss the dose-response.

**Telemedicine advocacy.** The post-2020 expansion of telemedicine has been defended on access, convenience, and cost grounds. The framework does not entail rejecting telemedicine — it entails recognizing it as a lower-bandwidth clinical instrument than the in-person encounter. The same encounter conducted in person and over video is not the same encounter for the patient's autonomic system, and the diagnostic information available to the clinician is also not the same. The mainstream framing treats the modality as interchangeable.

**"Placebo response" reductionism.** The standard cognitive-behavioral framing treats interpersonal-physiological findings as expectancy, demand, and contextual effects rather than a real causal pathway. The autonomic-measurement literature does not support this — the effect-of-person response is observable in dogs, lower organisms, and electrophysiological recordings that bypass conscious report. The placebo framing is tied to subjective-rating endpoints; the autonomic framing is tied to physiological endpoints. They do not measure the same thing.

**Polyvagal theory.** Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory shares much with the effect-of-person framework — both treat the vagal-tone response to social signals as load-bearing. The disagreement is about substrate. Polyvagal theory locates the relevant physiology in the *ventral vagal complex* and treats safe social interaction as the proximate evoker. The effect-of-person framework locates the physiology in the *metabolic-and-structural state* — the parasympathetic-expansive state is fundable only by adequate oxidative metabolism, progesterone, thyroid. The clinical implication of the difference: whether somatic-nervous-system work alone can restore the response in a patient with significant metabolic depletion, or whether the metabolic substrate has to be restored in parallel. Peat's framework says the latter.

**Cognitive-behavioral therapy.** CBT treats interpersonal difficulties as cognitive-behavioral targets. The framework does not deny thoughts and behaviors are real, but a patient whose physiology has been chronically suppressed by mass-mediated isolation, hypothyroidism, and chronic stress will find that CBT alone produces verbal compliance without autonomic change. The intervention has to land at the autonomic-and-metabolic layer for durable recovery.

**Wellness-culture "vagus nerve" interventions.** The popular hack literature (cold exposure, breathing exercises, humming, gargling, ear stimulation) advertises direct autonomic manipulation as self-administered therapy. The framework does not deny direct vagal interventions can shift autonomic state transiently — it denies the transient shift is equivalent to the autonomic state evoked by genuine in-person contact, and it denies the metabolic substrate can be bypassed by direct nervous-system manipulation.

## Data gaps

**The autonomic signature has not been mapped at scale.** Lisina's 1957 finding, the subsequent Soviet work, and the modern HRV-and-social-engagement literature converge on the same observation, but a systematic dose-response study — autonomic measurements across well-characterized social contexts in well-characterized populations — has not been done.

**The metabolic downstream of repeated evocation has not been measured.** The framework predicts patients with regularly-evoked parasympathetic-expansive responses will show lower cortisol, lower FFA mobilization, higher thyroid conversion, better liver glycogen storage than matched patients with rarely-evoked responses. Longitudinal studies in retirement communities versus isolated-elderly settings would supply much of the variance; the relevant studies are not in the literature.

**Screen-substitution dose-response.** The framework predicts hours of daily screen exposure will correlate with suppressed effect-of-person physiology — lower HRV in the company of in-person others, lower vasodilation, lower vagal tone. The pediatric-screen-exposure literature is suggestive but does not measure the right endpoint.

**Sex differences.** The progesterone-and-thyroid substrate is sexually dimorphic. The framework predicts the effect-of-person response is stronger and more cyclically state-dependent in women. The empirical case is thin.

**Clinical-encounter modality comparison.** The framework predicts the same encounter in person, on video, and by phone produces different autonomic states and different diagnostic information. The relevant head-to-head studies have not been done at the required scale.

**The cumulative cost of chronic suppression.** Decades of suppressed parasympathetic-expansive response — through mass media, screen substitution, antiseptic clinical encounters, the loss of household and parish community — should produce an accumulated metabolic load. The loneliness-as-mortality-risk epidemiology supplies suggestive correlations (loneliness comparable to smoking), but the mechanistic case in the bioenergetic register is not developed.

**The role of touch.** The Soviet literature Peat read concentrated on visual-and-auditory presence. The role of touch (handshake, embrace, the affectionate hand on the shoulder, the clinician's touch) in evoking the parasympathetic-expansive response is plausible from the framework but under-developed in the corpus. The oxytocin-and-touch literature overlaps but does not engage the autonomic-and-metabolic story.

**Contemplative-tradition data.** Christian, Buddhist, and other contemplative traditions encode several thousand years of tacit knowledge about cultivating the parasympathetic-expansive response in small-group settings (lectio divina, communal worship, contemplative prayer, sangha practice). The empirical case is anecdotal-historical and has not been systematically engaged in the corpus.
