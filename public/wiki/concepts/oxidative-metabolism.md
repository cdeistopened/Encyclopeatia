# Oxidative Metabolism

Mainstream biology treats the complete oxidation of glucose to carbon dioxide and water as one option among several — a metabolic pathway that cells can use when oxygen is abundant, swap for fermentation when it is not, and discard altogether when they specialize for division or stress. Ray Peat inverted the picture. For him, the controlled flow of electrons from sugar to oxygen, producing [[carbon-dioxide]] at the far end, is not one pathway among many. It is the metabolism that organized animal life is, and every chronic disease is a partial reversion away from it. "When you interfere with this flow of electrons by doing something that blocks the ability to use oxygen, you revert to basically a slime metabolism — a very primitive, ancient, under-organized way of using energy," he told Danny Roddy in *Generative Energy* episode 35. Cancer, [[diabetes]], [[dementia]], heart disease, autoimmunity, and ordinary [[aging]] are not, in this framework, distinct illnesses with their own genetic and environmental causes. They are different organ-level expressions of the same underlying defect: cells that have stopped completing the oxidation of glucose, and have started producing [[lactic-acid]] instead.

This is the conceptual frame downstream of which the entire vault sits. [[Thyroid]] is the hormone that activates it. [[PUFA]] is the substance class that suppresses it. [[Carbon dioxide]] is its diagnostic product and its protective signal. [[Lactic acid]] is the marker of its failure. The [[Randle cycle]] is one of its sub-mechanisms. The [[Warburg effect]] is its pathological terminus in cancer. Glucose, sugar, fruit, salt, light, gelatin, aspirin, coconut oil, niacinamide, progesterone, and pregnenolone are recommended because, by different routes, they support it. To grasp Peat's framework without grasping oxidative metabolism is to be left with a list of dietary opinions and no spine.

## Energy Creates Structure

Before Peat's framework is biochemistry, it is a physics claim. Life, in his telling, is a flow of energy between thermodynamic poles, and the structure of a cell is what that flow leaves behind. "Life interposes itself between the 'poles' of energy flow, and the flowing energy creates organization and structure, as it is dissipated into heat," he wrote in [[energy-structure-and-carbon-dioxide-a-realistic-view-of-the-organism|*Energy, Structure, and Carbon Dioxide* (2011)]]. "Structures store some of the energy, and tend to increase in complexity, taking advantage of the flow of energy to create phase differences with expanded internal surfaces, like a finely mixed emulsion. Like a finely divided emulsion, the more highly energized the organism is, the stabler it is." The cell is not a watertight bag with a metabolism inside it. It is a metabolism, and the apparent structure — proteins, organelles, membranes — is the residue of the energy moving through.

The corollary is that the chemistry of the energy source matters. Peat returned to this point repeatedly. "I see the interaction between the flow of energy (e.g., between a sugar and oxygen) and the structure as one in which the flow is retarded by the structure, and used by the structure, in maintaining and complexifying itself," he wrote in [[generative-energy|*Generative Energy*]]. "If energy can do something, it does something, and what it does is to build structure. The chemical nature of the energy source (and of the energy 'sink,' which is usually oxygen) influences the nature of the structure-building." A cell running on the full oxidation of glucose is not just better fed than a cell running on glycolysis; it is being built differently.

Jeremy England's later thermodynamic work, which Peat tracked closely, made the same point in formal terms. "Shining light on simple molecules creates order. If you shine light on existing proteins, or in any way have an energy disequilibrium, you get a flow of energy through that gob of polymerized atoms. The flow of energy itself — as Jeremy England shows with simple atoms — will align these proteins according to where the electrons are flowing," he told Roddy in episode 35. Oxygen pulls electrons; sugar releases them; the controlled flow between them organizes the protein-water system of the cell. In an aged or sick cell, the flow is sluggish, the order frays, and the residue is the disorder we call disease.

## Warburg, Koch, Szent-Györgyi: The Lineage

Peat's framework did not appear from nowhere. It is a self-consciously revived twentieth-century lineage that the cancer establishment, in his telling, suppressed for commercial reasons. "From the 19th century until the second quarter of the 20th century, cancer was investigated mainly as a metabolic problem," he wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2 in Wounds, Sickness, and Aging* (2009)]]. "This work, understanding the basic chemistry of metabolism, was culminating in the 1920s in the work of Otto Warburg and Albert Szent-Györgyi on respiration. Warburg demonstrated as early as 1920 that a respiratory defect, causing aerobic glycolysis, i.e., the production of lactic acid even in the presence of oxygen, was an essential feature of cancer."

The point that "lactic acid in the presence of oxygen" is the cancer signature is doing enormous work in Peat's framework. Lactic acid production is normal when oxygen is genuinely insufficient — when an animal is running, for example, and tissue oxygen demand outruns delivery. The cancer cell, by contrast, makes lactate even when oxygen is available. The fermentation is not a response to hypoxia; it is the metabolism the cell has adopted. This is the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]], and Peat treated it as the prototype for a much broader class of disease states.

[[Otto Warburg]] was not alone. "Frederick Koch in the U.S., Otto Warburg, and Albert Szent-Györgyi were all seeing the creation of aspiring organisms as developing and progressing through the use of oxygen," Peat said on the [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|July 2016 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on the metabolism of cancer]]. "They saw that when you interfere with this flow of electrons by doing something that blocks the ability to use oxygen, you revert to basically a slime metabolism — a very primitive, ancient, under-organized way of using energy. All three of them saw cancer as using a primitive, pre-oxidative metabolism." Koch, working in Detroit, made the strongest form of the claim: the respiratory defect was responsible "for immunodeficiency, allergy, and defective function of muscles, nerves, and secretory cells, as well as cancer." Cancer was not unique. It was the most dramatic case of a defect that, in milder forms, was producing most of the diseases medicine was treating.

For this lineage Peat reserved a particular admiration that bordered on indignation at its later treatment. "For about 70 years, the cancer establishment denigrated Warburg for commercial reasons," he wrote in his [[cancer|cancer email exchange]]. "The present use of his name by mainline researchers for immature ideas about cancer physiology shows the quality of biomedical education in the US and Europe." The rediscovery of "Warburg metabolism" in the 2000s, in his view, was a partial and dishonest recovery — taking Warburg's observation while abandoning his interpretation. The fashionable framing treated aerobic glycolysis as "just another opportunity for killing the evil deviant cancer cell, rather than looking for the causes of the respiratory defect." Warburg's actual claim — that cancer is a respiratory defect first and a proliferation second, that restoring respiration can reverse it — was quietly dropped, because it implicated diet, hormones, and environmental toxins in carcinogenesis and pointed away from patentable therapies.

## The Mechanism, in One Pass

In a cell running oxidatively, glucose is broken down through glycolysis to pyruvate, pyruvate enters the [[mitochondria]] and is oxidized through the Krebs cycle, and the electrons that come off are passed down the respiratory chain to oxygen, generating CO2 and ATP. The full path yields roughly 36 ATP per glucose molecule and leaves carbon dioxide and water as the only products. A cell running glycolytically stops at pyruvate, hands the pyruvate's electrons to lactate dehydrogenase (which reduces it to lactate), and ends with about 2 ATP per glucose. "Glycolysis is very inefficient for producing usable energy compared to the respiratory metabolism of the mitochondria," Peat wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]], "and when lactate is carried to the liver, its conversion to glucose adds to the energy drain on the organism." Glycolytic cells are not merely producing less energy — they are also forcing the rest of the body to spend energy cleaning up after them. The mechanism is detailed in [[lactic-acid]].

The cytoplasmic redox state is the hinge. "Thyroid stimulation of oxygen consumption tends to prevent lactic acid production, because it keeps the cytoplasm in a state of relative oxidation, i.e., it keeps the concentration of NAD+ hundreds of times higher than that of NADH," Peat wrote in [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation* (2013)]]. "NADH is required for the conversion of pyruvate to lactate." A high NAD+/NADH ratio is the cell's signature of healthy oxidation; a falling ratio is the signature of slipping toward glycolysis. The mitochondrion is what holds the ratio steady, by consuming pyruvate and the electrons (or "hydrogens") from NADH at the rate they are produced. When mitochondrial respiration falters, NADH accumulates, pyruvate is dumped into lactate to regenerate NAD+ for glycolysis to continue, and the cell slides toward the Warburg state.

## Carbon Dioxide: The Product That Stabilizes the System

The product of complete oxidation is not waste. Carbon dioxide, in Peat's framework, is the gas that organizes the protein-water phase of the cell, and its presence is what allows oxidative metabolism to continue running. This claim is developed in full at [[carbon-dioxide]]. For the oxidative-metabolism frame, the load-bearing facts are these. CO2 is acidic in the Lewis sense — it binds avidly to amine groups on proteins and changes their electronic state. "Carbon dioxide doesn't have any protons, so if you think of proton release as the essence of an acid, you can't think of CO2 as an acid. But it's called a Lewis acid. Having two oxygens on one carbon, it's a very powerful electron binder. The instant it sits down on a protein, the protein shifts its electrons to be close to the center of the carbon dioxide molecule," Peat told Roddy in episode 35. "That's part of the sculpting effect of respiration. It intensifies whatever process was creating it in the first place. The more you breathe, the more able you are to breathe, in effect."

The implication is that oxidative metabolism is autocatalytic. A cell producing CO2 is producing the substance that keeps it producing CO2: stabilizing its mitochondria, protecting its proteins from glycation, occupying the amine groups that PUFA peroxidation products would otherwise attack. "In the way that carbon dioxide alters the shapes and electrical affinities of hemoglobin and other proteins, I propose that it increases the stability of the mitochondrial coacervate, causing it to 'recruit' additional proteins from its external environment, as well as from its own synthetic machinery, to enlarge both its structure and its functions," Peat wrote in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality* (2016)]]. CO2 builds the mitochondria that produce CO2.

Lactate does the opposite. "In the relative absence of carbon dioxide, or excess of alternative solutes and adsorbents, such as lactic acid, the stability of the mitochondrial phase would be decreased, and the mitochondria would be degraded in both structure and function," Peat continued. Lactate accumulation is not just a marker of the respiratory defect; it actively damages the mitochondria that would otherwise be repairing the defect. The slide is a positive feedback loop pointing the wrong direction. In episode 35 he put it bluntly: "Lactate should stand out as a polar opposite of carbon dioxide. I think a basic medical test should be the level of lactate in your blood versus total carbon dioxide. The higher lactate gets, the closer you are to being dead."

This is also why pure-glucose oxidation is metabolically privileged over fat oxidation, even when both are using oxygen. "When you oxidize carbohydrate, you get extra carbon dioxide as a byproduct. It's like insurance that you're going to reinforce the oxidizing process. But oxidizing fat — even if it's a safe saturated fat — you don't get such a return of carbon dioxide, and that increases the risk of falling over into the lactate, glycolytic slime metabolism," Peat said. The CO2 yield per oxygen consumed (the respiratory quotient) is higher for glucose than for fat, and in Peat's framework that difference compounds — more CO2 means more mitochondrial stability means more capacity to oxidize means more CO2.

## The Stress Metabolism: How the System Falls Apart

The collapse of oxidative metabolism follows a stereotyped sequence that Peat called "the stress metabolism" and that he treated as the unifying physiology of nearly every disease state. The trigger can be physical or psychological: trauma, infection, fasting, emotional strain, sleep loss, exposure to PUFA, exposure to estrogen. The signature is the same. "The features of the stress metabolism include increases of stress hormones, lactate, ammonia, free fatty acids, and fat synthesis, and a decrease in carbon dioxide," he wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]].

The chain runs like this. Stress hormones — adrenaline, cortisol — activate lipase in adipose tissue, releasing free fatty acids into circulation. Those fatty acids enter the cell and block glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]]. The cell, denied complete glucose oxidation, ferments glucose to lactate. Cortisol simultaneously breaks down protein into amino acids, some of which are converted to fatty acids and then back through "futile cycles" that liberate ammonia. Ammonia suppresses respiration further and stimulates glycolysis further. CO2 falls because the cell is no longer producing it; the protein-amine groups that CO2 was occupying are now exposed to glycation by the glucose that can no longer be oxidized. The proteins stiffen, the mitochondria accumulate calcium, the calcium triggers more excitation, and the excitation depletes more oxygen.

"Cancer metabolism or stress metabolism typically involves an excess of the adaptive hormones, resulting from an imbalance of the demands made on the organism and the resources available to the organism," Peat wrote. "Excessive stimulation depletes glucose and produces lactic acid, and causes cortisol to increase, causing a shift to the consumption of fat and protein rather than glucose. Increased cortisol activates the Randle effect (the inhibition of glucose oxidation by free fatty acids), accelerates the breakdown of protein into amino acids, and activates the enzyme fatty acid synthase, which produces fatty acids from amino acids and pyruvate, to be oxidized in a 'futile cycle,' producing heat, and increasing the liberation of ammonia from the amino acids. Ammonia suppresses respiratory, and stimulates glycolytic, activity."

What is striking about this account is that "cancer metabolism" and "stress metabolism" are the same thing. The metabolic state of a tumor cell and the metabolic state of a person under chronic stress, in a calorie deficit, or on a low-carbohydrate diet are not analogous — they are identical. "When you're starving, diabetic, or on a ketogenic diet, you're lowering the oxidative state of the cell. By making you less stable, it's opening up the opportunities for all of the processes of aging: creating collagen, free radical activities such as nitric oxide which can degrade your DNA," Peat said in episode 35. The fashionable framings of "fat-adapted" metabolism, in his view, were sales pitches for the same metabolic state that defines disease.

## Thyroid: What Activates Oxidative Metabolism

If oxidative metabolism is the desired state, [[thyroid]] is the hormone that produces it. "Thyroid is the essence of oxidation for living cells," Peat told Roddy in episode 35. "Thyroid is the hormone that activates our whole oxidative system and leads to the production of carbon dioxide when it's working properly," he said in the [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|July 2013 *EastWest Healing* episode on energy and metabolism]]. This is the central reason thyroid status is, in Peat's framework, the most important single variable in physiology. Thyroid does not "speed up" metabolism in some generic sense; it specifically activates the oxidative pathway, the cytochrome oxidase complex, the production of CO2, and the maintenance of the high NAD+/NADH ratio that prevents lactate accumulation. The full mechanism is at [[thyroid]].

Thyroid also produces mitochondria. "When the amount of thyroid hormone is increased, or when the organism adapts to a high altitude, the number of mitochondria increases. But in energy deficient states such as diabetes, they don't," Peat wrote in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality*]]. Mitochondrial biogenesis tracks the demand for oxidative capacity, and thyroid is what signals the demand. A thyroid-suppressed person is not just running slow; they are losing the cellular apparatus that would let them run fast again.

The diagnostic implications are direct. Because thyroid activates CO2 production and prevents lactate accumulation, the oxidative state is measurable from the outside. Temperature reflects the heat that complete oxidation produces. Pulse reflects the rate at which the system is running. Total CO2 in the blood reflects the steady-state production. Pulse oximetry combined with rebreathing can probe the Haldane effect directly. The clinical signal Peat cared about was not [[tsh|TSH]] — which he considered a fraud — but the combination of temperature, pulse, and the disappearance of lactate-driven symptoms. This is developed at [[broda-barnes]] and [[temperature-tracking]].

## PUFA: What Suppresses Oxidative Metabolism

If thyroid is the on-switch, [[PUFA]] is the off-switch, and Peat treated polyunsaturated fatty acids as the great chronic suppressor of oxidative metabolism in modern life. The full mechanism — PUFA's interference with thyroid hormone transport, its incorporation into [[cardiolipin]] in place of palmitate, its peroxidation to aldehydes that attack mitochondrial proteins, its conversion to inflammatory prostaglandins — is at [[pufa]]. For the oxidative-metabolism frame the load-bearing claim is that PUFA accumulation is the slow lever by which a person loses the ability to oxidize glucose, decade by decade.

"Polyunsaturated fats with inappropriate structure interfere with these adaptive flows of energy and substance in all of the known systems of cellular response," Peat wrote in [[energy-structure-and-carbon-dioxide-a-realistic-view-of-the-organism|*Energy, Structure, and Carbon Dioxide*]]. "These exogenous substances suppress the respiratory energy system, the intercellular communication systems, and the intracellular response systems. Immunodeficiency, autoimmunity, inflammatory diseases, aging, cancer, heart disease, nervous diseases, and hormonal imbalances are produced when these fats interfere with the spontaneous self-regulatory processes of the organism." This is not a list of side effects. It is one effect — respiratory suppression — expressed in different organs.

The cardiolipin substitution is particularly diagnostic. Cardiolipin is the phospholipid that holds [[cytochrome-c-oxidase]], the terminal enzyme of the respiratory chain, in its functional configuration. In a healthy mitochondrion, cardiolipin is rich in palmitate. In an aged or PUFA-loaded mitochondrion, palmitate is displaced by polyunsaturated fatty acids, the enzyme conformation is disrupted, and respiratory capacity falls. "Palmitic acid, which is a major component of the cardiolipin which regulates the main respiratory enzyme, becomes displaced by polyunsaturated fats as aging progresses. Copper tends to be lost from this same enzyme system, and the state of the water is altered as the energetic processes change," Peat wrote in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality*]]. Aging, in this account, is largely the substitution of the wrong fat into the wrong enzyme over a lifetime of dietary exposure.

The experimental data Peat cited was striking. Mitochondria from animals fed an essential-fatty-acid-free diet had "a great increase in the activity of cytochrome oxidase, and adding an 'essential fatty acid' strongly inhibited this enzyme's activity," he wrote in [[chronic-fatigue|*Chronic Fatigue*]], citing Kunkel and Williams. "While thyroid supplementation produces increased activity of cytochrome oxidase, the avoidance of unsaturated oils would probably, to a great extent, make thyroid supplementation unnecessary to maintain a high rate of health-supporting mitochondrial respiration." The PUFA depletion protocol, in other words, recapitulates thyroid supplementation at the level of the enzyme that matters most.

## The Crabtree Effect and Fat Burning

Two sub-mechanisms deserve their own brief treatment because they recur throughout downstream articles. The first is the Crabtree effect — the suppression of respiration by glycolysis. The Pasteur effect is the textbook case: when oxygen is present, fermentation stops, because the cell would rather oxidize pyruvate than ferment it. The Crabtree effect is the inversion: when glucose floods a cell whose mitochondria are already compromised, the increased glycolytic flow itself suppresses respiration further, even though oxygen is available. "The Crabtree effect, which is the suppression of respiration by glycolysis, is often described as the simple opposite of the Pasteur effect, in which respiration limits glycolysis to the rate that allows its product to be consumed oxidatively. But the Pasteur effect is a normal sort of control system; when the Pasteur effect fails, as in cancer, there is glycolysis which is relatively independent of respiration, causing sugar to be consumed inefficiently," Peat wrote in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality*]]. The Crabtree effect is the cellular version of what happens in a diabetic given more sugar: the sugar goes in, but it goes to lactate because the respiratory defect prevents it from going anywhere else.

The second is the [[Randle cycle]], the substrate competition between fat and glucose. Peat treated fat oxidation, even of saturated fats, as a metabolically inferior fuel relative to glucose, for three reasons that compound. Fat oxidation produces less CO2 per oxygen consumed. Fat oxidation, once activated, suppresses glucose oxidation through the Randle effect, forcing whatever glucose is present into the lactate pathway. And fat oxidation in a PUFA-loaded cell generates peroxidation products that further damage the mitochondrion. "If you burn fatty acids, that stops you from oxidizing glucose; instead, you'll turn it into lactic acid," Peat said in [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|the 2013 *EastWest Healing* episode]]. "Diabetes consists of living on fatty acids and not being able to oxidize glucose, but still turning glucose into lactic acid, so that lactic acidemia becomes a problem in diabetes." The diabetic is not a person who cannot get glucose into the cell — "if you're making lactic acid, the sugar is getting into the cell" — but a person whose cells, awash in fatty acids, cannot oxidize it once it arrives.

## Uncoupling: Why Inefficiency Is Sometimes Better

A counterintuitive feature of Peat's framework is that mitochondria producing CO2 without ATP — the so-called "uncoupled" state — are protective rather than wasteful. "When mitochondria are 'uncoupled,' they produce more carbon dioxide than normal, and the mitochondria produce fewer free radicals. Animals with uncoupled mitochondria live longer than animals with the ordinary, more efficient mitochondria, that produce more reactive oxidative fragments," Peat wrote in [[protective-co2-and-aging|*Protective CO2 and Aging* (2011)]]. The uncoupled mitochondrion is running its electron transport chain at maximum velocity but releasing the energy as heat rather than capturing it as ATP. The faster the chain runs, the fewer electrons leak into superoxide production, and the more PUFA-derived peroxidation products get oxidized away. "Wasteful energy — in the sense of pouring out carbon dioxide and producing heat as your only product — is insurance," Peat told Roddy in episode 35.

This reframes a number of recommendations that look puzzling from an efficiency-maximizing standpoint. [[Thyroid]] hormone is partly uncoupling; aspirin is uncoupling; DNP, the discontinued weight-loss drug, was a strong uncoupler with several of thyroid's other effects. "Aspirin and thyroid (T3) increase uncoupling. A drug that used to be used for weight reduction, DNP, also uncouples mitochondrial metabolism, and, surprisingly, it has some of the beneficial effects of thyroid and aspirin. It stimulates the consumption of lactic acid and the formation of carbon dioxide," Peat wrote in [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]]. The heat that warm-bodied animals produce is not metabolic waste; it is the cost of maintaining the high electron-throughput state that prevents PUFA from doing its damage.

This is also why temperature is the most reliable Peat metric. A person running hot is uncoupling, producing CO2, and clearing PUFA peroxidation products. A person running cold is over-coupled, producing less CO2 per glucose, and accumulating the products that PUFA generates when respiration slows.

## Cancer: The Terminus of the Defect

For Peat, cancer is not a unique disease. It is the endpoint of the metabolic shift that every other chronic disease is partway along. "Warburg believed that mitochondrial respiration is the controlling influence in cell differentiation, and cancer is a reversion to a primitive form of life based on a 'respiratory defect,'" Peat wrote, paraphrasing Warburg in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality*]]. Differentiation requires the energy that only oxidative metabolism delivers. When the energy is insufficient, cells lose the capacity to differentiate, and they fall back on the older program of dividing. "Cancer involves a shift in the direction of reduction — an idea that has been around for over 60 years, but kept in the background by the genetic dogma. Oxidative metabolism shifts the balance away from cell multiplication, allowing specific functions, and so allows an organism to recover from cancer," Peat wrote in his [[cancer|cancer email]].

This puts the genetic theory of cancer in its place. Genetic mutations are real, but they are downstream. "Harry Rubin's studies of cells in culture have expanded Warburg's picture of the process of cancerization, showing that genetic changes occur only after the cells have been transformed into cancer," Peat wrote. The cell adopts the Warburg metabolism first; the mutations accumulate later, in the context of the disorganized redox state and the PUFA-derived aldehydes that the failing respiration produces. Treating the mutations is treating the smoke, not the fire.

The therapeutic implication is that restoring oxidation should restore differentiation, and in many cases it does. "Simply stopping excessive excitation tends to restore the dominance of oxidative respiration over glycolysis," Peat wrote in [[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy* (2013)]]. "Increasing carbon dioxide lowers the intracellular pH, as well as inhibiting lactic acid formation, and restoring the oxidation of glucose increases CO2." The full mechanism is at [[warburg-effect]] and the protocol at [[anti-stress-diet]].

## Aging: The Same Defect, Slowed Down

If cancer is the terminus, ordinary [[aging]] is the same trajectory at a slower rate. The PUFA accumulates over decades; the mitochondria lose function; CO2 production falls; lactate creeps up; proteins glycate; collagen stiffens; the brain's respiratory enzyme activity drops. "With aging, cells have less ability to produce energy, and are often more easily stimulated. The accumulation of polyunsaturated fats is one of the factors that reduce the ability of mitochondria to produce energy. Increased estrogen exposure, decreased thyroid hormone, an increased ratio of iron to copper, and lack of light, are other factors that impair the cytochrome oxidase enzyme," Peat wrote in [[cancer-disorder-and-energy|*Cancer: Disorder and Energy*]]. Each of these factors is a different way of suppressing oxidative metabolism. Aging is what their cumulative action looks like over a lifetime.

The brain is particularly exposed because it is the most oxidatively demanding organ. "The brain has a high rate of oxidative metabolism, and so it forms a very large proportion of the carbon dioxide produced by an organism. It also governs, to a great extent, the metabolism of other tissues, including their consumption of oxygen and production of carbon dioxide or lactic acid. Within a particular species, the rate of oxygen consumption increases in proportion to brain size, rather than body weight," Peat wrote in [[protective-co2-and-aging|*Protective CO2 and Aging*]]. Brain function and oxidative metabolism are tightly coupled, which is why [[dementia]] in Peat's framework is fundamentally a respiratory defect of brain tissue. "In Alzheimer's disease, brain respiratory metabolism is inhibited, creating a carbon dioxide deficiency with an excess of lactic acid and ammonia. Both Alzheimer's disease and multiple sclerosis involve depressed brain metabolism combined with an inflammatory process," he wrote in [[homeostasis-and-aging|*Homeostasis and Aging* (1999)]]. The same chain runs from PUFA to estrogen to thyroid suppression to mitochondrial damage to CO2 deficit to glycation of proteins like tau — Alzheimer's pathology is the late stage of a process that begins, decades earlier, with the slow PUFA-driven displacement of palmitate from cardiolipin.

Even the developmental signature reverses with age. "If the intrauterine experience, with low oxygen and high carbon dioxide, serves to 'reprogram' cells to remove the accumulated effects of age and stress, and so to maximize the developmental potential of the new organism, a life that's lived with nearly those levels of oxygen and carbon dioxide might be able to avoid the progressive silencing of genes and loss of function that cause aging and degenerative diseases," Peat speculated in [[protective-co2-and-aging|*Protective CO2 and Aging*]]. The fetus is the maximally oxidative organism. Adulthood is a process of falling away from that condition.

## Measurement: Reading the Oxidative State From Outside

The conceptual claims have direct clinical signatures. The oxidative state is measurable, and Peat treated several routine observations as more reliable than any blood panel. Body temperature reflects the heat output of complete oxidation. Resting pulse reflects the steady-state energy throughput. Total CO2 (or bicarbonate) in venous blood reflects the rate of mitochondrial CO2 production. Lactate reflects the failure of complete oxidation. Achilles reflex relaxation time reflects the rate at which calcium is cleared from muscle, which depends on mitochondrial ATP. The classical Peat sign of well-functioning metabolism — pulse around 85, temperature near 98.6°F by mid-morning, lactate low, salt cravings sated — is the externally visible signature of oxidative metabolism running freely.

"Just by increasing CO2 — breathing in a bag for a minute or so several times a day — you will lower your serum lactic acid. It is only in the last few years that people are recognizing you can diagnose cancer just by an increased chronic lactic acid excess in the blood," Peat said on the [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|July 2016 cancer episode]]. The fact that lactate is a diagnostic for cancer — not just for muscular exertion — is the clinical confirmation of the framework. The same biomarker that signals a respiring cell's distress signals a tumor cell's stable metabolism.

The reverse intervention follows. Restoring oxidative metabolism means restoring its conditions: thyroid sufficiency, PUFA depletion, glucose availability, CO2 elevation, freedom from the chronic-stress hormone milieu. "The 'treatment' for intracellular fatigue consists of normalizing thyroid and steroid metabolism, and eating a diet including fruit juice, milk, some eggs or liver, and gelatin, assuring adequate calcium, potassium sodium, and magnesium, and using supplements of niacin-amide, aspirin, and carbon dioxide when necessary," Peat wrote in [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]]. "Simply increasing carbon dioxide decreases lactic acid and ammonia, increases GABA (the sleep improving nerve inhibitor), and regulates mineral and water disposition." The constellation of foods, hormones, and substances that recurs throughout the vault is not an arbitrary list. Every item on it supports oxidative metabolism at a specific point in the chain. The synthesis is at [[anti-stress-diet]] and [[energy-restoration]].

## The Antioxidant Inversion

Peat's framework reverses one of the most successful ideas in twentieth-century nutrition. Mainstream theory holds that oxidation is damaging and that antioxidants protect against it. Peat held the opposite: the controlled oxidation of glucose to CO2 is what cells exist to do, and the "free radical damage" attributed to oxidation is the signature of incomplete oxidation, not its excess.

"Running the oxidative process through the mitochondria very fast happens to be an antioxidative process in terms of avoiding toxic free radicals," he told Josh Rubin in [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|the 2013 *EastWest Healing* episode]]. "The faster you burn oxygen usefully oxidizing sugars, the less you have to worry about taking care of the free radical toxic products that develop when you slow down the respiratory process by burning fats, especially polyunsaturated fats." The free radicals are produced by electron leakage at a slow-running respiratory chain. A fast-running chain produces fewer of them and consumes those that do appear.

"I think all of these so-called antioxidants, like ascorbic acid, when it's really protecting you and working the way it should, it's a powerful oxidant," Peat said in episode 35. "Dehydroascorbate is the predominant form in a healthy working cell, and that shifts in the direction of ascorbic acid (reduced form) in cancer and stress cells. The whole idea of antioxidants has been skewed heavily by denying the role of carbon dioxide and oxidation as a protective effect. The stronger the oxidation in the normal route, the safer you are." Vitamin C works because it can be oxidized to dehydroascorbate, accepting an electron from something more damaging. A cell rich in dehydroascorbate is a cell in a healthy oxidized state. The pop-nutrition picture has the polarity backward.

This is the conceptual move that separates Peat from every "antioxidant" supplement protocol on the market. The goal is not to mop up oxidants. The goal is to oxidize glucose so completely that no problematic intermediates accumulate in the first place.

## Counter-arguments

- **The respiratory-quotient indifference of mainstream metabolism.** Conventional bioenergetics treats fat oxidation and glucose oxidation as roughly interchangeable from the standpoint of ATP yield per unit oxygen consumed, with fat actually edging glucose on energy-per-gram. Peat's claim that the CO2 yield matters as much as the ATP yield — because CO2 itself does metabolic work — has no clean orthodoxy-side equivalent. Mainstream physiology measures CO2 as exhaust.
- **The ketogenic-diet literature.** Within mainstream medicine, ketogenic and very-low-carbohydrate diets are endorsed for epilepsy, used experimentally in glioblastoma, and championed by a sizable community for general metabolic health. The Peatian position — that ketosis is a form of the stress metabolism and a slide toward the Warburg state — contradicts this literature directly. The standard counter-evidence (improved blood glucose, weight loss, seizure control on ketogenic diets) is real and not addressed in detail by Peat's framework, which tends to attribute the apparent benefits to the elimination of inflammatory carbohydrate sources rather than to ketogenesis itself.
- **The "Warburg-as-anabolic-adaptation" school.** Modern cancer biology has partly recovered Warburg's observation but interpreted it differently. The current consensus is that tumor cells favor aerobic glycolysis not because they cannot respire but because the precursors of glycolysis support the biosynthetic demands of rapid proliferation (the Vander Heiden/Cantley interpretation). On this view, the Warburg effect is an adaptive choice by metabolically competent cells, not a defect. Peat's claim that mitochondrial respiration is genuinely impaired in cancer cells is contested by this literature.
- **The lactate-as-fuel literature.** Recent work (Brooks et al.) has rehabilitated lactate as a normal inter-organ fuel, shuttled between glycolytic and oxidative tissues. Peat's "lactate is poison" framing was developed before this literature matured and is largely incompatible with it. The Peatian position would presumably be that the lactate shuttle reflects pathological accumulation rather than physiological signaling, but this is not directly addressed in his writing.
- **PUFA essentiality.** Mainstream nutrition continues to classify linoleic and alpha-linolenic acid as essential, and the Institute of Medicine sets minimum intakes. Peat's claim that humans synthesize sufficient prostaglandin precursors from saturated fat under most conditions, and that PUFA at modern intake levels is universally toxic, is heterodox.
- **The genetic theory of cancer.** Peat's revival of the metabolic-origin theory is, in mainstream oncology, a minority position. Genome sequencing of tumors has demonstrated extensive somatic mutation, and the conventional view holds that driver mutations precede and cause the metabolic shift, not the reverse. Peat's reading of Harry Rubin's culture experiments — that the metabolic shift precedes the mutations — is not part of any mainstream consensus.
- **The thyroid testing controversy.** Endocrinology continues to rely on TSH as the primary thyroid marker. Peat's claim that TSH is fraudulent and that temperature/pulse should be the diagnostic standard is directly opposed to ADA, ATA, and Endocrine Society guidelines.

## Data gaps

- **Quantitative thresholds.** Peat does not specify, for any given individual, how much CO2 is "enough" or how much PUFA accumulation is "too much." The framework is qualitative throughout. Whether the marginal benefit of further increasing CO2 production tapers, plateaus, or reverses at high metabolic rates is not addressed.
- **The Warburg-school internal debate.** Peat treats Warburg, Koch, Szent-Györgyi, and Albert Lehninger as if they spoke with one voice. They did not — Warburg's later positions diverged from Szent-Györgyi's in important ways, and the differences are not engaged in Peat's writing.
- **Modern mitochondrial biology.** Peat's framework was largely set in the 1970s and updated thereafter through additions rather than revisions. Discoveries about mitochondrial DNA inheritance, the mitophagy/biogenesis balance, the role of PGC-1α as a master regulator, and the metabolic flexibility literature are not integrated into the framework. Whether the existing framework accommodates these findings or is contradicted by them is unresolved.
- **The intestinal microbiome's role.** Peat addressed endotoxin and intestinal sources of inflammation, but the microbiome-as-metabolic-organ literature (short-chain fatty acid production, butyrate as fuel, microbial CO2 production) is not engaged.
- **Pediatric oxidative metabolism.** Children's metabolic rates are far higher than adults' in proportion to body mass, but Peat addressed pediatric application only sporadically. The thresholds and signs that distinguish a healthy high metabolism from an over-stressed one in a child are not laid out.
- **The exercise paradox.** Peat distinguished "concentric" from "eccentric" muscular work and considered some exercise restorative and some destructive, but did not develop this distinction into a complete framework. Whether endurance training, resistance training, or specific modalities support or undermine long-term oxidative metabolism — and at what dose — remains underdeveloped.
- **The hyperoxia-hypoxia paradox.** Peat noted that both excess oxygen (hyperbaric) and deficient oxygen (hypothyroid hyperventilation) act like CO2 deficiency, and that mild hypoxia (high altitude) raises tissue CO2 and is paradoxically protective. The conditions under which oxygen administration helps versus harms are not clearly specified.
- **Quantitative PUFA-depletion kinetics.** The "two to four year" timeline for tissue PUFA depletion is repeated throughout the corpus, but the underlying turnover kinetics are not derived from primary data within Peat's writing. Individual variation is not addressed.
