# Sugar

The single most-referenced unresolved link in this vault is `[[sugar]]`. Thirty-six articles point to it: hub concepts ([[blood-sugar]], [[oxidative-metabolism]], [[randle-cycle]]), substances ([[fructose]], [[sucrose]], [[honey]], [[milk]], [[orange-juice]], [[pufa]]), and the condition pages ([[hair-loss]], [[hypothyroidism]], [[anxiety-depression]], [[heart-disease]]) that all eventually route back to whether the body can hold its sugar metabolism intact. That gap is fitting. Sugar is not a single substance in Peat's writing. It is the *category name* for the family of small carbohydrates the cell can oxidize cleanly. Fructose, sucrose, glucose, lactose, ripe fruit, honey, and milk all belong to this class. Starch, by way of digestion, eventually joins it, but at a cost the umbrella concept does not pay. This article is about the framework, not the mechanism. Mechanism work belongs at [[blood-sugar]] (the cellular glycemic cascade), [[randle-cycle]] (the sugar–fat competition), and at each substance page.

The framework-level claim is one Peat repeated in nearly every interview from 2008 forward: sugar is the body's preferred, cleanest, most metabolically protective fuel. He stated it most directly in the [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|first *Ask the Herb Doctor* sugar episode, September 2010]]:

> "Sugar is really the ideal energy exchange substance. It's useful for practically all organisms, and it is something that we can store and use for building all of the big molecules. When it's metabolized, it releases carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide is really a more universal substance than oxygen for the support of life."

Everything else in this article follows from that sentence. If sugar oxidizes cleanly to [[carbon-dioxide]], and CO2 is protective (see [[carbon-dioxide]] for the full mechanism), then the food categories that produce the most CO2 per gram of oxygen consumed will be the most protective. Sugar produces about fifty percent more CO2 per unit oxygen than fat. The body whose cells can burn sugar to CO2 is the body that resists stress, infection, calcification, fibrosis, and the slow drift toward [[cancer]] that Peat traced through the [[warburg-effect]] across decades of writing. The body that has lost the ability to burn its sugar — because circulating free fatty acids are blocking the oxidative pathway through the [[randle-cycle]] — is the body that ages, inflames, and fails.

That is the inversion. The mainstream picture treats sugar as the poison that breaks the body's metabolism. Peat treated impaired sugar oxidation as the disease, and sugar as the substrate the body needs to repair it.

## The mainstream "sugar is poison" position and how it captured nutrition

To take Peat's framework seriously, one has to first understand the cultural position he was inverting. The "sugar is poison" narrative is not one position but a stack of distinct claims, each with a partial constituency and a partial scientific lineage:

1. **John Yudkin (1972)** — *Pure, White and Deadly*. Yudkin argued that sugar raised blood lipids and drove heart disease. Peat actually credited Yudkin's experimental observations as the trigger for his own thinking about sugar, but he reinterpreted Yudkin's finding (sugar raises cholesterol) as a protective adaptation through which the body produces more [[progesterone]] under stress. The data was correct; Yudkin's framework around it was the error.

2. **Robert Lustig (2009)**. The viral *Sugar: The Bitter Truth* lecture, framing fructose as a hepatotoxin metabolically analogous to ethanol. In the [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|*Ask the Herb Doctor: Sugar Myths I*, September 2011]] episode Peat addressed Lustig directly. Lustig had used the words "toxic," "poison," and "evil" eighteen times in a ninety-minute talk. Peat's response was that fructose is actually the antidote for ethanol poisoning — a large dose of fructose accelerates alcohol detoxification — and that fructose is "very protective of the liver." Many other toxins are also detoxified in the presence of fructose. The "fructose = ethanol" frame inverts the actual biochemistry.

3. **The HbA1c / advanced glycation end products (AGEs) frame**. The idea that sugar in circulation glycates proteins and accelerates aging. Peat's reading, drawn from Fu et al.'s work on glycated proteins, is that the glycation seen in diabetics is "actually caused more powerfully by polyunsaturated fatty acids" through their oxidative breakdown products, not by sugar itself ([[2011-10-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-ii|*Sugar Myths II*, October 2011]]). The AGE problem is mostly a [[pufa]] problem.

4. **The fructose-malabsorption clinical literature**. IBS patients sometimes show symptoms after fructose loads. Peat's answer was that the problem is the gut, not the fructose; gut dysbiosis from chronic starch and PUFA causes fructose to ferment rather than absorb cleanly. Fix the gut and fructose is tolerated again.

5. **The dental-caries epidemiology**. The most defensible surviving mainstream claim. Peat acknowledged this only narrowly: sticky sugars and constant grazing are bad for teeth; the substance itself is not the issue in the way the caries literature implies. Mineral density, salivary flow, and overall thyroid status matter more.

6. **Cancer-feeds-on-sugar / PET-scan epidemiology**. The observation that tumors avidly consume glucose. Peat agreed with the observation and inverted the prescription. The tumor's glucose appetite is fixed; starving the host of sugar only forces the host's cortisol to break down host tissue to feed the tumor. This is treated more fully at [[cancer]] and [[warburg-effect]].

The cumulative effect of these positions is the modern dietary background assumption that sugar is the universal threat, the white poison whose only debate is how strictly to avoid it. Peat's position is that each of the six lines of argument is either wrong (Yudkin, Lustig, AGE attribution) or wrongly prescribed (cancer, caries, malabsorption). The protective effect of sugar is large and consistent across the corpus, and the harms are nearly all misattributions — what should be blamed on PUFA, on starch, on endotoxin, or on glycogen depletion gets blamed on sugar.

## Why Peat thought sugar was the protective fuel

The protective-fuel argument has four legs, each grounded in mechanism but compressed here so that the unique signal of this page is the *case*, not the chains.

### 1. Sugar oxidation produces more CO2 per oxygen consumed

CO2 is the protective molecule in Peat's framework (see [[carbon-dioxide]] for the full Bohr-effect and calcification mechanism). Sugar oxidation produces roughly fifty percent more CO2 per quantity of oxygen consumed than fat oxidation does. In the [[2010-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-i|*Sugar I* episode]] Peat put it directly: "Oxidation of sugar is metabolically efficient in many ways, including sparing oxygen consumption. It produces more carbon dioxide than oxidizing fat does, and carbon dioxide has many protective functions, including increasing Krebs cycle activity and inhibiting toxic damage to proteins."

The CO2 produced by sugar metabolism stabilizes calcium ions in solution. It deposits into bone as the first mineral substrate (calcium carbonate, before calcium phosphate). It prevents calcium from depositing in arteries, kidneys, and soft tissues. A high-sugar diet without vitamin D produced strong, well-calcified bones in rat experiments Peat cited; a high-starch diet without vitamin D produced rickets. The variable was CO2 production, driven by the higher metabolic rate sugar supports.

### 2. Sugar supports thyroid hormone activation

The liver converts inactive T4 into active T3 only when it has sufficient glucose. From the [[glucose-and-sucrose-for-diabetes|*Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes* newsletter]]: "Triiodothyronine (T3) is mostly produced in the liver, depending on the availability of glucose to the liver, allowing a sensitive adjustment of metabolism to nutrition." When liver glycogen runs out and the body resorts to cortisol to maintain blood sugar, T4-to-T3 conversion drops to almost zero. The thyroid axis is sugar-dependent in a way the mainstream picture entirely misses (full mechanism at [[thyroid]] and [[blood-sugar]]).

Fructose specifically "acts very much like T3" in Peat's reading ([[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|*Sugar Myths I*]]). Both glucose and fructose increase T4-to-T3 conversion; fructose additionally activates cytochrome oxidase — the terminal enzyme of the respiratory chain — directly. This is the catalytic effect that distinguishes fructose from glucose and that the modern "fructose = hepatotoxin" frame fails to account for.

### 3. Sugar turns off the stress hormones

The cortisol-adrenaline cascade that runs from missed meals is treated in detail at [[blood-sugar]] and [[stress]]. The framework point here: providing sugar lowers stress hormones, and stress hormones are catabolic. From the master quote compilation: "It turns off the cortisol which breaks down your tissue protein to make sugar out of it. If you provide the sugar in the diet, you turn off the stress reaction. Sugar restrains the activation of stress hormones like adrenaline and growth hormone, and messengers like serotonin, that mobilize free fatty acids." The chronic stress phenotype — anxious, sleepless, inflamed, slowly losing muscle and bone — is in large part the phenotype of insufficient sugar in the diet.

### 4. Sugar protects against PUFA-driven damage

This is the relationship hardest to see if one accepts the mainstream attribution. The damage commonly blamed on sugar — glycation, insulin resistance, fatty liver, atherosclerosis — is in Peat's reading mostly driven by [[pufa]] and PUFA-derived free fatty acids that block sugar oxidation through the [[randle-cycle]]. Sugar in the diet has a competitive effect against fat mobilization: it suppresses the lipolysis that frees PUFA into circulation. In the [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|*Sugar II* episode]] Peat described how "when we get enough sugar, we inhibit the release of fats from storage." Eating sugar reduces the free-fatty-acid load that is doing the actual damage.

The corollary is uncomfortable: a diet that combines high PUFA with low sugar is the worst-of-both-worlds metabolic state. The PUFA blocks glucose oxidation; the lack of dietary sugar forces lipolysis to free more PUFA into circulation. This is, very nearly, the standard American dietary pattern of the last fifty years, and Peat saw the rise of [[obesity]], [[diabetes]], and [[heart-disease]] as evidence of exactly that pattern's effect.

## The four primary sugars

Peat's "sugar" is not a homogeneous substance. The four primary natural sugars play distinct roles, and the framework only makes sense once their differences are clear.

**Fructose**, the catalytic protector. Bypasses Randle inhibition (it enters glycolysis below the PFK regulatory step that fatty acids block). Activates pyruvate dehydrogenase, the gatekeeper of pyruvate oxidation. Acts like T3 on the liver. Stimulates glycogen replenishment more effectively than glucose. Detoxifies ethanol and protects the liver from endotoxin. Increases whole-body metabolic rate by up to fifty percent in some experiments. Found in ripe fruit, honey, and as half of sucrose. The single most defended substance against the modern critique. Detail at [[fructose]].

**Sucrose**, table sugar, the disaccharide of glucose and fructose. Combines the catalytic effect of fructose with the conventional glucose pathway. Stimulates regeneration of pancreatic beta cells. Peat consistently endorsed white sugar in coffee, milk, or fruit juice as a clean way to deliver the fructose-glucose pair. Detail at [[sucrose]].

**Glucose**, the universal cellular fuel. The bloodstream's standard currency. Preferred by the brain, red blood cells, and the kidneys. Inhibits cortisol when delivered to the liver as glycogen. Without fructose to catalyze its oxidation, glucose loads stimulate insulin more aggressively and are more easily diverted into fat. Pure-glucose foods (pure starch, pure dextrose) are therefore not Peat's preferred form. Detail at [[glucose]] and [[blood-sugar]].

**Lactose**, milk sugar. A disaccharide of glucose and galactose, slowly absorbed because of milk's accompanying protein and fat. Galactose has its own metabolic effects, including potentially supporting cataract formation in galactose-overloaded animals (Peat noted this and recommended adequate B vitamins and thyroid). The slow-release profile of lactose makes it particularly stable. The milk-and-fruit pair is the canonical Peat protein-and-sugar combination. Detail at [[milk]].

## The Randle cycle: why sugar's reputation is a misattribution

The [[randle-cycle]] is the single most important mechanism for understanding the inversion. (Full mechanism at [[randle-cycle]]; one paragraph here for the framework argument.) When free fatty acids are abundant in the bloodstream, they outcompete glucose at the cellular level — the mitochondrion preferentially burns fat, and glucose backs up unburned. The result looks like "high blood sugar" on a lab panel. Mainstream interpretation: too much sugar coming in. Peat's interpretation: not enough sugar being burned, because PUFA-derived free fatty acids are blocking the oxidative pathway. The diabetic "respiratory defect" is a fat-oxidation problem that has been miscategorized as a sugar problem for fifty years. This is the single most important point in the framework, and once it is internalized the rest of the inversions follow naturally.

## Practical sources Peat endorsed

The protective frame is not a license for unlimited refined sweets. Peat's actual food endorsements were specific:

- **Ripe fruit** — oranges, grapes, watermelons, mangoes, papaya, ripe bananas (only when fully ripened past the starch-to-sugar conversion). Avoid serotonin-heavy fruits (pineapple, plums, prunes, kiwi). Detail at [[fruit]].
- **Orange juice** — Peat's most distinctive endorsement. High in potassium (which functions like insulin in moving glucose into cells without an insulin spike), magnesium, vitamin C, and the anti-inflammatory flavonoid naringin. Pulp-free is preferred to avoid bacterial fermentation in the gut. Detail at [[orange-juice]].
- **Honey** — Peat called it "the best refined sugar — bees refined it rather than a factory." High mineral content, antimicrobial properties, and historically stable across thousands of years. Detail at [[honey]].
- **White sugar in coffee or milk** — a clean delivery of sucrose. Peat consistently endorsed this against the cultural taboo. Sucrose has been used to pack open-heart surgical wounds and promotes healing better than antibiotics in some accounts he cited.
- **Milk** — provides lactose, calcium, and protein in a slowly absorbed package. Calcium "powerfully stimulates energy metabolism" and prevents sugar from being stored as fat. Detail at [[milk]].
- **Maple syrup** — acceptable, though Peat warned about dark, concentrated, oxidized sugars (molasses, agave) that may contain allergens or industrial contaminants.

Avoid: high-fructose corn syrup (industrially contaminated, often with heavy metals in older analyses); artificial sweeteners (they trigger insulin secretion without providing fuel, increasing cortisol and confusing metabolic signaling); fermented "health" foods high in lactic acid (kefir, sauerkraut at high intake) — Peat developed migraines after a daily kefir pint and traced it to lactic-acid load on the liver.

## The mainstream's strongest cases — handled one at a time

The protective-fuel framework has to address mainstream nutrition's strongest empirical claims directly. Here are the four hardest cases.

### NAFLD (non-alcoholic fatty liver disease)

Mainstream: high-fructose intake drives hepatic de novo lipogenesis and is the principal dietary cause of NAFLD. Peat's reading: NAFLD is principally a PUFA storage and oxidation pathology, with [[endotoxin]] from gut dysbiosis as the second driver. Fructose, in the dietary context Peat recommends (with adequate protein, micronutrients, and low PUFA), is "the best stimulant for the liver to store glycogen" rather than to make fat. In NAFLD studies that show fructose-driven lipogenesis, the experimental diets are typically high in PUFA, low in micronutrients, and delivered as isolated fructose loads without the accompanying protein, calcium, and saturated fat of whole-food sources. The "fructose is toxic to the liver" claim is sensitive to dietary context in a way that the standard framing obscures.

### Fructose malabsorption and IBS

Mainstream: a substantial fraction of IBS patients show breath-test evidence of fructose malabsorption, with symptoms on fructose loading. Peat's reading: the gut is the problem, not the fructose. Chronic [[pufa]] suppresses gut motility and immune competence; starch feeds the bacterial fermentation that displaces normal absorption; the small intestine becomes overgrown and the fructose ferments instead of absorbing. The clinical implication is to fix the gut (carrot salad, occasional broad-spectrum digestive support, low-PUFA diet) rather than to permanently avoid fructose. Empirically, many people who fail fructose tolerance tests on a standard diet pass them again after months of gut restoration.

### Dental caries

Mainstream: dietary sugar is the principal driver of cavities through *Streptococcus mutans* metabolism on tooth surfaces. This is the most defensible mainstream claim against Peat's framework, and he engaged with it only narrowly. His response amounts to: (a) constant grazing and sticky sugar films are bad; large discrete servings followed by mouth rinsing are not the same exposure; (b) thyroid status, mineral density, and saliva flow do most of the work that the caries literature attributes to sugar; (c) hypothyroid populations have terrible teeth on any diet. The framework does not claim sugar is good for teeth in the way it claims sugar is good for metabolism; it claims the dental risk is local, modulated by overall metabolic health, and not a reason to restrict sugar systemically.

### Insulin resistance

Mainstream: chronic high-sugar intake drives insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and eventually type 2 diabetes. Peat's reading: insulin resistance is driven by the chronic presence of free fatty acids in the bloodstream — a PUFA and stress phenotype — not by dietary sugar. Indeed, the historical record of high-dose-sugar diabetes treatment from the late nineteenth century is one Peat returned to repeatedly. In the [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|*Sugar II* episode]] he described the work of two doctors in France and England who in the 1880s recognized terminal diabetes patients were dying of starvation: "What they did was let them eat all the sugar they wanted — approximately 11 or 12 ounces, or about 350 grams a day — and instead of dying over a period of several days, the conversion of protein to sugar decreased and finally stopped and they recovered." The mechanism, in retrospect, is that exogenous sugar suppresses cortisol-driven gluconeogenesis from host tissue. Sugar feeds the pancreas's regenerative capacity — sucrose stimulates beta-cell regeneration in vitro — while PUFA kills beta cells.

These are not glib dismissals. Each mainstream position has empirical evidence behind it. Peat's framework is that the evidence is real but the causal attribution is wrong: the proximate harms in each case are downstream of PUFA, of glycogen depletion, of gut dysbiosis, or of cortisol, and the cure is to fix those upstream variables rather than to restrict the sugar that protects against them.

## The "sugar feeds cancer" inversion

The most psychologically loaded mainstream claim is that sugar feeds cancer. Peat agreed with the observation — tumors are avid consumers of glucose, as the [[warburg-effect]] demonstrates — and inverted the prescription. From the [[2011-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-myths-i|*Sugar Myths I*]] episode: "If you look at the blood sugar in an animal or person carrying a tumor, their blood sugar generally is at least normal. Even if they don't eat any sugar, they'll still keep their blood sugar at about the same level. The cachexia, or tissue wasting, that is the worst feature of cancer is usually produced by the stress hormones converting your protein and fat tissues into food for the cancer."

The tumor will get its glucose either way. Restricting dietary sugar simply forces the host's cortisol to break down host tissue to supply it. Peat further cited research where high concentrations of glucose were infused directly into tumor-feeding arteries: the tumor was killed without harm to surrounding tissue, because the tumor's unlimited appetite caused it to consume itself. This is treated more fully at [[cancer]] and at the [[warburg-effect]].

## The historical record

The sugar-as-medicine record is deeper than most contemporary nutrition writing acknowledges. Honey was a wound dressing across Mediterranean and Egyptian medicine for thousands of years. White sucrose was used in open-heart surgery to pack wounds when antibiotics were unavailable, with better scarring outcomes than antibiotics produced. Fructose was a diabetes treatment in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, until the discovery of insulin in 1922 made it commercially obsolete. The carbohydrate diet that William Brown described in 1936 — fat-free, high-sugar — cured his lifelong migraines permanently. The Zamenhof chicken-brain experiments (glucose injection through the eggshell at the brain-growth pause point) demonstrated direct glucose-dependence of late-fetal brain development. The "gestational diabetes" phenomenon that contemporary obstetrics flags as pathology Peat read as the body's correct response: pregnant women raise blood sugar to feed the fetus's developing brain, and women whose doctors restrict that elevation may produce smaller-brained children.

None of this proves Peat's framework on its own. The cumulative weight of it — that the historical and experimental record of sugar as medicine is substantial and was forgotten rather than refuted — is what the framework argues from.

## How sugar should be eaten

The protective frame is contextual. Peat never endorsed eating refined sugar in isolation. The practical pattern, summarized from across the corpus:

1. Sugar with protein and saturated fat together. Milk and fruit; orange juice with cheese; coffee with cream and sugar; ice cream as a meal. The protein moderates the insulin response; the saturated fat slows absorption and works with fructose to stimulate pyruvate dehydrogenase.
2. Distributed across the day, including at night. Liver glycogen depletes during sleep; the nocturnal cortisol-and-free-fatty-acid surge that wakes many people at 3 a.m. is well treated by a small sugar-and-salt snack at bedtime. A glass of warm milk with two or three tablespoons of sugar is a Peat staple.
3. Paired with salt and calcium. The sugar-salt-calcium triad supports metabolic rate independently. From the [[2010-10-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-sugar-ii|*Sugar II*]] episode: "Even if you're deficient in thyroid hormone, you can keep the activity going to a great extent with sucrose, salt, and calcium."
4. Sourced from fruit and milk when possible, supplemented with honey or refined sugar as needed. Fruit provides the potassium that allows sugar to be handled with less insulin secretion. Milk provides the calcium and slow-release lactose.
5. Avoided when concentrated, oxidized, or contaminated. Dark sugars, agave, high-fructose corn syrup, and industrial fruit-juice concentrates with peel-residue contaminants are all to be avoided. Pure crystalline white sugar — surprising as it is to the modern ear — is among the cleanest delivery forms.

The protocol page [[anti-stress-diet]] gives the action-layer expression of this framework.

## Counter-arguments

The protective-fuel framework remains a minority position in contemporary nutrition science, and the mainstream pushback is real. The strongest counter-arguments:

1. **The fructose-NAFLD literature is large and growing.** Lustig and colleagues, and a substantial body of metabolic-ward studies, show measurable hepatic de novo lipogenesis on isolated fructose loads. Peat's response (that this is context-dependent and PUFA-mediated) requires more direct experimental evidence than the corpus currently provides. The fructose-NAFLD critique cannot be fully dismissed; it can only be reframed.

2. **Dental caries epidemiology is highly consistent across populations and time periods.** Even allowing for the role of thyroid status and mineral density, the population-level correlation between sugar availability and caries rate is among the most robust findings in nutrition epidemiology. Peat's narrow engagement with the dental literature is the weakest point in the framework.

3. **The "small amounts of fructose tolerated, larger amounts not" pattern in fructose-malabsorption testing is reproducible.** Peat's gut-restoration answer may be correct in principle, but the time scales required (months to years of low-PUFA diet) are difficult for individuals to execute.

4. **Modern obesity and type 2 diabetes incidence rose alongside sugar consumption.** The temporal correlation is real even if Peat is correct that PUFA was the causal driver. The counter-argument is that the visible variable was sugar, and visible variables can carry true causal weight.

5. **No published RCT comparing a high-sugar, low-PUFA diet against the standard American diet over multi-year metabolic endpoints exists.** The framework is supported by mechanism, by historical case series, by Peat's clinical observation, and by the substantial animal literature on PUFA — but the gold-standard human evidence Peat's framework would predict has not been run.

6. **The fructose-uric-acid pathway** drives the gout and metabolic-syndrome critique that Lustig advances. Peat acknowledged elevated uric acid as a potential antioxidant adaptation rather than a disease state, but the cardiovascular epidemiology of uric acid remains contested.

7. **Insulin resistance can be measured directly and does worsen on high-sugar loads in many subjects in the short term.** Peat's framework predicts that the relationship reverses on a low-PUFA diet over months; demonstrating that prediction requires longer trials than have been run.

8. **The "sugar lowers cortisol" claim oversimplifies.** Acute sugar can lower cortisol; chronic high-sugar intake in some metabolic states can produce its own dysregulation. The framework is more nuanced than the slogan suggests.

A reader convinced by Peat's framework will note that several of these counter-arguments amount to "we have not yet run the experiment that would distinguish the frameworks." A reader skeptical of Peat's framework will note that this is precisely the position any heterodox science occupies, and that until the experiments are run, the standard advice has the weight of established practice behind it. Both readings are reasonable.

## Data gaps

What Peat did not address, or addressed only obliquely:

- **Pediatric sugar intake.** Almost nothing in the corpus addresses sugar specifically for children — the framework points toward fruit, milk, and honey rather than restriction, but the dosing and developmental considerations are not detailed.
- **Pregnancy beyond the gestational-diabetes inversion.** The Zamenhof brain-growth point is made repeatedly, but the practical recommendations for sugar intake across the trimesters are thin.
- **Athletic performance carbohydrate timing.** Peat addressed exercise broadly (he favored brief concentric exercise, distrusted endurance training) but did not engage the carbohydrate-periodization literature that competitive endurance athletes work from.
- **Fructose tolerance variation.** Peat's framework treats fructose as universally protective when delivered with the right matrix; the genetic and microbiome variation in fructose handling is not directly engaged.
- **Sucrose vs. glucose+fructose mixtures.** Peat consistently endorsed sucrose, but did not detail whether crystallized sucrose, fructose+glucose solutions, and HFCS (when uncontaminated) are bioequivalent.
- **Sugar in the context of established type 2 diabetes with high medication burden.** The 19th-century case-series record Peat cited is the foundational evidence, but the framework's translation to contemporary diabetics on metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 agonists is not detailed in the corpus.
- **The microbiome era.** Most of Peat's writing on gut function predates the modern microbiome literature. The framework's intersection with detailed bacterial-population science remains to be developed.
- **Glucose monitoring in healthy populations.** Continuous-glucose-monitor data was not available during the bulk of Peat's writing; the framework's response to "post-prandial spikes in healthy people" is implicit rather than direct.
