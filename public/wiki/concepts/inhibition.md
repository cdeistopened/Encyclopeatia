# Inhibition

Inhibition has a bad name in America. The word arrives through popular Freudianism — libido must (or must not) be inhibited; the inhibited person is the timid one — and through the synaptic neurochemistry of the SSRI era, where inhibition is what specialized neurons do at named receptors, a microscopic event in a wiring diagram. Both readings miss what Peat, following Sechenov, Vedensky, Pavlov, and the Soviet physiological tradition, took the word to mean. Inhibition in that tradition is a *general organismic state* — a property any cell can enter — and it is not the absence of activity but its complement: an active, energy-consuming, structurally protective phase during which the cell restores its reserves, refuses irrelevant stimuli, and prepares for the next demand. Chapter 10 of [[book-mind-and-tissue|*Mind and Tissue*]] is the most concentrated statement in Peat's corpus, running forward through [[insomnia-and-hyperactivity|*Insomnia & Hyperactivity*]] (1994), [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|*Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities*]] (2009), the *Sleep* newsletter, and his 1975 [[a-biophysical-approach-to-altered-consciousness|*A Biophysical Approach to Altered Consciousness*]]. The frame is one of Peat's most underused; it explains, in one move, why coffee calms hyperactive children, why insanity may be a protective process the body undertakes when overwhelmed, why a hot bath and a bedtime snack can interrupt a migraine, and why "selective inhibition" — the brain's refusal of most of what it could attend to — is what *attention* actually is.

## Sechenov and Vedensky: The Founders of Inhibition Physiology

The Soviet line begins with Ivan Sechenov, "the father of Russian physiology," who made the first observation of nerve-cell inhibition: a frog's spinal reflexes, such as kicking, could be *suppressed* by intense stimulation of the sensory nerves. The reflex did not weaken because the muscle tired; it failed because something upstream had been turned off. Sechenov's frog was the first demonstration that the nervous system contains an active braking mechanism — not an excitation that runs down, but an inhibition that can be summoned.

Nikolai Vedensky took the observation deeper. He found that an individual nerve became inhibited when stimulation was too intense or too frequent, *and that this inhibition was not fatigue*. The critical test: the inhibited nerve could begin functioning again immediately under weaker stimulation. A fatigued tissue cannot be wakened by a softer touch; it needs rest, fuel, time. An inhibited nerve was already prepared, holding itself out of the cycle of excitation, awaiting an appropriate signal. The discovery split inhibition off from exhaustion as a categorically different state.

The Vedensky picture lets a single observation do enormous work. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|*Mind and Tissue*]]: "a given stimulation can be excitatory or inhibitory, depending on the functional state of the nerve cells; a given substance can be either excitatory or inhibitory, depending on its concentration; the physical state of cell proteins can be used to determine the state of the cell." The corollary becomes the substrate of Peat's whole framework: there is no isolated "inhibitory transmitter" doing inhibition at a fixed synapse. There is a cell in a particular metabolic-structural state, and that state is what inhibition *is*.

## Pavlov's Protective Inhibition: Psychosis as the Brain's Self-Rescue

Pavlov extended the picture from single nerves to whole organisms. Particular behaviors could be inhibited; the entire nervous system could go into an inhibited state. Most importantly: inhibition is not an inactive state. The inhibited cell is cut off from stimuli and undergoes a process of restoration. During prior excitation, "there is a build-up of ammonia, lactic acid, and inorganic phosphate within and around the nerve cell, as well as a depletion of energy reserve and even a change in the condition of the cell proteins themselves." Inhibition is the cell turning inward to clear those metabolic products and rebuild its substrate. It is housekeeping, not silence.

Pavlov's most provocative extension — and the one that gives Peat's framework its distinctive clinical edge — was the proposal that *psychosis itself* may be a form of protective inhibition. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|*Mind and Tissue*]]: "Pavlov suggested that psychosis may be a state in which the nervous system goes into partial inhibition to restore itself, and prevent complete exhaustion and death." Insanity, on this reading, is not a malfunction to be punished out of the patient. It is the nervous system's last-ditch attempt to disconnect from intolerable stimulation while there is still enough metabolic capacity left to rebuild. The schizophrenic withdrawal, the catatonic stillness, the depressive valley — read as protective inhibition, these are not the disease but the body's response to it.

R. D. Laing arrived at the same idea independently in England and the United States — "that insanity is potentially a recuperative process, if we don't punish patients with our treatments." The therapeutic implication is direct. Help the patient regain his nervous "energy potential" through nutrition, rest, warmth, [[thyroid]], and gentle stimulation, and the protective inhibition does its work and resolves. Force the system out of inhibition with stimulants, ECT, or aggressive antipsychotics, and the recuperative window closes before the cells have rebuilt their reserves. The right response to a brain in protective inhibition is to feed it, warm it, darken its room enough to let it sleep, and *wait*.

Sleep itself, on the Pavlovian reading, is the most general form of protective inhibition — the convergence with the [[sleep]] entry. Sleep is not the brain "shutting down" any more than the inhibited nerve is fatigued; it is the whole organism entering the high-readiness restorative mode single cells enter under Vedensky's weak stimulation. *Extending* sleep cuts in half the time needed to recover reflex function after spinal damage (Asratyan and Simonov). Soporifics — anesthesia, electrosleep, [[progesterone]], even mild [[caffeine]] in the right dose — all participate in the same protective shift, and can accelerate recovery from any condition where the underlying problem is energetic depletion.

## Two Zones of Inhibition: The High-Energy Resting State

Peat's most original move on the Soviet inheritance was to formalize the *two-zones* picture. Between full death and full activity, inhibition is not a single state but two, sitting on *opposite sides* of activity rather than on a single continuum from active to inhibited. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|*Mind and Tissue*]]:

> We can think of two zones of inhibition, separated by a state of activity: The "lower" zone somewhat resembles lower forms of life, and borders on death (the "parabiotic" state), while the higher zone of inhibition is a highly structured state with higher energy reserves even than the active state — it is a state of "high readiness."

The lower zone is the inhibition of exhaustion — stimulation gone on too long, the cell driven into a partly-denatured, ammonia-loaded, ATP-depleted state. It looks like rest, but it is collapse. Hibernation is its species-level analog; coma, advanced dementia, the torpor following a 36-hour shift, the catatonic floor of a schizophrenic crisis — all are parabiotic. Recovery is slow because the structural substrate has been damaged.

The upper zone is something else entirely: a *more* structured, *higher*-energy state than wakeful activity itself. This is what Vedensky-style weak stimulation produces — the cell that returns to function immediately when softly touched. Peat ties it directly to the [[structured-water]] picture: "The various states of the nerve cell can be appropriately thought of as different degrees of 'tightness' of the protein gel structure of the cell substance. Mild mechanical or chemical stimulation (e.g., small doses of caffeine) will inhibit the nerve tissue, and in doing so will make the cells impermeable to penetration by foreign substances such as dyes, and will improve their resistance to oxygen deprivation." The blood-brain barrier is the upper-zone tightness of cortical neurons made visible: cells exclude what they don't need because their protein-water gel is in the structured, high-energy phase. Lower-zone cells admit dyes, ions, and foreign molecules indiscriminately. Two inhibitions, two protein states, two completely different prognoses.

The clinical signature of the upper zone — pure deep visual blackness at bedtime, vivid hypnagogic imagery, sleep within minutes, rested mornings — is what Peat's readers learn to recognize. The lower zone is the buzzing-confusion half-sleep of someone too long sleep-deprived, the fogged visual field, the morning hangover-grogginess. "If we go too long without sleep, this clarity is replaced by a buzzing confusion and an unsatisfying kind of half-sleep: We have apparently built up the low-energy condition in our nerve cells to the point that our primary inhibition is defective."

The relationship to [[bioenergetics]] and [[consciousness]] is direct. Adequate [[thyroid]], abundant [[glucose]] oxidation, structured [[carbon-dioxide]]-rich cytoplasm, low [[free-fatty-acids]] — this is the state from which upper-zone inhibition is accessible. A hypothyroid, PUFA-loaded, glycogen-depleted, [[serotonin]]-elevated brain can only enter the lower zone. The metabolic chain that supports waking energy is the same chain that supports restorative inhibition, which is why "energy and inhibition" is one phrase in Peat, not two.

## Selective Inhibition as the Substrate of Attention

The framework's most unexpected payoff is its account of *attention*. Mainstream cognitive neuroscience treats attention as the brain's *selection* and *amplification* of relevant stimuli — a spotlight, a workspace, a top-down enhancement signal. Peat, following Pavlov and Luria, inverts the picture. What we call attention is mostly a pattern of *inhibitions* — the brain's refusal to respond to the infinite irrelevant signals it could attend to. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|*Mind and Tissue*]]:

> Just as the organism uses inhibition to protect itself from excessively strong stimuli which would destroy it, it can use inhibition to avoid the endless fatigue of being alert to irrelevant and "small" stimuli… By controlling our perceptions, selective inhibition is obviously participating in the control of our behavior. Just as concentration is necessary for clear perceptions, patterned inhibition is necessary for controlled behavior.

Hyperactivity and attention deficit — the syndrome Peat lists alongside schizophrenia, depression, and the manic-depressive family in [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|*Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities*]] — are, in this reading, failures of selective inhibition. The cortex cannot disengage from distracting stimuli. Peat quotes Luria on the frontal-lobe-damaged dog: it "cannot perform tasks involving delayed responses under ordinary conditions, but can do so provided that irrelevant, distracting stimuli are removed (if the animal is kept in total darkness, if tranquilizers are administered, and so on)." The dog has not lost memory or motor capacity; it has lost the inhibitory function that lets the frontal lobes hold a plan against the immediate sensory field. See [[adhd]] for the condition-level treatment.

The clinical correlate that gives the framework its punch: *coffee, in moderate doses, can produce attention in hyperactive children*. The orthodox label is "paradoxical." There is nothing paradoxical about it once inhibition has been correctly understood. "The discovery that coffee (or amphetamines) will control hyperactivity in children seems to relate to caffeine's ability to produce inhibition, as well as excitation — the effect depends both on the dose and the prior state of the tissue." A small dose of caffeine in a glucose-depleted, [[adrenaline]]-elevated child supplies the weak stimulation Vedensky showed pushes nerves into the upper zone. The frontal lobes acquire enough metabolic substrate to hold the inhibitory pattern selective attention requires. The stimulant's calming effect is direct evidence for the Soviet model: the dose moves the cells from the lower zone, through activity, into the upper zone of high-readiness inhibition.

The same logic explains why small caffeine can help some cases of [[schizophrenia]], why coffee before bed can accelerate sleep onset in adrenaline-driven insomnia, and why stimulant withdrawal in chronic users produces not sleep but its opposite. The effect is not on a "wake-promoting" axis; it is on the structural-energetic state of the tissue. See [[caffeine]] for the substance-level treatment.

## Practical Protocols

The framework licenses a particular set of interventions — more "Russian sanatorium" than "American clinic." They share the property of supplying *weak stimulation* or *active rest* rather than pushing the system harder.

**Sleep extension and soporifics.** The Russian finding that extending sleep cuts spinal-injury reflex recovery time in half generalizes: sleep extension accelerates recovery from any condition where the underlying deficit is metabolic-energetic depletion. In overload, infectious convalescence, post-surgical recovery, or psychiatric crisis, the right move is to lengthen sleep as much as the patient can manage and support it with the chain that makes restorative sleep possible — warmth, [[salt]], [[sugar]], [[thyroid]], the carbohydrate-and-salt bedtime snack, [[gelatin]] for [[glycine]], [[progesterone]] for upper-zone GABA reinforcement. The "do something" instinct is exactly wrong; the recuperative window depends on letting protective inhibition complete its work. Full framework at [[sleep]].

**Selective caffeine in ADHD.** A small dose of [[coffee]] in the morning, with milk and sugar, never on an empty stomach, can produce the same calming effect stimulant medications produce — through the same mechanism — when the rest of the metabolic substrate is in place. A glycogen-depleted, hypothyroid, PUFA-loaded child tips into excitatory pre-anxiety; a metabolically supported child moves into the upper zone. First-line is the chain (salty-sugary meals, protein, thyroid, sleep, warmth, sunlight, low PUFA); caffeine becomes the high-leverage selective-inhibition promoter on top. See [[adhd]] for the condition protocol.

**Anesthetic and benzodiazepine bridges in psychiatric crisis.** The Pavlov-Laing reading licenses *deepening and supporting* protective inhibition rather than fighting it. *Mind and Tissue* notes the Russian use of "electrosleep" — low-amplitude pulses holding patients in anesthesia for therapeutic durations, including the 17-year-old dog on six months of electrosleep who emerged at 20 no longer senile. In modern Western practice, [[progesterone]] is preferable to a [[benzodiazepines|benzodiazepine]] when it can do the work, since it produces the high-energy resting state through GABA reinforcement without receptor-downregulation and withdrawal. Where progesterone is insufficient, a *short* benzodiazepine bridge — days or a few weeks — can stabilize while thyroid, sleep, and nutrition corrections take effect. The error is treating the bridge as the treatment.

**CO2 and bag-breathing.** [[Carbon-dioxide|CO2]] is one of the most direct inhibition-promoters available without prescription. [[Bag-breathing]], breath-holding, time at altitude, and nose-only breathing all raise arterial CO2, which binds to amine groups on neuronal proteins and supports the structured-cytoplasm state that *is* the upper zone (mechanism at [[consciousness]]). The hyperventilating panic-attack sufferer is in low-zone over-excitation because CO2 has been blown off; the paper bag restores it and the inhibitory state returns within breaths. For chronic over-excitation — anxiety, insomnia, racing thoughts — regular bag-breathing is one of the few non-pharmacological tools that reliably produces the upper-zone state.

## Counter-arguments

The dominant contemporary picture of inhibition is *synaptic* and *transmitter-specific*. GABA is the principal inhibitory transmitter; glycine handles spinal cord inhibition; benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and propofol all act on GABA-A and adjacent sites; the system is mapped at the molecular and circuit levels. This is the framework the SSRI era retained when it pivoted attention from inhibition to excitatory transmitters (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine). The Soviet "general organismic inhibition" Peat used does not appear in mainstream neuroscience textbooks; the discipline has decided the question by ignoring it. The framework's response is not to deny the synaptic GABA picture but to read it as a partial, downstream description of a cellular-structural state. A cell in the upper zone responds to GABA differently than a cell in the lower zone; the receptor pharmacology averages across both. The clinical observation that benzodiazepine tolerance and withdrawal exist, that the drugs lose efficacy over years, and that long-course patients develop worsening anxiety beneath the medication — these are predicted by the framework (synthetic potentiation does not rebuild the metabolic substrate) and unexplained by the synaptic model.

The terminology has been captured in the direction Western pharmacology found profitable. The "GABA system" is now spoken of as if it were the whole of inhibition; *protective inhibition*, *parabiotic state*, *high-energy resting state*, *upper zone*, *selective inhibition* — Peat's vocabulary — have no purchase in literature published after roughly 1980, though the underlying Pavlovian work was never refuted.

There are also speculative positions inside the framework. The Pavlov-Laing reading of psychosis as protective inhibition lines up with several clinical observations (spontaneous remission of acute psychosis, deterioration from aggressive early antipsychotic dosing) but is not the consensus reading and the modern serotonin-dopamine accounts have empirical support the framework does not fully engage. The two-zone picture itself is inferred from cellular staining, electrical, and behavioral data rather than directly measured. The framework's strongest case is the attention disorders, where the "paradoxical" calming effect of stimulants is unexplained by the orthodox model and falls naturally out of the inhibition picture. Its weakest case is acute psychiatric medicine, where the alternative — protective inhibition plus metabolic support, minimal intervention — is genuinely risky for active suicidality, dangerous psychotic delusions, or substance-driven crises. Reading the framework as licensing under-treatment of acute crisis is a mistake the framework itself does not make.

## Data gaps

The two-zone picture has not been operationalized in a way modern neurophysiology can test. The prediction — that the same cell in the upper zone differs in protein-water phase, ion selectivity, membrane permeability, and structured-water content from the same cell in the lower zone — is measurable with high-field NMR, dye-exclusion assays, and modern cell-impedance techniques. The studies have not been done.

The selective-inhibition account of attention has not been distinguished from the standard top-down enhancement account in a clean experimental design. The prediction is that ADHD frontal cortices show specifically inhibitory deficits (failure to suppress task-irrelevant signals) rather than amplification deficits. The literature is divided. A study varying stimulant *type* — Vedensky-style weak stimulation (small-dose caffeine) versus strong stimulation (amphetamine) — against time-course of attention restoration would discriminate the frameworks.

The Pavlov-Laing reading is empirically open. Modern psychiatry has not done the comparison: acute psychotic patients with metabolic support and minimal pharmacology versus matched controls on standard antipsychotic protocols, scored at six months and two years. The Soteria-house tradition approximated this in 1970s American psychiatry with reportedly equivalent or better outcomes, but the studies were small, methodologically contested, and not replicated.

The caffeine-as-selective-inhibition-promoter claim in ADHD has not been formally tested against methylphenidate. The framework predicts small-dose caffeine (50–100 mg, with food, in a metabolically supported child) produces equivalent attention benefit in a substantial fraction. Clinical lore supports it; formal evidence does not exist.

The framework's largest unverified prediction is that chronic-fatigue, post-viral, long-COVID, and adrenal-exhaustion populations are stuck in lower-zone parabiotic inhibition, with the route out through weak stimulation rather than rest *or* exertion. The clinical observation that these patients deteriorate on graded exercise and stagnate on prolonged rest is consistent; the specific weak-stimulation protocol has not been worked out.
