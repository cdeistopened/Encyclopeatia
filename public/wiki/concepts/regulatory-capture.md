# Regulatory Capture

Ray Peat had a standing explanation for why the orthodox literature on polyunsaturated fats, soy infant formula, iron supplementation, fluoridation, electroconvulsive therapy, estrogen replacement, and the bisphosphonate prescription cascade was, in his reading, consistently and predictably wrong. It was not that the people in charge were stupid. It was not that they were corrupt in any straightforward sense. It was that the institutions that were supposed to evaluate those products had been captured, slowly and structurally, by the industries that profited from them. "Political scientists have recognized the process in which big corporations 'capture' the governmental agencies that were created to regulate them," he wrote in his 2007 newsletter [[vitamin-e-estrogen-antagonist-energy-promoter-and-anti-inflammatory|*Vitamin E*]]. "The editorial boards of professional journals can be captured even more cheaply than the agencies of government, and their influence can be even more valuable to industry. If science impinges upon the plans of an industry, it can be managed into compliance, when the industry controls the journals and the agencies that fund research." This is the political-economy axis of Peat's work — the load-bearing assumption underneath dozens of his nutritional and endocrine positions — and it is the explanatory frame that turns the disparate critiques of PUFA, fluoride, iron, and estrogen into a single coherent thesis. It is not "they are evil." It is "they are funded by the industry whose products they regulate, and that is enough to explain the result."

## The Pattern Peat Names

The 48 newspaper letters Peat published in the *Eugene Register-Guard* and the *Oregon Daily Emerald* between 1967 and 1991 are the densest single source for the capture frame, because the letters are short and the structure is forced into the open. The same pattern recurs across roughly thirty of the forty-seven: a regulatory body, a scientific authority, or a media outlet takes a position that happens to align with the financial interest of the industry it nominally checks; the actual scientific record turns out to be either thin, contradictory, or actively against the official position; and the inconvenient researchers, when they exist, have been quietly written out of the story.

The opening move is almost always epistemic rather than moral. In [[find-the-facts]] (June 1977), responding to local fluoridation proponents who would not publicly debate the evidence, Peat does not accuse them of bad faith. He asks them to "come right out and cite the particular scientific publications which support their position." When no such citations are forthcoming, the reasonable inference is not that the proponents are dishonest but that the institutional apparatus has been operating on professional authority rather than on the underlying primary literature for so long that the underlying literature is no longer available to it. In [[health-frauds]] (November 1985), critiquing the Oregon chapter of the National Council Against Health Fraud, Peat does not call its founders charlatans. He observes that the targets they have selected — algae dealers, fringe vitamin sellers — are "freakish and probably trivial," while the much larger health frauds in the legal drug supply, the seed oil industry, and the iron-supplementation industry are not on the list, "suggesting that they might be acting as a front for the drug industry." The frame is structural: a fraud-detection organization that systematically ignores the largest frauds is doing the work of the largest frauds, regardless of the intent of its members.

The institutions Peat returns to are the same ones, year after year: the FDA, the AMA, the major medical journals, the state agricultural universities, the National Institutes of Health, the regulatory committees of professional societies, and the local newspaper editorial boards that recycle their press releases. In [[dangerous-therapy]] (May 1985), reviewing the FDA's failure to remove electroshock devices from the market despite having classified them in its highest risk category, Peat puts the diagnosis explicitly: "The regulatory agencies are now acting flagrantly for the benefit of the big economic interests, and against the interests of the consumers. It would be nice if we could return to the rule of law." In [[bad-partnership]] (March 1985), commenting on a state-funded promotion of agricultural pesticides, he generalizes: "When professors, who are agents of the state, accept any research funds from private industry, and then participate in any government decision which affects the profits of that industry, it certainly doesn't look good." The mechanism is funding-stream entanglement; the effect is the indistinguishability of state expertise from industry advocacy; the result is that the citizen who tries to "follow the science" ends up following the financial interest of the industry without realizing it.

The newspaper letters were written in Peat's twenties and thirties, before the bioenergetic framework consolidated. The intellectual continuity is striking. Forty years later, in the [[where-peat-evolved|where Peat evolved]] reconstruction of his position-shifts, the meta-position on regulatory capture is one of the few places where his framing did not move at all. "From the 1973 fluoride letters through the late critiques of pharmaceutical marketing, Peat's position on the corruption of medical science, the capture of regulatory agencies, and the suppression of inconvenient research is stable. Specific cases changed; the meta-position didn't." The newsletters of the 2000s and 2010s say the same thing the 1973 letters said, in more elaborated form, with a larger corpus of cases.

## Five Canonical Cases

### Vegetable oils, Procter & Gamble, and the American Heart Association

The seed-oil case is the load-bearing example because it is the place where Peat's nutritional framework and his political economy meet most cleanly. The relevant industrial history is laid out in the dedicated [[seed-oils]] and [[pufa]] entries; the capture story is what makes the history readable as a single sequence rather than a coincidence.

Cottonseed oil was a byproduct of the cotton industry, used through the nineteenth century as lamp fuel, soap stock, and machine lubrication. When petroleum chemistry took over the industrial-lubricant market in the early twentieth century, the cottonseed crop became a stranded asset. Procter & Gamble's hydrogenation of cottonseed oil into Crisco, marketed from 1911 as a butter substitute, was the first large-volume redirection of an industrial oil into the food supply. The story of how a substance recently sold as machinery lubricant was rebranded as a heart-protective nutrient is the subject of the [[seed-oils]] page. The capture story is the question of who allowed it.

The answer Peat returned to repeatedly was the American Heart Association and the medical journals that took its positions as authoritative. The AHA's early funding came in large measure from Procter & Gamble — the 1948 Procter & Gamble donation that put the AHA into national prominence is on the public record — and the lipid hypothesis that the AHA promoted from the late 1950s onward conveniently identified the saturated animal fats of butter, lard, and tallow as the cause of heart disease and the polyunsaturated seed oils of Crisco, margarine, and corn oil as the protective alternative. In the [[vitamin-e-estrogen-antagonist-energy-promoter-and-anti-inflammatory|2007 *Vitamin E* newsletter]] Peat described the result: "The meat industry had found that the polyunsaturated oils were valuable in animal feed, since they suppressed metabolism and made it cheaper to fatten the animals, and these antithyroid oils were next marketed as 'heart protective' human foods, though by suppressing the thyroid and destroying vitamin E, they actually contributed to both heart disease and cancer." The substance that the agricultural literature had identified as a thyroid suppressant valuable for fattening livestock cheaply was repackaged as a human nutrient on the strength of a research program whose principal funder was the company that needed the substance to be a human nutrient.

The counter-tradition existed. Mary Enig, the biochemist whose analytical work on the fatty-acid composition of American foods produced the data on which the modern reevaluation of saturated fat rests, was working through the 1970s and 1980s in what amounted to an academic underground; her papers on the trans-fat contamination of hydrogenated vegetable oils were rejected by the major nutrition journals for over a decade. The Anti-Coronary Club trial of 1957, in which Dr. Norman Jolliffe placed participants on a prudent diet of corn oil, margarine, fish, chicken, and cold cereal and saw eight heart-disease deaths in the prudent-diet group versus zero in the meat-three-times-a-day controls — with Jolliffe himself dying of vascular thrombosis in 1961 — was not absorbed into the institutional memory. The framework that would have made these findings legible to the mainstream nutrition literature was inaccessible because the institutional apparatus had been calibrated, over decades, to a different framework that better served the financial interests of its principal funders. See the [[seed-oils]] and [[pufa]] entries for the substantive mechanism; the point here is that the suppression of the counter-evidence was not a single act of fraud but the systemic equilibrium of a funding structure that selected for one set of conclusions over another.

### Soy infant formula and the redirection of an agricultural surplus

The soy infant formula case follows the same structural arc with a different commodity. The 1970s pediatric pivot toward soy formula as a safe alternative to cow's milk was, in Peat's reading, the redirection of an enormous agricultural surplus — soybeans grown to feed livestock and produce industrial oils — into a medical product whose safety case was thin. In [[other-factors]] (November 1980), commenting on declining SAT scores in the wake of widespread soy-formula and birth-control-pill exposure during the 1960s and 1970s, Peat noted dryly: "Last year the government advised against the use of soy in baby 'formulas,' shortly after it was discovered that such vegetable oils can cause brain damage." The discovery had been available in the animal literature for decades; the policy response came only after the surplus had already been monetized.

The mechanism — soy's isoflavone load functioning as an environmental estrogen, its phytate load locking up zinc and iron, its goitrogen content suppressing thyroid in developing infants — is treated in detail in the [[soy]] entry. The capture component is the institutional silence. Pediatric societies that would have raised an alarm at a synthetic estrogen prescribed for infants did not raise the alarm at a phytoestrogen-rich plant protein that achieved the same endocrine effect through a different molecular route, because the regulatory category for "natural foods" had been carved out for political rather than physiological reasons. The agricultural lobby that needed a market for surplus soy meal and the pediatric establishment that needed a low-cost alternative to scarce or expensive infant formulas converged on a recommendation that the underlying endocrinology did not support. The institutional position lagged the physiological evidence by several decades, and the lag is what regulatory capture predicts.

### Fluoridation and the aluminum smelters

The fluoride case is the one Peat wrote about most consistently across the longest span. The 1973 letter [[fluoride-pollution]] — six sentences long, addressed to local legislators — asks the elementary question: "With fluoride pollution of our food steadily increasing, why do the legislators want to spend our money to put additional fluoride into our drinking water? Do they even know how much fluoride is in our food?" The follow-up letter [[find-the-facts]] (June 1977) addresses the more sophisticated question: why the local fluoridation proponents, when challenged, retreat to professional authority rather than producing the underlying scientific publications.

The institutional history Peat tracked is now widely documented. Industrial fluoride — hydrogen fluoride and the fluorosilicates — is a waste stream of the aluminum smelting industry, phosphate fertilizer production, and uranium processing. Through the 1940s and 1950s the cost of disposing of fluoride waste was rising; the same period saw the rise of a public-health campaign, supported by the U.S. Public Health Service, to add the waste compound to municipal water supplies as a dental-caries preventive. The principal scientific advocate, Dr. Harold Hodge, was simultaneously serving as a consultant to the Manhattan Project (which had its own fluoride-exposure problem from uranium hexafluoride processing) and to the aluminum and phosphate industries. The dental-caries evidence, in the studies that actually controlled for confounders, was weaker than the public-relations campaign claimed; the skeletal-fluorosis evidence, the thyroid-suppression evidence, and the IQ-effect evidence were either ignored or actively suppressed.

In the [[find-the-facts]] letter Peat does not present this history. He simply asks the proponents to cite their evidence. The fact that they cannot — that they fall back on professional authority — is, in his framework, the diagnostic signature of capture. A genuinely evidence-based program would have evidence to cite. A capture-based program has only authority, because the evidence, on close inspection, runs in the other direction.

### ECT, the American Psychiatric Association, and the drug companies

Electroconvulsive therapy is the case in which the regulatory failure is most starkly documented in the public record. The 1985 [[dangerous-therapy]] letter compresses the case into four paragraphs. The FDA classified electroshock devices in its highest risk category, citing "health risks including brain damage." By law, devices in the highest risk category were to be removed from the market if investigation did not establish their safety. The investigation did not establish their safety. The devices remained on the market. The American Psychiatric Association, the professional society that should have led the call for their removal, instead campaigned for their continued use. The drug companies that produced the anesthetics and muscle relaxants used in modern ECT administration — and that profited from each procedure — were the principal funders of the relevant APA committees.

The one well-designed sham-controlled trial Peat references in the letter is decisive on the science. "In one controlled study, the electrodes were applied to the patients' legs, instead of to the head, and the beneficial effects were just as good — and the risk of brain damage was obviously less." A sham procedure with electrodes on the legs produced the same therapeutic effect as the procedure with electrodes on the head, at lower neurological cost. This is, on its face, the kind of finding that should have ended a clinical practice. It did not, because the institutional apparatus that should have ended it was funded by the institutional interest that needed it to continue. The contrast Peat draws is sharp: "Physicians are careful to warn the public of the need for controlled studies before therapies using natural substances can be accepted; shouldn't removal of part of the brain have an equivalent scientific evaluation?" The double standard — strict evidentiary requirements for cheap natural interventions, lax requirements for expensive proprietary procedures — is itself the diagnostic signature.

### Estrogen, the synthetic-hormone industry, and the *JAMA* advertising base

The estrogen-as-hormone-replacement case is the one Peat tracked over the longest span and treated as the cleanest instance. The opening paragraphs of the [[vitamin-e-estrogen-antagonist-energy-promoter-and-anti-inflammatory|2007 *Vitamin E*]] newsletter give the structural diagnosis: by the 1940s, the estrogen industry had concluded that vitamin E research was impinging on its commercial interests, because vitamin E behaved as an antiestrogen and was being promoted by the Shute brothers for the circulatory diseases of pregnancy. The response was not to engage the vitamin E literature on its merits but to manage the channels through which the vitamin E literature reached physicians. "Edward L. Bernays had already been in business for decades, teaching corporations and governments how to 'engineer consent.'... In association with the American Medical Association (the editor of *JAMA* actually helped the tobacco industry design its campaigns), Bernays ran a campaign to convince Americans that smoking was good for the health. The drug industry began using his techniques in sometimes crude but always effective ways. Estrogen was named 'the female hormone;' natural hormones, including estrogen and progesterone, were claimed, without any research, to be inactive when taken orally. Physician-shills were created to claim wonderful effects for estrogen. The vitamin status of the tocopherols was denied; as recently as the 1970s (and maybe later), university professors of dietetics were flatly saying 'no one needs vitamin E.'"

The mechanism described here is the editorial board, not the regulatory agency. "Although *JAMA* was eventually forced to give up its revenue from cigarette advertising, it didn't suffer at all, because of the vast advertising campaigns of the estrogen industry. *JAMA* obviously wouldn't want to publish anything suggesting that vitamin E, or progesterone, or thyroid, might be beneficial because of its antagonism of the harmful effects of estrogen." The substantive endocrinology is treated in the [[estrogen]] and [[progesterone]] entries; the capture story is that the principal English-language general medical journal of the twentieth century selected the manuscripts it published in part on the basis of the advertising revenue it derived from the synthetic-estrogen industry, and the cumulative effect on the field was several decades of professional consensus in favor of a hormone replacement strategy whose risk profile, once finally tested in the Women's Health Initiative, was much worse than the strategy's promoters had claimed.

## How Peat's Epistemology Handles Capture

If the institutional channels that should produce reliable medical knowledge have been compromised at the funding-source level, then the working scientist or clinician cannot defer to those channels. Peat's methodology, across the newsletters and the published interviews, is a set of compensating habits.

**Read the primary literature directly, not the review articles.** The review article is the natural site of editorial capture. The author of a review selects which primary papers to cite and how to weight them, and the editorial board of the journal selects which reviews to publish; both selections can be influenced by funding without anyone having to commit overt fraud. The primary literature, by contrast, is harder to shape. In a 2012 episode of *Politics and Science* Peat described the routine: read the abstract, then ignore the abstract and read the methods and the raw data. The abstract is the place where the author tells the reader what conclusion to draw; the methods and data are the place where the reader can decide what conclusion the experiment actually supports. The two diverge often enough that the abstract is no longer a reliable summary.

**Track the funding source.** In [[bad-partnership]] (1985), the diagnostic question is whether the state university research that produced the agricultural recommendations was funded by the chemical industry that profited from those recommendations. In [[money-or-morality]] (1985), the question is whether the university research park accepts classified defense contracts that compromise its public-interest mandate. In every case, the funding source is a piece of the evidence. A research program funded entirely by a company that profits from the conclusion is not, on its own, evidence of fraud; it is, on its own, evidence that the result needs independent replication before it can be relied on.

**Weight older literature, Russian literature, and obscure literature more heavily than recent prestige-journal English-language literature.** This is the move that strikes mainstream readers as eccentric and that becomes legible once the capture frame is taken seriously. The pre-1950s American literature on PUFA toxicity, the Soviet literature on radiation biology and on CO₂ as a respiratory regulator, the German literature on cellular respiration through Warburg, the East European endocrinology that Peat cited extensively — all of it predated, or sat outside of, the funding structure that captured the postwar Anglophone medical literature. The work was not necessarily better. It was less likely to have been shaped by the specific industrial interests that shaped the postwar American synthesis. Triangulating across literatures produced from different funding ecosystems is, in Peat's epistemology, a way of partially decapturing the evidence base.

**The bibliography-as-trail method.** A newsletter or article without a bibliography is, in this framework, untrustworthy on its face. A newsletter with a long bibliography is verifiable: the reader can pull each cited paper and check whether the abstract matches the data, whether the claim Peat is making is the claim the data supports. Most of Peat's published newsletters carry bibliographies in the dozens to hundreds of references. The criticism — that he sometimes cites supportive papers and ignores contrary ones — is genuine and is treated in **Counter-arguments** below. The defense is that the bibliography is at least present, and the reader can do the work of cross-checking; with the captured prestige literature, the trail has been laundered away and the reader cannot.

**The "follow the small player, not the big institution" heuristic.** When the institutional consensus is on one side and a small number of independent researchers — Mary Enig on PUFA, John Yiamouyiannis on fluoride, Henry Lemon and the Shute brothers on estrogen and vitamin E, Carl Pfeiffer and Carlton Fredericks on the nutritional treatment of psychiatric conditions — are on the other, the prior should not be that the institutional consensus is correct. The prior should be neutral, and the substantive evidence should decide. The small player is at least not captured. The big institution may be. The asymmetric financial incentive runs in one direction.

## What This Is Not

The capture frame is not a conspiracy theory. There is no central cabal directing the flow of medical research; there is no smoke-filled room. What there is, in Peat's account, is an equilibrium. Profit-motivated industries fund the research, the journals, and the professional societies that produce and disseminate medical knowledge. Over time, the framing those industries prefer becomes the framing of the field, not through any single decision but through the accumulation of small selection effects: which research gets funded, which manuscripts get published, which speakers get invited to the conferences, which textbooks get adopted, which clinicians get promoted. No individual participant has to act in bad faith. The pattern emerges from the structure.

This is the move that distinguishes Peat's frame from the populist anti-establishment literature it superficially resembles. The Mercola-style "they are lying to you" rhetoric posits intentional concealment by a coordinated bad-faith actor. The Peat frame posits something subtler and harder to address: that good-faith individuals working within a compromised institutional structure will reproduce the structure's biases without recognizing they are doing so. The medical-school professor who tells his students that "no one needs vitamin E" is not, on this view, a liar. He is repeating what he was taught, in a curriculum shaped by textbooks written by authors whose research was funded by the synthetic-estrogen industry. He believes what he is saying. The capture is at a level above his individual decision-making.

The frame is also not a rejection of institutional medicine as such. Peat repeatedly praised the institutional achievements that predated or escaped the capture — the mid-century Soviet radiation biology, the German cellular physiology tradition, the pre-1950s American endocrinology, the suppressed work on metabolic cancer therapy by W.F. Koch and Otto Warburg — and his own framework was an explicit attempt to recover and integrate institutional findings that the captured later literature had buried. The complaint is not that medicine became scientific; it is that the science was rerouted, between roughly 1940 and 1970, into channels that better served the financial interests of the postwar pharmaceutical, petrochemical, and agricultural industries, and that the rerouting has not been undone.

## When the Framework Misfires

Honest reportage of the capture frame requires noting where it goes wrong. The structural risk of any institutional-skepticism epistemology is that it becomes unfalsifiable. If every piece of evidence that contradicts the framework can be attributed to industry suppression, and every piece of evidence that supports the framework can be presented at face value, then the framework has become a closed system that cannot be tested against the world.

There are places in Peat's writing where this slippage is visible. The dismissal of the entire Western mainstream nutrition literature on omega-3 cardioprotection — including the relatively well-designed VITAL and REDUCE-IT trials — relies in part on attributing the findings to industry capture rather than engaging with the methodological details. The selective citation pattern noted in the [[ray-peat]] biographical hub is real: Peat consistently cited primary papers that supported his framework and consistently underweighted primary papers that complicated it. A reader who treats Peat as a single source of truth will systematically miss the parts of the literature that complicate his positions. A reader treating Peat as one synthesizing voice among several, with the responsibility of cross-checking against mainstream sources and against the primary literature directly, gets more out of him.

The harder failure mode is the source-not-method failure: the dismissal of a piece of evidence on the basis of who funded it rather than on the basis of what the data show. Peat's tendency was, on the whole, to do the second — he engaged the methods and the data — but the writing sometimes elided the distinction, and a reader following the framework casually can come away thinking that funding-source critique is sufficient to dismiss evidence. It is not. A finding from an industry-funded study can be correct; a finding from an independently-funded study can be wrong. The funding source raises the prior for independent replication; it does not, by itself, refute the finding. The framework only works when the funding critique is paired with a substantive engagement with the data.

There is also a temporal failure mode: the capture frame works much better as an account of the 1940-1990 period than as an account of the 2010s and 2020s, in which the replication crisis, the rise of preprint servers, the proliferation of independent research outlets, and the partial discrediting of the captured journals have meaningfully shifted the institutional landscape. The frame is not obsolete — the financial entanglements remain — but its application to current literature requires more case-by-case work than its application to the postwar decades.

## Counter-arguments

**The "peer review plus randomized controlled trials solves capture" position.** The mainstream defense is that the modern apparatus of peer-reviewed RCT-based evidence is precisely designed to eliminate the funding-bias effect: the methods are pre-registered, the data are made available, the protocols are standardized, the statistics are checked. If a result holds up under that apparatus, the source of funding should be irrelevant. The Peat-side response is that the apparatus has itself been captured — that pre-registration is often partial, data sharing is often incomplete, statistical practice often reflects what the funder wants to find, and the meta-analyses that synthesize the results are themselves vulnerable to the same selection effects as primary studies. The replication crisis literature (Ioannidis 2005 on why most published research findings are false, the OPEN Science Collaboration's reproducibility data, the systematic failures in nutrition epidemiology that the Mendelian randomization movement is now exposing) lends partial support to the Peat-side response. The two sides agree more than the rhetoric suggests: both acknowledge that the apparatus is imperfect; they disagree on how imperfect, and on whether the remaining imperfections produce systematic bias or merely random noise.

**The "Peat is paranoid" critique.** The strongest version of this critique notes that Peat's framework is structurally vulnerable to the same selection effects he diagnoses in the mainstream: a researcher who has decided that the mainstream is captured can find a captured-mainstream explanation for any inconvenient finding, just as a mainstream researcher who has decided that Peat is paranoid can find a paranoid-Peat explanation for any of his anomalous findings. The two framings are not symmetric — Peat's framework makes specific predictions about funding-stream effects that can be checked, while the "Peat is paranoid" framing makes no specific predictions — but the symmetry is enough that the discount the reader applies to Peat's claims should not be zero. The careful reader's posture is that the framework is a hypothesis worth taking seriously, generating testable predictions about which industry-funded findings will not replicate when independently tested, and that the predictions should be evaluated case by case rather than en bloc.

**The "industry pays for most science, get over it" pragmatist response.** A common centrist response is that industry funding is simply the empirical reality of modern science — that since the 1980s the public funding stream for basic biomedical research has shrunk while the private funding stream has grown, and that demanding industry-free research is demanding research that does not exist. The pragmatist response continues: given that the funding will continue to come from industry, the appropriate strategy is disclosure plus methodological rigor, not the wholesale dismissal Peat-side critics sometimes seem to propose. This argument has real weight. Peat himself, when pressed, was not in fact demanding the wholesale dismissal; his position was closer to the disclosure-plus-rigor position, with the additional claim that the rigor needed to compensate for the funding bias was substantially greater than what mainstream medicine actually applies. The disagreement is one of degree, not kind.

**The replication-crisis advocates' partial agreement.** A growing body of mainstream methodologists — Ioannidis, Brian Nosek, the Center for Open Science, the Cochrane Collaboration's harder critics — has converged on a diagnosis that overlaps substantially with Peat's. They typically do not use the language of "regulatory capture," but they document the same effects: industry-funded studies systematically reach more favorable conclusions than independently funded studies of the same intervention; journal selection systematically favors positive results, which interacts with industry funding to produce a literature biased toward effectiveness claims; review articles systematically cite the literature that the dominant industrial framing finds congenial. The convergence of independent methodological critics on a diagnosis structurally similar to Peat's is the strongest external evidence that the framework is tracking something real.

## Data Gaps

The framework is harder to evaluate quantitatively than it should be. A systematic funding-source-to-finding analysis across the canonical capture cases — what fraction of the pro-PUFA-cardioprotection literature was funded by the seed-oil industry, what fraction of the pro-fluoridation literature was funded by the aluminum smelting and phosphate fertilizer industries, what fraction of the pro-HRT literature was funded by the synthetic-estrogen producers — does not exist in a single accessible source. The case-by-case histories exist (in popular books, in some academic monographs, in the work of historians of medicine), but the aggregated quantitative picture that would let the reader assess the strength of the funding-bias effect across the canonical cases has not been compiled.

Comparative case studies across industries are also missing. The PUFA case, the fluoride case, the estrogen case, the ECT case, and the soy formula case appear in the Peat corpus as separate parallel accounts of the same structural pattern. A formal comparative analysis — what was the time lag between the emergence of contradicting evidence and the institutional response in each case? what were the structural features that made some cases easier to reverse than others? — would strengthen the framework and would help identify the points at which institutional capture is most tractable to reform. The Peat corpus suggests the comparative analysis is worth doing without doing it.

The contemporary application of the framework needs more case-level work. The replication-crisis-era evidence (post-2010) for and against the framework is scattered across hundreds of individual studies. A systematic effort to identify which of the canonical Peat positions have been vindicated, which have been refuted, and which remain open in the light of the 2010s-2020s mainstream literature would substantially clarify the framework's current status. Some of this work exists in scattered form (the saturated-fat reversal, the partial reevaluation of cholesterol, the shifting consensus on dietary salt, the continuing controversy over hormone replacement therapy), but the integrated assessment is missing.
