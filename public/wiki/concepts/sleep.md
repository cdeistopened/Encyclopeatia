# Sleep

Mainstream sleep science treats darkness as the body's natural ally and sleep as its quiet refuge. Ray Peat treated darkness as a stressor and sleep as the body's best defense against it. This reversal runs through everything he wrote and said about the subject for over forty years. In his [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|"Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities" (2009)]] newsletter, Peat proposed that "nocturnal sleep has the special function of minimizing the stress of darkness itself," a formulation that redefines what sleep is for, what disrupts it, and what restores it. If darkness is a metabolic insult that damages cells, depletes glycogen, and triggers a cascade of [[stress]] hormones, then sleep is not merely rest but an active protective state. This perspective explains why the conventional advice to sleep in total darkness struck Peat as misguided, why he recommended eating sugar and salt at bedtime, why he considered [[melatonin]] dangerous, and why he placed [[thyroid]] supplementation at the center of his treatment for insomnia. The people who arrive at these ideas searching for the reason they wake at 3 AM will find that Peat had a precise metabolic explanation for that too.

## Darkness as a Stressor

The most counterintuitive position in Peat's sleep framework is that darkness itself harms the body. He did not treat this as a metaphor or a psychological observation. In his [[darkness-water-osteoporosis|"Darkness, Water, Osteoporosis" (1985)]] newsletter, he wrote that "darkness does in some way 'poison' respiration, and that light enhances it, possibly by the indirect mechanism of disposing of electrons that have leaked out of the usable-energy pathway." The mechanism was cellular and energetic: mitochondria accumulate damage as the hours of darkness progress, and light reverses that damage during the day.

Peat returned to this idea across four decades of writing because the evidence kept reinforcing it. In [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|"Using Sunlight to Sustain Life" (1996)]], he described the cellular process directly: "The energy-producing part of cells, the mitochondrion, shows signs of being increasingly damaged as the night progresses, but they are gradually restored to their normal condition during the daytime light hours. This means that our greatest ability to resist stress is in the late afternoon, and we are most susceptible to injury at dawn." The practical implication is stark. Every night involves cumulative cellular damage, and every day involves partial repair. In winter, when nights are long and days are short, the damage outpaces the repair, creating what Peat described as "a cumulative increase in our susceptibility to stress-injury during the winter months."

The epidemiological evidence aligned with the biology. "For many years, it has been known that the death rate increases during the winter months and also increases at night," Peat wrote in the same newsletter. "Most deaths occur just before dawn when the body is in its least efficient state." This was not a coincidence of schedule or activity level but a direct consequence of the nocturnal stress cycle reaching its peak intensity.

The effect appeared even in healthy young people. When young sailors spent six months in the continuous polar night of Antarctica, they developed the same signs of nocturnal stress typically seen in elderly people. "Even healthy young people (and animals) experience some degree of nocturnal stress as soon as the light is turned off at night," Peat wrote, "and their body responds with an increased production of adrenalin and cortisol." The difference between a young, metabolically healthy person and an older, hypothyroid one is not whether nocturnal stress occurs but how severe its consequences are.

## The Nocturnal Hormone Cascade

Night is the most metabolically dangerous period of the twenty-four-hour cycle. During sleep and darkness, the body's protective hormones decline while its stress hormones rise. Peat described this pattern repeatedly because it explains so many otherwise puzzling symptoms, from night sweats to morning stiffness to the 3 AM waking pattern that plagues millions of people.

[[Cortisol]], [[adrenaline]], [[prolactin]], [[growth-hormone|growth hormone]], and [[estrogen]] all rise during the night. [[Thyroid]] function and [[progesterone]] fall. In his [[growth-hormone-hormone-of-stress-aging-death|"Growth Hormone: Hormone of Stress, Aging, Death" (2013)]] newsletter, Peat addressed the apparent paradox that growth hormone rises during sleep: "The increase of GH during sleep might seem to be utterly incompatible with the idea that it is a stress hormone, but in fact the other stress hormones, adrenalin, cortisol, and prolactin also tend to increase during night-time sleep. Thyroid function and progesterone function decrease at night. As I have argued previously, darkness is one of our major stressors."

The consequences of this hormonal shift are measurable and, over time, destructive. In his [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone" (2006)]] newsletter, Peat described what happens to blood and tissue during the dark hours: "The disappearance of water from the blood, as it moves into the tissues during the night, makes sleep resemble a state of shock or inflammation." This was not a superficial comparison. Blood thickens as water leaks into tissues, edema develops, and the viscosity changes increase the risk of clotting and cardiovascular events. Peat noted that "since rats, that are active at night, experience the same blood thickening, it's actually the darkness, rather than sleep, that creates this 'inflammatory' state." The variable is the absence of light, not the absence of activity.

Bone loss in women occurs almost entirely at night, when cortisol is high. Peat pointed out the irony that "the hormones that are commonly said to prevent bone loss, estrogen and growth hormone, are high at night, rising along with cortisol." The hormones that actually protect bone, thyroid and progesterone, are at their nadir. This nightly catabolic window compounds over months and years, helping to explain why osteoporosis accelerates with age and hypothyroidism.

In a [[2019-10-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-brain-barriers|2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on brain barriers]], Peat identified the three substances most critical for minimizing nocturnal stress: "Calcium, sugar, and thyroid are the three crucial things for minimizing that nocturnal rise of stress."

## Why You Wake at 3 AM

The experience of waking between 1 and 3 AM, often with anxiety, a pounding heart, or a sense of dread, is one of the most common sleep complaints. Peat's explanation was precise and mechanistic. The liver stores [[glucose]] as glycogen, and this reserve fuels the brain during the hours of sleep. In most adults, glycogen stores deplete after three to four hours. When they run out, the body must mobilize alternative fuel sources to keep the brain alive.

Peat described the cascade in his [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|"Solving Some of the Problems of Aging" (1991)]] newsletter, drawing from personal experience: "In 1979... I began to wake up every night, usually between 2 and 4 AM." He identified the mechanism as falling [[blood-sugar|blood sugar]] triggering a compensatory surge of [[adrenaline]] and [[cortisol]]: "I got in the habit of drinking milk or orange juice to raise my blood sugar enough to lower my adrenalin (and cortisol) so that I could return to sleep."

Sophie Lamb, herself recovered from eight years of severe insomnia, explained the same mechanism on Peat's behalf during a [[2019-07-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-herbalist-sophie-lamb|2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]]: "A healthy individual should be able to get an eight-hour sleep because our glycogen stores feed the active brain through the night." When glycogen is insufficient, "we get a rise in stress hormones (cortisol and adrenaline) to catabolize fat and muscle for energy. A side effect of stress hormones is mental alertness, which you don't want at night." Her summary captured Peat's core insight: "Insomnia is actually a daytime disorder presenting itself at night."

The 90-minute metabolic cycle that structures sleep stages becomes more intense as liver fuel dwindles. Peat explained in a [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on weight gain]]: "Dream cycles go with drops in blood sugar and surges of nerve chemicals like adrenaline. If your body is well rested, dreams are likely to be pleasant. Nightmares usually mean your body is stressed and not getting what it needs."

When blood sugar drops far enough, the body shifts to burning [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]], and Peat considered this shift profoundly damaging. In a [[2019-04-27-jodellefit-jodellefit-stress-and-your-health|2019 *Jodellefit* episode]], he described the consequences: "When glycogen is depleted, you mobilize free fatty acids to oxidize for energy. This requires more oxygen and is inefficient." The free fatty acids suppress mitochondrial respiration, producing [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] and damaging mitochondria in the process. "Bone loss in women happens primarily at night; the calcium shows up in the morning urine. This degeneration happens because we run out of glycogen."

The familiar morning blood sugar spike that diabetics call the "Dawn Phenomenon" is, in Peat's reading, simply the stress hormone surge visible as glucose. In a 2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode, he explained: "The stress hormones that raise temperature and pulse around dawn also increase blood sugar. Diabetics call it the 'Dawn Phenomenon,' but it's the result of stress hormones rising during the night."

## The Bedtime Snack Protocol

Peat's most practical recommendation for improving sleep was also his simplest: eat before bed, and eat again if you wake during the night. The logic follows directly from the glycogen-depletion mechanism. If the body runs out of fuel three to four hours into sleep, the solution is to start with more fuel and to replenish it when it runs low.

He recommended sugar and [[salt]] specifically because both lower [[adrenaline]] and [[cortisol]]. In his [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|"Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities" (2009)]] newsletter, Peat wrote: "Salty and sugary foods taken at bedtime, or during the night, help to improve the quality and duration of sleep. Both salt and sugar lower the adrenalin level, and both tend to raise the body temperature." He returned to this recommendation in his [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone" (2006)]] newsletter: "Since glucose and salt are used to treat shock (intravenous 7.5% salt solutions are effective), it seems appropriate to use carbohydrate (preferably sugar, rather than starch) and salty foods during the night, to minimize the stress reaction."

The specific foods Peat recommended included orange juice with a pinch of salt, warm [[milk]], ice cream, honey, and fruit. When asked in a [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on endocrinology]] about eating during the night, he was emphatic: "I think a lot of people would be happier and healthier if they would eat during the night. People who resist eating near bedtime or during the night often have more stress and more hypoglycemic insomnia. Most people can get back to sleep if they have orange juice, lemonade, or ice cream."

He also explained why these foods work: "It's very important to restore your glycogen level during sleep. I think that's one of the main purposes of sleep, to get the glycogen back into your brain and heart cells so you can expend it to resist stress during the daytime."

For people who wake at predictable times, Peat recommended a preemptive approach. In the 2013 Hashimoto's episode, he said: "I advised people to set an alarm to wake up shortly before their usual waking time and eat something with carbohydrate, milk, juice, or a cracker. Within a week, they were sleeping through the night." The mechanism was the gradual restoration of liver glycogen capacity: each night of adequate carbohydrate intake rebuilt the reserves that had been chronically depleted by nocturnal stress.

The callers who told Peat about their nightmares received the same advice. In a 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hot flashes, he said: "I've known people who had nightmares every hour during the night. By waking up and having orange juice or milk, they could lengthen the time between nightmares. In a week or so, you can get your liver stocked up with enough glucose to sleep through the night."

When asked about ice cream specifically, Peat responded with his characteristic directness: "It has a good balance because it has fat, protein, and sugar. It can help a lot of people go to sleep."

## Melatonin: The Hormone of Darkness

Peat was unequivocal in his opposition to melatonin supplementation. In an era when health food stores promoted melatonin as a sleep aid and cancer preventive, Peat described it as dangerous and immunosuppressive. In [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|"Using Sunlight to Sustain Life" (1996)]], he wrote: "Many health food stores are now selling melatonin, to reduce sleep and 'prevent cancer.' They have taken some information out of context, and don't realize how dangerous melatonin is. It makes the brain sluggish, causes the sex organs to shrink, and damages immunity by shrinking the thymus gland."

He classified melatonin as a stress marker, not a sleep hormone: "It is the hormone of darkness and winter, and is produced in the pineal gland by any stress which increases adrenalin." Melatonin is produced when the body is under stress, not when the body is at peace. The fact that it rises at night does not make it beneficial any more than the fact that cortisol rises at night makes cortisol a sleep hormone. Both are products of nocturnal stress, and both cause damage when chronically elevated.

The specific harms Peat attributed to melatonin included suppression of [[thyroid]] function, suppression of [[progesterone]] production, immune suppression through thymus atrophy, and reproductive suppression. He described it as the hormonal signature of winter and hibernation, conditions he associated with metabolic decline rather than health. "Adequate sun light suppresses the formation of melatonin," he noted, reinforcing his broader view that light is protective and darkness is harmful.

## Light at Night

Peat's position on light exposure during sleep inverted the conventional recommendation. Where most sleep hygiene advice emphasizes complete darkness, Peat recommended using bright incandescent lights in the evening and did not advocate for total darkness during sleep. His reasoning was grounded in the cellular biology of free radical accumulation.

In the [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]], a caller asked about using 250-watt bulbs before bed and upon waking. Peat endorsed the practice: "Studies on plant tissues showed that any tissue put in sunlight or UV light got some excited free radicals. Shining red light on those would immediately quench the excited electrons. If you keep living tissue in the dark, it starts building up those excited electrons." Red and infrared light, in his view, neutralized the cellular damage that accumulated during darkness by quenching the free radicals that accumulate when mitochondria are stressed.

In [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|"Using Sunlight to Sustain Life"]], he offered specific practical guidance: "In fall and winter, use very bright incandescent lights daily from sunset until bedtime. Expose as much skin as possible to the bright light; even a minute is better than nothing." He also recommended infrared bulbs with clear glass as "especially beneficial." Notably, he suggested that "a few seconds of penetrating bright light are enough to break the free radical chain reactions, slowing the production of toxic substances, which tend to increase in concentration during nocturnal stress." Even intermittent exposure carried protective value.

The brightness threshold was important. "If artificial light is bright enough, it is as effective as sunlight at stopping the stress reaction, but people seldom use lights that are bright enough." The ordinary lamp on a bedside table falls far short of the intensity Peat considered therapeutic. He recommended ten 150-watt incandescent bulbs for brief exposures every two to three hours as potentially more effective than continuous exposure to a single 100-watt light.

His newsletter on [[the-dark-side-of-stress-learned-helplessness|"The Dark Side of Stress" (2016)]] added a neurological dimension: "Bright light can reverse the cholinergic effects of inescapable stress." Light was not merely a metabolic intervention but a neurological one, countering the brain chemistry of helplessness and depression.

## Thyroid: The Master Sleep Regulator

If Peat had one recommendation for insomnia, it was thyroid supplementation. He considered hypothyroidism the most common underlying cause of sleep disturbance, and his own experience with thyroid supplementation became one of the most frequently repeated stories in his career. In [[insomnia-and-hyperactivity|"Insomnia & Hyperactivity" (1994)]], he described the transformation: "Immediately, I was able to sleep easily and deeply, and my need for food decreased. It was obvious that thyroid was having a quieting effect on my whole metabolism. I slept more efficiently, woke up refreshed, and had abundant energy during the daylight hours."

The mechanism explains why this is not paradoxical. Conventional thinking treats thyroid as a stimulant, which would seem to worsen insomnia. Peat's framework treats it as the foundation of cellular energy, which is required for relaxation. "It is a simple fact that nerve cells must have an abundance of energy if they are to be able to relax," he wrote. "Every physician knows about the convulsions produced by extreme hypoglycemia, and some know that a lack of oxygen can produce convulsion, but few realize that a high energy state is needed for stable nervous relaxation."

The brain, like muscle, must spend energy to relax. A nerve cell that cannot produce adequate ATP cannot maintain its resting potential, and the result is restlessness, hyperactivity, and insomnia rather than the calm that energy deficiency might seem to predict. Thyroid supplementation, by increasing the rate of ATP production, enables the cellular relaxation that sleep requires.

Peat's confidence in this approach was reinforced by decades of clinical observations. "In the last 20 years, I have seen almost everyone's insomnia disappear when they correct their hypothyroidism, sometimes just with dietary changes, but more often with a thyroid supplement," he wrote in the same newsletter. "Many times, people have told me that they get to sleep within a few minutes when they take a minimal dose of thyroid at bedtime."

He told one of the most striking stories in a 2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode: "I once met a doctor who hadn't slept for three nights. I gave him 10 micrograms of Cytomel [T3]. The next night he told me, 'That stuff is better than morphine.'"

The connection between thyroid and glycogen storage provides a second mechanism. Peat explained in the [[2019-07-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-herbalist-sophie-lamb|2019 episode with Sophie Lamb]]: "Thyroid is essential for storing glucose as glycogen in the liver and muscles. When you run out of glycogen, you mobilize free fatty acids." Without adequate thyroid function, the liver cannot stock up on glycogen during the day, and the resulting shortfall guarantees nocturnal hypoglycemia, adrenaline surges, and waking. The thyroid deficit thus manifests as both kinds of sleep disturbance: difficulty falling asleep (because nerve cells cannot relax) and difficulty staying asleep (because glycogen stores run out).

## Progesterone and the GABA Connection

[[Progesterone]] functions as a natural sleep aid through its effects on the nervous system. Unlike pharmaceutical sedatives, which suppress brain activity, progesterone promotes what Peat called the "high energy resting state" of nerve cells. In his [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|"Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities"]] newsletter, he described the mechanism: "An individual cell behaves analogously to the whole person. A baby's 'high energy resting state' is paralleled by the stable condition of a cell that is abundantly charged with energy; ATP and carbon dioxide are at high levels in these cells. Progesterone's effects on nerve cells include favoring the high energy resting state, and this is closely involved in progesterone's 'thermogenic' effect, in which it raises the temperature set-point."

The pharmacological mechanism involves the [[GABA]] receptor. Progesterone and its metabolites, including allopregnanolone, bind to the GABA receptor and reinforce its inhibitory, calming effects. Peat discussed this in his [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity" (2009)]] newsletter: "For years, it has been known that progesterone and pregnenolone act on the GABA receptor, to reinforce the protective, inhibitory effects of GABA." He also noted that progesterone acts on the glycine receptor, "increasing inhibition," and that the soy isoflavone genistein "antagonizes the inhibitory effect of glycine." Estrogen, predictably, has the opposite effect: it inhibits GABA's action and lowers the temperature set-point.

The temperature connection is direct. Progesterone raises body temperature; [[estrogen]] lowers it. Because adequate temperature improves deep slow-wave sleep (the restorative phase), progesterone's thermogenic effect contributes to sleep quality through a pathway independent of its GABA activity. The characteristic sleep pattern of hypothyroidism, old age, and estrogen dominance is a decrease in deep slow-wave sleep, which Peat linked to serotonin's torpor-inducing effects. "Several studies have found that anti-[[serotonin]] drugs improve sleep, and also reduce symptoms of schizophrenia and depression," he noted in the same newsletter.

Insomnia around menopause illustrates the hormonal connection. Peat wrote in his [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|"Solving Some of the Problems of Aging"]] newsletter: "Insomnia and hot flushes commonly occur around the time of menopause. These and other symptoms of menopause can be produced by an excess of cortisol, as in Cushing's syndrome. The increase of cortisol during the night is probably responsible for 'night sweats.' When progesterone is deficient, cortisol, even in 'normal' amounts, has a stronger effect."

## Temperature: Warm Feet, Good Sleep

Temperature regulation during sleep received considerable attention from Peat because the nightly temperature drop affects every metabolic process. In his [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone"]] newsletter, he wrote: "Temperature falls during sleep. Recent experiments show that hypothermia during surgery exacerbates the edema produced by stress, and that hypertonic (hyperosmotic or hyperoncotic) solutions alleviate the swelling." He speculated that light's direct action on cells may help prevent swelling, and that the body's own infrared emissions serve a similar function. The practical conclusion was straightforward: "Whatever the mechanism is, adequate temperature improves sleep, and an excessive nocturnal temperature drop probably increases edema, with all of its harmful consequences."

In the [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|"Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities"]] newsletter, he stated the principle explicitly: "Increased body temperature improves sleep, especially the deep slow wave sleep. A hot bath, or even warming the feet, has the same effect as thyroid in improving sleep."

Peat's practical advice on temperature was characteristically specific. In the [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 weight gain episode]], he said: "Don't have a cold head or cold feet. Some people sleep better taking a warm foot bath and wearing wool socks and a cap." When a caller asked about losing weight during sleep, Peat warned that a cold room is not the harmless metabolic accelerator many assume: "If your body temperature drops because you are slowing down to resist stress, you turn on inflammatory hormones. If your feet reach 90 degrees Fahrenheit (cold), they are producing toxic stress substances."

The relationship to thyroid function is circular and reinforcing. Hypothyroidism causes an inability to maintain body temperature through the night, which worsens the edema and stress hormone production that already characterize nocturnal physiology. Thyroid supplementation restores both temperature regulation and deep slow-wave sleep. This is why Peat considered a hot bath before bed a stopgap that addressed the symptom (low temperature) while thyroid correction addressed the cause.

## Sleep and Torpor

Peat drew a sharp distinction between restorative sleep and the torpor-like state that characterizes sleep in hypothyroidism, depression, schizophrenia, and aging. These are not versions of the same thing. In [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|"Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities"]], he wrote: "In several ways, torpor is the opposite of sleep. Rapid eye movement (REM), that occurs at intervals during sleep and in association with increased respiration, disappears when the brain of a hibernating animal falls below a certain temperature."

The distinction is metabolically precise. Torpor involves a decrease in temperature and metabolic rate, resembling the hibernating state. Actual restorative sleep involves high metabolic activity, glycogen synthesis, memory consolidation, and protein renewal. Peat noted that "hibernating animals periodically come out of torpor so they can sleep," which demonstrates that the two states are fundamentally different. The animals must warm up and achieve genuine sleep in order to recover from the damage that torpor itself causes.

"Torpor is the opposite of restful sleep, and with aging, depression, hypothyroidism, and a variety of brain syndromes, sleep tends toward the hypothermic torpor." The serotonin connection reinforces this: serotonin promotes torpor and interferes with learning, while anti-serotonin drugs improve genuine deep sleep and reduce symptoms of both schizophrenia and depression.

Babies illustrate the opposite extreme. They have the highest metabolic rates, the highest body temperatures, and they spend the most time in deep sleep. "Babies spend most of their time sleeping, and during life the amount of time spent sleeping decreases, with nightly sleeping time decreasing by about half an hour per decade after middle age." The progressive decline in sleep quality and duration tracks exactly with the progressive decline in metabolic rate and body temperature.

## Glycogen, the Brain, and Deep Sleep

Sleep serves a specific biochemical function in Peat's framework: the restoration of glycogen in the brain and other critical organs. In a 2008 *Politics & Science* episode on thyroid and regeneration, he described the process: "The brain synthesizes and stores glycogen, similar to muscles and the liver. With aging or stress, the glycogen is depleted, and it takes deep sleep to regenerate the glycogen. The glycogen seems to be essential. Even if you have circulating glucose, the glycogen is used for those stress-adaptive processes."

This means that even when blood sugar is normal, the brain requires its own local fuel reserves. Glycogen stored within brain cells provides the rapid energy needed for the brain's continuous adaptive work, and only deep sleep allows this glycogen to be rebuilt. "Having all of your biochemical machinery available, thyroid, vitamins, minerals, and so on, is necessary, but then deep sleep is needed to restore the glycogen so that you can meet the next stresses."

The connection to memory consolidation follows naturally. If deep sleep is when brain glycogen is restored and protein synthesis occurs most rapidly, then any disruption of deep sleep impairs both the brain's energy reserves and its structural maintenance. Peat included memory consolidation as one of sleep's "subsidiary functions," secondary to the primary function of defending against darkness but essential for cognitive health.

In his [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone"]] newsletter, Peat connected the decline of sleep quality in old age to the decline of protein synthesis: "Good sleep requires fairly vigorous metabolism and a normal body temperature. In old age, the metabolic rate is decreased, and sleep becomes defective. Protein synthesis declines with aging, as the metabolic rate slows. At least in the brain, protein synthesis occurs most rapidly in deep sleep." The slow-wave sleep that declines with age is precisely the sleep stage during which the brain rebuilds itself.

## Sleep Apnea and Carbon Dioxide

For [[sleep-apnea|sleep apnea]], Peat's analysis focused on respiration and [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] rather than the mechanical obstruction model that dominates conventional treatment. In his email correspondence, he wrote that "thyroid is the main regulatory and adaptive substance for respiration" and dismissed the purely mechanical explanation of apnea: "I think it's common to call the apnea 'obstructive' when someone is fat, but it's probably essentially the same condition, filtered through the mechanical medical mind."

The substances Peat recommended for apnea all share the property of stimulating respiration: "Several things have been very effective, for example the drug Diamox, acetazolamide, stimulates respiration by changing CO2 and pH; caffeine, thyroid, and progesterone are the more natural things that stimulate respiration." Each of these increases carbon dioxide retention or respiratory drive by a different mechanism, but the common thread is supporting the body's ability to maintain adequate breathing during sleep.

The connection to hyperventilation adds another layer. In his [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone"]] newsletter, Peat noted that "in sleep, as in shock, hyperventilation is common, and it sometimes produces extreme vasoconstriction, because of the loss of carbon dioxide." Losing carbon dioxide through hyperventilation constricts blood vessels, reducing oxygen delivery to the brain and other organs at precisely the time when they are already under nocturnal stress. Thyroid's ability to maintain adequate carbon dioxide production addresses this problem at its metabolic root.

## Gut Health and Nighttime Insomnia

The gut emerged in Peat's later work as a surprisingly important factor in sleep quality. In his email correspondence, he stated: "Endotoxin from intestinal bacteria increases histamine and serotonin; poor digestion is probably the most common cause of insomnia." Both [[histamine]] and [[serotonin]] promote wakefulness, and their elevation by bacterial endotoxin provides a mechanism by which digestive problems can prevent sleep even when blood sugar, thyroid function, and other factors are adequately addressed.

Peat's recommendations for reducing endotoxin's effects on sleep included avoiding starches at night (which feed intestinal bacteria), eating a raw carrot in the afternoon to suppress bacterial growth and sweep the intestine, and using cascara sagrada for its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects. In a 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode, he described the nighttime gut mechanism: "One thing that happens at night is the slowing of the digestive process, which can allow bacteria to become more toxin-producing. Keeping your intestine clean, for example, eating a raw grated carrot in the afternoon, stimulates the intestine and suppresses bacterial growth, reducing the stress load."

In severe cases, he noted that an antibiotic such as tetracycline could reduce the intestinal [[inflammation]] causing insomnia, and that the antihistamine diphenhydramine could help by blocking the histamine that endotoxin promotes. He was careful to distinguish this from the use of pharmaceutical sleep aids: "Histamine and serotonin promote wakefulness, and cyproheptadine blocks those, but its other effects aren't very pleasant."

## Aspirin, Glycine, and Other Aids

Several substances received secondary mention in Peat's sleep recommendations. [[Aspirin]] "can help with sleep," he wrote in an email, "but you should try it first in the afternoon, because sometimes its first effect can stimulate your metabolism and delay sleep." He recommended that anyone using aspirin regularly consume some [[vitamin-k|vitamin K]], for example by eating liver once a week.

[[Glycine]], whether from [[gelatin]] or as a supplement, promotes sleep through its inhibitory action on the nervous system. Peat noted in his [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]] newsletter that "glycine is recognized as an 'inhibitory' neurotransmitter, and promotes natural sleep." The similarity between glycine and GABA receptors suggests they may share signaling pathways, and both are sites where progesterone acts to reinforce calm, inhibitory nerve function.

[[Valerian]] received a cautious endorsement. When asked about it in the 2013 Hashimoto's episode, Peat said: "That mechanism is very good. It's a very safe drug, but using a drug of that sort is potentially habit-forming."

On the question of occasional long sleep, Peat was relaxed: "If you wake up feeling refreshed, I think it's very good to now and then get some extra sleep. Under-sleeping increases nitric oxide, and catch-up sleep lowers it."

## The 3 AM Wake — Mechanism and Protocol

The waking complaint Peat heard most often was the same one he had treated in himself: a hard wake between 1 and 4 AM with a racing heart, fixed mind, and inability to fall back asleep. The mechanism, covered in detail in [[blood-sugar]] and [[glucose]], is precise. The [[liver]] stores roughly three to four hours of glycogen, which the [[brain]] draws on during sleep. When that reserve runs out — earlier in hypothyroid people, earlier in anyone whose dinner was protein-and-vegetables without starch or fruit, earliest in someone whose liver is fatty with stored PUFA — blood sugar falls. The body surges [[adrenaline]] and [[cortisol]] to mobilize [[free-fatty-acids]]. Adrenaline is, among other things, an alerting chemical. The 3 AM wake is the subjective experience of the adrenaline-cortisol surge that the failing liver requires to keep the brain alive.

This is not a sleep-architecture problem. It is a fuel problem with a sleep-disturbing side effect. Mainstream sleep aids — antihistamines, benzodiazepines, melatonin, "sleep hygiene" — work by sedating the cortex so the user does not consciously register the adrenaline. The glycogen still depletes; the adrenaline still surges; the bone still loses calcium into morning urine; the [[mitochondria]] still take the night's PUFA-and-lactic-acid hit. Peat's approach inverts the logic: supply the [[glucose]] that prevents the emergency rather than suppressing awareness of it.

### The bedtime carb-and-salt protocol

The pre-sleep meal should contain enough carbohydrate to top off liver glycogen and enough salt to lower [[adrenaline]] preemptively. Peat's recommended foods were specific and unfussy: orange juice with a pinch of salt, warm [[milk]] with [[honey]], ice cream, a few tablespoons of honey in water with salt, fruit with cheese. The carbohydrate should be quickly absorbed — sucrose, fructose, lactose, honey — rather than starch, which Peat associated with delayed glucose release, increased serotonin from intestinal bacteria, and morning grogginess. The salt requirement is independent. From [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|"Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities" (2009)]]: "Salty and sugary foods taken at bedtime, or during the night, help to improve the quality and duration of sleep. Both salt and sugar lower the adrenalin level, and both tend to raise the body temperature." And: "Hypothyroidism tends to cause the blood and other body fluids to be deficient in both sodium and glucose. Consuming salty carbohydrate foods momentarily makes up to some extent for the thyroid deficiency."

Ice cream worked well in his recommendation because it carries the whole combination — sucrose, lactose, salt, fat to slow absorption, calcium, protein — in one bite. Peat described it as having "a good balance because it has fat, protein, and sugar. It can help a lot of people go to sleep." A small bowl an hour before bed suffices for most people.

### The salt-water nightcap by the bed

For the actual 3 AM wake the protocol shifts from prevention to rescue. Peat's recommendation was to keep something within arm's reach — orange juice, a thermos of warm milk, or a glass of water with sugar and a generous pinch of salt — and drink it the moment the wake registers, rather than lying there hoping to drift back. From [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|"Solving Some of the Problems of Aging" (1991)]], on his own habit: "I got in the habit of drinking milk or orange juice to raise my blood sugar enough to lower my adrenalin (and cortisol) so that I could return to sleep."

The salt-and-sugar water variant — "Peat's salt nightcap" in his readership — is the protocol stripped to its active ingredients. A few tablespoons of [[sugar]] (or honey) and a quarter teaspoon of salt in warm water by the bed gives the [[liver]] glucose and the adrenal axis [[sodium]] simultaneously, without the volume of orange juice or the dairy load of milk. For people sensitive to either at 3 AM, salt-sugar water is the minimum effective dose. From [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|"Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone" (2006)]]: "Since glucose and salt are used to treat shock (intravenous 7.5% salt solutions are effective), it seems appropriate to use carbohydrate (preferably sugar, rather than starch) and salty foods during the night, to minimize the stress reaction." The 3 AM stress state is, metabolically, a small inflammatory shock; the rescue mirrors the medical treatment.

### When to set the alarm

For people whose wakings are predictable — the 2:30 AM person, the 3:15 AM person — Peat recommended a preemptive approach. From the 2013 Hashimoto's episode: "I advised people to set an alarm to wake up shortly before their usual waking time and eat something with carbohydrate, milk, juice, or a cracker. Within a week, they were sleeping through the night." The mechanism is glycogen capacity. Each night the liver is fed before it empties, storage capacity expands slightly; over a week or two the wake-time drifts later, and eventually the person sleeps through unaided. The intervention trains the liver, not the brain. The upstream chain matters too: enough carbohydrate during the day, enough [[thyroid]] to store it as glycogen, enough salt to keep [[aldosterone]] from running cortisol high, and avoidance of the long fasts (skipped breakfast, late dinner) that drain glycogen ahead of bedtime. The 3 AM wake is the night-shift symptom of a day-shift problem.

## REM, Dream Architecture, and Thyroid

The 90-minute cycle that structures a night of sleep is the architecture in which REM ([[rapid eye movement]]) is embedded — four to six episodes per night, lengthening toward morning. In mainstream sleep science REM is treated as a memory-consolidation phase or a brainstem curiosity. In Peat's framework REM is the brain's [[orienting reflex]] — the curiosity-and-exploration mode — running internally on the same neural substrate that runs waking curiosity. The deeper treatment is in [[consciousness]]; this section covers what depends on the sleeper's metabolic state.

### REM is a high-energy state

The first inversion: REM is metabolically intense. From [[thyroid-insomnia-and-the-insanities-commonalities-in-disease|"Thyroid, Insomnia, and the Insanities" (2009)]]: "rapid eye movement (REM), that occurs at intervals during sleep and in association with increased respiration, disappears when the brain of a hibernating animal falls below a certain temperature." Respiration, brain glucose use, and CO2 production all rise during REM. A torpid brain — suppressed temperature, suppressed [[thyroid]], suppressed [[oxidative-metabolism]] — cannot afford REM and largely loses it. Hibernating animals do not dream; they enter torpor, which Peat described as "the opposite of restful sleep." When they need real sleep, they have to warm up first.

This explains the clinical pattern in hypothyroidism, depression, and aging. Hypothyroid sleep is long, heavy, unrefreshing, dream-poor or nightmare-prone, with morning grogginess that resembles a hangover. The high-energy REM phases never engaged because the underlying metabolic rate could not support them. Treatment is not a sedative but [[thyroid]] correction, which raises the temperature setpoint, refills [[liver]] glycogen capacity, and brings REM back online. From [[insomnia-and-hyperactivity|"Insomnia & Hyperactivity" (1994)]]: "I slept more efficiently, woke up refreshed, and had abundant energy during the daylight hours."

### Progesterone and the REM/orienting circuit

Peat's most distinctive contribution on REM was identifying [[progesterone]] as a direct REM-promoter through the brain stem reticular formation. From [[consciousness-nootropics-and-progesterone|*Consciousness, Nootropics, and Progesterone* (January 2018)]]: "The reticular activating system of the brain stem, which is responsible for waking alertness and muscle relaxation, is central to the orienting reflex, and is responsive to progesterone. This reticular formation of the brain stem is also responsible for the REM, rapid eye movement, phase of sleep, in which dreams occur. REM sleep and the orienting reflex both promote flexibility and fluidity of thinking, with increased sensitivity to motion, and they both show a unique electrical activity pattern, the pontogeniculo-occipital (PGO) wave… It has been suggested that the presence of these waves in REM sleep 'indicates that the brain is in a state of more-or-less continual orienting.'" The clinical implication is direct: "When progesterone is given during sleep, it increases the amount of REM."

The same circuit runs daytime curiosity and nighttime dreaming. A brain with adequate progesterone, thyroid, and clean oxidative metabolism dreams more, more vividly, and in a way that resembles exploration. A brain with low progesterone, suppressed thyroid, high serotonin, and PUFA-loaded membranes has fragmented REM, anxious or repetitive dreams, and the characteristic 3 AM wake from a nightmare — structurally, an adrenaline-driven failure of the orienting circuit to hold its exploratory mode against the rising stress signal.

### Dream vs. rumination, PUFA, and reading your own REM

Peat treated dreaming and rumination as different states. Dreaming is the orienting reflex running well — curious, generative, PGO-rich. Rumination is the stress signal hijacking the same circuit — adrenaline and cortisol driving worry loops, often as fragmented half-dreaming in the hour or two before the 3 AM wake. The fix for rumination is not a sedative; it is the same chain that restores REM proper — [[thyroid]], [[progesterone]], adequate glycogen, warm feet, low PUFA. The relation to [[consciousness]] is direct: the metabolic phase that supports waking exploration supports dreaming exploration; the collapse that produces helplessness in waking produces rumination in sleep.

PUFA-loaded membranes degrade REM by suppressing [[cytochrome-oxidase]] (limiting ATP for metabolically expensive REM) and by interfering with the rapid coordinated firing the PGO waves represent. The drift toward fragmented REM with age tracks the accumulation of [[arachidonic-acid]] and [[DHA]] in brain phospholipids, just as the drift toward earlier waking tracks the loss of [[liver]] glycogen capacity. The long-term solution is shared: PUFA depletion, thyroid support, adequate carbohydrate, the [[anti-stress-diet]] chain.

On reading dream content, Peat was conservative. Asked whether returning dream recall signaled improvement, he answered: "Not necessarily, it's the whole sleep structure that's important." Vivid recall with morning energy, warm hands and feet, and stable mood is a different signal from vivid recall with the 3 AM wake, sweat, and an anxious morning. Dream content is downstream of metabolism; read it that way.

## Light Environment — Day, Evening, and the Blue-Light Question

If darkness is the stressor and light the protective signal, the daily light environment is one of the larger and least-attended-to inputs to sleep quality. Peat's recommendations invert several pieces of conventional sleep hygiene.

### Bright, full-spectrum daytime

The first input is bright daytime light — ideally sunlight, on skin and into the eyes, for some portion of the day. From [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|"Using Sunlight to Sustain Life" (1996)]]: "Daylight stimulates our ability to use oxygen for energy production, and protects our tissues from some of the free-radical toxins that are produced by normal metabolism, by stress, or by radiation… Daylight stops the stress reaction, and protects our immune system." Bright daytime appropriately suppresses [[melatonin]] during the day, so the small endogenous rise at night signals "darkness," not "chronic light deprivation." Office workers behind plate glass receive intensities one or two orders of magnitude below outdoor levels; the body reads that as continuous twilight. For windowless work, Peat recommended supplementary [[incandescent]] light — bright, broad-spectrum, weighted toward red and near-infrared — in short bursts every few hours: "A few seconds' exposure to the direct light of ten 150 Watt incandescent bulbs, for just a few minutes every two or three hours, might provide more effective protection than continuous exposure to a single 100 Watt light." Intensity matters; a bedside lamp does not count.

### Warm, low-intensity evening light

In the evening the input switches direction. The goal is not to suppress melatonin indefinitely but to wind metabolism toward a warm, lower-intensity state with the spectrum shifted toward red. Peat's preferred evening environment was incandescent light, candles, or low-temperature [[red-light|red lamps]]. The reasoning was metabolic — red and infrared wavelengths quench accumulated free radicals and support [[cytochrome-oxidase]] — and signaling — warm-spectrum light matches the metabolic state the body should be entering, while blue-rich light tells the [[retina]] it is still daytime. From the [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]]: "If you keep living tissue in the dark, it starts building up those excited electrons." Evening incandescent exposure helps discharge that buildup before sleep, rather than carrying it into the nocturnal stress load.

### The blue-light and blue-blocking question

The mainstream rule — "no screens before bed; if you must, use blue-blocking glasses" — is partially aligned with Peat's framework and partially not. Aligned: blue light at night damages [[cytochrome-oxidase]], contributes to retinal injury (especially in PUFA-loaded membranes), and signals "daytime" to a system trying to settle into evening metabolism. From the [[aging-eyes-infant-eyes-and-excitable-tissues|"Aging Eyes" newsletter]]: "Cytochrome oxidase is one of the enzymes damaged by stress and by blue light, and activated or restored by red light, thyroid, and progesterone." Misaligned: the mainstream framing reads blue light primarily through the melatonin-suppression lens. In Peat's reading, melatonin suppression is broadly protective; blue light is harmful for the other reasons, not because it suppresses melatonin.

Peat's practical guidance on blue-blocking glasses was conditional. If you can change the room — incandescent bulbs, candles, dim red bedside lamp — that is the better intervention, because it changes the actual light environment rather than filtering what reaches the eye. Blue-blocking glasses are a workaround for environments you cannot control: open-plan offices, hospitals, late-night driving, unavoidable screen work. The [[aging-eyes-infant-eyes-and-excitable-tissues|"Aging Eyes" newsletter]] explicitly endorses "wearing orange lenses" when the environment cannot be controlled. The combination of a cold bedroom and a blue-screen evening — the modern Western default — is precisely the combination most likely to fragment sleep, since cold pushes the brain toward torpor while blue light blocks the warm-spectrum evening signal.

## Melatonin — Degeneration Marker, Not Sleep Hormone

The full case against supplemental [[melatonin]] lives on the [[melatonin]] page; the relevant points for the sleep frame are concentrated here.

### High melatonin = low metabolism

Peat's contrarian framing was that high circulating melatonin is a marker of metabolic failure, not of healthy sleep biology. From [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|"Using Sunlight to Sustain Life" (1996)]]: melatonin is "the hormone of darkness and winter, and is produced in the pineal gland by any stress which increases adrenalin." A high melatonin reading is reading the same thing elevated [[cortisol]] or elevated [[free-fatty-acids]] are reading — a body in stress-defense, with suppressed [[thyroid]], suppressed [[progesterone]], suppressed body temperature, and a shrinking [[thymus]]. The convention of treating melatonin as a "sleep hormone" mistakes a stress signal for its transient sedative side effect. The clinical pattern follows: the populations with the highest endogenous melatonin — elderly, hypothyroid, seasonal-affective, night-shift workers — are the populations with the worst sleep quality, the most fragmented REM, the most prominent 3 AM waking. If melatonin were genuinely a sleep hormone, high melatonin should predict good sleep. It predicts the opposite.

### Why supplemental melatonin "works"

Typical supplements deliver 1–10 mg against the body's own ~0.5 mg/night production, flooding the system with the hormone that mediates winter, hibernation, [[thymus]] atrophy, progesterone suppression, and thyroid suppression. The user falls asleep because the body reads the flood as a strong "shut down for winter" signal. The fall-asleep effect is real; so is the rest of the cascade. From [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|"Using Sunlight to Sustain Life"]]: "They have taken some information out of context, and don't realize how dangerous melatonin is. It makes the brain sluggish, causes the sex organs to shrink, and damages immunity by shrinking the thymus gland." The "antioxidant" defense runs into the problem Peat documented for [[estrogen]]: protective effects at pharmacological extremes don't translate to physiological doses, and at physiologically relevant concentrations melatonin acts as a pro-oxidant, raising [[nitric-oxide]] activity. The supplement industry has sold a hibernation hormone as a sedative and a degeneration marker as an antioxidant.

### The SAD-lamp angle, reread

Conventional sleep medicine treats winter blues as "circadian dysregulation" treated by morning light, and winter insomnia as a separate problem treated by melatonin. In Peat's reading these are the same problem with one intervention. Winter is a melatonin-elevated state caused by light deficit. Elevated melatonin suppresses [[thyroid]], suppresses [[progesterone]], lowers body temperature, drains [[liver]] glycogen capacity, and shifts metabolism toward torpor — presenting as low daytime mood and fragmented night sleep. The intervention is bright full-spectrum light during the day (real SAD lamp at adequate intensity, [[incandescent]] bulbs near the workstation, midday walk outside), which suppresses melatonin, supports thyroid, and restores the metabolic substrate for both waking mood and REM. Adding supplemental melatonin doses more of what the body is already over-producing. The 5-mg pre-bed melatonin tablet is, structurally, what a 5-mg pre-bed [[cortisol]] tablet would be: dosing a stress hormone to treat a stress-hormone-driven complaint. No one does the latter because the cortisol cascade is well-known; people do the former because the melatonin cascade is not.

For jet lag, chronic insomnia, and shift work, Peat routed users to [[thyroid]], [[progesterone]], adequate carbohydrate and salt, and bright morning light at the new local time — not to supplemental melatonin. Anyone who uses it anyway should understand they are dosing a hibernation hormone, and should monitor body temperature, morning energy, [[libido]], TSH, and immune function.

## The Unified View

Peat's framework connects all of these observations into a single coherent picture. Darkness stresses cells by damaging mitochondrial respiration. This triggers the release of cortisol, adrenaline, prolactin, growth hormone, and estrogen while thyroid and progesterone function decline. As glycogen stores deplete during the night, free fatty acids are mobilized, which further suppress glucose oxidation and damage the mitochondria. Blood thickens, temperature drops, edema develops, and bone is lost. Sleep exists to minimize this damage by reducing the metabolic cost of consciousness during the period of greatest vulnerability.

Everything that supports cellular energy production improves sleep: thyroid hormone, progesterone, adequate carbohydrate, salt, warm temperature, bright light in the evening, and deep slow-wave sleep itself (which rebuilds glycogen and synthesizes protein). Everything that depletes energy or amplifies stress worsens sleep: hypothyroidism, darkness, fasting, cold, PUFA-laden fat stores, intestinal endotoxin, serotonin, estrogen dominance, and melatonin.

The dietary advice sounds almost absurdly simple: eat some salty, sugary food before bed and again if you wake up. But Peat's advice was simple because the mechanism was specific. Salt and sugar lower adrenaline. Thyroid enables glycogen storage. Progesterone calms nerves through GABA. Warm feet maintain metabolic rate. Bright evening light quenches free radicals. Each intervention targets a specific node in the nocturnal stress cascade, and together they address a problem that sleeping pills merely mask.
