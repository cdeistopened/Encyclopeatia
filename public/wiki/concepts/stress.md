# Stress

The popular concept of stress is psychological. The patient feels overwhelmed; the doctor recommends meditation, breathing exercises, a yoga class, a long weekend. In Ray Peat's framework this picture has the polarity reversed. Stress is not what is happening in the patient's mind. It is what is happening in the patient's cells when the rate at which energy is being demanded outruns the rate at which energy can be supplied, and the felt sensation of being overwhelmed is the downstream signal of an upstream metabolic failure. "The biological idea of stress refers to the difficulty of adapting, and this involves energy, structure, and insight/orientation," Peat wrote in [[stress-and-water|*Stress and Water* (2015)]]. "Given enough energy, we can often adjust our structure to achieve full adaptation, and with insight, we can minimize the amount of energy and structural change needed." Energy is the master variable. Insight and meditation are downstream supports; they cannot substitute for a cell that has the resources to meet demand.

This inversion is the load-bearing claim of this article. Stress, properly understood, is not the stressor (the demand) but the *response* — and the response is a stereotyped biochemical cascade whose downstream products are themselves further stressors. A cell deprived of the substrate to make ATP through complete oxidation (see [[oxidative-metabolism]]) calls on adrenaline; adrenaline mobilizes free fatty acids; the free fatty acids block glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle]]; lactate accumulates; cortisol rises to break down protein for gluconeogenesis; the catabolism liberates tryptophan and elevates serotonin; the gut wall leaks; endotoxin enters circulation; aromatase activity rises and estrogen climbs; the loop closes by suppressing thyroid and depleting the protective steroids that would otherwise have buffered the whole sequence. This is not a metaphor. Each step has a measurable molecular signature, and Peat's clinical method consisted of intervening at the points where the substrate could be restored before the chain ran to its terminus.

The conceptual debt is to [[hans-selye|Hans Selye]], whose General Adaptation Syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion) Peat inherited verbatim and then extended into the bioenergetic register. Selye had located the exhaustion phase in adrenal cortical failure. F.Z. Meerson, whose Soviet laboratory worked through the 1960s and 1970s, traced it down to the mitochondrion. Peat's framework is the continuous extension of that line. The vault entries on [[hans-selye]], [[cortisol]], [[learned-helplessness]], [[progesterone]], [[blood-sugar]], and [[oxidative-metabolism]] are all subdivisions of the territory this article maps at the top level. The unique signal here is the cascade itself, the chronic-versus-acute distinction, the protective-versus-damaging discrimination, and the operational hierarchy of substrates by which the cascade is interrupted.

## The Selye inheritance, in one pass

Selye's claim was that diverse causes converge on a small number of cellular events. The adrenal cortex hypertrophies, the thymus shrinks, the gastrointestinal mucosa ulcerates and bleeds, and a stereotyped triad of histological changes appears whether the inciting stressor was cold, surgical trauma, hormone overdose, foreign-body irritation, or psychological deprivation. Peat extended Selye's framework without abandoning it. "One of the most broadly substantiated principles in biology is that a great variety of harmful causes all lead to a few forms of biological harm — the concept of the stress reaction shows the powerful implications of the principle," Peat wrote in [[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]. "Stress, no matter what the specific cause, has a particularly destructive effect on three organ systems: The nervous system, the immune system, and the reproductive system. Inflammation, lipid peroxidation, tissue atrophy, the 'calcium catastrophe' (when almost anything goes wrong, calcium can transmit and amplify and extend the problem, but isn't itself the source of the problem), mitochondrial decay, and similar events help to define the stress reaction in greater detail."

The principle is Selye's. The enumeration of converging events — lipid peroxidation, calcium catastrophe, mitochondrial decay — is Peat's filling in of what Selye's exhaustion phase looks like at the molecular level. The full biographical and intellectual debt is at [[hans-selye]]; this page assumes that frame and builds on it.

The single most important Peat amendment to Selye concerned the location of the exhaustion. "Hans Selye showed that the thymus shrinks very early in the stress reaction. In his understanding of the process, when adaptation was followed by the 'exhaustion phase,' the adrenal glands had simply become exhausted from overuse. F. Z. Meerson's work showed that cortisol, and the free fatty acids mobilized by stress, have a toxic influence on the mitochondrial energy production system. Both cortisol and the free fatty acids block the efficient use of glucose for producing energy, creating a diabetes-like condition. The exhaustion problem caused by excessive stress is generalized, not just a matter of adrenal insufficiency" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). The adrenal is not where the exhaustion lives. The mitochondrion is.

The second amendment was the recognition that Selye's "anti-stress hormones of the adrenal cortex" had been narrowed by post-1949 pharmacology into a single molecule — cortisone, and its synthetic derivative prednisone — when Selye's own data had pointed at [[progesterone]] first. The clinical implications follow on the [[hans-selye]] and [[progesterone]] pages. For this article the operational point is that the anti-stress hierarchy is not a single hormone but a pool of protective steroids, and the felt experience of stress is largely the experience of that pool being depleted faster than it can be regenerated.

The third amendment was Meerson's bridge from endocrinology to cellular bioenergetics. "Meerson's work created the basis for understanding several degenerative processes, especially the phenomenon of 'excitotoxicity,' in which the combination of excessive stimulation and deficient energy supply damages or kills cells" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). Excitotoxicity is the cell-level statement of why the GAS becomes terminal: cells that fire while energy-starved kill themselves. This is what underlies the brain damage of chronic stress, the cardiac damage of catecholamine excess, the muscle damage of cortisol-driven gluconeogenesis, and the gastrointestinal bleeding that Selye had observed in 1936 without yet knowing the mechanism. The full respiratory-defect frame is at [[oxidative-metabolism]].

## The cascade

What happens, step by step, when a cell can no longer make enough ATP to meet demand:

**Step one: adrenaline.** The fastest and most reversible response. Glycogenolysis in the liver, lipolysis in adipose tissue, the heart rate rises and the airways open. If the [[liver]] has glycogen to release, adrenaline buys the system enough time to find a meal, escape the predator, or finish the work. The 8-hour glycogen buffer of a healthy adult (see [[blood-sugar]] for the supply-side mechanics) is the substrate Peat's framework most wants preserved, because adrenaline-without-glycogen has nowhere to go but the next step.

**Step two: cortisol.** When glycogen is gone, cortisol rises to break down protein for gluconeogenesis. The catabolism is structurally destructive — muscle, thymus, skin, and finally intestine and liver are cannibalized to feed the brain. The full mechanism, dosing implications, and protective-steroid buffering are at [[cortisol]]. The key point for this article is that cortisol's catabolism is the price the system pays for the unmet substrate demand: every gram of muscle converted to sugar is a gram of structure that the body will have to rebuild, and the rebuilding requires the very thyroid-and-progesterone-and-pregnenolone tone that cortisol is depleting.

**Step three: free fatty acids.** Both adrenaline and cortisol mobilize fatty acids from adipose tissue. In a person whose adipose is rich in palmitate and stearate (saturated fats) the mobilized substrate is metabolically clean. In a person whose adipose is rich in linoleic and arachidonic acid (modern Western diets, see [[pufa]]) the mobilized substrate is metabolically catastrophic. PUFA inhibits glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle]], peroxidizes to aldehydes that damage mitochondrial proteins, converts to inflammatory prostaglandins, and produces a [[free-fatty-acids|free-fatty-acid load]] that perpetuates the very hypoglycemia that mobilized it. "About 15 minutes after injecting this nutritional dose of emulsified soy oil intravenously, people would get hyperglycemic," Peat noted in the chapter material — the Randle effect from oleate or linoleate is instantaneous, and the hyperglycemia that follows is not the body holding too much sugar but the body unable to *use* the sugar in the presence of the lipid block.

**Step four: lactate.** With glucose oxidation blocked at the mitochondrion, the cell completes glycolysis but cannot oxidize pyruvate. Pyruvate is reduced to lactate, NAD+ is regenerated, and glycolysis can continue — at the cost of producing the metabolite Peat called "the polar opposite of carbon dioxide." Lactate destabilizes the mitochondrial phase, raises the cytoplasmic pH in a catabolic direction, and inhibits the very respiration that would clear it. The full mechanism is at [[lactic-acid]] and [[oxidative-metabolism]]. The cascade-specific consequence is that lactate's accumulation marks the metabolic floor at which the cell has reverted to the slime-metabolism Peat traced through Warburg and Koch.

**Step five: estrogen.** As cortisol catabolizes muscle and tissue, the protective steroid pool depletes. Estrogen rises relative to progesterone — partly because the hypothyroid liver of a chronically stressed person cannot clear it, partly because cortisol itself activates aromatase, and partly because the free fatty acids are themselves substrate for further estrogen-amplifying pathways. "Puberty, because of hormonal changes, especially increased estrogen, can be seen as the first stage of a chronic stress, resembling diabetes, since elevated free fatty acids cause 'insulin resistance,' with slightly impaired oxidation of glucose. The thymus shrinks considerably at puberty, under the influence of the hormonal changes and the increased free fatty acids (caused mainly by estrogen)" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). Estrogen produces shock — Selye showed this in the 1940s ([[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|*Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer*]]) — and progesterone reverses it. The chronic-stress state is, structurally, a chronic estrogen-dominant state with a depleted progesterone pool, and the failure mode of the cascade is partly the failure of progesterone to be available when the system needs it.

**Step six: serotonin.** Cortisol's catabolism liberates tryptophan from muscle protein. Tryptophan in excess converts to [[serotonin]], which Peat treated not as a happiness hormone but as the chemical signature of [[learned-helplessness]] — a glycolysis activator, an ATP suppressor, a stimulator of corticotropin-releasing hormone (which raises cortisol further), and a parasympathetic-shutdown signal in its own right. "Serotonin reduces energy production in muscles; this is probably related to the muscular dystrophy it produced in Selye's experiments," Peat wrote in [[endotoxin-stress-depression|*Endotoxin, Stress, Depression*]]. Serotonin closes a loop that began with adrenaline by elevating the cortisol that drove the catabolism that elevated the tryptophan that became the serotonin. The full SSRI inversion (effective antidepressants lower serotonin over time; the dominant pharmacology has the polarity backward) is at [[learned-helplessness]].

**Step seven: endotoxin.** "When physical and mental activity are intensified to meet an adaptive crisis, the adrenergic sympathetic nervous system optimizes muscular energy production, but inhibits the digestive system. In the state of anxious-helpless withdrawal, the parasympathetic and serotonergic systems demobilize the active neuroskeletomuscular systems, and activate the bladder, intestine and stomach, tending to empty the contents by causing diarrhea, vomiting, and urination" ([[endotoxin-stress-depression|*Endotoxin, Stress, Depression*]]). The chronic version, less dramatic, is the increased gut-wall permeability of the cortisol-and-serotonin-saturated patient, and the consequent translocation of bacterial endotoxin into portal circulation. Endotoxin is itself pro-inflammatory and lactate-elevating, and "the inflammation-producing effects of lactate, serotonin, and endotoxin are overlapping, additive, and sometimes synergistic, along with histamine, nitric oxide, bradykinin, and the cytokines" (same source). The seventh step closes the cascade by adding a gut-derived amplifier that perpetuates the inflammatory tone the first six steps generated. Selye had observed it as the bleeding of stress ulcers in 1936; Peat traced it through to the modern endotoxin literature.

The point of laying out the cascade is that the seven steps are not independent. Each step lowers the threshold for the next, and the system has positive-feedback signatures at multiple junctions (cortisol → estrogen → cortisol; serotonin → CRH → cortisol → tryptophan → serotonin; FFA → Randle → lactate → mitochondrial damage → reduced ATP → more adrenaline → more FFA). The reason chronic stress is qualitatively different from acute stress is that the loops have had time to entrench. An acute stressor in a metabolically well-resourced person runs to step one or two and dissipates. A chronic stressor in a depleted person runs all seven and becomes self-sustaining.

## Protective vs damaging: the hormesis question, briefly

The wellness-culture frame popularized over the last two decades — cold plunges, fasted training, intermittent fasting, sauna stacks, "controlled stress" as a means of biological resilience — is in Peat's reading a category error built on the conflation of intermittent stress *signals* with chronic stress *states*. The full counter is at [[hormesis]]; what matters here is the distinction Peat drew between a *challenge* (within the body's energetic budget, met with pleasure, builds structure) and a *stressor* (exceeds the budget, met with cortisol-and-PUFA mobilization, leaves a scar). "A teenager meeting a challenge — like a tennis game or building something — builds the body and skills. That is not stress. Stress is a demand put on you that you didn't ask for and don't have the ability to do easily and with pleasure. Doing a boring, stupid job that you don't love is poisoning your tissues constantly because you aren't getting pleasure out of the activity" ([[2019-04-27-jodellefit-jodellefit-stress-and-your-health|*Jodellefit*, April 2019]]).

The variable that distinguishes the two is not the intensity of the demand. It is the substrate state of the cell receiving it. A well-fed, well-rested, euthyroid person walking briskly in cold morning air is performing a challenge; an underfed, hypothyroid, cortisol-saturated person sitting in a 38°F ice bath is performing a stress experiment on a system already running on its survival metabolism. The molecular markers the hormesis literature points to as evidence of adaptive benefit — heat shock protein activation, AMPK signaling, sirtuin induction, autophagy — are, in Peat's reading, the molecular evidence that the cell has been forced into survival metabolism. They are not signs that the cell has been improved. They are signs that the cell has paid a structural price to survive. The ratchet click of the scar accumulates. See [[hormesis]] for the radiation-industry origin of the doctrine and the full Peat counter; see [[oxidative-metabolism]] for what the well-resourced alternative actually looks like.

The discriminator that matters clinically: does the demand leave the patient warmer, more energetic, hungrier in a healthy way, and sleeping better? Or does it leave them colder, more tired, off their appetite, and waking at 3 a.m. with a racing pulse? The first signature is challenge. The second is the cascade running.

## The cortisol-glycemic loop, in its proper place

The single most operationally important point of intervention in the cascade is the cortisol-glycemic loop, and it lives upstream of nearly every "stress management" intervention the wellness culture markets. "When blood sugar isn't able to produce the carbon dioxide it should, it is wasted and becomes lactic acid. This raises the pH of the cell interior, creating a catabolic, excited state. Brain cells ultimately die or are blocked. In reaction to falling glucose oxidation, adrenaline rises. Adrenaline should bring up glucose and stop the stress reaction. But if you don't have enough stored glucose, you resort to cortisol. Cortisol provides glucose by breaking down muscle, thymus, and skin cells. If that continues, you get a chronic stress cycle: low blood sugar, increased adrenaline, increased cortisol, and breakdown" — Peat in the [[2018-06-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-positive-thinking-sleep-and-repair|June 2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on sleep and repair]].

The Selye-era observation that grounds this loop is the 300 mg% rule: blood glucose at roughly 300 mg/dL was, in Selye's anaphylaxis experiments, "the completely protective level" against the deadliest allergens, while insulin-induced hypoglycemia made the mildest allergen lethal. The detail is developed at [[blood-sugar]]. The principle for this article is that the felt experience of stress is, more often than not, the felt experience of falling blood sugar, and the intervention that breaks the cascade most reliably is not psychological but glycemic: orange juice with salt, milk with honey, ripe fruit, the small frequent meals that keep the liver topped up so adrenaline never has to fall through to cortisol. "I've known people who had nightmares every hour during the night. By waking up and having orange juice or milk, they could lengthen the time between nightmares. In a week or so, you can get your liver stocked up with enough glucose to sleep through the night" (*Hot Flashes, Night Sweats*, August 2012).

This is the reason "stress management" via meditation, mindfulness, or breath work — while not actively harmful — is downstream of the metabolic substrate question. A patient with intact liver glycogen, adequate progesterone, and functional thyroid will derive real benefit from meditation. A patient running on the cortisol cascade will find that meditation calms the felt experience temporarily without altering the underlying biochemistry, and the cascade resumes the moment the meditation ends. Peat addressed the Buteyko literature in this register: "Although it is easy to dismiss Buteyko's emphasis on the 'Intentional Cessation of Deep Respiration' as a therapy, his work on the importance of carbon dioxide is sound. When I realized that many hypothyroid people compensate by producing huge amounts of adrenaline, which helps to sustain their blood sugar and their nervous energy, and that adrenaline tends to cause hyperventilation, I saw that 'intentional regulation of respiration' might work in these people to reduce hyperventilation just as psychotherapy, reassurance, meditation, or taking a nap can help to control hyperventilation and other effects of excess adrenaline and anxiety" ([[stress-and-water|*Stress and Water* (2015)]]). The breathing exercise lowers the adrenaline tone; it does not raise the thyroid that would have prevented the adrenaline elevation in the first place. The same point holds for every cognitive or behavioral stress intervention: it operates downstream of the substrate layer where the cascade was actually initiated.

## Acute vs chronic: why the same hormones do opposite things at different durations

A stress response of minutes is not the same physiology as a stress response of months, and the failure to distinguish them is one of the most common errors in popular stress writing. The acute response — adrenaline-dominated, sympathetic, glycogen-mobilizing, alert, lean, fast — is what the body evolved to handle. It runs to completion in minutes, dissipates as the substrate is restored, and leaves the system with no permanent scar. The chronic response — cortisol-dominated, FFA-saturated, lactate-elevating, thymus-shrinking, brain-atrophying — is what the body never evolved to handle and what defines nearly every degenerative condition in Peat's framework.

The distinguishing variable is duration relative to substrate. A predator chase that lasts ninety seconds runs on adrenaline and glycogen; the deer either escapes or doesn't, and the survivors return to baseline within an hour. A chronic financial worry that lasts six months, in a person whose liver cannot store glycogen because the thyroid is suppressed because the [[pufa]] burden is high because the estrogen is elevated because the cortisol is depleting the progesterone, becomes a structural condition. The same hormonal signals — adrenaline, cortisol, prolactin, growth hormone — that were adaptive in the ninety-second case are catabolic in the six-month case. The hormones did not change. The duration relative to the substrate did.

This is why Peat returned so often to the question of *darkness as a chronic stressor*. "The increase of GH during sleep might seem to be utterly incompatible with the idea that it is a stress hormone, but in fact the other stress hormones, adrenalin, cortisol, and prolactin also tend to increase during night-time sleep. Thyroid function and progesterone function decrease at night. As I have argued previously darkness is one of our major stressors" ([[growth-hormone-hormone-of-stress-aging-death|*Growth Hormone: Hormone of Stress, Aging, Death*]]). The eight hours of darkness most people sleep through are eight hours during which the cortisol-glycemic loop is running on whatever glycogen the liver had at bedtime. A liver with eight hours of glycogen sleeps through it; a liver with three hours of glycogen wakes at 3 a.m. with the cortisol surge that the cascade has been escalating to since midnight. The clinical signature — early-morning waking, racing pulse, night sweats, the cluster of "menopausal" or "andropausal" symptoms in stressed midlife adults — is the chronic stress response running its overnight cycle.

The hamster observation Peat reported from his own laboratory is the most striking simple confirmation: "In 1971 I found that none of the sexually mature hamsters in our lab had any detectable thymus glands during the winter, though they all had them in the summer." A laboratory animal in a controlled environment, on a stable diet, with no obvious external stressor, lost an entire endocrine organ on the seasonal cycle of darkness alone. The chronic-stress framework is what reconciles the observation with the rest of the biology.

## Learned helplessness as the terminal phenotype

If the cascade running to its terminus produces a measurable behavioral signature in animals, it is the [[learned-helplessness]] state — the parasympathetic-shutdown phenotype Curt Richter described at Johns Hopkins in 1957 and Martin Seligman labeled in 1967. The full account is at [[learned-helplessness]]; the cascade-specific point is that the rats who drowned in five minutes after inescapable shock died with hearts "filled with blood, revealing the dominant activity of the vagal nerve, secreting acetylcholine" ([[the-dark-side-of-stress-learned-helplessness|*The Dark Side of Stress*]]). The autonomic nervous system had flipped from sympathetic (the cascade's first steps) to parasympathetic (the cascade's exhausted floor), and the heart stopped in the relaxed, blood-filled position that defines dorsal-vagal collapse.

This is what Selye's exhaustion phase looks like at the level of a whole organism. The biochemistry is high cortisol, high serotonin, high acetylcholine, high nitric oxide, low T3, depleted progesterone — every step of the cascade running at chronic amplitude, the autonomic balance flipped, the protective steroid pool gone, the mitochondrial respiratory capacity collapsed. The animal that watched another rat escape and was then able to swim for days had not learned anything cognitive; its autonomic balance had shifted, by the single observation of escape as possible, back to the sympathetic side, and the cellular substrate for swimming was once again available.

The clinical syndromes that share this physiology are extensive: major depression, generalized anxiety, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue, post-viral fatigue, autoimmune flares, post-traumatic stress, recurrent infection, the broad cluster of "menopausal" and "andropausal" complaints. Mainstream medicine treats these as separate diagnostic entities. Peat treated them as different organ-level expressions of the same cascade running at chronic amplitude. The recovery levers are largely shared across the categories, and that fact is itself diagnostic — when six labeled conditions remit in parallel under the same metabolic intervention, the labels were obscuring a common upstream cause.

## The anti-stress hierarchy

The interventions Peat treated as primary, in rough order of leverage:

**Sugar.** The single most powerful anti-stress intervention is keeping the [[liver]] topped up with glycogen, and the substrate that does this best is sucrose-containing food — ripe fruit, milk, orange juice, honey, the small frequent meals that keep blood sugar from falling far enough to trigger adrenaline (see [[blood-sugar]] for the operational protocol). "Sometimes foods that are essential nutrient rich — eggs, orange juice, oysters, liver — or just pleasurable, milk shakes, ice cream etc. (especially at bedtime, to improve sleep), can break the pattern, and sometimes supplementing thyroid or pregnenolone can do it" ([[anxiety-and-mental-stress|*Anxiety and Mental Stress* email Q&A]]). The principle is that hunger is the leading edge of the cascade, and the operational rule is never to let yourself get hungry enough to call on stress hormones.

**Salt.** Salt restriction is itself a form of stress. "A poor reaction to stress, with increased cortisol, can raise the body temperature by accelerating the breakdown and resynthesis of proteins, but adaptive resistance to stress increases the temperature by increasing the consumption of oxygen and fuel. In the presence of increased cortisol, abdominal fat increases, along with circulating fatty acids and calcium, as mitochondrial respiration is suppressed" ([[salt-energy-metabolic-rate-and-longevity|*Salt, Energy, Metabolic Rate, and Longevity*]]). Sodium is required for the active transport that maintains cellular gradients; without it, aldosterone and cortisol rise to spare what little sodium is available, and the cascade runs at a higher baseline. Peat's recommendation — salt to taste, generously, all the time — is the operational counterpart to the cardiology profession's prohibition.

**Thyroid.** The hormone that activates oxidative metabolism is also the hormone that prevents the cascade from running. The full mechanism is at [[thyroid]]. The stress-specific point is that "an intense stress can cause the hormones to get stuck in an inefficient state, often with suppressed thyroid function and decreased steroid production... sometimes supplementing thyroid or pregnenolone can do it" ([[anxiety-and-mental-stress|*Anxiety and Mental Stress*]]). The chronic-stress patient is functionally hypothyroid even when their TSH is in range, because the conversion of T4 to T3 is suppressed by elevated cortisol, reduced glucose availability, and the [[pufa]] burden in the liver. Supplemental T3 in this state is not endocrine replacement; it is breaking the loop that keeps the patient hypothyroid.

**Progesterone.** The original anti-stress steroid, in Selye's data and in Peat's clinical practice. Progesterone increases cholinesterase activity (clearing the acetylcholine that mediates the parasympathetic shutdown of learned helplessness), opposes estrogen (the shock hormone), calms the GABAergic system, and protects the thymus. "In situations where they were studying learned helplessness, which produces increased acetylcholine and nitric oxide, they found that either progesterone or thyroid (T3) would block the formation of that behavior and keep them from dying prematurely" ([[2013-09-20-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-learned-helplessness-nervous-system-and-thyroid-questionaire|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, September 2013]]). The full clinical use is at [[progesterone]].

**Light.** Bright light suppresses melatonin, lowers nitric oxide, raises body temperature, shifts the autonomic balance toward sympathetic activation, and reverses the cholinergic effects of inescapable stress. "Bright light, without excess ultraviolet, should be available every day," Peat wrote in [[the-dark-side-of-stress-learned-helplessness|*The Dark Side of Stress*]]. The chronic stress patient is often a chronic-darkness patient, and the seasonal pattern of depression and the seasonal pattern of cold-and-flu susceptibility share this physiology. Daylight is not a wellness adornment; it is part of the substrate.

**Carbon dioxide.** "Buteyko devoted his career to showing that sufficient carbon dioxide is important in preventing an exaggerated and maladaptive stress response. He showed that a deficiency of carbon dioxide (such as can be produced by hyperventilation, or by the presence of lactic acid in the blood) decreases cellular energy (as ATP and creatine phosphate) and interferes with the synthesis of proteins (including antibodies) and other cellular materials" ([[stress-and-water|*Stress and Water* (2015)]]). The protective role of CO2 — Lewis-acid stabilization of proteins, mitochondrial coacervate maintenance, occupation of amine groups that PUFA peroxidation products would otherwise attack — is developed at [[carbon-dioxide]] and [[oxidative-metabolism]]. The operational implication is that the chronic-stress patient is also a chronic-hyperventilator, and bag breathing for one minute several times a day measurably lowers serum lactate.

**Gelatin and glycine.** "A generous supply of glycine/gelatin, against a balanced background of amino acids, has a great variety of antistress actions" ([[gelatin-stress-longevity|*Gelatin, Stress, Longevity*]]). The amino acid balance of pure muscle meat is the same balance produced by extreme stress (cortisol breaking down muscle for sugar); glycine from gelatin restores the balance and antagonizes the catabolic pathways. The 1930s clinical use of gelatin to control stress-induced bleeding was a working pharmacological intervention that the post-1960s pharmaceutical era abandoned without justification.

**Niacinamide and aspirin.** The pharmacological tools for breaking the FFA arm of the cascade. Niacinamide inhibits the lipase that liberates fats from adipose; aspirin blocks prostaglandin synthesis downstream. Together they reduce the FFA pool that drives the Randle block, allowing cells to oxidize glucose normally and lowering the cortisol-glycemic loop's amplitude. See [[blood-sugar]] for the diabetes-specific application.

The hierarchy is roughly ordered by leverage and reversibility. Sugar and salt can be implemented within a meal. Thyroid and progesterone require dosing and feedback. Light and CO2 are environmental. Gelatin and the pharmacology are pharmacological. The point of laying them out as a hierarchy is that "stress management" is not a single intervention but a stack, and the substrate-restoring interventions sit below the psychological ones because the psychological ones cannot work in a substrate-depleted cell.

## Why "stress management" via meditation is downstream of glycemic control

This is the operational claim of the article. The popular wellness picture treats stress as a psychological condition treatable by psychological intervention. Peat's framework treats stress as a metabolic condition that produces psychological symptoms, and the psychological interventions operate at the layer above the metabolic substrate that actually drives the condition.

The evidence runs through several converging observations.

First: the symptom-and-recovery time course of meditation versus glycemic restoration. A patient with chronic 3 a.m. waking who begins a meditation practice typically reports modest improvement over weeks to months and substantial relapse during stressful periods. The same patient given orange juice with salt at the bedside, milk and honey at bedtime, and supplemental thyroid if indicated typically reports significant improvement within days and durable improvement over weeks as the liver glycogen buffer rebuilds. The first intervention modulates the felt experience; the second alters the substrate. The durability of the second is not coincidence.

Second: the polyvagal observation that the autonomic shutdown of learned helplessness can be reversed by metabolic intervention but not by purely cognitive intervention in chronic cases. T3 prevents and reverses learned helplessness in the experimental literature ([[the-dark-side-of-stress-learned-helplessness|*The Dark Side of Stress*]]). No purely cognitive intervention has been shown to do this in the rodent model. The implication for human patients is that some fraction of the population whom CBT fails to help is not failing CBT; CBT is failing the substrate.

Third: the iatrogenic observation that diagnostic labeling itself can produce the learned-helplessness state, and the cognitive-frame interventions designed to address this often miss the underlying physiology. "Yes, and [helplessness] can be brought on by anything that overwhelms your self-regulation. I think a diagnosis often creates a learned helplessness situation, and the person lets their serotonin get out of control" ([[2019-10-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-brain-barriers|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, October 2019]]). The diagnosis triggers the cascade; the cascade is what the patient now has; treating the cascade with cognitive frame interventions alone leaves the cascade running.

The point is not that meditation is useless. Meditation, breath work, social contact, environmental enrichment, and the other cognitive-behavioral interventions for chronic stress are real and worth doing. They are *downstream* of the substrate question. A patient with intact liver glycogen, adequate progesterone, functional thyroid, and the daily substrate inputs will get real value from meditation; a patient running on cortisol and serotonin will find that meditation calms the felt experience temporarily without breaking the loop. The proper sequence is metabolic substrate first, autonomic/cognitive intervention second. The wellness culture has the order reversed, and the iatrogenic cost of the reversal is the substantial population of chronically stressed patients who spend years on meditation apps without ever being asked whether their liver can store glycogen overnight.

## Counter-arguments

**Lazarus appraisal theory.** Richard Lazarus's transactional model of stress treats the stress response as fundamentally a function of cognitive appraisal — the same stressor produces different responses in different people based on how they appraise it as a threat, a challenge, or neutral. This is the dominant framework in psychological stress research, and it has substantial empirical support: cognitive reappraisal interventions do measurably reduce subjective stress in many laboratory and clinical settings. Peat's framework would not deny the appraisal layer but would locate it downstream of substrate: the patient whose substrate is intact is more likely to appraise a demand as a challenge; the patient whose substrate is depleted is more likely to appraise the same demand as a threat. Lazarus's framework describes the perceptual cognition; it does not address the metabolic substrate that determines which cognition the patient is capable of. The two frameworks operate at different levels and the disagreement is largely about which level is causal.

**Polyvagal theory.** Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory describes the autonomic-shutdown observation that Selye, Richter, and Peat all encountered, and frames it as a dorsal-vagal "freeze" response. The somatic-experiencing and trauma-informed therapy practitioners working in this frame would agree with Peat that the helpless state is autonomic rather than cognitive. The disagreement is whether the autonomic state can be unwound primarily through nervous-system-targeted somatic work or whether the metabolic substrate has to be addressed in parallel. Peat's position is the latter, and the polyvagal literature has not generally taken up the bioenergetic question.

**Neuroendocrine adaptation and the "stress is good for you" school.** Robert Sapolsky's chronic-stress research and Bruce McEwen's allostatic-load framework describe the same syndrome Peat traced but stop at the neuroendocrine layer (high cortisol, depleted protective steroids, autonomic dysregulation). These frameworks are largely compatible with Peat's at the descriptive level but do not engage the upstream substrate question (thyroid, PUFA, sugar, endotoxin, [[carbon-dioxide]]) that Peat treated as causal. They also tend to recommend interventions at the neuroendocrine layer (exercise, sleep, social support, mindfulness) rather than at the metabolic substrate layer, and the difference in intervention emphasis matters clinically. A related "stress is good for you" school within evolutionary medicine treats the GAS as adaptive in principle and pathological only at extremes; Peat's framework largely agrees with the adaptive interpretation of acute stress but treats the chronic case as a separate physiological category whose pathology is not "too much of an adaptive response" but "the substrate-depletion state that the response was meant to be brief enough to avoid."

**Allostatic load.** McEwen's allostatic-load concept — that the cumulative wear-and-tear of repeated stress responses produces measurable systemic damage — is largely consonant with the chronic-stress account above. The disagreement is over whether the allostatic load can be measured most usefully through the cortisol-and-cardiometabolic markers McEwen emphasized or through the broader Peat-style substrate panel (temperature, pulse, T3, total CO2, lactate, progesterone-to-estrogen ratio, FFA). The two frameworks are partly complementary; the Peat framework adds the metabolic-substrate axis that the allostatic-load literature has not fully integrated.

**The serotonin-deficit model of depression.** The dominant psychiatric framework treats depression as a low-serotonin state and recommends drugs that raise synaptic serotonin. Peat's framework — that learned helplessness and depression are high-serotonin states and that effective antidepressants lower serotonin over time — runs against this received pharmacology. The literature has begun to converge on the Peat position (Moncrieff and Horowitz 2022; Seligman and Maier's own 2016 walk-back of the original learned-helplessness theory) but the SSRI prescribing patterns have not. See [[learned-helplessness]] for the full inversion.

**The "stress as engineering metaphor" problem.** Selye coined the biological use of "stress" by inverting the engineering meaning (the force applied to a material) into the response meaning (the material's reaction to the force). The terminological cost has been substantial — eighty years of imprecise usage in both popular and scientific literature. Walter Cannon's "fight or flight" and the "homeostasis" framework had pre-existed Selye's coinage and arguably should have been preserved. The cost of the terminology is real but is now largely sunk.

**Tobacco-industry funding of Selye's later work.** Mark Petticrew and Kelley Lee documented (*AJPH* 2011) that Selye's research program received substantial tobacco-industry funding in the 1960s and 1970s and that his late-career emphasis on stress as the master cause of cardiovascular disease was prepared collaboratively with tobacco-industry attorneys. This does not invalidate the underlying GAS framework, which has been replicated independently for sixty years, but it does require honest reckoning with the political-economic context of the stress concept's popularization. See [[hans-selye]] for the longer discussion.

## Data gaps

**Childhood and prenatal stress.** Selye worked almost entirely in adult rats. Peat addressed pediatric stress only sporadically. The ACE (adverse childhood experiences) literature provides epidemiological signal that has not been integrated with the Selye-Peat mechanistic framework; the developmental version of the cascade — how prenatal stress and early adversity map onto adult HPA-axis dysregulation, what the thymus-shrinking observations mean for child immunology — is plausible from the framework but under-developed in the corpus.

**Sex differences.** Selye worked mostly in male rats. Peat's progesterone-focused recovery protocol applies cleanly to women but is less clear for men. DHEA, pregnenolone, and testosterone substitute partially; the male-specific anti-stress steroidogenesis is sketched in fragments rather than developed systematically.

**The Soviet stress literature.** Meerson's bioenergetic extension of Selye, and the broader Soviet stress-research program of the 1960s through 1980s, remains poorly translated and poorly cited in the Anglophone literature. Whether Meerson's specific findings on mitochondrial damage and excitotoxicity replicate fully in modern laboratories is partly known and partly not.

**Quantitative dose-response.** Peat consistently recommended progesterone, thyroid, gelatin, sugar, salt, light, and the rest of the anti-stress hierarchy without specifying the dose-response curves with the precision that modern evidence-based medicine prefers. The Katharina Dalton clinical literature grounds some of the progesterone work; the broader stress-protection indication remains empirical.

**Trauma-specific physiology.** Peter Levine's somatic experiencing and Bessel van der Kolk's trauma literature address a specific subtype of inescapable-stress exposure — single-event trauma with arrested fight-or-flight — that maps imperfectly onto the chronic helplessness physiology. The relationship between acute trauma physiology and chronic-cascade physiology is plausibly close but not engaged in the Peat corpus.

**Chronic vs acute markers.** The corpus contains scattered observations about which biomarkers distinguish acute (adaptive) from chronic (pathological) stress responses, but a systematic clinical panel — temperature, pulse, lactate, free fatty acids, T3, progesterone, total CO2, endotoxin markers — that would allow practitioners to stage a patient along the cascade has not been laid out. Practitioners working in the framework largely do this by feel.

**HPA-axis pharmacology.** The interaction between exogenous corticosteroids (prednisone for autoimmune disease) and the protective-steroid pool was sketched by Peat ("you can take a cortisol pill or prednisone and have very little harm from it if you're fairly saturated with progesterone, DHEA, and testosterone") but the formal pharmacology of the cortisol-progesterone-DHEA buffering relationship has not been worked out at the level of dosing recommendations.

**Modern environmental contributors.** The corpus references seed oils, endotoxin, and chronic psychological stress as substrate-loaders for the cascade. The contributions of EMF exposure, microplastics, heavy-metal accumulation, and recent vaccine-related immune dysregulation are largely outside the corpus.

**The hormesis biphasic curve.** Peat's strong claim that the wellness-culture stressors (cold plunge, sauna stacking, fasted training, intermittent fasting) push patients toward the chronic cascade is supported by mechanism but undertested in long-term outcome studies. The Finnish sauna mortality data, the Norwegian endurance-cardiac literature, and the cold-exposure brown-fat research are largely post-Peat and have not been engaged by Peat-aligned practitioners in detail. See [[hormesis]] for the framework-level discussion.
