# Free Fatty Acids

In mainstream medicine, free fatty acids are treated as a routine energy substrate -- mobilized during fasting, burned by muscles, and otherwise not given much thought. Ray Peat treated them as one of the most dangerous variables in the body. A persistent elevation of free fatty acids in the blood is, in his framework, the common thread linking [[diabetes]], [[heart-failure|heart failure]], [[cancer]], [[aging]], and [[shock]]. Free fatty acids suppress [[glucose]] oxidation, poison the [[mitochondria]], amplify [[stress]] hormones, and drive the metabolic shift from efficient oxidative energy production toward the fermentative state Peat identified with disease and death. "In recent years, people are seeing that the level of free fatty acids -- which in our population means mostly unsaturated fats -- has an extremely close connection to your likelihood of dying from just about anything: shock, aging, cancer, heart disease, and infection," he explained in a [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 episode on diabetes and saturated fats]].

The critical distinction, and the one that separates Peat's analysis from conventional lipid science, is that not all free fatty acids are equally harmful. The damage depends overwhelmingly on the degree of unsaturation. Saturated free fatty acids, in Peat's framework, are protective and self-limiting. Polyunsaturated free fatty acids are toxic and self-amplifying. Most of what follows concerns the latter -- because most of what circulates in a modern person's bloodstream is the latter.

## What Free Fatty Acids Are

A dietary fat -- whether [[butter]], [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], or corn oil -- consists of fatty acid chains attached to a glycerol molecule, forming triglycerides. In that bound form, fats are relatively inert and water-insoluble, which is why they can be stored safely in fat cells. Free fatty acids are what results when enzymes (lipases) break these bonds during stress, fasting, or hormonal signaling, releasing individual fatty acid chains into the bloodstream.

As Peat explained to listeners in a [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 episode on diabetes and nerve damage]]: "When you need energy, you have to activate enzymes that break the free fatty acids loose. When that happens from stress, hunger, or fasting, these free fatty acids get into the bloodstream, enter cells, and are available for energy production in the absence of glucose. The trouble is that process also tends to keep the stress going."

The distinction matters because a fatty acid's biological behavior depends on its chemical structure. A saturated fatty acid like [[stearic-acid|stearic acid]] has a long, stable chain of carbon atoms fully bonded with hydrogen. It resists oxidation, burns cleanly in the mitochondria, and -- crucially -- inhibits the stress hormones that triggered its own release. A polyunsaturated fatty acid like [[linoleic-acid|linoleic acid]] or [[arachidonic-acid|arachidonic acid]] has multiple double bonds that make it chemically unstable, prone to oxidation, and toxic to the very mitochondria that are supposed to burn it. These PUFA-derived free fatty acids do the opposite of calming the stress response: they amplify it.

## The Vicious Circle: How PUFA Turns Stress Into a Spiral

The core mechanism Peat described is a feedback loop. When blood sugar drops -- from fasting, stress, or inadequate food -- [[adrenaline]] is released to mobilize stored energy. Adrenaline activates the lipase enzymes in fat cells, which break triglycerides apart and release free fatty acids into the blood.

In a body whose fat stores are predominantly saturated, these released fatty acids provide clean fuel and then shut the process down. As Peat wrote in his newsletter *Fats, Functions & Malfunctions*: "In good health, especially in children, the stress hormones are produced only in the amount needed, because of negative feedback from the free saturated fatty acids, which inhibit the production of adrenalin and adrenal steroids, and eating protein and carbohydrate will quickly end the stress."

But when fat stores are loaded with [[PUFA]] -- the condition of virtually every person raised on a modern diet of [[seed-oils|seed oils]] -- the feedback goes in the wrong direction. "When the fat stores contain mainly PUFA, the free fatty acids in the serum will be mostly linoleic acid and arachidonic acid, and smaller amounts of other unsaturated fatty acids. These PUFA stimulate the stress hormones, ACTH, [[cortisol]], adrenaline, [[glucagon]], and [[prolactin]], which increase lipolysis, producing more fatty acids in a vicious circle." The stress response, rather than being self-limiting, becomes self-amplifying.

Peat distilled this contrast sharply in his [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 KMUD episode]]: "If you compare stearic acid (butter/beef fat) and linoleic acid (corn oil), the butter turns off adrenaline and cortisol, while the corn oil turns them on."

The physical chemistry explains why polyunsaturated fats are preferentially released during stress. As Peat described, lipolytic enzymes act on the surface of the fat droplet inside the cell. Fatty acids with greater water solubility are more exposed to these enzymes and liberated first. The omega-3 fatty acids, with only two saturated carbons in their tail, are the first to go; the omega-6 fatty acids next. Saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids are selectively retained. This means that during stress, the bloodstream is flooded with precisely the fatty acids that are most toxic and most capable of perpetuating the stress.

## The Randle Cycle: Free Fatty Acids vs. Glucose

The mechanism by which free fatty acids suppress [[glucose]] oxidation was described by P.J. Randle in 1963 and became one of the most important concepts in Peat's framework -- the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]. Peat explained it plainly in his [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|2011 diabetes episode]]: "Some call it the Randall Cycle, but it's just a competition. When you raise your free fatty acids, you inhibit the ability to oxidize glucose. Stress increases free fatty acids. Oxidizing glucose is what you need to overcome stress."

The inhibition operates at specific enzymatic points. Peat identified at least two: "There are two clear points where free fatty acids inhibit the use of glucose: [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|pyruvate dehydrogenase]] (which you need to burn glucose) and they stimulate glucagon. Glucagon turns on the synthesis of glucose at the expense of protein. Glucagon then stimulates the release of *more* fatty acids."

When free fatty acids block pyruvate dehydrogenase, glucose can still enter the cell, but it cannot be fully oxidized in the mitochondria. Instead, it is converted to [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] -- the hallmark of the fermentative, low-energy metabolic state that Peat associated with [[cancer]], [[aging]], and every degenerative disease. As he wrote in his newsletter *Glucose and Sucrose for Diabetes*: "Free fatty acids suppress mitochondrial respiration, leading to increased glycolysis (producing lactic acid) to maintain cellular energy. The suppression of mitochondrial respiration increases the production of toxic free radicals, and the decreased [[carbon-dioxide]] makes the proteins more susceptible to attack by free radicals."

The lactic acid produced under these conditions creates its own problems. It stimulates the release of [[endorphins]], which are themselves lipolytic -- releasing still more free fatty acids. Acting through inflammatory [[cytokines]] like interleukin-6, lactate shifts the hormonal balance toward catabolism and tissue wasting. The free fatty acids and the lactic acid together inhibit pyruvate dehydrogenase, compounding the block on glucose oxidation.

This is why Peat rejected the conventional understanding of diabetes as a disease of excessive sugar. The real problem, in his view, was excessive fat in the bloodstream blocking the cell's ability to use sugar. "Diabetics typically have elevated lactate, which shows that glucose doesn't have a problem getting into their cells, just getting oxidized," he wrote in an email exchange on diabetes. The glucose gets in; it just cannot be burned properly, because free fatty acids have disabled the oxidative machinery.

## Free Fatty Acids and the Heart

The failing heart provided Peat with one of his clearest illustrations of the free fatty acid problem. In a [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|2013 interview on energy and metabolism]], he traced the sequence: "When you're sitting at rest, the quiet muscle burns almost pure sugar and oxygen -- pure glucose and oxygen. But as soon as it starts exercising, it shifts over to fat oxidation. At maximum exercise, it's burning mostly fat and very little oxygen. It produces quite a bit of lactic acid because it's wasting sugar."

The failing heart, he continued, "does the same thing. It's exerting its maximum, and in doing that, it's burning fatty acids primarily rather than sugar." The shift to fat oxidation reduces [[carbon-dioxide]] production, impairs calcium regulation, and progressively degrades the heart muscle's ability to contract.

In his newsletter *Heart and Hormones*, Peat described the therapeutic principle that followed: "This sequence, from reduced efficiency of energy production to heart failure, can be opposed by agents that reduce the availability of fatty acids and promote the oxidation of glucose. [[Niacinamide]] inhibits the release of free fatty acids from the tissues, and [[thyroid]] sustains the oxidation of glucose." He noted that the FDA had approved a drug to inhibit fatty acid oxidation in the heart, but that niacinamide already accomplished a related effect by preventing the free fatty acids from being released in the first place.

A small amount of sugar proved remarkably effective in the experimental literature Peat cited. Providing diabetic or fasting animals with just 0.8% sucrose in their drinking water "restored the calcium binding and heart function, without increasing either thyroid hormone or insulin." By lowering free fatty acids, the sugar allowed glucose oxidation to resume, and the heart recovered.

## Estrogen: The Master Promoter of Free Fatty Acids

[[Estrogen]] occupies a central position in Peat's account of free fatty acid toxicity because it drives the process at multiple levels simultaneously. In his [[2013-07-17-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-energy-and-metabolism|2013 EastWest Healing interview]], Peat laid out the mechanism: "Estrogen preferentially causes [polyunsaturated fats] to be liberated from storage. So under the influence of estrogen, all of the free fatty acids increase. Women have, on average, a higher level of free fatty acids circulating. Estrogen preferentially brings the polyunsaturated fats out of storage, especially the very long-chain polyunsaturated ones. These are the ones that most interfere with other levels of [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]]."

The interference operates at every step of the thyroid axis. Estrogen blocks the enzymes that secrete thyroid hormone from the gland. The polyunsaturated free fatty acids it liberates displace [[thyroxine]] from its transport proteins in the blood. They inhibit the conversion of T4 to the active T3 form. And they suppress the tissue response to thyroid hormone at the cellular level. "At many levels, estrogen and the polyunsaturated fats are working together to inhibit the function of the thyroid hormone and the oxidative system," Peat concluded.

His newsletter *Fats and Degeneration* described the additional mechanism through which estrogen promotes [[arachidonic-acid|arachidonic acid]] release specifically: "Estrogen activates the phospholipase which liberates arachidonic acid, and [[progesterone]] inhibits that phospholipase." The arachidonic acid, once free, converts into [[prostaglandins]] -- the inflammatory mediators that Peat considered "probably our biggest inflammatory problem."

Women's higher susceptibility to [[diabetes]], [[autoimmune-diseases|autoimmune diseases]], and [[alzheimers-disease|Alzheimer's disease]] connects directly to this mechanism. As Peat noted in *Fats, Functions & Malfunctions*: "Women are known to have a greater susceptibility than men to lipolysis, with higher levels of free fatty acids in the serum and liver, because of the effects of estrogen and related hormones." The higher circulating DHA in women's blood, the greater incidence of dementia (two to three times higher than men), and the metabolic shift toward fat oxidation that estrogen produces all follow from this single hormonal effect.

## Growth Hormone and the Death Hormone

[[Growth-hormone|Growth hormone]] entered Peat's analysis as another driver of the free fatty acid problem. Like adrenaline, growth hormone mobilizes fatty acids from storage, but it adds a specific bias: it promotes the oxidation of the most unsaturated fatty acids, "in a process that appears to specifically waste energy," as Peat wrote in his newsletter on growth hormone.

The connection to aging was direct. W.D. Denckla's search for a pituitary "death hormone" (DECO -- decreasing consumption of oxygen) led him to observe that removing the pituitary drastically slowed aging in experimental animals. Peat saw the mechanism clearly: "Simply increasing the amount of free fatty acids in the blood will act like DECO or 'the death hormone.'" Growth hormone, by chronically elevating free fatty acids and biasing their composition toward polyunsaturated types, creates the metabolic conditions that Denckla had identified as the drivers of aging.

The onset at puberty is telling. Growth hormone rises, free fatty acids increase, a mild diabetes-like insulin resistance develops, and this "tends to increase progressively with age." Estrogen, which stimulates both [[prolactin]] and growth hormone secretion, compounds the effect. Peat noted that "thirty years ago, people were warning that estrogen contraceptives might produce diabetes, because they caused chronic elevation of growth hormone and free fatty acids."

## Free Fatty Acids, Serotonin, and the Brain

The relationship between free fatty acids and [[serotonin]] was one of the more surprising connections in Peat's framework. In a [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|2011 discussion of glycemia and sugar]], he explained: "Actually, it is free fatty acids that cause your brain to take up more tryptophan and make more serotonin. You produce more free fatty acids when your blood sugar is low and when you are under stress. So, stress, starvation, or hypoglycemia will make you produce free fatty acids, which send serotonin into your brain and trigger the cortisol stress reaction."

This overturns the popular claim that eating sugar raises serotonin. The mechanism runs through [[albumin]]. Free fatty acids in the blood displace [[tryptophan]] from albumin binding sites, allowing more tryptophan to cross the blood-brain barrier and be converted to serotonin. Since serotonin activates the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis, the result is more cortisol, more lipolysis, and more free fatty acids -- another self-amplifying loop.

The brain accumulates PUFA progressively with age, and every night the problem intensifies. As Peat described in a [[2019-01-24-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fats-and-questions|2019 interview on fats]]: "Every night your brain is renewing itself, but unfortunately, during the night, free fatty acids in the blood rise. By the time you're 40, the brain finds polyunsaturated fatty acids in the bloodstream that add to the damage. Every night when you're over the age of 40, your brain is at risk of deteriorating faster."

The nightly rise in free fatty acids is a consequence of the overnight fast. Without incoming food, glycogen stores deplete, adrenaline rises, and lipolysis begins. In a body loaded with PUFA, this nightly cycle delivers a fresh dose of polyunsaturated free fatty acids to every organ, including the brain. The progressive accumulation of PUFA in brain tissue with age, combined with this nightly exposure, provides a mechanism for the gradual cognitive decline Peat associated with the modern diet.

## Shock: The Acute Catastrophe

If chronic elevation of free fatty acids drives the slow diseases of aging, their acute surge drives the fast disaster of [[shock]]. Peat wrote in *Fats, Functions & Malfunctions*: "When stress is very intense, as in trauma or sepsis, the reaction of liberating fatty acids can become dangerously counter-productive, producing the state of shock. In shock, the liberation of free fatty acids interferes with the use of glucose for energy and causes cells to take up water and calcium (depleting blood volume and reducing circulation) and to leak ATP, enzymes, and other cell contents, in something like a systemic inflammatory state often leading to death."

The proof that PUFA is centrally involved came from a striking experimental finding: "The remarkable resistance of 'essential fatty acid deficient' animals to shock shows that the polyunsaturated fats are centrally involved in the maladaptive reactions of shock." Animals raised without dietary PUFA simply did not develop the cascading cellular failure that kills animals in shock. The cellular changes of shock -- calcium retention, membrane leakiness, suppressed energy production -- are, as Peat observed, the same changes seen in aging and degenerative disease, compressed into hours rather than decades.

## Excitotoxicity and Neurodegeneration

Free fatty acids participate in [[excitotoxicity]] -- the process by which overstimulation of nerve cells causes their death through calcium overload. [[Estrogen]] increases the activity of the excitatory neurotransmitter [[glutamate]], and glutamate in turn increases the release of free fatty acids. [[DHA]], more potently even than arachidonic acid, inhibits the reuptake of excitatory amino acids, prolonging their toxic effects. Increased intracellular calcium activates phospholipases, liberating still more free fatty acids from cell membranes, creating yet another self-reinforcing destructive cycle.

Peat saw this as a probable factor in [[alzheimers-disease|Alzheimer's disease]], and noted that "the process by which excitotoxicity kills a cell is probably a foreshortened version of the aging process." Higher levels of DHA and total PUFA in the plasma are found in demented patients, and the breakdown products of DHA in the brain, including neuroprostanes, accumulate with aging.

## The PUFA Detoxification Bottleneck

In an email exchange on PUFA, Peat described the body's detoxification pathway: "The main way they are detoxified is by attaching glucuronic acid, making them water soluble, so they leave in the urine. Keeping the free fatty acids low in relation to albumin, they will largely be carried bound to the albumin to the liver." The liver, when well-energized with thyroid hormone and glycogen, can process and excrete excess PUFA as a toxin.

But there is a bottleneck. Fat cells preferentially oxidize saturated fatty acids for their own energy, so the stores tend to become more unsaturated with age regardless of current diet. The PUFA are more water-soluble and easier to release during stress. Over decades, this means the fat stores become increasingly toxic, and each stress event releases a more poisonous payload than the last. "As the fat tissues become more burdened with arachidonic acid, they release it more easily in response to moderately lipolytic stress signals," Peat wrote in *Fats and Degeneration*. "This could explain the increased levels of free fatty acids and lipid peroxidation that occur with aging."

Several studies Peat cited suggested that the degree of unsaturation in stored fats is fundamentally related to lifespan: long-lived species have lower degrees of unsaturation in their tissue fats, and caloric restriction (which slows aging) decreases the age-related accumulation of fatty acids with four and five double bonds.

## Reducing Free Fatty Acids: Practical Interventions

Peat's therapeutic logic followed directly from the mechanisms he described. The goal was to keep free fatty acids low -- especially polyunsaturated ones -- and to restore the conditions for complete [[glucose]] oxidation:

**[[Niacinamide]]** was Peat's first-line recommendation for suppressing free fatty acid release. As he explained in the [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|2014 nerve damage episode]]: "Niacinamide has many effects, but the first one is inhibiting the lipase that liberates fats from your fat stores." In his newsletter *Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context*, he added that niacinamide "also helps to limit the wasteful conversion of glucose into fat" and noted evidence that diabetics are chronically deficient in niacin, possibly because excess free fatty acids divert tryptophan from niacin synthesis into serotonin synthesis.

**[[Aspirin]]** works through complementary mechanisms. It inhibits lipolysis by acting on both phospholipase and the hormone-sensitive adipose lipase. It blocks the conversion of liberated arachidonic acid into inflammatory [[prostaglandins]]. And it shifts mitochondrial metabolism away from fat oxidation and toward glucose oxidation, increasing [[carbon-dioxide]] production. As Peat wrote in *Aspirin, Brain, and Cancer*: "The shift away from fat oxidation under the influence of aspirin doesn't lead to an accumulation of free fatty acids in the circulation, since aspirin inhibits the release of fatty acids from both phospholipids and triglycerides. [[Estrogen]] has the opposite effects." He recommended aspirin in the evening specifically, "to prevent the increase of free fatty acids during the night."

**[[Sugar]] and frequent meals** suppress lipolysis directly by maintaining blood sugar and insulin levels. When a person eats adequate carbohydrate, insulin inhibits the lipase enzymes in fat cells, reducing the release of free fatty acids. As Peat wrote: "When a normal person, or even a 'type 2 diabetic,' is given a large dose of sugar, there is a suppression of lipolysis, and the concentration of free fatty acids in the bloodstream decreases." [[Fructose]] is the most effective carbohydrate for restoring sodium retention and opposing the metabolic consequences of elevated free fatty acids.

**[[Coconut-oil|Coconut oil]]** occupies a unique position. Its short and medium-chain saturated fatty acids are oxidized rapidly -- "like sugar," as Peat described them -- and compete with the stored PUFA for oxidative machinery. In an email on the Randle cycle, Peat explained: "When the body contains a lot of PUFA, eating coconut oil increases oxidative metabolism, partly because of the shorter fatty acids that are more quickly oxidized, like sugar, and partly because of the antimetabolic effects of the PUFA that they displace." A small amount with each meal provides a few hours of thyroid-supportive, anti-PUFA effect.

**[[Thyroid]] support** activates the mitochondrial oxidative enzymes that burn glucose completely to carbon dioxide, reducing the cell's dependence on fatty acid oxidation. Thyroid also increases mitochondrial [[cardiolipin]], the phospholipid essential for cytochrome c oxidase activity, and promotes the liver's ability to detoxify and excrete PUFA.

**[[Progesterone]]** opposes estrogen's pro-lipolytic effects, specifically inhibiting the phospholipase that liberates arachidonic acid. [[DHEA]] and [[pregnenolone]] provide additional protective opposition to the estrogen-cortisol axis.

**Avoiding [[PUFA]]** in the diet is the most fundamental intervention. Every gram of polyunsaturated fat consumed either gets burned (producing [[lipid-peroxidation|lipid peroxidation]] products), stored (increasing the toxicity of future stress responses), or must be detoxified by the liver. Reducing intake allows the body's stores to gradually become more saturated over time -- a process Peat estimated requires years, but which progressively reduces the danger of each stress event.

**Adequate [[salt]]** prevents the sodium depletion that triggers adrenaline, which triggers lipolysis. "A low salt diet increases the free fatty acids, leading to insulin resistance," Peat wrote in *When Energy Fails*, noting that salt restriction increases adrenaline and, through the chain of lipolysis, worsens the very metabolic problems it was supposed to address.

## The Unified Picture

Free fatty acids are not a peripheral detail in Peat's framework. They are the metabolic crossroads where [[stress]], [[estrogen]], [[PUFA]], [[thyroid]] suppression, [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]] production, and [[aging]] converge. The pattern repeats across conditions: stress liberates free fatty acids; if those acids are polyunsaturated, they block glucose oxidation, suppress thyroid function, promote inflammation, stimulate further stress hormone release, and drive the cell toward the fermentative, low-energy state that defines disease. The body, designed to use saturated free fatty acids as a self-correcting energy reserve during brief stress, is defeated by a fat composition it never evolved to handle.

As Peat put it in *Fats, Functions & Malfunctions*: "There is a growing recognition that a persistent increase of free fatty acids in the serum, which is seen in shock, heart failure, and aging, indicates a bad prognosis." His entire dietary and therapeutic program -- the sugar, the coconut oil, the aspirin, the niacinamide, the thyroid, the avoidance of PUFA -- can be understood as a systematic effort to keep free fatty acids low and saturated, restoring the conditions under which stress is self-limiting rather than self-destroying.
