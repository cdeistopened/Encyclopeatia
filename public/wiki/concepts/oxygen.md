# Oxygen

Oxygen is the molecule at the electron-accepting end of the respiratory chain. Sugar's energized electrons, released by glycolysis and the Krebs cycle inside [[mitochondria]], flow down the cytochromes and reach oxygen at cytochrome c oxidase, where they combine with two hydrogens to form water. [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]] is produced upstream, in the [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|decarboxylation]] of pyruvate and the Krebs-cycle steps that follow — oxygen's exhaust, the gas left over after the carbon skeleton has been completely oxidized. Ray Peat's framework inverts the mainstream emphasis. The thing that limits health in modern life is not the supply of oxygen, which is essentially unlimited in any room at sea level, but the *delivery* of oxygen, which is gated almost entirely by CO2. Hemoglobin in a person hyperventilating at sea level is saturated to 99 percent and unable to release that oxygen at the tissue; hemoglobin in a person quietly breathing at 10,000 feet is saturated to 88 percent and delivering oxygen efficiently into every capillary. The mainstream framing reads the first state as healthy and the second as sick. The reverse is closer to the truth.

This article is the pair to [[carbon-dioxide]], which carries the full mechanism. The CO2 page is not re-walked here. What this page does is hold up oxygen itself: what it does, why "more is better" is the wrong frame, where it actually damages, and what the practical implications are for the person trying to raise tissue oxygen rather than blood saturation.

## What oxygen actually does

In the cell, oxygen does one thing that matters: it pulls electrons off the end of the respiratory chain. The four-electron reduction of O2 to two H2O is what makes the path from glucose to ATP thermodynamically possible. Without an electron acceptor, glycolysis stalls at lactate, the Krebs cycle backs up, and ATP yield collapses to roughly two molecules per glucose instead of the thirty-six available through complete oxidation. The full mechanism is at [[energy-production]] and [[oxidative-metabolism]]. The load-bearing claim for this page is that oxygen is the *sink*, not the *driver*. The drivers are the substrate (glucose), the hormonal signal that opens [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|the gateway enzyme]] ([[thyroid]] hormone), the cofactors that move electrons, and the lipid environment in which the chain sits ([[cardiolipin]]). Oxygen is downstream of all of them.

Peat made the point compactly on the 2020 [[2020-01-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-oxygen-saturation-lactic-acid-thyroid-vaccines-pufas|*One Radio Network* episode on oxygen saturation]]: "Oxygen is receiving electrons. Sunlight puts electrons into the form of sugar. That is the source of all our energy: energized electrons from the sun in sugar. When we consume it, if we are really healthy, it goes all the way down to oxygen, forming water. So the cycle is complete. But if we aren't healthy, then the sugar electron energy stops very near where it started and comes out as lactic acid." The work oxygen does is to close the loop. If anything upstream is broken — substrate, cofactor, enzyme conformation, mitochondrial structure — adding more oxygen does not fix the upstream defect. It can make the defect worse, by giving the broken machinery more material to leak from.

The combustion metaphor is wrong at the level of mechanism. The cell does not burn glucose with oxygen the way a flame burns wood with air; it transfers electrons stepwise down a chain of carriers, captures most of the released energy as ATP, and uses oxygen at the last step only because oxygen is the most thermodynamically favorable acceptor available. Substituting "fuel for the body" for "terminal electron acceptor" produces every popular-physiology mistake at once: that more oxygen means more energy, that hyperbaric chambers should improve healing, that oximetry numbers are a fitness score, that altitude is a deprivation rather than an exposure.

## The Bohr effect: why more CO2 means more tissue oxygen

The most important fact about oxygen, in Peat's framework, is that hemoglobin's affinity for it is regulated by [[carbon-dioxide]]. This is the [[concepts/bag-breathing|Bohr effect]] — more precisely, the Bohr-Haldane effect, when both directions of the coupling are considered. In the lungs, where oxygen pressure is high and CO2 has just been exhaled, hemoglobin binds oxygen tightly. In the capillaries, where tissue CO2 is high (because cells are producing it), the CO2 binds to hemoglobin's amine groups, allosterically changes the protein's conformation, and releases the bound oxygen exactly where it is needed. CO2 is the signal that says "deliver oxygen here." Hemoglobin without that signal holds onto its oxygen and carries it back to the lungs.

Peat developed this on the 2010 [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|*Ask the Herb Doctor* altitude episode]]: "The Haldane-Bohr effect explains the fact that oxygen, when it sticks to hemoglobin, changes the hemoglobin molecule, causing the carbon dioxide to come loose. And when you have a high concentration of carbon dioxide down in your capillaries, the carbon dioxide sticking to the hemoglobin causes the oxygen to come loose and become available to the tissues." The same effect, in milder form, applies to proteins throughout the body. Adequate tissue CO2 keeps every protein in a conformation that releases its bound oxygen where it is needed; inadequate CO2 locks oxygen to molecules where it can attack the polyunsaturated fats it should not be touching.

The clinical consequence is that pulse oximetry is reading the wrong number. A 99-percent saturation in a hyperventilating person tells you oxygen is bound to hemoglobin and unable to come off, not that tissues are oxygenated. An 88-percent saturation in a warm, relaxed person tells you the oxygen is *being used* — the meter is reading the equilibrium left after delivery, not the supply. From [[altitude-and-mortality|*Altitude and Mortality*]]: "The loss of carbon dioxide from the lungs in the presence of high oxygen pressure, the shift toward alkalosis, by the Bohr-Haldane effect increases the blood's affinity for oxygen, and restricts its delivery to the tissues, but because of the abundance of oxygen in the lungs, the blood is almost completely saturated with oxygen." High saturation can mean impaired delivery. On the 2020 *One Radio Network* episode Peat gave his personal calibration: "Just waking up in the morning when I'm feeling best, my oxygen saturation will go down to 90% or 89%. If I'm under stress, my finger gadget might go up to 98% or 99%... I think the average person ought to be around 93% to 95% saturation."

A few practical corollaries: cold fingers raise the reading (they do not extract oxygen from passing blood); warm fingers lower it. Hyperventilating raises it; relaxed nasal breathing lowers it. Altitude lowers it; hyperbaric chambers raise it. In every case the higher number tracks with worse utilization. "Raise tissue oxygen" and "raise blood oxygen saturation" are nearly opposite interventions.

## Hypoxia: real versus apparent

Genuine tissue hypoxia exists and is sometimes the right diagnosis. Pneumonia, severe asthma, pulmonary embolus, severe anemia, CO poisoning, congestive heart failure with pulmonary edema, and acute mountain sickness in a poorly-adapted climber all involve a real deficit at the cellular level. The diagnostic signature is pulse oximetry below about 90 percent in a warm patient, with cyanosis, dyspnea on exertion that does not resolve with rest, and laboratory evidence of reduced delivery or impaired exchange. In those cases, supplemental oxygen is the appropriate intervention and the Peat framing does not apply.

*Apparent* hypoxia is something else. The patient feels short of breath, dizzy, anxious, cold in the extremities, tingling around the mouth. The pulse oximetry reads 98 or 99. There is no pulmonary disease, no anemia, no carbon monoxide exposure. The presentation looks like oxygen deprivation but the meter shows oxygen abundance. What is happening, in Peat's reading, is that the patient is hyperventilating, blowing off CO2, alkalinizing the blood, locking oxygen onto hemoglobin, and starving the tissues of oxygen *delivery* despite full saturation. The same constellation appears in panic attacks, in chronic anxiety, in the hyperventilation syndrome of mid-twentieth-century internal medicine textbooks, and in the post-COVID fatigue states some clinicians now recognize as dysautonomic hyperventilation. Low CO2, high O2 saturation, tissue oxygen starvation. The fix is to restore CO2.

The two states demand opposite treatments. Real hypoxia: oxygen, treat the underlying pulmonary or cardiac problem. Apparent hypoxia: [[bag-breathing|bag breathing]], slow the respiratory rate, restore the chemoreceptor setpoint over weeks. Confusing the two — giving oxygen for a hyperventilation episode, or sending a patient with pneumonia home with a paper bag — does real harm. The clinical signs that distinguish them (warm extremities or cold, the pulse and temperature pattern, the presence or absence of tetany) are well known to internists who have not been trained out of them.

Peat treated the apparent-hypoxia state as the more common one in modern populations. A chronically [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid]] person, running on adrenaline and free fatty acids because the thyroid signal is too weak to maintain oxidative metabolism, will hyperventilate at rest. The hyperventilation drives down CO2, drives up lactate, locks oxygen onto hemoglobin, and produces the cold hands, brain fog, air hunger, and morning fatigue that no amount of inhaled oxygen will fix. From [[altitude-and-mortality|*Altitude and Mortality*]]: "If hypothyroid people, with increased adrenalin and lactate, are hyperventilating even at rest and at sea level, when they go to a high altitude where less oxygen is available, and their absorption of oxygen is impaired by lactic acidemia, their 'oxygen debt,' conceived as circulating lactic acid, is easily increased." The intervention is at the level of metabolism, not the level of oxygen supply.

## Oxygen toxicity: when too much causes damage

Peat returned repeatedly to a body of evidence mainstream medicine has known for a century and routinely fails to integrate: oxygen, at higher-than-ambient partial pressures and prolonged exposures, damages tissues. The mechanism is clean. In stressed mitochondria, or in tissues exposed to oxygen partial pressures higher than the chain can handle, electrons leak prematurely, generating superoxide and the hydroxyl radicals that drive [[lipid-peroxidation]]. PUFA in cellular membranes is the primary target. The aldehyde products (4-hydroxynonenal, malondialdehyde) attack cytochrome oxidase itself — the enzyme that should have consumed the oxygen safely. The defect compounds. Three settings make the toxicity unambiguous.

**Retinopathy of prematurity.** Twentieth-century neonatology gave high-concentration oxygen to premature infants on the assumption that since their lungs were underdeveloped, supplemental oxygen could only help. The result was a generation of blind babies. Excess oxygen, in the developing retina, drives vasoconstriction (oxygen displaces CO2, which is the vasodilator), abnormal vessel growth on rebound, retinal detachment, blindness. From [[aging-eyes-infant-eyes-and-excitable-tissues|*Aging Eyes, Infant Eyes, and Excitable Tissues*]]: "Premature babies are often given extra oxygen, which tends to cause vasoconstriction by displacing carbon dioxide." The mechanism is the Bohr effect running in reverse against the developing retinal vasculature. Yandell Henderson had already worked out in 1928 that oxygen given without CO2 would produce exactly this pattern of damage; the hospital practice of giving high-flow oxygen without CO2 mixing was a structural mistake driven by a misreading of the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation and never fully corrected.

**Hyperbaric overuse.** Hyperbaric oxygen therapy has real applications: CO poisoning, decompression sickness, gas embolism, refractory infections, select wound-healing indications in irradiated tissue. Outside those indications, the chamber is being marketed for athletic recovery, "anti-aging," cognitive enhancement, and general wellness. "At hyperbaric pressure, oxygen causes seizures, as well as damage to the lungs and other tissues" ([[aging-eyes-infant-eyes-and-excitable-tissues|*Aging Eyes*]]). The dose at which acute oxygen toxicity becomes clinically obvious is well above the partial pressure used in most wellness chambers, but the chronic effects of repeated mild hyperoxia — accelerated [[lipid-peroxidation]], displaced CO2, suppressed mitochondrial biogenesis — are exactly what the chamber claims to prevent.

**Lipid peroxidation acceleration.** Wherever PUFA has accumulated in cellular membranes — which is most modern tissue — higher oxygen partial pressures accelerate the peroxidation chain. The 100% oxygen of adult respiratory care produces measurable pulmonary lipid peroxidation within hours; 35-40% mixtures produce slower damage over days. The population-wide PUFA load that arrived with seed oils made oxygen therapy more dangerous than it had been in Henderson's era — same oxygen, more peroxidative damage. "The polyunsaturated fats make the lungs more sensitive to excess oxygen or hyperventilation, they make the eyes more sensitive to light, and they make the brain more sensitive to fatigue" ([[aging-eyes-infant-eyes-and-excitable-tissues|*Aging Eyes*]]). The toxicity scales with the lipid environment.

The general rule: oxygen at room-air partial pressure delivered to a CO2-rich tissue is benign; oxygen at higher partial pressures, or oxygen delivered to a CO2-depleted tissue, is damaging. The cell did not evolve in air enriched beyond 21 percent oxygen, and forcing higher partial pressures on it produces predictable harm in proportion to dose and duration.

## What raises tissue oxygen

Because tissue oxygen is gated by delivery, the interventions are those that raise CO2 or that improve the oxygen-using capacity of the mitochondrion. Each is developed at its own page:

- **[[bag-breathing|Bag breathing]]** — fastest demonstration of the Bohr effect; rebreathing exhaled air raises inspired CO2, restores delivery, warms the hands within a minute.
- **[[walking|Walking]] and low-intensity activity** — raise CO2 production through mild metabolic demand without driving hyperventilation.
- **[[altitude|Altitude]] adaptation** — shifts the chemoreceptor setpoint, raises baseline CO2, induces mitochondrial biogenesis that lingers for months after return to sea level.
- **[[thyroid|Thyroid]] optimization** — restores cytochrome oxidase activity, NAD+/NADH ratio, and mitochondrial mass. Hypothyroidism is, at the tissue level, an oxygen-utilization defect.
- **Adequate sugar** — glucose oxidation yields more CO2 per oxygen (respiratory quotient ~1.0) than fat (~0.7) or ketones.
- **PUFA depletion + saturated fats** — palmitate displaces PUFA in [[cardiolipin]], restoring cytochrome oxidase function.
- **Warmth** — cold drives adrenaline, free fatty acids, the Randle block, and lactate.
- **Carbonated mineral water and bicarbonate** — modest CO2 elevation through the gut.
- **External CO2 exposure** — carbonated baths, CO2 bags at room temperature. CO2 is more soluble in tissue than in air, so it moves in against the apparent gradient.
- **Sun and [[red-light|red light]]** — activate cytochrome c oxidase directly.
- **[[aspirin|Aspirin]]** — mild mitochondrial uncoupler; suppresses the prostaglandin cascade that drives hyperventilation.

The list deliberately omits "deep breathing exercises," oxygen bars, hyperbaric chambers, and supplemental oxygen at altitude. Those raise blood saturation while lowering tissue delivery — the opposite of what is wanted.

## What lowers tissue oxygen

The mirror list:

- **[[pufa|PUFA accumulation]]** — cardiolipin substitution, aldehyde adducts on cytochrome oxidase, iron-catalyzed peroxidation. Full mechanism at [[pufa]] and [[lipid-peroxidation]].
- **Iron overload** — accelerates Fenton chemistry on PUFA.
- **[[hyperventilation]]** — locks oxygen onto hemoglobin, produces the apparent-hypoxia state.
- **Anemia** — reduces hemoglobin; a real-supply problem distinct from the delivery problems.
- **[[hypothyroidism]]** — suppresses cytochrome oxidase, mitochondrial biogenesis, and CO2 production.
- **Chronic [[stress]]** — adrenaline raises respiratory drive, mobilizes FFA, raises lactate.
- **[[endotoxin]]** — suppresses mitochondrial respiration via cortisol and inflammation.
- **Cold** — same adrenaline-FFA-lactate cascade as stress.
- **Estrogen excess** — raises respiratory drive directly; the typically lower CO2 tolerance of women traces in part to this.
- **Sleep apnea and disordered breathing** — variable hypercapnia followed by arousal-driven hyperventilation; no stable setpoint.
- **Chronic endurance training** — "very well-trained athletes typically go around with an elevated lactic acid in their blood. Even days after their last exercise, they've suppressed their carbon dioxide" ([[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 KMUD altitude]]).
- **Excess supplemental oxygen without CO2** — displaces CO2, lowers delivery despite raising saturation.

## Counter-arguments

**"More oxygen is always better."** The premise behind consumer oxygen therapy, the hyperbaric wellness market, and the standard hospital response to dyspnea. The mechanistic counter: oxygen at supraphysiologic partial pressure does not increase tissue delivery once hemoglobin is saturated. The 1-2 percent additional saturation gained by going from room air to 30 percent oxygen in a person whose Bohr effect is intact makes no delivery difference. The 30 percent additional saturation gained by going from 90 to 100 percent in a CO2-depleted person makes delivery *worse* by locking oxygen onto hemoglobin. The "more is better" position survives on the strength of the cases where it is true (acute hypoxia, CO poisoning, decompression sickness) being widely known, and the cases where it is false (hyperventilation syndrome, wellness hyperbaric) being less examined.

**Hyperbaric advocacy.** The HBO industry promotes the chamber for stroke recovery, TBI, cognitive enhancement, athletic performance, anti-aging. The peer-reviewed evidence outside FDA-approved indications is thin and selectively published. The mechanistic case ignores both the Bohr effect (which gates delivery) and the lipid-peroxidation acceleration from forcing oxygen partial pressure above what the chain can handle. Some applications may prove out (wound healing in radiation-injured tissue is the strongest case); the wellness-market expansion is well ahead of the evidence.

**Pulse oximetry as the gold standard.** Emergency medicine treats SpO2 as the primary respiratory vital sign. Peat's objection: the number does not track tissue oxygen except in real-hypoxia states. The mechanistic Peat position is that the meter reads hemoglobin saturation, not tissue delivery, and the inference from saturation to delivery requires a functional Bohr effect, which in modern populations is often not the case. The disagreement is not over what the meter reads but over what it means.

**The "more breathing = more oxygen" intuition.** Yoga, Pilates, diaphragmatic-breathing training, and athletic coaching all tell people that deeper, fuller breathing improves oxygen status. The intuition is exactly inverted. Deeper breathing at rest is hyperventilation by another name; it blows off CO2 and worsens tissue delivery. Relaxed nasal breathing producing a *slower* rate and longer comfortable breath-hold is the actual marker of high tissue oxygen. The Wim Hof method — trained controlled hyperventilation — is the strong form of the practice Peat warned against. The subjective effects (euphoria, tingling, time distortion) are the symptoms of acute hypocapnia repackaged as a feature. The chronic adaptations are precisely opposite to altitude adaptation.

**The Warburg-without-Peat reading.** Mainstream oncology treats aerobic lactate production as a target rather than a diagnosis — cancer cells produce lactate because they adapted to do so for proliferative advantage, and blocking the production is therapy. Peat's reading was that the lactate production is the *cause* of the cancerous state, reversible through CO2, thyroid, sugar, PUFA depletion. The two framings agree on the observation and disagree on what to do about it. The Peat framing implicates diet, hormones, and environmental PUFA in carcinogenesis and points away from patentable therapies; the mainstream framing points toward drug development.

**The exercise-physiology orthodoxy.** Most modern exercise physiology treats lactate as a benign intermediate or a useful fuel. Peat treated it as the polar opposite of CO2 and an active driver of mitochondrial damage. The textbook position is that VO2 max is the gold-standard fitness measure; the Peat position is that VO2 max measures the lactate threshold as much as cardiovascular capacity — how stress-tolerant the metabolism is, not how oxidatively healthy. The two metrics correlate in healthy young people and diverge in chronically stressed or PUFA-loaded ones.

## Data gaps

- **The "right" oxygen saturation.** Peat reported 88-90 percent as his best-feeling baseline and 93-95 percent as a reasonable average. Population variance, the interaction with altitude history, and the relationship to chronic CO2 status are not systematically mapped. The medical norm of 95-100 percent is operationally defined by what oximetry shows in resting adults, not by direct outcome correlation.
- **Bohr-effect quantitation in disease.** The shift in the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve under hyperventilation, hypothyroidism, anemia, and chronic stress is described in textbooks but rarely measured at the bedside.
- **Threshold for chronic oxygen toxicity.** The acute threshold (around 2.0 atm absolute for CNS toxicity, lower for pulmonary toxicity over hours) is known from dive medicine. The chronic threshold — what mildly elevated oxygen exposure, over what timescale, in what lipid environment, produces measurable damage — is not characterized.
- **CO2-supplemented oxygen therapy.** Henderson's 1928 case for mixing 7 percent CO2 with supplemental oxygen has never been formally re-tested, despite the rediscovery of "permissive hypercapnia" in mechanical ventilation as an evidence-based protective strategy.
- **Pulse oximetry under altered metabolic states.** How the meter performs in hypothyroid patients, in patients with PUFA-loaded cardiolipin, in patients with chronic respiratory alkalosis from anxiety — and whether the number is interpretable on the standard scale — is unaddressed in device validation literature.
- **The lactate paradox mechanism.** Why altitude-adapted athletes produce less lactate at maximal work is attributed to "altitude adaptation" without specification. Peat's CO2-retention explanation fits the data but is not consensus; bicarbonate-shift and cardiovascular-adaptation accounts compete for the same space.
- **Long-term hyperbaric exposure for wellness.** Whether repeated mild hyperbaric exposure produces cumulative oxidative damage, and whether the antioxidant adaptations it induces are net protective or net suppressive, is not characterized.
- **Mountain-population centenarian data.** The longevity-and-altitude correlation is robust at the population level but confounded with genetics, diet, activity, and cosmic-radiation profile. The CO2 mechanism is consistent with the data but not the only possible mechanism.
- **Developing-tissue sensitivities beyond the retina.** Retinopathy of prematurity establishes that the developing retina is sensitive to supraphysiologic oxygen; analogous brain, lung, and kidney sensitivities are less well characterized. Current neonatology titrates to 88-94 percent saturation in preterm infants as an empirical compromise rather than a mechanistically grounded protocol.
