# The Conditional Reflex

There is a Russian adjective, *uslovny*, that means *conditional* in the sense of *stipulated*, *agreed upon*, *contingent on a state of affairs*. It comes from a root that also gives the words for *covenant*, *condition of a contract*, and *speaking together*. When Ivan Pavlov used the phrase *uslovny refleks* in the 1920s to describe the brain function he had discovered, he meant: a reflex that the organism *recognizes* as contingent on a present arrangement of the world. The bell predicts the food today; if tomorrow the bell no longer predicts the food, the reflex will fade, because intelligence requires that the link not outlast the condition that produced it.

When the work was translated into English in the 1920s and 1930s, Pavlov's *uslovny refleks* became *conditioned reflex* — the past participle, naming a thing that has been *done to* the organism. The grammatical shift inverted the underlying philosophy. A conditional reflex is something the organism does; a conditioned reflex is something done to the organism. The first frame keeps the animal in the center as an intelligent, world-tracking creature; the second pushes the animal to the side and puts the experimenter at the center, with the animal as a machine in which behaviors are installed by repetition.

Peat returned to this slip many times across his corpus, but the canonical statement is Chapter 4 of his 1976 book *Mind and Tissue*, titled "Reflecting Conditions." He treated it as one of the load-bearing critiques in the whole framework — not a footnote about translation, but a diagnosis of how a century's most important physiological discovery got turned, in the United States, into the licensing document for everything from Skinnerian token economies to applied behavior analysis to behaviorist economics. The original conditional reflex was a mode of intelligence. The American conditioned reflex is a mode of mechanical compulsion. Recovering the difference is, for Peat, recovering Pavlov.

## The translation error (*uslovny* → *conditional*, not *conditioned*)

The linguistic argument is laid out in *Mind and Tissue* with unusual care, because Peat understood that the case rests on it. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|Ch. 4]]:

> In English, the word "condition" has two groups of meanings. One group clusters around the idea of "stipulation," as in "he accepts the proposal with certain conditions," and another cluster has to do with something closer to a thing's state of being, as in "the car (or athlete) is in good condition." Our English word derives from Latin for "speaking together," and in Russian there is a word, *uslovni*, with a similar origin relating to speech and stipulation. However, Russian has also adopted the Latin-based word, *konditsiya*, with its meaning limited to our "state of being" cluster, making it impossible to confuse the two meanings: Air-conditioning and athletic conditioning use the Latin-derived word, while contractual and linguistic conditions use the Slavic word. In Pavlov's work, the "conditional reflex" was understood as the "stipulated or agreed-upon (*uslovni*) reflex," but this essential point has been almost entirely lost in a false translation.

Russian, in other words, has two separate words for two separate concepts that English collapses into one. *Uslovni* covers contractual and linguistic stipulation — a covenant, an agreed-upon condition, a recognized contingency. *Konditsiya* covers state of being — the *condition* an athlete is in, the *conditioning* of a room's air. When Pavlov named his reflex, he used the first word. He meant: the organism's recognition of a stipulated, contingent regularity. The English translation chose *conditioned* — the past participle of *to condition* in the second sense, the *konditsiya* sense — and produced a meaning Pavlov never intended: a reflex that has been *put into a certain state* by an external process.

The slip is mechanical to a Russian-speaker. It is invisible to an English-speaker, because English has no separate word for the first sense. The word "condition" in English does double duty for both — for the stipulation (the *uslovny* sense) and for the state-of-being (the *konditsiya* sense) — and the choice of which sense to translate into governs whether you end up with Pavlov's actual concept or its opposite. The early American translators chose the wrong sense. The result, in Peat's read, was that the United States got a Pavlov who had never existed, and used him to authorize a psychology Pavlov would not have recognized.

The most pointed sentence in the chapter is Peat's aside about Watson:

> Watson presented himself as a student of the famous Pavlov, and argued for an unconscious psychology, with the result that our culture has lost the meaning of the century's greatest work in psychology and brain physiology. (Sometimes I wonder whether J. B. Watson wasn't vacationing in Bermuda during the time he claimed to be studying with Pavlov.)

The parenthetical is funny; the substantive claim under it is not. Watson, the founder of American behaviorism, advertised himself as Pavlov's American interpreter. The Pavlov he interpreted bears no recognizable relation to the Pavlov who wrote the Russian sources. The translation error was not just a translator's choice. It was the entry-point through which a Russian tradition that took the organism seriously got turned into an American tradition that did not.

## What Pavlov actually meant (organism-context coupling, not stimulus-response)

The conditional reflex, in Pavlov's own framing, is a kind of intelligent perception. The dog hears the bell, smells the food, learns that *here, today, in these conditions*, the two are linked. The reflex is the organism's *recognition* of the link. Pavlov was emphatic that this is a cognitive, holistic activity — not a stamp-and-impress installation of a motor program. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|Ch. 4]]:

> Pavlov was the son of a priest, and studied at a seminary school, so he was thoroughly saturated with the Greek-derived ideas of the Eastern Church. For Pavlov, the world's essential property is that it is full of potential, and that we will know more in the future than we do at present. This centrality of time permeates the tradition of Pavlov. If time is real and the world is the source of knowledge, then science is seen as a basic life function, in which the brain is the organ for knowing the world and maintaining an equilibrium with it. The essential feature of the conditional reflex, as understood by Pavlov, was its *temporariness*; if it is a recognition of a temporary conjunction of events in the world, intelligence requires that it not persist longer than the condition in the environment does. But when learning has to do with the way the world actually works, one insight is enough to fix the knowledge forever; Pavlov said that when a cat learns how to open the latch to its cage, such learning has taken place, and the cat is doing something equivalent to science.

Three properties drop out of this passage that are absent from the American "conditioned reflex":

**Temporariness.** A conditional reflex tracks a temporary state of the world. If the world changes, the reflex revises itself. Intelligence requires this — a brain that kept ringing every time it heard a bell, long after the bells had stopped being associated with food, would be a stupid brain. The American conditioned reflex, by contrast, is supposed to be *slow to decay*. The classical experimental paradigm tests *extinction* — the gradual dying-out of the response after the reinforcement is withdrawn — and the slow extinction is taken as a feature, not a bug, of the underlying mechanism. Peat: "in the West, our idea of the 'conditioned reflex' has been exactly the opposite: we have emphasized its difference from insight-learning by ascribing to it a kind of brainless sluggishness, in which the role of time is reversed — it is slow to form, and slow to decay."

**Insight.** A conditional reflex can be formed in a single trial when the conjunction it tracks is a real one. The cat that opens the latch is, in Pavlov's framing, *doing science* — assembling a perceptual gestalt of "this latch, this motion, opens this cage." The link is fixed not because it has been hammered in by repetition but because the cat has *recognized* a stable feature of the world. American behaviorism systematically excluded this kind of single-trial learning from its definition of conditioning, treating it as a separate phenomenon ("insight learning," after Köhler's apes) that the framework had to set aside as unexplained. Pavlov treated it as the *paradigm case* of the conditional reflex; the American tradition treated it as the exception that didn't fit.

**Organism-context coupling.** The conditional reflex is the organism's tracking of *this situation, here, now*. It is not a behavior detached from the perceptual field. The dog's salivation at the bell is a fragment of the dog's [[image-and-perception|*obraz*]] of the situation — the bell is now part of the perceptual gestalt of "food is coming," and the salivation is the appropriate organismic response to that gestalt. Strip away the gestalt and the salivation makes no sense. The American conditioning paradigm, by treating the salivation as an isolated motor response and the bell as an isolated stimulus, threw away exactly the part of the phenomenon that made it intelligent.

The contrast with the American reading was already at the surface in Pavlov's late writing. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|Ch. 4]]: "American Behaviorism has given to the phrase 'conditioned reflex' a connotation of 'involuntary, unconscious behavior which is built up by repetition.' In Pavlov's use of the phrase, though, there was no such implication. In his usage, this brain function can be instantaneous and insightful. His purpose was to explain the way we *are*." The Russian Pavlov was developing a theory of intelligent organismic perception. The American Watson was building a theory of mechanical stimulus-response. The translation hid the fact that they were doing different things.

Anokhin, in the post-Pavlovian Soviet tradition, was even sharper about what the American reading had thrown away. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|Ch. 4]], summarizing Anokhin: "there cannot exist a conditioned reflex of the heart, or of other isolated components. A fragmented, isolated approach results in an immense number of experimental errors, in particular, when a component of a reaction taken separately, often for methodological reasons, is considered to be some special conditioned reflex." The conditional reflex is a *whole-organism* activity. Asserting otherwise — locating the reflex in a specific organ, a specific neural pathway, a specific response channel — was, for the post-Pavlovian Russians, the methodological error that the American tradition committed as a matter of course. It is the same error treated in [[image-and-perception|*Image and Perception*]] under the heading of motor-program primacy: the American tradition consistently took an isolated output channel as the unit of analysis and missed the whole-organism activity that was the actual subject matter.

## Watson and Skinner's edifice resting on the wrong word

John Watson's 1913 paper "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" is conventionally dated as the founding document of American behaviorism. The paper claimed Pavlov as scientific predecessor and proposed that psychology should abandon the study of consciousness and limit itself to stimulus-response relationships objectively measurable from outside the organism. The Pavlov on which Watson rested his case was the *conditioned*-reflex Pavlov — the version in which the experimenter installs links in a passive organism by repetition. The Pavlov who actually existed — the *conditional*-reflex Pavlov — explicitly insisted on the centrality of consciousness, of insight, of the whole-organism perceptual field. Watson, on Peat's reading, never engaged the real Pavlov. He built his program on the mistranslated version and called it Pavlovian science.

B. F. Skinner extended the program in a particular direction. The Skinnerian *operant* conditioning paradigm (the rat in the Skinner box, pressing the lever for food) is even further from Pavlov's actual concept than the classical Watsonian paradigm. The operant paradigm dispenses entirely with the perception-of-conjunction that Pavlov took as the heart of the conditional reflex; it treats behavior as a stream of motor outputs whose frequency can be raised or lowered by the contingent application of reinforcement, without any reference to the organism's perceptual gestalt of the situation. Skinner argued explicitly against the use of "inner" or "mentalistic" vocabulary — the *obraz*, the perceptual integration, the organism's recognition of the contingency — and treated all of it as scientifically illegitimate. The Skinnerian framework is a maximally clean expression of what Peat called the American tradition: the organism as black box, behavior as motor output, learning as the shaping of output frequencies by external reinforcement.

The full edifice rests on the assumption that this picture is consistent with Pavlov's actual work. It is not. Pavlov's work is consistent with a perceptual-integration framework in which the conditional reflex is an instance of intelligent world-tracking. The Watson-Skinner edifice deleted the world-tracking and kept the mechanical link, and the entire subsequent literature in American psychology took the Watson-Skinner edifice as the established science of Pavlov.

Peat's diagnosis of why this happened is unsentimental. The translation error happened first; the political and economic interests that benefited from the mistranslation happened second. From [[book-mind-and-tissue|Ch. 4]]: "Besides giving us a mistaken interpretation of Pavlov's work with the conditional reflex, the tradition started by J.B. Watson and continued by B. F. Skinner helped to make psychology remote from physiology as well as from" the rest of the biological sciences. And, in the same chapter, Peat's gloss on the deeper political fit: "American Behaviorism is a fine ideology for a society that wants to combine authoritarian government with a system of specialized training for specialized jobs, because it claims that finely controlled behavior must be specialized and exists as a constraint on behavior." The mistranslated Pavlov was good ideology. The original Pavlov was not.

## Implications: behavior therapy, ABA, animal training all built on misread Pavlov

If the conditional reflex is what Pavlov meant — an intelligent, temporary, context-sensitive recognition of a conjunction — then a remarkable amount of twentieth-century clinical and pedagogical practice rests on a category error. The list is long.

**Behavior therapy.** The classical behavior-therapy paradigm (systematic desensitization, exposure-and-response-prevention, aversive conditioning) treats the patient's symptom as a conditioned response in the Watsonian sense — a motor or autonomic output that has been mechanically linked to a stimulus and that can be extinguished by re-pairing the stimulus with a different outcome. The Pavlovian (in the original sense) reframing is that the symptom is a fragment of an *obraz* the patient holds of the situation — a perceptual gestalt in which the elevator, the spider, the social encounter is currently part of a configuration that the patient's whole organism reads as dangerous. The therapy that works does so by enabling the patient to assemble a different *obraz* of the situation; the part that the conditioning vocabulary captures — the re-pairing of stimulus with non-aversive outcome — is one route into the *obraz* shift, but not the mechanism. The cleanest evidence is that behavior therapies that engage the patient's perceptual frame explicitly (cognitive-behavioral therapy, the third-wave acceptance-and-commitment approaches) tend to outperform pure conditioning paradigms in the long run.

**Applied behavior analysis (ABA).** ABA — the dominant framework in autism therapy, in special education for behavioral disorders, in many institutional settings — is the most direct contemporary descendant of the Skinnerian operant tradition. It treats the child's behavior as an output to be shaped by reinforcement and the child as a system of input-output relations to be optimized. The framework's clinical effectiveness in narrow skill-acquisition domains is real (it can install specific motor or verbal behaviors more efficiently than less-structured approaches), but its broader applications — using the same reinforcement-shaping logic to address the child's perceptual or motivational organization — rest on the Skinnerian assumption that there is nothing else to address. The Pavlovian (conditional-reflex) reframing is that what looks like a behavioral output is a fragment of the child's *obraz* of the situation, and that engineering the output without engaging the *obraz* tends to install mechanical compliance without restoring the orienting-reflex capacity the framework assumes is missing. The neurodiversity-advocacy critiques of ABA (Devon Price, the autism self-advocacy literature) converge descriptively on this point from a different theoretical base — the experience of ABA, from inside, is of having one's perceptual and motivational organization overridden by an external trainer rather than being supported in its own development. The Pavlovian framework predicts this outcome and gives it a mechanistic account.

**Animal training.** The dog-training literature, the dolphin and orca training paradigms in commercial entertainment, the laboratory operant paradigm, all rest on the same Skinnerian foundation. The framework works, in the sense that it produces reliable installed behaviors. It does not work, in the sense that the trained animal frequently displays the same stereotypies, learned helplessness, novelty-aversion, and behavioral collapse under stress that the Pavlovian framework would predict from an organism whose conditional-reflex apparatus has been chronically overridden. The contrasting tradition — Karen Pryor's clicker training, the positive-reinforcement-with-choice paradigms in zoo enrichment, the methods that grew up explicitly to address the orca and dolphin welfare problems of the Skinnerian SeaWorld era — accidentally rediscovers some of the conditional-reflex framework's commitments. The animals are treated as orienting organisms whose participation has to be solicited rather than as motor systems whose outputs have to be shaped.

**Behavioral economics.** The contemporary nudge-and-architecture tradition (Thaler, Sunstein, the behavioral-insights teams in governments) is one level removed from operant conditioning but rests on a similar premise — that human behavior is shaped by the immediate stimulus environment, that the agent's perceptual or motivational organization is treated as a black box, and that the policy intervention works by altering the stimulus environment without engaging the agent's *obraz* of the choice situation. The framework works in narrow domains (organ-donor defaults, retirement-savings auto-enrollment) and conspicuously fails to scale to domains where the agent's perceptual frame is the actual variable. The behavioral-economics critiques of recent years (the replication-crisis literature on the priming effects, the difficulty of moving from laboratory results to policy outcomes) follow predictably from a framework that has misidentified its unit of analysis.

**Pedagogy.** The behaviorist tradition in American education — Edward Thorndike's law of effect, the programmed-instruction movement, the contemporary token-economy classrooms, the broader regime of test-and-incentive that dominates K-12 — rests on the conditioned-reflex assumption that learning is the installation of behaviors by reinforcement. The Pavlovian alternative, in the conditional-reflex sense, would put the orienting reflex at the center: the child's encounter with novelty, the assembly of perceptual gestalts, the recognition of stable features of the world (the cat learning the latch, in Pavlov's phrase). The pedagogical literature that explicitly takes this frame (Vygotsky, Bruner, the discovery-learning and inquiry-based traditions) sits in chronic tension with the behaviorist mainstream. The chronic tension is, in Peat's read, a direct downstream consequence of the original mistranslation. See the next section.

## Implications for education (Peat's *Education and Reeducation* arc)

In the September 2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode titled "Education and Reeducation," Peat brought the conditional-reflex framework directly to bear on the question of how human beings actually learn. The episode is named for the central paradox: American education systematically *trains* students in habits and content that the same students then have to *un-train* themselves out of in order to think clearly. Peat's own ten-plus-year project of "education and reeducation" with Andrew and Sarah Murray on the show is the exemplar — every episode is, in part, the work of disassembling things they were taught in medical school. The same arc applies to most chronic-illness patients, who have to unlearn the standard nutritional advice (fasting good, sugar bad, fat good, salt bad, PUFA good, saturated bad) before they can adopt the metabolic recovery levers that actually work.

The conditional-reflex framework gives this arc its mechanism. American education installs the wrong learnings precisely because it operates in the conditioned-reflex mode — repetition, reinforcement, test-and-incentive — rather than in the conditional-reflex mode of supporting the orienting reflex toward genuine features of the world. The student who has been *conditioned* (in the Watson-Skinner sense) into a body of doctrine has not *recognized* (in the Pavlov sense) anything about the world; she has had a set of motor and verbal outputs installed by repetition, and she will defend the outputs because they are not anchored in the world but in her training. The defenders of nutritional orthodoxy who cannot give up the low-fat advice in the face of overwhelming counter-evidence are not, on this frame, behaving irrationally; they are behaving exactly as conditioned-reflex training predicts. Reeducation requires getting past the trained outputs to the underlying perceptual capacity for recognizing what is actually in front of the patient.

The bioenergetic substrate makes this concrete. From the same 2019 episode: "the happiest animals have the lowest serotonin, as well as lower acetylcholine and generally a little higher adrenaline. They are very alert, learn very well, and have very low serotonin." Alert, low-serotonin animals are conditional-reflex animals — their orienting reflex is intact, their perceptual gestalt of the situation is being built and updated, they are tracking the world. Helpless, high-serotonin animals (see [[learned-helplessness]]) are conditioned-reflex animals in the failure mode the American framework treats as normal — they have been *put into a state* by inescapable stress, and they can no longer assemble fresh *obrazy* of new situations. The two modes of "learning" run on different metabolism. Conditional-reflex learning is the high-energy oxidative-respiration mode; conditioned-reflex learning is what happens to an organism whose metabolic state has been pushed toward shutdown.

The pedagogical implication is direct. To support real learning, you support the metabolic state that enables the orienting reflex. From [[orienting-reflex|*The Orienting Reflex*]], the protocol levers are the same ones that recover learned helplessness: T3, sugar, salt, light, [[progesterone]] (in women) and [[pregnenolone]]/[[dhea]] (in both sexes), environmental enrichment, social contact, suppression of [[pufa|PUFA]] and [[estrogen]] and [[serotonin]] elevation. A child in this state assembles new perceptual gestalts readily, recognizes new features of the world quickly, fixes the learnings in the single-trial mode Pavlov took as the paradigm case. A child in the opposite state — chronically stressed, hypothyroid, PUFA-loaded, sleep-disrupted, anchored to screens — has had her conditional-reflex apparatus driven into the failure mode the American educational system then takes as normal, and pedagogy that operates in the conditioned-reflex mode has to fill the resulting gap with mechanical repetition. Peat's frame predicts (correctly, on the contemporary educational outcomes data) that this pedagogy will produce students who can pass tests but cannot recognize features of the world.

The "reeducation" arc — what Peat was doing with the Murrays for a decade — is the same protocol applied to adults whose conditional-reflex apparatus has been suppressed by training. Restore the metabolic substrate; expose the patient to situations that require new perceptual integrations; permit, rather than override, the orienting reflex; trust the cat-and-the-latch process to fix the new knowledge in single trials when the conjunction is real. The frame predicts (and the clinical experience confirms) that this works far better than re-conditioning by repetition. Real learnings stick because they are anchored in the world; trained behaviors fade as soon as the contingency that produced them ends.

## Practical protocols

**For parents.** The default mode of American parenting — reward charts, time-outs, behavior-modification plans, the broader regime of conditioning the child into appropriate outputs — operates in the Watson-Skinner mode. The Pavlovian (conditional-reflex) alternative is to support the child's orienting reflex: novel environments (outdoors, varied work, varied conversation), perceptual challenges the child can actually meet (not screen-mediated spurious novelty), and the metabolic substrate that lets the orienting reflex run (warmth, sugar, salt, milk, fruit, gelatinous broths, adequate sleep, sunlight). A child whose [[orienting-reflex|orienting reflex]] is intact will learn appropriate behavior by recognizing real features of the family and social environment far more reliably than a child whose orienting capacity has been overridden by a token economy.

**For teachers.** The conditioned-reflex mode of teaching (drill, repetition, test-and-incentive) reliably installs outputs that look like learning on the test and turn out not to be anchored in the world. The conditional-reflex alternative requires fewer mechanical interventions and more support of the student's perceptual encounter with the subject matter. The discovery-based, inquiry-based, and Vygotskian traditions in pedagogy operationalize this in different ways; the common thread is that the teacher's job is to orient the student toward genuine features of the world and let the single-trial recognition do the actual learning. The metabolic substrate matters here too — a hypothyroid, PUFA-loaded, sleep-disrupted, sugar-deprived student is operating with a degraded orienting-reflex apparatus and will be far more dependent on mechanical repetition than a student whose metabolism is in good order.

**For institutions practicing ABA, behavior therapy, or operant training.** The framework predicts that effectiveness will be highest for narrow skill-acquisition tasks (a specific motor or verbal output to be installed) and will fail for anything requiring the organism's perceptual or motivational reorganization. Where the institution's actual goal is the latter (autism therapy that aims at the child's overall flourishing, depression treatment that aims at the patient's recovered capacity for engagement, animal training that aims at the animal's stable behavioral repertoire under varied conditions), the operant framework should be supplemented (or replaced) by approaches that engage the organism's *obraz* of the situation directly. The clinical/training literature that does this well — Pryor's clicker training with positive choice, the third-wave CBT variants that integrate perceptual reframing with behavior change, the autism-acceptance approaches that center the child's perceptual world — accidentally recovers the conditional-reflex framework.

**For clinical practice with chronic-illness patients.** The American medical education system installs conditioned-reflex outputs in physicians (the standard nutritional advice, the standard pharmacological protocols, the standard diagnostic categories) that often have to be unlearned before the physician can recognize the patient's actual condition. The reeducation arc Peat modeled across his transcripts is the practical form. Restoring the patient's metabolic substrate restores the patient's conditional-reflex apparatus; restoring the physician's willingness to recognize what is in front of her, rather than what was installed by training, requires a parallel process.

**Avoid spurious-novelty environments.** The contemporary screen-mediated attention economy (smartphones, social media, rapid-cut video) systematically produces the *appearance* of novelty while denying the orienting reflex the integrated perceptual gestalt that completes the cycle. The result, as discussed in [[orienting-reflex]], is a brain trained out of orienting — habituated to spurious novelty without ever assembling a real *obraz*. The conditional-reflex framework predicts that this environment is particularly destructive to children, whose orienting-reflex apparatus is still developing; the contemporary educational outcomes data (declining reading comprehension, declining attention spans, rising ADHD diagnoses, rising adolescent depression) are consistent with the prediction.

## Counter-arguments

**Behaviorism's empirical results.** A defender of the American behaviorist tradition might grant that Pavlov's original framing was richer than the Watson-Skinner version and still argue that operant conditioning produces measurable, replicable behavioral effects across a wide range of species and tasks. The empirical claim is substantially correct in narrow domains: operant principles describe a real slice of behavior. The Pavlovian framework does not deny this; it denies that operant principles describe the central organizing principle of nervous-system function. The standard reply — that the organism is doing something between the stimulus and the response, and the something is the interesting part — applies here. Operant conditioning works precisely to the extent that it does not require the organism's perceptual reorganization, and fails precisely when it does. The narrow empirical effectiveness is real and consistent with the Pavlovian critique.

**ABA-for-autism literature.** The clinical literature on ABA for autism shows measurable improvements in specific skill domains (language production, self-care, reduction of dangerous behaviors) compared to no-treatment controls. The framework's defenders argue that these gains justify the approach. The Pavlovian (and contemporary neurodiversity-advocacy) critiques converge on three counter-points: (1) the improvements are largely in trained-output domains and do not generalize well to the child's overall perceptual or motivational organization; (2) the long-run outcomes for ABA-treated children include elevated rates of PTSD-like symptoms and adult mental-health problems that the original outcome metrics did not capture; (3) the alternative approaches that engage the autistic child's perceptual world directly (relationship-based, child-led, the Floortime/DIR tradition, the emerging neuro-affirming methods) produce comparable skill outcomes with substantially fewer of the iatrogenic harms. The empirical case for ABA over alternatives is, in the contemporary literature, considerably weaker than its institutional dominance would suggest.

**Modern behavioral economics.** The Thaler-Sunstein nudge tradition has been highly visible and policy-influential; its defenders argue that it has produced demonstrable improvements in domains from retirement savings to organ donation. The framework's recent troubles are well-documented — the social-psychology priming-effects literature has largely failed to replicate, the laboratory-to-policy translation has been less reliable than its early proponents predicted, the framework's success in narrow defaults-architecture domains has not extended to the broader behavior-change domains it was advertised for. The Pavlovian diagnosis is that the framework's narrow successes (changing defaults in choice architecture) work because they engage the agent's perceptual gestalt of the situation through a back-door route (changing what counts as the available options) rather than through the mechanism the framework actually proposes (mechanical stimulus-response). The framework's broader failures follow from its inability to engage the *obraz* directly.

**Predictive-coding and computational rehabilitation of behaviorism.** Contemporary computational neuroscience has partly rehabilitated some of the behaviorist commitments by treating conditioning as a special case of Bayesian model-updating. The convergence with the Pavlovian framework is partial: both frameworks reject the bottom-up reflex-arc picture, both treat perception as an active construction. The divergence is on the substrate. The computational literature treats the brain as a computer running predictive algorithms; the Pavlovian framework treats the metabolism as the substrate of perception and rejects the algorithm-substrate separation. Peat's reply to predictive-coding is the same as his reply elsewhere — the structure is partly right and the substrate is wrong.

**Cultural-historical-activity theory.** The Vygotskian tradition (Vygotsky, Leontiev, Luria) develops the post-Pavlovian framework explicitly and is in substantial agreement with the conditional-reflex frame outlined here. The tradition is sometimes invoked against Peat's reading as evidence that the Soviet developments were not all in agreement with what Peat called "the Pavlovian tradition"; in fact, the Vygotskian tradition is a deepening of Pavlov's conditional-reflex framework along the developmental dimension, and Peat treated it as continuous with his own reading. The disagreements are within the Russian tradition; the American tradition is outside it.

**"Translation matters but the empirical work stands."** Some philosophers of science have argued that even if the *uslovny → conditioned* translation lost something philosophically, the empirical work in the American behaviorist tradition is independently valid and does not depend on the philosophical framing. The reply is that empirical work in psychology is never independent of its framing; what an experimenter measures, what an apparatus records, what counts as a behavior to be explained — all of this is settled by the theoretical framework before any measurement happens. The American behaviorist apparatus measured exactly what the Watson-Skinner framework treated as the unit of analysis (the isolated motor output) and was systematically blind to what the Pavlovian framework treated as the unit (the whole-organism perceptual gestalt). The empirical record is not theory-neutral. The translation error structured what the science could see.

## Data gaps

**The Russian primary literature.** As with the rest of the *Mind and Tissue* framework, Peat's reading of Pavlov rests on translations that are partial and dated. The detailed argumentation Pavlov made about the conditional reflex in his late writings (the 1930s lectures, the Wednesday discussions, the unpublished notes) is not fully available in English. A serious contemporary engagement would require either the original Russian or substantially better translations than now exist.

**The fate of the *uslovny → conditioned* mistranslation in subsequent translation.** Peat asserts that the mistranslation occurred in the early English-language translation of Pavlov's work and has persisted since. The specific translators, the dates, the alternative translations considered, the moments at which the choice could have been made differently — none of this is laid out in *Mind and Tissue* at the detail it would take to settle the question historically. The case rests on the linguistic argument plus Peat's general read of what the American behaviorist tradition did with Pavlov; a more thorough history of Pavlov in English remains to be written.

**Quantitative comparison of conditional-reflex vs. conditioned-reflex framing.** The framework predicts that pedagogy, therapy, and animal training that operate in the conditional-reflex mode produce better long-run outcomes than the same activities operating in the conditioned-reflex mode. The prediction has been partially tested (the comparison of ABA with alternatives, the comparison of behaviorist with inquiry-based pedagogy, the comparison of operant with positive-reinforcement-with-choice animal training) but never under a unified theoretical framing that would let the conditional-reflex prediction be assessed directly. The pieces of the case exist in the separate literatures; the synthesis does not.

**Developmental data on conditional-reflex capacity.** The framework predicts that conditional-reflex capacity is a developmental capability that requires adequate metabolic substrate to mature. The supporting evidence is largely indirect (the [[learned-helplessness]] and orienting-reflex literatures, the rodent enrichment experiments, the chronic-stress-and-cognitive-development literature). Direct longitudinal measurement of children's conditional-reflex capacity as a function of metabolic state would test the prediction directly and has not been done.

**Cross-cultural comparison.** The framework predicts that cultures whose pedagogy and child-rearing practices operate in the conditional-reflex mode (the Russian tradition itself, certain indigenous learning traditions, the Montessori and Waldorf approaches in the West) produce different patterns of adult cognitive functioning than cultures operating in the conditioned-reflex mode. The descriptive literature contains scattered hints (the cross-cultural variation in attention style, the difference between rote-memorization and discovery-based educational traditions) but no systematic test under the Pavlovian framing.

**Modern operant-conditioning revisions.** Contemporary operant theorists (the Skinnerian descendants, the post-Skinnerian behavior-analysis tradition) have substantially modified the framework in directions that bring it partway back toward the Pavlovian original — incorporating notions of "stimulus equivalence," "relational frame theory," and the "verbal behavior" extensions that engage perceptual and conceptual organization. These developments are not engaged in *Mind and Tissue* (1976) or in the indexed Peat corpus, and the relationship between contemporary behavior analysis and the Pavlovian framework is an open question.
