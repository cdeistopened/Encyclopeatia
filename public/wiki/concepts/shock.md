# Shock

> **Safety note.** Acute shock — hypovolemic, cardiogenic, septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic — is a medical emergency. Untreated, it kills within minutes to hours. This page is a framework discussion of the *physiology* of shock as Peat understood it through Hans Selye, George Washington Crile, and F.Z. Meerson, and of the chronic low-grade-shock states Peat's diet and hormone framework was designed to address. It is **not** first-aid guidance. If someone is in acute shock — cold, clammy, confused, with a thready pulse and falling blood pressure — call emergency services. The interventions below are for the convalescent phase and for chronic shock-like states, not for replacing intravenous resuscitation, vasopressors, antibiotics, epinephrine, or surgical control of hemorrhage.

Shock is the place where Peat's framework and the framework of emergency medicine touch most closely and diverge most sharply. The emergency physician sees shock as a clinical syndrome — inadequate tissue perfusion, classified by cause, treated by addressing the cause and supporting the circulation. Peat read the same syndrome through Selye's lens as the *terminal phase* of the General Adaptation Syndrome: the point at which the stress cascade documented across [[stress]] has run out the substrate, depleted the protective steroids, switched cells out of oxidative metabolism, and tipped the whole organism into failure. The two readings are not incompatible. Emergency medicine keeps the patient alive long enough for metabolic recovery to happen. Peat's framework is concerned with what determines whether the recovery actually happens — and with the much commoner condition of *chronic low-grade shock,* in which a person is not dying but is living in a permanently compromised version of the same physiology.

The unique signal of this page, relative to its neighbors: [[stress]] covers the cascade in general; [[hans-selye|Selye]] covers the inheritance; [[adrenaline]] covers the acute glucose-rescue signal; [[cortisol]] covers chronic catabolism; [[lactic-acid]] covers the metabolic floor; [[endotoxin]] covers the gut-derived amplifier. This page covers the *failure mode* — what happens when the cascade runs to its terminus, what Peat learned from the surgeons who studied shock before Selye made stress famous, and what the framework recommends for the recovery edge.

## Selye's classification

Hans Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome ([[hans-selye]]) was a three-stage description of how an organism responds to any sustained noxious stimulus. The stages are *alarm,* in which the body mounts an emergency response (adrenaline, glucocorticoids, immune mobilization); *resistance,* in which the body adapts and appears to function normally despite the continuing stressor (the cost is paid quietly through depletion of the protective steroids); and *exhaustion,* in which adaptation fails and the same hormonal signals that were adaptive in the alarm phase become catabolic and destructive. Shock is what exhaustion looks like when it tips over into acute organ failure. Selye himself described the alarm phase as "the initial shock phase of the stress reaction" — a single dose of estrogen, he had shown, was sufficient to duplicate the syndrome in animals ([[bleeding-clotting-cancer|*Bleeding, Clotting, Cancer*]]).

Peat preserved Selye's three-stage architecture and added two clarifications. The first was the relocation of exhaustion from the adrenal cortex to the mitochondrion. F.Z. Meerson's Soviet laboratory showed that the limiting factor was upstream — the mitochondria of heart, brain, and intestine had been damaged by sustained cortisol and free fatty acid exposure. "The exhaustion problem caused by excessive stress is generalized, not just a matter of adrenal insufficiency" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). Shock is the moment when generalized mitochondrial failure crosses a circulatory threshold and tissues begin to die. The second clarification concerned the shock-versus-antishock axis. Selye had identified an opposition between estrogen (shock) and progesterone (antishock) in the 1940s: "He demonstrated that a large dose of estrogen created a shock condition. Progesterone imitated all the features of the stress-protecting hormones, so animals didn't need their adrenal glands if they had a steady supply of progesterone" (same source). The pharmaceutical industry narrowed the "anti-stress" pole to cortisone and prednisone and largely forgot the progesterone arm. Peat's reading restored it.

The pre-Selye contribution that anchors the framework is George Washington Crile's. "Before Hans Selye made stress famous, an American surgeon named George Crile in Cleveland over 100 years ago was studying shock from surgery" ([[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|*Eluv: Effects of Stress and Trauma* (2014)]]). Crile dropped surgical mortality from 4.4% (1908) to 1.8% (1913) through "anoci-association" — preventing the brain from registering the noxious signals — combining scopolamine (antihistamine), local anesthesia, and pre-operative thyroid supplementation. Two of Crile's findings became permanent furniture. First, the vagal mechanism: "He showed that stimulating the vagus nerve was the basic signal for turning on shock. What happens in shock is that signals are sent to all of your body cells, turning off the ability to use oxygen. It imitates death. When a person in shock is cut, their blood stays bright red because they aren't using the oxygen. It's like turning off the thyroid in a nervous way" ([[2019-09-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fascinating-insights-into-mr-thyroid|*One Radio Network: Mr. Thyroid* (2019)]]). Bright-red venous blood in shock is the visible signature of cells refusing to respire. Second, the thyroid prerequisite: hypothyroid patients tolerate surgical stress poorly because "their cortisol is already high" ([[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|*Eluv* (2014)]]).

## Types of shock

The clinical taxonomy distinguishes shock by cause. None of what follows substitutes for emergency assessment.

**Hypovolemic shock** is the loss of effective circulating blood volume — hemorrhage, severe dehydration, burn third-spacing, or the capillary leak that follows the other shock types. The chronic version matters as much as the acute. "If old age is commonly a hypovolemic condition, then the common salt restriction for old-age hypertension is just as irrational as is salt-restriction in pregnancy or in shock. Thyroid (T3), glucose, sodium, magnesium and protein should be considered in any state in which weakened homeostatic control of the composition of plasma is evident" ([[eclampsia-in-the-real-organism-a-paradigm-of-general-distress|*Eclampsia in the Real Organism*]]). Hypoalbuminemia, low sodium intake, hypothyroidism, and chronic dehydration produce a low-grade hypovolemic state that, in a depleted person, tips into the multi-organ-failure picture hospitals see as "frailty."

**Cardiogenic shock** is failure of the pump — usually a large infarction or sustained arrhythmia. The framework intersects through the FFA-and-mitochondria mechanism: a heart already running on PUFA-displaced cardiolipin and elevated free fatty acids enters acute ischemia with reduced reserve. "There is a growing recognition that a persistent increase of free fatty acids in the serum, which is seen in shock, heart failure, and aging, indicates a bad prognosis" ([[free-fatty-acids|*Fats, Functions & Malfunctions*]]).

**Septic shock** (and its closely related endotoxic shock) is, in Peat's reading, the most directly addressable from the framework. See the dedicated section below.

**Anaphylactic shock** is the systemic mast-cell-mediated reaction to an allergen — histamine and serotonin flood the circulation, capillaries leak, blood pressure collapses, airways constrict. Peat traced the same physiology through Selye's collaborators in Canada: "About 40 or 50 years ago, people associated with Hans Selye in Canada did research on allergy and the shock produced by allergens. In one study, they graded allergens from the potentially deadly walnut oil down to mild allergens like pollen. They gave that range of allergens to animals that had glucose infused into their bloodstream to give them two to three times the normal concentration. Even the most deadly allergen didn't do anything worse than maybe give them the sniffles. Insulin-induced hypoglycemia, conversely, made the mildest allergen lethal" ([[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|*Ask the Herb Doctor: Allergy* (2016)]]). The blood-sugar level at the moment of antigen exposure determines whether the same allergen produces a sneeze or kills the animal. The "300 mg% rule" — protective serum glucose roughly twice normal — sits underneath the chronic-anaphylaxis observation Peat made: a population kept perpetually carbohydrate-restricted is a population whose mast cells are pre-loaded to overreact. The estrogen-driven mast-cell amplification is laid out at [[histamine]].

**Neurogenic shock** is the loss of sympathetic vascular tone — spinal injury, severe vasovagal events, certain overdoses — producing widespread vasodilation and falling blood pressure. The chronic version is the easy-fainting, low-blood-pressure phenotype Peat associated with hypothyroidism: "People generally compensate for that exaggerated activity of the vagal nervous system by pumping out extra adrenaline. Surprisingly, many people are running with both sides of their autonomic nervous system at an extremely high rate. If something interferes with their production of adrenaline, they drop into the vagal state and move towards shock or fainting" ([[2019-09-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fascinating-insights-into-mr-thyroid|*One Radio Network* (2019)]]).

## What's actually happening metabolically

The cascade detailed in [[stress]] is the same cascade that runs in shock, compressed into minutes and pushed past the threshold at which any single step can still be reversed. The shock-specific terminal features:

**Energy collapse.** The cell cannot make enough ATP to maintain its ion gradients. The sodium-potassium ATPase fails. Intracellular sodium rises, water follows it, the cell swells. Calcium leaks in, activating proteases and phospholipases. "Inflammation, lipid peroxidation, tissue atrophy, the 'calcium catastrophe' (when almost anything goes wrong, calcium can transmit and amplify and extend the problem), mitochondrial decay, and similar events help to define the stress reaction in greater detail" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). In shock the calcium catastrophe is minute-scale.

**Lactic acidosis.** Without enough oxygen or functioning mitochondria, glycolysis cannot couple to oxidative phosphorylation; pyruvate accumulates and is reduced to lactate. "When a person has an accident, or surgery, and goes into shock, the degree of lactic acidemia is recognized as an indicator of the severity of the problem" ([[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation|*Fatigue, Aging, and Recuperation*]]). Serum lactate is the most reliable single prognostic marker in modern intensive care. Notably, the same source reports the counterintuitive finding that "lactated Ringer's solution has been commonly used to treat these people, to restore their blood pressure. But when prompt treatment with lactated Ringer's solution has been compared with no early treatment at all, the patients who are not 'resuscitated' do better than those who got the early treatment. And when Ringer's lactate has been compared with various other solutions, synthetic starch solutions, synthetic hemoglobin polymer solution, or simply a concentrated solution of sodium chloride, those who received the lactate solution did least well." Pouring lactate into a person whose problem is lactate excess is, in Peat's reading, a category error. The full mechanism is at [[lactic-acid]].

**Capillary leak.** The endothelium loses its barrier function. Plasma escapes into the interstitial space; remaining blood becomes hyperviscous; tissue edema rises; perfusion drops further. Peat traced it through the histamine-and-serotonin pathway: "a single large dose of estrogen produces the same syndrome as traumatic shock, with water leaving the bloodstream, veins relaxing, blood pressure collapsing, tissues swelling. Histamine is one of the two downstream amines (alongside serotonin) that estrogen mobilizes to accomplish those effects" ([[histamine]]). The endogenous mediators of shock are the same endogenous mediators of chronic estrogen excess, in pharmacological doses.

**Cellular potassium loss.** As ATP fails, the cell loses its high intracellular potassium. Released K+ contributes to cardiac dysrhythmias. The intracellular cation balance required for protein synthesis and resting membrane potential breaks down. The practical signature is the hyperkalemia of severe shock and the intracellular K depletion that often persists into recovery.

These four features — energy collapse, lactic acidosis, capillary leak, potassium loss — define shock as a *cellular* condition, not merely a circulatory one. The blood pressure cuff registers what the cells are doing.

## The endotoxin role in shock

Most "septic shock" is endotoxin-driven. The full mechanism is at [[endotoxin]]. The shock-relevant points are that bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a sufficient cause of the full shock syndrome — capillary leak, hypotension, cardiac depression, multi-organ failure — and that endotoxin escape from the gut is a normal feature of any sufficient stressor, not only of overt infection. "Just a mental stress, for example, will shift the blood away from your digestive system to your legs and arms, the fight or flight reaction. If that persists or is very intense, the intestine loses its barrier function and bacteria can go right through the membrane lining the intestine into the bloodstream" ([[2010-11-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endotoxins|*Ask the Herb Doctor: Endotoxins* (2010)]]).

This means the shock following hemorrhage, surgery, burn, or trauma — events with no obvious infectious cause — is partly endotoxic. The adrenaline-driven shutdown of intestinal blood flow creates the conditions for LPS to translocate through the compromised gut wall. A liver running on adrenaline and depleted glucocorticoids cannot clear the LPS load; it spills into systemic circulation; the syndrome of "septic shock" begins in the absence of any external pathogen. This is the mechanism by which post-traumatic and post-surgical patients develop multi-organ dysfunction days after the original insult, even when the surgical field is sterile.

Peat's answer to the question "what would you recommend for someone experiencing septic shock?" was direct: "Sugar, aspirin, naloxone or naltrexone, antibiotics and laxatives" ([[septic-shock|email exchange]], citing studies showing beneficial effects of aspirin in inflammation, sepsis, and neuroprotection). The aspirin and naloxone components have explicit literature support. The sugar restores defending substrate. The antibiotics and laxatives reduce the bacterial load producing endotoxin. None of these substitute for emergency care; they are the framework's add-ons to it.

## Peat's interventions in chronic low-grade shock

Most acute shock requires emergency medicine. The framework's contribution sits at two edges: the chronic low-grade-shock state and the *recovery* phase, where the question becomes whether the patient actually heals.

**Warming.** Body temperature drops in shock and in chronic low-grade shock. "Since the surgery turning on the stress hormones turns off the thyroid function, temperature tends to drop drastically during and following surgery. In proportion to how the temperature falls, the person's recovery is impaired. So everything that will keep your temperature up during and after surgery helps" ([[2014-01-01-eluv-eluv-effects-of-stress-and-trauma-on-the-body|*Eluv* (2014)]]). The intervention is unglamorous — warm clothes, warm room, warm food, hot drinks, avoidance of cold — but it is the single most reliable signature of the cascade slowing.

**Sugar plus salt.** The most operationally important intervention. Sugar refills the glycogen adrenaline is trying to mobilize; salt expands plasma volume and supports thyroid function. The mechanism is at [[adrenaline]] and [[blood-sugar]]; the shock-relevant point is that a person in chronic low-grade shock has falling blood sugar, compensating adrenaline, contracting plasma volume, and recruited cortisol covering the gap. Orange juice with salt is the framework's first move. Studies of hypertonic-saline-and-dextran resuscitation in severe shock (cited at [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation]]) support the same principle at higher acuity.

**Gelatin.** Bone broth and glycine-rich gelatin (see [[bone-broth]]) oppose the inflammatory and excitotoxic amino acids (tryptophan, methionine, cysteine) that cortisol is liberating from muscle. In chronic low-grade shock, muscle catabolism is the structural cost; gelatin replaces lost protein with a profile that does not feed the cascade further.

**Progesterone for women.** The most direct restoration of the Selye-identified antishock pole. "Progesterone imitated all the features of the stress-protecting hormones, so animals didn't need their adrenal glands if they had a steady supply of progesterone" ([[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]). In women with chronic low-grade shock signatures — fluid retention, cold extremities, low pulse pressure, fatigue, anxiety, the menopausal cluster — restoration of progesterone tone is the framework's most reliable single intervention. Full mechanism at [[progesterone]].

**Thyroid replacement.** Crile's 1908 observation has not been improved on. The hypothyroid patient is in a chronic low-grade shock state because their cells cannot make enough CO2 or ATP to defend against the stressors a euthyroid person handles unconsciously. "Supplementing thyroid, basically" was Peat's answer to how Crile lowered surgical mortality further. "He would give usually three or four grains of Armour thyroid — sufficient to lower the TSH to zero" ([[2019-09-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fascinating-insights-into-mr-thyroid|*One Radio Network* (2019)]]). Detailed dosing at [[thyroid]].

**Niacinamide.** Niacinamide opposes the lipolysis adrenaline and cortisol drive — lowering FFA, restoring Randle balance, reducing lactate generation. "The two chemicals that are most practical for lowering free fatty acids and stopping the stress reaction are niacinamide and aspirin" ([[free-fatty-acids]]).

**Aspirin.** Aspirin is the framework's single most broadly useful intervention against the shock cascade — anti-prostaglandin, anti-platelet, pro-mitochondrial in low doses, with surprisingly direct support in the sepsis literature. Full frame at [[aspirin]].

None of these substitutes for emergency care in acute shock. All address the *physiology* emergency care is trying to support.

## Tracking signal in chronic low-grade shock

The clinical picture is recognizable once the framework names it:

- **Cold extremities** — adrenergic vasoconstriction compensating for sluggish basal metabolism.
- **Low blood pressure with high pulse** — heart rate compensating for reduced stroke volume and reduced effective circulating volume.
- **Low energy with paradoxical 3 AM wakefulness** — the chronic adrenaline tone the depleted glycogen reserve cannot suppress overnight (see [[adrenaline]] for the 3 AM cascade).
- **Fluid retention** — aldosterone-driven sodium-and-water retention following reduced effective circulating volume.
- **Easy fainting on standing** — vagal-or-adrenergic decompensation of a circulatory system running with too little reserve.
- **Cold intolerance** — the hypothyroid signature Crile identified surgically.
- **Sleep that does not refresh** — the night spent running the cortisol-glycemic loop.

The clinical opposite — warm, hungry, energetic, sleeping through the night, blood pressure stable on rising — is the framework's signature of recovery.

## Counter-arguments

Mainstream emergency medicine for acute shock is, by Peat's own implicit acknowledgment, largely vindicated. IV fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, antibiotics, surgical hemorrhage control, epinephrine for anaphylaxis, and modern sepsis bundles save lives no diet or hormone framework will save. The framework explicitly does not propose replacing any of this. The septic-shock exchange — "sugar, aspirin, naloxone or naltrexone, antibiotics and laxatives" — includes antibiotics. The framework's claim is *additive,* not subtractive.

The narrower mainstream-vs-Peat disagreements that matter:

- **Lactated Ringer's as default resuscitation fluid.** The mainstream position is that lactate is metabolized to bicarbonate in the liver and acts as a buffer; Peat (citing comparative trials at [[fatigue-aging-and-recuperation]]) argued adding lactate to a lactic-acidemic patient does measurable harm. The modern human resuscitation literature is more mixed than the animal models suggest. The framework prefers hypertonic saline or balanced crystalloids without exogenous lactate.
- **Salt restriction in shock states and the elderly.** The mainstream "salt restriction lowers blood pressure" position has been substantially walked back in the last decade, though hospital-default low-sodium diets persist. Peat's position — that a hypovolemic elderly person needs *more* salt — is supported by [[salt]] and an increasingly mainstream-friendly evidence base.
- **Hypothyroidism as a pre-surgical concern.** Peat's position (Crile's, really) — that pre-operative thyroid optimization reduces post-operative complications — is increasingly accepted in mainstream surgical literature. The disagreement is narrowing.
- **Estrogen as a pro-shock hormone.** The mainstream framework treats estrogen as cardioprotective and neuroprotective; Peat treated it as the single most reliable producer of the shock syndrome in animal models. This is the most substantial disagreement. Dedicated frame at [[estrogen]].

The disagreements are not symmetric. Mainstream emergency medicine is correct about most of what it does in acute shock. Peat's framework is correct about much of what mainstream medicine does *not* do — the substrate state *before* the shock, the recovery *after* it, and the chronic low-grade picture in patients who are not dying but not well.

## Data gaps

- **Dose-response curve for sugar and salt in early shock.** Peat's writing is unambiguous about direction but rarely quantitative about timing or amount. The Selye anaphylaxis data — 300 mg% glucose as protective — is the clearest single number; the operational question of how much oral sugar, how fast, in a patient with declining mental status, is not addressed because the framework explicitly defers to emergency care at that acuity.
- **Interaction with modern vasopressors.** The framework has nothing direct to say about whether norepinephrine, vasopressin, or angiotensin II infusions interact favorably or unfavorably with the substrate-restoration approach. The implicit assumption is they support the circulatory side while the framework supports the metabolic side, but the interaction has not been systematically studied.
- **Quantitative role of endotoxin in non-septic shock.** The claim that hemorrhagic, traumatic, and surgical shock are partly endotoxic is mechanistically plausible and supported by gut-translocation studies, but the *fraction* of the syndrome that is endotoxin-mediated versus directly hypovolemic or cardiogenic has not been resolved.
- **Heat shock proteins.** The mainstream "HSP activation is protective" literature sits in some tension with Peat's general suspicion of [[hormesis]] arguments. The shock-relevant question (do HSP-inducing interventions help recovery from shock?) is open.
- **Gender asymmetry.** The framework gives progesterone a central role for women but does not specify the equivalent for men with comparable force. Pregnenolone, DHEA, testosterone are all candidates, but the operational hierarchy is less worked out than the progesterone-for-women case.
