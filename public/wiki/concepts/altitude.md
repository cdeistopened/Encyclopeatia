# Altitude

The longevity-and-altitude correlation is one of the oldest unexplained findings in actuarial medicine. Insurance companies have known for more than a century that cancer mortality, heart disease mortality, and most-cause mortality fall as elevation rises. The mountain populations that dominate the world's lists of extreme-age centenarians — the Caucasus, the Andes, Hunza, the high valleys of Kashmir — are not on those lists because of yogurt or apricots or "good genes." They are on those lists, in Ray Peat's framework, for the same reason a Russian rat taken to 17,000 feet sheds an implanted cancer that a sea-level rat dies from: the air at altitude is thin in oxygen, and thin-oxygen air forces the body to retain a higher concentration of the gas that protects it from the inside out — [[carbon-dioxide]].

Peat returned to this argument for fifty years. It is the through-line of the 2006 [[altitude-and-mortality|*Altitude and Mortality*]] newsletter (densest text), the 1999 [[homeostasis-and-aging|*Homeostasis and Aging*]] newsletter (where the Kashmir Alzheimer's data first appeared), the 2011 [[protective-co2-and-aging|*Protective CO2 and Aging*]], the 2016 [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality*]], and at least seven distinct interview episodes between 2009 and 2021. The 2010 [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|*Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on altitude]] is the densest single hour Peat ever spent on the subject. Both treat altitude not as an exotic high-mountain topic but as a window onto the chemistry of aging itself.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Pull air pressure down and the Haldane effect lets the body keep a higher partial pressure of CO2 in tissue. CO2 then binds to amino groups, displaces lactic acid, stabilizes mitochondrial structure, opens blood vessels, releases oxygen from hemoglobin precisely where it is needed, and protects every protein in the body from the slow glycation and lipid-peroxidation damage that otherwise accumulates with age. Lower atmospheric oxygen pressure produces higher tissue oxygen *utilization*. The chronic-cold-and-cancer reality at sea level is, in Peat's diagnosis, a slow CO2 starvation. Altitude is the inversion. The CO2 mechanism in detail is in [[carbon-dioxide]] and is not re-walked here.

## The actuarial argument

Peat's strongest opening move on this topic was almost always insurance data — old, large, and immune to dietary confounds. From the [[2012-03-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-acidity-x-alkalinity|March 2012 *Acidity X Alkalinity*]]: "As long ago as 1910, the insurance companies knew that people who lived at very high altitudes had a very low cancer mortality." The pattern repeats in essentially every altitude-themed interview. From the [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 KMUD altitude episode]]: "100 years ago, insurance companies already knew — the actuaries were looking at the mortality figures for different diseases — that cancer, for example, was much less common in all of the high cities of the world."

The mainstream medical interpretation of this data, when noticed at all, ran straight into a contradiction with the radiation-causes-cancer model. Denver gets more cosmic radiation than New Orleans; New Orleans has more cancer. "[Scientists] were sure that those figures must be wrong because they knew radiation causes cancer, and the radiation in Denver is much higher than in New Orleans... But the figures show that the cancer rate in New Orleans and San Francisco is much higher than in Denver. They said that just must be a mistake, but the insurance companies have had the figures for over 100 years" ([[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|KMUD 2010]]).

The radiation paradox dissolves with Linear Energy Transfer. From the [[2009-04-20-politics-and-science-politics-science-ionizing-radiation-in-context-part-1|*Politics and Science*, April 2009]]: "Cosmic rays at high altitude don't deliver much energy or damage to your tissues. But at lower altitudes, where they are less intense, they are more able to react with your tissues and cause damage." Even [[linus-pauling|Linus Pauling]] missed this — assuming, like everyone else, that Denver's higher cosmic-ray exposure made it more dangerous than the coast. The opposite was true. High-energy cosmic radiation passes through tissue without ionizing it; the lower-energy secondary cascade at sea level is what damages DNA.

The cleanest demonstration that the relationship is continuous, not threshold-based, is the New Mexico data Peat cited repeatedly: from below sea level up to about 12,000 feet, improvement is continuous ([[altitude|altitude email]]). The [[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|*One Radio Network*, August 2019]]: "Studies in New Mexico and Switzerland have shown heart disease decreases in proportion to increasing altitude. Every thousand feet, there was about a 2% decrease in mortality." Living higher is, by this measure, equivalent to living younger.

## The Caucasus, the Andes, Hunza, Kashmir

The longevity-cluster populations Peat invoked were never offered as a single causal claim — he was scrupulously honest about the unreliability of pre-modern mountain birth records. The pattern was offered as a clue. From the [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 altitude episode]]: "About 1950, the first time I heard about it was a Peruvian who was brought to tour the United States as an oddity. The church record showed him as being 187 years old. After a couple of weeks visiting around the US, he was sent back to Peru and died shortly after that. And Old Parr in London — he was a guy in the country that was famous for being 150-something. The King had him brought to London to study, and he died shortly after living the high life in London."

The Old Parr / Peruvian-Indian story is a parable about altitude *adaptation* — what is gained over decades at altitude can be lost over weeks at sea level. The high-altitude resident's tissues are CO2-enriched and lactate-clean; descend to London and the chronic acidemia and adrenergic stress resume within days. Ascent at speed is a stressor; chronic residence is the protective state.

The most striking population datum Peat cited was from Kashmir. From the 1999 [[homeostasis-and-aging|*Homeostasis and Aging*]] newsletter, and repeated in the 2006 [[altitude-and-mortality|*Altitude and Mortality*]]:

> Since permanent high altitude residents chronically retain a larger amount of carbon dioxide in their tissue, I have looked for data on the incidence of degenerative brain disease among high altitude populations. In Kashmir, a house-to-house study of more than 66,000 people found that nearly one percent of the population suffered from some neurological impairment, such as cerebral palsy, epilepsy, mental retardation or stroke. But no cases of Alzheimer's disease or multiple sclerosis were found. In India, the incidence of these diseases is much lower than in the U.S., but their absence in Kashmir is remarkable.

The absence — not the reduction — of Alzheimer's disease and multiple sclerosis in 66,000 surveyed individuals is the kind of population datum that, properly understood, recasts how degenerative disease should be conceptualized. Both [[alzheimers-disease|Alzheimer's]] and [[multiple-sclerosis|multiple sclerosis]] involve excess brain lactate; both follow the [[warburg-effect|Warburg pattern]] of aerobic glycolysis in tissue that should be respiring efficiently. In a population where chronic CO2 retention is keeping lactic acid out of nervous tissue, the conditions that lactic acid drives do not appear. The suicide-rate inversion is part of the same pattern: "The countries with the highest suicide rates — South Korea, Lithuania, Bangladesh — they are very low altitude. The lowest suicide rates are generally in Mexico, Colombia, and Bolivia — high altitude countries" ([[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|August 2019]]). The mainstream myth that high altitude *causes* depression-and-suicide through serotonin reduction has the sign exactly inverted. Serotonin does fall at altitude, and that fall is part of why altitude is protective. See [[serotonin]] for why Peat treated reduced serotonin as a feature, not a bug.

## Why thin air keeps more CO2 in: the Haldane effect

The single physiological lever Peat returned to most often was the Haldane effect — the inverse of the better-known Bohr effect, and the under-taught half of the same physiology. The Bohr effect describes how CO2 in the capillaries displaces oxygen from hemoglobin, letting tissues extract the oxygen they need. The Haldane effect describes how oxygen in the lungs displaces CO2 from hemoglobin, letting it be exhaled. The two effects are opposite faces of one molecular machine.

At altitude, the partial pressure of inhaled oxygen is lower. There is less oxygen pressure pushing CO2 off hemoglobin in the lungs. CO2 therefore stays in the blood longer. From the 2006 [[altitude-and-mortality|*Altitude and Mortality*]]: "The Haldane-Bohr effect describes the fact that oxygen and carbon dioxide destabilize each other's binding to hemoglobin. When oxygen pressure is high, the blood releases its carbon dioxide more easily. In stormy weather, or at high altitude, the lower oxygen pressure allows the body to retain more carbon dioxide."

In the [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 episode]] Peat extended the Haldane effect to proteins beyond hemoglobin — a generalization that, if correct, is the molecular link between altitude and longevity:

> A few people who have tested other proteins find that the Haldane-Bohr effect applies to proteins in general. When there's a lot of carbon dioxide, it basically changes the pH or the isoelectric point of the protein, making it less accessible to oxygen. That in itself is a protection against the attack of oxygen against proteins.

This is the carbamino mechanism, detailed in [[carbon-dioxide]]. The altitude implication is that mountain residents are running their lifelong protein-protection chemistry at higher CO2 saturation than coastal residents, every day, asleep and awake. The cornea is one tissue where this is visible. "You can see that in the cornea; it becomes more compact at higher altitude. People often notice that their nearsightedness improves by two or three diopters" ([[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010]]). Peat noticed it in himself at his summer house in Coeneo, Mexico (6,600 feet): "my nearsightedness always improves when I'm there for a few weeks" ([[altitude|altitude email]]).

## The lactate paradox

The "lactate paradox" is textbook physiology's name for an observation that should have led it to Peat's framework decades ago. At high altitude, with adaptation, well-trained athletes can work at maximal output *without* producing the lactic acid that the same effort produces at sea level. The expectation — that lower oxygen would force more anaerobic glycolysis — is exactly backwards.

The 2006 [[altitude-and-mortality|*Altitude and Mortality*]] explains why it isn't really a paradox: "Carbon dioxide, retained through the Haldane effect, accounts for the lactate paradox, by inhibiting cellular excitation and sustaining oxidative metabolism to consume lactate efficiently." The 1999 [[homeostasis-and-aging|*Homeostasis and Aging*]] put it more compactly: lactic acid isn't produced during hypoxic exercise "since carbon dioxide is retained by the Haldane effect, in which the low oxygen pressure fails to displace all of the carbon dioxide carried in the blood cells."

The same physiology runs in reverse at sea level. Hyperventilation, hypothyroidism, adrenergic stress, lactate from a fatigued muscle, lactate from a tumor — all displace CO2 from hemoglobin binding sites and tip the cell from oxidative respiration toward [[lactic-acid|lactic-acid]] production. The full Warburg inversion is in [[oxidative-metabolism]] and [[warburg-effect]]; the altitude implication is that mountain residents start the day with the CO2/lactate balance tipped toward oxidative metabolism in a way sea-level residents have to work for and rarely achieve.

The two famous "lactate diseases" in Peat's framework — [[cancer]] and [[diabetes]] — are both altitude-protected. From the 1999 newsletter: "Lactic acid tends to be always present in the blood in hypothyroidism, diabetes, and obesity — insufficient carbon dioxide is produced, and lactic acid is produced even without oxygen deprivation or stress." And: "Cancer, the classical disease of extreme 'aerobic glycolysis,' is also negatively associated with increased altitude. Mortality from heart disease, too, decreases with increasing altitude."

## Mitochondrial proliferation: the Russian rats

The structural change altitude produces in mitochondria is the single most important piece of the longevity argument, because mitochondria are the cellular organelle whose decline most clearly tracks aging. From the [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 episode]]: "The Russians were the ones that really pioneered this. After Yandell Henderson, the Russians took up the study and found that if an animal was kept at high altitude for a few months, when it went back to low altitude, it lived the rest of its life retaining about twice as many mitochondria as it had at lower altitude. Meaning it was basically twice as efficient."

The same Russian groups ran the famous cancer-regression experiment: rats given carcinogens and taken to 17,000 feet recovered at high rates with or without chemotherapy, while sea-level controls all died ([[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|*Metabolism of Cancer*, July 2016]]). In the 2010 KMUD episode Peat gave the cleaner version: "The ones that were left at low altitude, 100% of them died. The others that were taken to 17,000 feet, 50% of them spontaneously threw off the cancer."

The mechanism is in the 2016 [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality*]] newsletter, where Peat proposed that CO2 itself stabilizes the mitochondrial coacervate: "In the way that carbon dioxide alters the shapes and electrical affinities of hemoglobin and other proteins, I propose that it increases the stability of the mitochondrial coacervate, causing it to 'recruit' additional proteins from its external environment... In the relative absence of carbon dioxide, or excess of alternative solutes and adsorbents, such as lactic acid, the stability of the mitochondrial phase would be decreased, and the mitochondria would be degraded in both structure and function."

The implication is that altitude does not just protect existing mitochondria; it calls more of them into existence. [[thyroid]] hormone produces the same response. Same 2016 newsletter: "When the amount of thyroid hormone is increased, or when the organism adapts to a high altitude, the number of mitochondria increases. But in energy deficient states such as diabetes, they don't." Both interventions converge on the same physiology — higher tissue CO2 — by different routes. Thyroid raises CO2 by accelerating its oxidative production; altitude raises CO2 by limiting its respiratory loss.

The [[naked-mole-rat|naked mole rat]] is the same experiment running in nature. From the 2011 [[protective-co2-and-aging|*Protective CO2 and Aging*]] newsletter: "The naked mole rat, another small animal with an extremely long lifespan (in captivity they have lived up to 30 years, 9 or 10 times longer than mice of the same size) has a low basal metabolic rate... They live in burrows that are kept closed, so the percentage of oxygen is lower than in the outside air, and the percentage of carbon dioxide ranges from 0.2% to 5% (atmospheric CO2 is about 0.038)... these animals are native to the high plains of Kenya and Ethiopia, where the low atmospheric pressure would be likely to increase the level of carbon dioxide in their tissues." A nine-to-ten-times-mouse lifespan in a high-CO2 burrow on the East African plateau is, in this reading, the same correlation Kashmir's Alzheimer's-absence and New Mexico's heart-disease data show, applied across species.

## Mountain sickness and acetazolamide

If altitude is so protective, why do unadapted ascenders develop acute mountain sickness, sometimes fatally? Peat's answer is that mountain sickness is not a sign that altitude is dangerous; it is a sign that the body's adaptive machinery cannot keep up with the speed of ascent. From the [[altitude-and-mortality|*Altitude and Mortality*]] newsletter: "The standard drug for preventing it is acetazolamide, which inhibits carbonic anhydrase and causes carbon dioxide to be retained, creating a slight tendency toward acidosis. This treatment probably mimics the retention of carbon dioxide that occurs naturally in altitude adapted people."

The mechanism is hyperventilation. The unadapted ascender, sensing low oxygen, breathes harder; the harder breathing blows off too much CO2; the resulting alkalosis constricts cerebral blood vessels, leaks the capillaries, and produces the lung and brain edema that kills. Acetazolamide blocks carbonic anhydrase, traps CO2 in tissue, restores the slight acidosis, and turns a failed adaptation into a successful one chemically. Peat in the [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 episode]]: "I have friends now who take a bottle of carbon dioxide with them when they go to Vail for high-altitude skiing. When they start getting mountain sickness, they take some carbon dioxide."

The clinical application Peat pressed hardest during COVID was the structural identity between mountain sickness and severe COVID lung disease. From the [[2021-09-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-september-2021-kmud|*Ask the Herb Doctor*, September 2021]]: "In the first weeks of the pandemic, two doctors in New York realized that the symptoms of COVID were essentially the same as the symptoms of high-altitude sickness. People who don't adapt to very high altitudes develop edema of the lungs and general inflammatory systems — a failure of the tissues to use oxygen properly. One of the established treatments for mountain sickness is a diuretic called acetazolamide. It restores the level of carbon dioxide in your body and the acidity." Permissive hypercapnia — letting CO2 accumulate by under-ventilating — is the right intervention for the same reason acetazolamide is the right intervention at altitude. The full cold-and-flu CO2-as-expectorant case is in [[cold-flu]].

Meerson's adaptation data sets the rate cap: ascending faster than 200 feet per day produces measurable stress, and rapid ascent to 10,000 feet without adaptation causes retinal bleeding in a large fraction of people, with presumed symptomless bleeding in other organs ([[altitude-and-mortality|*Altitude and Mortality*]]). Two hundred feet per day is slow. Most modern airplane-and-rental-car ascents to ski destinations exceed that by an order of magnitude.

## Bag breathing as a stand-in

Most people who would benefit from altitude cannot move. Peat's stand-in is the simplest intervention in his framework — rebreathing exhaled air from a paper bag. [[bag-breathing]] covers the technique in detail. The altitude-specific case is the equivalence Peat drew between living high and breathing into a bag: both are mechanisms for raising tissue CO2 by limiting its respiratory loss.

"On one of the Everest expeditions, they were treating people by putting them in a big plastic bag with oxygen. That's a fairly standard way to treat them, and it works. But they found that it was the carbon dioxide that they were breathing out that was accumulating in the bag, which was the really therapeutic agent" ([[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010]]). Same episode: "I've seen people with high blood pressure bring their blood pressure down 30 points in a day or two just by bag breathing repeatedly." The mechanism of repeated CO2 exposure is desensitization of the ventilatory drive: "Each time you increase the CO2 to an uncomfortable level, it's suppressing the lactate a little bit and lowering the adrenaline, free fatty acids, and many things that cause you to hyperventilate. So you're basically curing your tendency to breathe too much by adapting to repeated exposures to extra CO2."

Bag breathing, acetazolamide, baking soda in water, and altitude itself all work through the same pathway. They differ in dose, duration, and convenience. The honest limit of bag breathing was something Peat acknowledged directly in the [[altitude|altitude email]] when asked whether bag breathing could substitute fully for mountain living: "No." A few minutes of CO2 elevation, repeated, is a useful and reliable stress intervention. A lifetime of CO2 elevation through residence at 6,000+ feet is a different physiological state. The stand-in is real but limited. Charlie's hour with a paper bag does not produce a Caucasian centenarian's mitochondrial density.

## Where in altitude is enough

Minimum effective dose, from the [[altitude|altitude email]]: 5,740 feet (1,750 m) is "high enough to make a difference." 6,000-6,600 feet — Peat's summer house in Coeneo, Mexico — was enough to produce visible refractive change "after a few weeks." 2,000 meters (~6,560 ft) "has a noticeable effect after a couple of weeks, higher is better." Duration: "A couple of months at altitude will usually cause changes that last for several months even at lower altitude." The dose-response curve runs roughly continuously from below sea level up to about 12,000 feet, with diminishing returns above 14,000 where unadapted headache becomes common. Mexico City at 7,500 ft suppresses asthma despite the worst air pollution in the hemisphere; Leadville at 10,000 ft is near the high end of comfortable North American residence.

Weather matters too. From the 2016 [[2016-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-metabolism-of-cancer|*Metabolism of Cancer* episode]] on the Denver-vs-Albuquerque question: "High humidity is a slight stress to the system. Middle-range humidity is good, and as much daylight as possible. 15 hours of daylight seems to be optimal for recovery from cancer." The dry, sunlit, near-equatorial Andean plateau is, by this framework, close to optimal — Peat agreed with Andrew Murray's summary in the same episode that "being in the Andes would be a good idea — not humid, close to the equator, and high elevation."

## The asthma exception, and why it isn't really an exception

The single condition Peat acknowledged that can be worse at altitude is asthma — and even there, the literature ran the opposite direction from the popular assumption. From the [[altitude|altitude email]]: "I should have said respiratory diseases, meaning things like emphysema and pulmonary fibrosis. Asthma is usually improved at high altitude, above 6000 feet, for several reasons. Even in polluted Mexico City, at 7500 feet, there's very little asthma, but people who vacation in Acapulco often get asthma." The [[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010 episode]] put the same point in terms of incidence vs onset: an established asthmatic shouldn't ascend rapidly to Leadville, but "living at a high altitude makes you much less likely to develop it than living at sea level." For pre-existing structural respiratory damage — emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis — where the obstructive defect prevents CO2 clearance even at sea level, descent is appropriate. For prevention of those conditions, altitude is protective.

## Adaptation: walk for a month before you run

Peat's adaptation advice was conservative and consistent. From the [[altitude|altitude email]]: "I think walking should be the main exercise for the first several weeks." The same caution applies on the body-recomposition front: "A friend of mine who was there at that altitude last winter for almost two months lost 20 pounds while he was there" ([[2010-07-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-altitude|2010]]).

The weight loss is partly metabolic acceleration — at altitude the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] tips in favor of glucose oxidation. "When you burn glucose, you get much more carbon dioxide per unit of oxygen used. So at high altitude, a person uses their oxygen more efficiently and they're more reluctant to burn their stored fats." Counterintuitively, metabolism gets faster *and* fat oxidation slows; the difference is made up by more efficient glucose burning and by increased caloric intake. The Indian Army's altitude-nutrition studies were aimed at keeping enough calories in the soldiers to prevent adaptive weight loss from becoming structural muscle loss. Practical translation: when moving to altitude, eat more and walk slow until the body settles down.

## What altitude shares with thyroid, red light, and bag breathing

Altitude is one member of a family of interventions that all converge on the same physiology — chronic CO2 elevation, mitochondrial proliferation, lactate and serotonin and cortisol suppression. The family includes [[thyroid]] supplementation (raises CO2 production at the source), [[red-light]] therapy (drives mitochondrial cytochrome-c-oxidase activity), [[bag-breathing]] (limits CO2 respiratory loss), [[baking-soda]] in water (introduces bicarbonate directly), [[aspirin]] (uncouples mitochondria, shifts metabolism toward CO2), and [[progesterone]] (calms ventilatory drive). They are convergent, not interchangeable. A sea-level hypothyroid with shallow chest breathing can recover much of what altitude provides through the rest of the family. An Andean villager whose other physiology is sound is running the entire family in parallel as the structural condition of life.

The shared output is what Peat called the high-energy state — efficient oxidative metabolism, low background stress hormones, warm extremities, an absence of edema and inflammation, the steady euphoria he described as the natural baseline of a healthy organism. That altitude appears to produce this state passively, simply by lowering atmospheric pressure on the inhabitant, is the strongest single argument for taking the framework seriously. The mountain populations who live to 100+ are not following a protocol. They are inhabiting one.

## Counter-arguments

**The recordkeeping critique.** The Andean / Caucasus / Hunza centenarian literature rests on pre-modern village records that demographers have demonstrated to be unreliable. Soviet-era propaganda incentives, religious-festival-based age inflation, and grandfather-grandson name sharing have inflated centenarian counts substantially. Old Parr at 152 and the 187-year-old Peruvian are folklore, not data. Peat never offered those counts as proof; what he relied on were insurance-actuarial data (cancer and heart disease mortality by altitude band) and clinical data (Kashmir Alzheimer's survey, Russian rat experiments) — data sets harder to dismiss.

**Confounding by genetics and diet.** Tibetan EPAS1 variants, Andean hemoglobin patterns, and Sherpa mitochondrial efficiency are real, generationally selected adaptations that may account for some of the longevity signal without invoking CO2 directly. Mountain diets also tend high in dairy, low in seed oils, light on processed grains — confounds the PUFA framework would itself predict as protective. The CO2 mechanism is likely doing real work; it is unlikely to be doing all the work. Blue Zone populations at low altitude (Okinawa, Sardinia, Nicoya) demonstrate that altitude is one longevity input, not the only one.

**The serotonin-and-suicide inversion contradicts mainstream psychiatry.** Mainstream literature treats serotonin elevation as therapeutic and reads the U.S. Mountain West's elevated regional suicide rates as evidence that altitude drives mood disorders. The U.S. data is heavily confounded by rural isolation, lower socioeconomic status, gun availability, and long-winter SAD. International cross-national data (Mexico, Colombia, Bolivia low suicide; South Korea, Lithuania, Bangladesh high suicide) reverses the U.S. signal and is probably the cleaner test — but the two readings cannot both be right and the disagreement is unresolved.

**Mitochondrial-biogenesis claims are species-extrapolated.** The "Russians showed altitude doubles mitochondria for life" claim rests on rat experiments from the 1960s-70s and has been only partially replicated in humans. Modern hypoxia-training research shows real but smaller mitochondrial-density gains in athletes that revert with detraining. Peat's framing of a permanent post-altitude doubling is probably overstated for the human case.

**Acute mountain sickness mortality is real.** The protective-altitude message coexists uneasily with documented HAPE and HACE deaths in unadapted ascenders, including metabolically healthy people who ignored the 200-feet-per-day Meerson rate cap. Acetazolamide is genuinely lifesaving on rapid ascent. The protective state requires adaptation; without it, altitude is dangerous.

**The COVID-mountain-sickness equivalence is contested.** Peat's framing of severe COVID as a CO2-deficiency syndrome treatable with acetazolamide-class drugs and permissive hypercapnia attracted attention in 2020 but never entered mainstream critical-care practice. Later mortality data did vindicate his ventilator-skepticism; the specific acetazolamide-as-treatment claim has weaker support.

## Data gaps

- **The intermittent-altitude question.** Most people who could plausibly add altitude to their lives could only do it part-time — a month a year, a weekend a month. Peat's email suggested a couple of months at altitude produces several months of benefit at lower altitude, but the dose-response curve for intermittent exposure is not characterized. Does weekdays at sea level and weekends at 7,000 feet do anything? The corpus does not say.

- **Pregnancy at altitude.** Peat noted the early embryo thrives at 10-15% CO2 and 5% oxygen — much higher than atmospheric ratios at sea level. He did not, however, address whether pregnant women should prefer altitude. Andean birth-weight data shows lower weights (traditionally read as harm), but the framework would predict the same babies should be metabolically healthier across the rest of life. Real gap.

- **Children and altitude.** Almost nothing in the corpus addresses pediatric altitude exposure specifically. The Kashmir survey covered all ages and the asthma-prevention claim implicitly favors altitude childhood, but whether moving a family with young children to mountain living produces measurable longevity benefit is unaddressed.

- **Altitude training vs altitude longevity.** Modern altitude-training literature is large, recent, and focused almost entirely on athletic performance (red cell mass, VO2 max) rather than longevity. Whether the performance-training adaptations confer the longevity adaptations is uncertain.

- **Hyperbaric oxygen therapy.** If high altitude works through CO2 retention, the inverse — hyperbaric oxygen — should harm by the same mechanism (Haldane runs the wrong way). The clinical literature shows benefit for narrow conditions (CO poisoning, diabetic wounds, decompression sickness). Peat alluded to this occasionally but never wrote a systematic position.

- **Carbogen, CO2 baths, transdermal CO2.** Yandell Henderson's 1930s carbogen-for-stroke protocol was a Peat influence, but he never wrote a focused article. Eastern European CO2 spa traditions similarly received passing mentions only. Whether these are reasonable sea-level stand-ins is unresolved in the corpus.

- **Long-term aging biomarkers in altitude residents.** A prospective study of telomere length, methylation age, and standard biomarkers in matched altitude-vs-sea-level populations would settle much of the debate. As of the corpus dates, no such study existed.
