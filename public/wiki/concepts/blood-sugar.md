# Blood Sugar

Ray Peat did not write a single defining essay on blood sugar — he wrote about it everywhere. The 2009 newsletter [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context*]] is the closest he came to a stand-alone treatment, but the framework runs through almost every interview from 2008 onward. The recurring claim is one line from that newsletter: "Unsaturated fatty acids activate the stress hormones, sugar restrains them." Almost everything else in Peat's clinical universe — [[thyroid]] function, [[cortisol]] excess, immune competence, sleep quality, the susceptibility to viral infection covered in [[cold-flu]] — runs through the question of whether the body can hold its blood sugar steady.

The orthodox picture is that high blood sugar is the disease, that insulin is the treatment, and that anyone whose fasting glucose rises must restrict sugar and starch in equal measure. Peat inverted this almost completely. In his framework, *falling* blood sugar is the upstream pathology. The cells' inability to oxidize the glucose that is present is the disease. And the body's elaborate hormonal response to hypoglycemia — adrenaline, cortisol, growth hormone, prolactin, free fatty acids, [[serotonin]] — is what we feel as anxiety, inflammation, insomnia, autoimmunity, fatigue, and eventually [[diabetes]] itself. This article is the upstream concept page for the [[diabetes]] article that follows.

## Selye's 300 mg% rule: blood sugar as the inflammation governor

The single most powerful experimental result in Peat's blood-sugar corpus is the [[hans-selye|Hans Selye]]-era observation that blood glucose level determines whether an organism can tolerate inflammatory stress at all. Peat cited it most explicitly in the [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|*Ask the Herb Doctor* Diabetes I episode, February 2014]]:

> "A man named Damke Wicks showed about 30 or 40 years ago that lowering blood sugar activates the inflammatory process, and doubling the blood sugar will stop allergies to almost everything. So every episode of hypoglycemia is stressing your immune system as well as activating the stress hormones that compensate by turning protein into sugar."

In the [[2016-03-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-allergy|*Ask the Herb Doctor* Allergy episode, March 2016]] he gave the operational number: blood glucose at ~300 mg% was "the completely protective level" against the deadliest allergens in the Selye anaphylaxis experiments. Insulin — i.e., induced hypoglycemia — made the mildest allergen deadly. The same model holds across virtually every inflammatory condition Peat discussed. Hypoglycemia is not a thing you experience occasionally between meals; it is the underlying signal that activates the body's entire injury-response apparatus.

This is the inversion that most distinguishes Peat from the mainstream picture. Mainstream medicine treats hyperglycemia as the threat. Peat treated hypoglycemia — including the *intracellular* hypoglycemia produced when [[pufa]]-derived free fatty acids block glucose oxidation (see [[randle-cycle]]) — as the threat. "The basic reason for hypoglycemia is that it isn't being oxidized fully and instead it's turning to lactic acid," he said in one interview synthesized in the chapter encyclopedia. The blood sugar number on the lab report is the surface phenomenon. The cell's ability to *use* its sugar is the substrate.

## The cascade: low glucose → adrenaline → cortisol → tissue breakdown

The cascade that runs from a missed meal to the disintegration of muscle, thymus, and skin is the most-walked path in Peat's blood-sugar writing. Every interview returns to it. The clearest single rendering is from the [[2018-06-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-positive-thinking-sleep-and-repair|June 2018 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on sleep and repair]]:

> "When blood sugar isn't able to produce the carbon dioxide it should, it is wasted and becomes lactic acid. This raises the pH of the cell interior, creating a catabolic, excited state. Brain cells ultimately die or are blocked. In reaction to falling glucose oxidation, adrenaline rises. Adrenaline should bring up glucose and stop the stress reaction. But if you don't have enough stored glucose, you resort to cortisol. Cortisol provides glucose by breaking down muscle, thymus, and skin cells. If that continues, you get a chronic stress cycle: low blood sugar, increased adrenaline, increased cortisol, and breakdown."

The 2009 newsletter [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context*]] gives the same loop in the form a physician would write it: "If protein is eaten without carbohydrate, it will stimulate insulin secretion, lowering blood sugar and activating the stress response, leading to the secretion of adrenalin, cortisol, growth hormone, prolactin, and other hormones. The adrenalin will mobilize glycogen from the liver, and (along with other hormones) will mobilize fatty acids, mainly from fat cells. Cortisol will activate the conversion of protein to amino acids, and then to fat and sugar, for use as energy."

Notice the asymmetry. Adrenaline is the appropriate first response — fast, glucose-mobilizing, reversible. It only works if the liver actually has glycogen to release. When it doesn't, the body falls through to cortisol, which is structurally catabolic. The [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|Jodellefit interview, June 2019]] makes the cost explicit: "Cortisol maintains your blood sugar level so that you can keep working, but it does it by converting your protein. Tissues, muscles, skin, thymus gland, lymphatic tissues in general, and finally the digestive system — liver and intestine — will be cannibalized to make sugar and energy under the influence of cortisol."

The thymus is the most sensitive of these tissues. From the [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|EastWest Healing interview, April 2011]]: "Cortisol maintains blood sugar by blocking the use of sugar for most purposes. Immune cells, which the thymus consists of, cannot use sugar in the presence of cortisol. Cortisol forces them to metabolize fats, and unsaturated fats will quickly kill the thymus. It only takes about three hours for the thymus gland to disappear during profound stress." This is the structural cost of a single severe blood-sugar crash, and it explains why every chronic-illness page in this vault eventually points back to glycogen stability.

## The liver as glucose buffer

The organ that decides whether the body ever has to enter the cortisol cascade is the [[liver]]. A healthy liver, holding adequate glycogen, lets you sleep through the night, fast for a few hours, run an unexpected errand, or sit through a stressful meeting without paying the protein-tax. A depleted liver makes every gap between meals a metabolic emergency.

From the chapter encyclopedia, summarizing the recurring Peat position: "If your thyroid and other hormones are in the right concentration, your liver should be able to store at least eight hours of glycogen. Everyone should be able to get through the night on the amount of glycogen in their liver without resorting to other sources of energy. However, when your thyroid is a little low, or if your estrogen is too high, your liver sometimes can hardly store any glycogen. Typically, when your glycogen runs out, you send out a surge of adrenaline, which will squeeze the last little bit of glucose out of your liver." The 24-hour fasting window of a healthy adult is roughly 8 hours of glycogen plus 16 hours of stress-hormone-mobilized fat and protein. Peat's framework wants to push the entire experience back into the first 8 hours by keeping the liver topped up.

Fructose is the substrate that does this better than any other sugar. "Thyroid deficiency is the most common reason. Selenium is needed to activate the thyroid, but to use selenium, you need glucose. Fructose happens to be the best stimulant for the liver to store glycogen" — from the [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|EastWest Healing interview, April 2011]]. The full mechanism is in [[fructose]] — bypass of the [[randle-cycle]] inhibition, direct activation of pyruvate dehydrogenase, no insulin surge — but the operational point for this page is that the liver's glycogen buffer prefers a sucrose-containing input (half fructose, half glucose) to pure starch or pure glucose.

The corollary is that estrogen excess and [[hypothyroidism]] both attack the buffer from the supply side. Estrogen blocks glycogen storage. Hypothyroidism slows the entire enzymatic chain that converts ingested sugar to stored glycogen. From the chapter encyclopedia, Peat speaking autobiographically: "I was always craving sugar until I took thyroid medication at about 40 years old. I suddenly had no more sugar cravings and could go eight hours without getting shaky." A person who *cannot* fast for eight hours without symptoms does not need willpower training. They need a functioning liver, which usually means functioning [[thyroid]].

## The T3 feedback loop: why blood sugar regulates the thyroid that regulates blood sugar

The recursive piece of Peat's argument — the one that makes blood sugar a hub concept rather than a downstream variable — is the liver's role in converting inactive T4 into active T3. From the [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|Diabetes I episode]]: "When cells can't get enough glucose, it not only turns on the stress hormones, it turns off the production of thyroid hormone. If the cell isn't able to take up and use glucose, the liver becomes unable to activate the pre-thyroid hormone, thyroxine (T4), turning it into the active T3."

The 2009 newsletter puts the same loop in a single sentence: "Sugar and thyroid hormone (T3, triiodothyronine) correct many parts of the problem. The conversion of T4 into the active T3 requires glucose, and in diabetes, cells are deprived of glucose. Logically, all diabetics would be functionally hypothyroid."

This is why Peat's framework cannot be reduced to "eat more sugar" or "take thyroid." Each enables the other. A hypothyroid liver can't store glycogen, can't activate T4 to T3, can't dispose of [[estrogen]], and can't detoxify [[endotoxin]] from the gut — all four of which feed back into lower blood sugar tolerance, more frequent stress-hormone surges, more catabolic damage. The intervention is upstream of all of them: keep the substrate (sugar, salt, minerals) available so the liver can do its work, and the system stabilizes from the inside.

## The free-fatty-acid trap

If a thyroid-deficient liver is the *supply-side* problem with blood sugar regulation, free fatty acids are the *demand-side* problem — they jam the lock that lets cells use the sugar in the bloodstream. The full mechanism is in [[randle-cycle]] and [[free-fatty-acids]]; the blood-sugar-specific implication is that high circulating fatty acids produce a clinical picture indistinguishable from "diabetes" while the actual lesion is the cell's inability to oxidize glucose that is present in abundance.

From the [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|Diabetes I episode]]: "In hyperglycemia, what you have is the body's adjustment to the inability of cells to get enough energy because they can't oxidize glucose because the fats are blocking it. That's been known since the 1960s, called the Randle cycle, in which free fatty acids block the use of glucose. The body tries to overcome that poisonous effect of the free fatty acids by increasing glucose production. It gets around the block just by stuffing more glucose into the blood. To a great extent, the rising blood sugar shouldn't be fought in itself. The cause that interferes with the use of glucose should be concentrated on."

Where do the free fatty acids come from? Two sources, both stress-driven. First, *mobilization from body fat* — adrenaline and cortisol liberate stored fats from adipose tissue when blood sugar falls, which is precisely how the body amplifies the very hypoglycemic state it is trying to escape. Second, *dietary intake of [[pufa]]* — once stored in tissue (the half-life is two to four years), these fats are released during every stress episode and produce the Randle-cycle block at higher amplitude and longer duration than saturated fats would. "About 15 minutes after injecting this nutritional dose of emulsified soy oil intravenously, people would get hyperglycemic," Peat noted, citing the experimental work he revisited often. The Randle effect from oleate or linoleate is "instantaneous." The downstream glycation damage — what mainstream practice calls HbA1c and blames on sugar — is, in Peat's reading, mostly the work of PUFA-derived oxidation products binding to proteins rather than glucose binding to them.

The clinical implication is that a person with rising fasting glucose has, in Peat's framework, three real problems and one apparent one. The apparent one is the glucose number. The real ones are: (1) the [[pufa]] body load that produces the Randle block, (2) the stress system that mobilizes the fatty acids in the first place, and (3) the [[hypothyroidism]] that disables glycogen storage and T3 conversion. Lowering the apparent problem with insulin or a low-carb diet attacks none of the underlying three. See [[diabetes]] for the full clinical implication.

## Niacinamide and aspirin: the pharmacological anti-stress duo

Peat's two preferred pharmacologic tools for breaking the free-fatty-acid loop and pulling blood sugar stability back toward normal were [[niacinamide]] and aspirin. From the [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|Diabetes II episode, March 2014]]:

> "If you focus on stopping the liberation of fatty acids from your tissues and inhibit their conversion to prostaglandins, you can usually very quickly lower your blood sugar quite a bit and feel better. The two chemicals that are most practical for lowering free fatty acids and stopping the stress reaction are niacinamide and aspirin. Niacinamide has many effects, but the first one is inhibiting the lipase that liberates fats from your fat stores."

Niacinamide blocks lipolysis at the fat cell. Aspirin blocks prostaglandin synthesis downstream. Together they reduce the free-fatty-acid pool that drives the Randle block, allowing the cell to oxidize glucose normally — which lowers the compensatory hyperglycemia without any insulin involvement. This is conceptually the opposite of standard diabetes pharmacology, which lowers blood sugar by *removing it from the blood* (insulin) rather than by *enabling cellular use of it*.

The dosing belongs in [[niacinamide]] and the application to inflammatory conditions in [[aspirin]] and [[cold-flu]]. The blood-sugar-specific point is that the entire intervention can be conceived as removing the inhibitors to glucose oxidation rather than reducing the substrate.

## The overnight curve: why cortisol peaks at dawn

The most clinically vivid application of Peat's blood-sugar framework is the overnight glycogen drain and the cortisol-adrenaline-prolactin surge that accompanies it. This is the mechanism behind 3 AM waking, night sweats, "menopausal" hot flashes, restless sleep in the elderly, and the morning anxiety that millions of people interpret as a separate psychiatric condition.

The clearest single passage is from the August 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hot flashes and night sweats, where Peat described what happens to a man's hormones under chronic stress:

> "Stress causes you to waste stored glucose, leading to fluctuating episodes of hypoglycemia. This triggers the release of cortisol to break down tissue into sugar. Cortisol is usually highest around dawn. If you are stressed, cortisol and estrogen can go extremely high, bringing blood sugar up. This rise in blood sugar lowers the adrenaline system, allowing the skin to produce sweat and become hot. The sweating and sense of heat go with very high cortisol."

The intervention was direct: "So if he woke up around 2:00 or 3:00 AM and had a glass of orange juice with a little salt, that might help?" — "Yes. I've known people who had nightmares every hour during the night. By waking up and having orange juice or milk, they could lengthen the time between nightmares. In a week or so, you can get your liver stocked up with enough glucose to sleep through the night." Same episode, summarizing the women's literature: "In the last few years, studies have shown that menopausal hot flashes can be prevented with sugar. A big dose of carbohydrate at bedtime works better than estrogen."

The [[2018-06-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-positive-thinking-sleep-and-repair|June 2018 sleep episode]] expanded the loop: "The main thing causing age-related interrupted sleep is decreased thyroid function slowing digestive processes. As blood sugar falls in darkness, you experience a toxic reaction to whatever is in your intestine. Irritation, inflammation, absorption of endotoxin, and surges of serotonin from the intestine get into the bloodstream, finish off your stored glycogen, and wake you up with a stress reaction."

That last passage is the link between blood sugar and the gut-axis material covered in [[endotoxin]] and [[cold-flu]]. A low-glycogen liver and an irritated bowel are not separate problems. They reinforce each other through the night, and the felt experience is interrupted sleep, sweating, nightmares, and a wired-tired morning. Peat's intervention sequence: a carrot salad or cooked mushrooms in the afternoon to clear the bowel; a low-protein, high-carbohydrate supper before bed (ice cream, salty chicken broth, milk with honey); and orange juice with salt at the bedside for the inevitable 2–3 AM glycogen-depletion wake-up until the liver is built back up.

## The "never be hungry" principle: grazing as anti-stress practice

The protocol-level operationalization of all the above is the principle Peat returned to in nearly every interview: don't let yourself get hungry. From the [[2011-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-energy-production-diabetes-and-saturated-fats|November 2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] on energy production: "If you eat frequently and thoroughly avoid stress-causing foods — don't let yourself get hungry enough to call on stress hormones to liberate fat — you can very quickly shift over to efficient metabolism. Frequent feeding, always with sugar and absolutely no PUFA, allows the slow disposition of the unsaturated toxic fats. The liver treats PUFA like other toxins; if it has the energy, it detoxifies them for excretion."

Hunger, in Peat's framework, is not virtue. It is the leading edge of the stress cascade. From the [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|Jodellefit interview]]: "Good nutrition should include plenty of carbohydrate so that you don't have to rely on cortisol to convert protein into glucose. Usually, something more than 100 grams per day of carbohydrate — preferably in the form of sucrose — is needed. If you're very active physically, 200 or 300 grams a day of carbohydrate, especially sugar, can be restorative to the brain."

From the [[sugar|*Sugar* email exchange]]: "To prevent stress, or to replenish glycogen stores after stress, your appetite for it is likely to be a good guide." Sugar craving is read as data, not as a moral failing or an addiction. From the same email: "There is a great anti-sugar cult, with even moralistic overtones, equating sugar craving with morphine addiction. Sugar craving is usually caused by the need for sugar, generally caused by hypothyroidism."

The grazing principle is the operational opposite of intermittent fasting, time-restricted eating, and the broader wellness-culture asceticism around food. The full counter-argument is below; the affirmative version is that the body running on small, frequent, sucrose- and salt-bearing meals — with milk and orange juice as the most-cited delivery systems — keeps the liver topped up, keeps cortisol low, keeps free fatty acids out of circulation, and keeps the T3 conversion humming. See [[anti-stress-diet]] for the full menu architecture.

## Why milk and orange juice

Of all the things Peat suggested people eat, two recur as the architectural foundations of stable blood sugar: milk and orange juice. From the [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|Jodellefit interview]]: "The reason I talk so much about milk and orange juice is that they are the most available foods that have the most components that tend to shut down excess cortisol production, lower adrenaline, and reduce serotonin and nitric oxide."

The mechanisms are distinct but converge. Orange juice provides fructose (~half the sugar in [[sucrose]]), the catalytic substrate that bypasses the Randle block and refills liver glycogen. It carries potassium — "Potassium, which is very abundant in all fruits, acts like insulin. So you don't have to secrete very much insulin for the same disposition of glucose," Peat noted in the chapter encyclopedia. It carries naringin (anti-inflammatory) and magnesium. The starch-equivalent in calories would spike insulin and contribute to fat deposition; the orange-juice version raises blood sugar smoothly and supports the liver.

[[Milk]] provides calcium (anti-PTH, anti-parathyroid-driven inflammation, anti-fat-deposition), lactose (a mild sugar, slowed by protein and fat), high-quality protein, and saturated fat that further moderates the absorption curve. From the 2012 [[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues* newsletter]]: "A daily diet that includes two quarts of milk and a quart of orange juice provides enough fructose and other sugars for general resistance to stress, but larger amounts of fruit juice, honey, or other sugars can protect against increased stress, and can reverse some of the established degenerative conditions."

The fruit-and-milk pairing is, in Peat's framework, almost a complete blood-sugar stabilization protocol on its own. The carbohydrate refills the liver; the calcium accelerates the cell's ability to use it; the protein and fat slow the absorption curve so insulin doesn't spike; the minerals support the enzymes that convert T4 to T3. Pairing them with a protein meal — Peat's "fruit before protein" rule — pre-stocks the liver with glycogen so the insulin response to amino acids doesn't trigger the compensatory cortisol release.

## The diabetes inversion

Because this article is the upstream concept for the [[diabetes]] page that follows, the framing matters: in Peat's view, diabetes is not a disease of too much sugar. It is a disease of cells starving for sugar in the presence of high blood sugar. From the [[2018-06-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-positive-thinking-sleep-and-repair|June 2018 episode]]: "Over 40 or 50 years, I've only found two or three people who had a sane diagnosis. In the 1940s, it was known that excessive cortisol would create all the symptoms of diabetes — wasting away of muscles and tissues, turning them into glucose. But practically, doctors simply look for high blood sugar and prescribe insulin. I don't recall running into someone whose cortisol, prolactin, parathyroid hormone, and growth hormone were considered in the diagnosis."

From the 2009 newsletter: "'Diabetes' is often the diagnosis, when excess cortisol is the problem. The hormones have traditionally not been measured before diagnosing diabetes and prescribing insulin or other chemical to lower the blood sugar." The same newsletter on the historical literature: "Antiserotonin drugs can sometimes alleviate stress and normalize blood sugar. Simply eating sucrose was recently discovered to restrain the stress hormone system."

This is not casual contrarianism. From the [[2014-03-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-ii-and-how-to-restore-and-protect-nerves|Diabetes II episode]]: "There were experiments in which if part of a dog's pancreas was removed, leaving a smaller reserve of insulin-producing cells, a single injection of a large dose of cortisol was enough to make the dog permanently diabetic. Once the tissues are unable to get glucose just from that one episode of stress-induced lack of insulin, the cells are basically dying because they can't get the glucose they need. So they call on fatty acids from stores, and the fatty acids, in turn, kill the remaining or regenerating insulin-producing cells. So it starts a vicious circle."

Cortisol kills the pancreas. Free fatty acids finish it. The cascade that starts at "I skipped breakfast" ends, decades later, at full-blown type 2 diabetes. The intervention point is anywhere on the cascade; the earlier, the cheaper. See [[diabetes]] for the full clinical-management framework; see [[anti-stress-diet]] for the preventive practice.

## Recommended ranges

Peat was sparing with hard numbers, but the corpus yields a usable architecture:

- **Daily carbohydrate**: 100 g minimum for a sedentary adult; 200–300 g for active people, "preferably in the form of sucrose" ([[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|Jodellefit]]).
- **Daily added sugar**: "Up to ten ounces can be o.k., depending on your metabolic rate and needs. Budd and Piorry used up to 12 ounces per day therapeutically" ([[sugar|aspirin/sugar email]]).
- **Foundation feeding**: two quarts of milk and one quart of orange juice as a baseline; more during higher-stress periods ([[sugar-issues|*Sugar Issues*]]).
- **Sources, in order**: ripe fruits (oranges, grapes, watermelon, guava), honey, white sucrose; lactose from milk; minimal grain or legume starch; no [[pufa]]-containing fats.
- **Bedtime carbohydrate**: a glass of milk with honey, ice cream, or salty chicken broth before bed to pre-stock liver glycogen and reduce the 2–3 AM cortisol surge.
- **Timing**: small, frequent, never long enough between meals to feel hunger; aim to feel warm hands and feet, not edgy or shaky.

These are guidance ranges. They don't substitute for tracking the actual experience — Peat's preferred biomarkers were pulse rate, body temperature, sleep continuity, and absence of stress-driven cravings rather than a fasting glucose number.

## Counter-arguments

The blood-sugar position above runs against most of contemporary nutritional consensus. The article would be incomplete without naming the contradictions.

**Low-carbohydrate and ketogenic diets.** The dominant modern view is that carbohydrate is the metabolic problem and that lowering insulin via carbohydrate restriction is therapeutic. Peat treated this as exactly inverted: the chronic low-carbohydrate state forces the body onto fat oxidation, mobilizes free fatty acids, blocks the Randle cycle, suppresses T3 conversion, and raises cortisol. "Within two or three days of fasting or low-carb dieting, a person's metabolic rate drops tremendously. They lose mostly muscle tissue because of the stress produced by low blood sugar. It is the degree of free fatty acids being mobilized that suppresses your ability to burn calories" — [[2011-04-27-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|EastWest Healing]]. Symptom relief on a keto diet, in this framework, is the temporary calming of an over-stimulated insulin-spiking starch metabolism, not a structural fix. The cost is cortisol, lost muscle, and eventual hypothyroid features. Many practitioners report the opposite from their patients; the dispute is real and unresolved.

**Intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating.** The "16:8" and longer-fasting protocols popular in the wellness world ask the body to spend 8–24 hours in the cortisol-mobilization phase that Peat's framework treats as the disease. "Just 24 hours of fasting is enough to damage all of your functions, especially energy production," Peat said in the March 2020 *Politics and Science* episode. The autophagy and insulin-sensitivity benefits cited by IF proponents are, in Peat's reading, mostly the effects of *removing* the [[pufa]]-laden processed foods that those same people would have eaten otherwise. The benefit comes from what you stopped eating, not from how long you went without food. People with intact liver glycogen and high metabolic rate generally cannot tolerate IF without becoming irritable, cold, and insomniac.

**Glycemic index as the right framework.** The mainstream "low GI is good, high GI is bad" model treats blood sugar fluctuation itself as the problem and treats slow-absorbing complex carbohydrates as the solution. Peat's framework treats *starch* as the problem (persorption damage per Volkheimer, endotoxin from incomplete digestion, sharp insulin spikes from glucose-only loads) and *sucrose* — half fructose — as protective. "Orange juice and sucrose have a lower glycemic index than starch or whole wheat or white bread," Peat pointed out in the chapter material. The body handles fructose-bearing sugars with less insulin and more direct liver-glycogen replenishment than it handles "complex carbs."

**Sugar feeds cancer.** The Otto-Warburg-derived popular claim — cancer cells consume glucose at high rates, therefore restricting glucose starves the tumor — was a position Peat directly rejected. The cancer's high glucose appetite is met by cortisol-driven cannibalization of host tissues whether or not sugar is eaten. Restricting dietary sugar accelerates the wasting; providing sugar lowers cortisol and slows the host-tissue loss. See [[cancer]] for the full position.

**Insulin resistance as the central pathology.** Mainstream endocrinology treats insulin resistance as the cause of metabolic disease; sugar restriction follows from the model. Peat treated insulin resistance as a downstream symptom of [[pufa]] accumulation, free-fatty-acid-driven Randle block, and hypothyroidism. The cause is not too much insulin; it is the lipid environment that blocks glucose oxidation in the first place. The intervention is removing PUFA and supporting thyroid, not lowering carbohydrate.

**HbA1c as a sugar-driven marker.** Peat read the literature on advanced glycation end products as showing that most "glycation" damage in diabetics is actually PUFA-derived oxidation products binding to proteins, not glucose binding to them. "Most of the so-called glycation is really the oxidative breakdown fragments of the polyunsaturated fats," he said in one of the *Sugar Myths* episodes. The HbA1c lab value still tracks long-term blood sugar well, but the *damage mechanism* mainstream practice attributes to it may be largely PUFA-driven. This is a contested empirical claim.

**Adrenal fatigue.** A common alternative-medicine framing — that the adrenals "wear out" from chronic stress and need to be "supported" with herbs or low-dose hydrocortisone — was something Peat treated with skepticism. The state being described is real, but it is a *glycogen* problem, not an adrenal problem. Refilling the liver buffer with sucrose, salt, calcium, and protein removes the demand for cortisol; the adrenals respond by normalizing. Stimulant adaptogens and exogenous cortisol address the symptom and not the cause.

## Data gaps

Peat's blood-sugar framework was developed primarily on adult, ambulatory, free-living patients in mostly North American contexts. Several common questions are explicitly under-served by the corpus.

- **Type 1 diabetes.** The framework above is overwhelmingly about Type 2. Peat supported exogenous insulin where the pancreas had been destroyed but the corpus does not contain a focused Type 1 treatment. Pediatric Type 1 should be managed by standard pediatric endocrinology.

- **Pediatric blood sugar.** Peat almost never specified pediatric doses or ranges. Children's higher metabolic rates and faster glycogen turnover mean the adult heuristic should apply more strongly, but the specifics are absent.

- **Pregnancy and gestational diabetes.** Peat addressed pregnancy blood sugar approvingly — "Glucose is essential for the baby's brain development" ([[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|Diabetes I]]) — but did not write a focused piece on managing gestational diabetes within his framework. He viewed the diagnostic criteria as re-pathologizing a normal pregnancy state; what to do when given the diagnosis is under-developed.

- **Continuous glucose monitoring.** CGMs became consumer products after most of Peat's productive writing. A Peat-lens reading would attend less to peak height after a meal and more to overnight trough depth, but this is inferred.

- **Exercise and athletics.** Peat preferred short, intense activity over endurance work, but the operational fueling protocols for athletes (carb per hour, carb-protein ratios, recovery feeding) are sparse.

- **Alcohol and blood sugar.** Peat treated fructose as the antidote to alcohol toxicity but did not address chronic alcohol use's effects on glycogen storage and stress-hormone tone.

- **Newer non-caloric sweeteners.** Stevia, allulose, and monk fruit were not in his corpus. The "sweet-taste-triggers-cortisol" mechanism would predict similar problems, but this is inference.

- **PUFA-clearance timeline.** Peat said "about four years" for full tissue PUFA turnover, but the evidence is thin. Practical blood-sugar improvements often appear in weeks, well before structural lipid turnover completes.
