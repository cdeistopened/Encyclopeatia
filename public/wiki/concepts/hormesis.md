# Hormesis

Hormesis is the doctrine that a small dose of a poison, or a brief episode of a stressor, is positively good for the organism — a "biphasic" response in which the substance harms at high doses and helps at low ones. In wellness vocabulary it underwrites cold plunges, saunas, fasted training, intermittent fasting, hypoxic chambers, and the slogan that what doesn't kill you makes you stronger. In regulatory vocabulary it underwrites the argument that tobacco smoke, radon, asbestos, dioxin, low-level radiation, and ambient air pollution should not be regulated below a "threshold" because, below that threshold, they confer benefit. The two vocabularies are usually pursued by different people. They are the same argument.

Ray Peat treated the entire frame as a public-relations artifact. He traced its modern form to a specific industry — the post-war nuclear weapons and nuclear power complex — and to a small group of paid researchers with T.D. Luckey at the center. He treated its translation into wellness culture as a downstream popularization of the same argument: that intermittent doses of damage train the organism toward a higher functional state. And he counterposed his own framework, in which the variable that matters is not the *signal* (a brief insult) but the *state* (whether the cell has the energetic resources to recover, or whether the recovery leaves a permanent scar).

The inversion in one sentence: *intermittent stress signals are not the same as chronic energy state, and the heat shock proteins, autophagy, cortisol pulse, and lactate spike that hormesis advocates point to as evidence of adaptive benefit are, in Peat's reading, the molecular evidence that the cell has been forced into a survival metabolism it will not fully come back from*. The recovery is partial, the scar accumulates, and the ratchet is the aging process itself.

## The fraud line: how "radiation hormesis" came to be

The historical claim that has to be stated first, because nothing in the rest of Peat's framework on this topic makes sense without it, is that the modern concept of hormesis was manufactured by industry as a defense against pollution litigation. Peat was specific about the names, the institutions, and the dollar amounts.

"Fifty years ago, the government had a campaign to tell people that radiation was good for you — the idea of 'radiation hormesis,' that a little poison is good for you. That is a perversion of the idea of stress" — from the [[2019-04-27-jodellefit-jodellefit-stress-and-your-health|April 2019 *Jodellefit* episode on stress and health]]. The campaign was not abstract. "In the 1950s, the Atomic Energy Commission found that bomb tests were causing cancer, but to explain away radioactive elements in babies' teeth (Project Sunshine), they invented the idea that small doses are good for you" — from the [[2020-02-19-butter-living-podcast-butter-living-podcast-fertility-pregnancy-and-development|February 2020 *Butter Living Podcast*]]. The radiation industry then exported the rhetorical structure to other regulated pollutants.

The fullest historical telling comes from the [[2017-12-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diagnosis|December 2017 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on diagnosis]]:

> By the 1940s, industries started producing a reaction to their polluting processes. Atomic bombs were being exploded in Nevada, poisoning everyone downwind all the way to New York. People were getting concerned about the quality of air and water; city smog was killing people. The industries started fighting back, saying, "Look at history; if something didn't kill you, it made you stronger." [...] Industries started funneling huge amounts of money. One current professor in Boston has received more than $40 million in research to promote this phony idea of hormesis. About 15 years ago, they started defining it as a biphasic response, meaning a small amount moves biology in one direction, and a larger amount moves it in the other. Basically, the idea was that a little bit of poison is good for you.

The redefinition is important. Hormesis as the word existed in nineteenth-century pharmacology — Arndt and Schulz had proposed something like a biphasic dose-response for medicines in the 1880s. What changed in the 1990s and 2000s, in Peat's reading, was that the term was rehabilitated and re-funded as a generalized regulatory argument: every pollutant has a threshold, below which it is not only harmless but beneficial; therefore the regulation of low doses is itself a public-health harm. "That developed into the argument by big think tanks that the government should stop regulating industrial pollution because the regulation is causing damage to public health by denying us the 'beneficial' pollution" (same episode). The argument is, at face, absurd. It also won. Most modern regulatory thresholds rest on it.

The personnel pipeline ran through T.D. Luckey, who served the nuclear industry for, by Peat's count, fifty years. From the [[2011-03-30-politics-and-science-politics-science-obfuscation-of-radiation-science-by-industry|March 2011 *Politics and Science* episode on obfuscation of radiation science]]:

> T.D. Luckey [...] was interviewed by one of these "rock ladies" talking about his radioactive rock that he liked to rub. Since his history for 50 years has been serving the nuclear industry to tell people that radiation is good for you rather than harmful, I realized that with Obama and his nuclear industry support, T.D. Luckey and the radioactive rocks were just part of a new wave of promotion of radiation security ideas. [...] When I was talking about Ernest Sternglass and his low-level radiation book, people who worked in the nuclear industry told me, "Oh, that's rubbish. No one should read that stuff. It's all phony." I found out that they had been given weekly seminars at the nuclear factory to debunk people like John Gofman, Linus Pauling, and Sternglass. T.D. Luckey was one of the regulars at those industry seminars to educate the workers that those people were such lunatics and phonies that no respectable person should even look at their publications.

The institutional capture was explicit in the trade press. From the 2011 newsletter [[radiation-and-growth|*Radiation and Growth*]]: R. E. Alexander, "a former chairman of the Health Physics Society's public relations committee, told the society's board of directors that Ernest Sternglass's work had led to publicity that was damaging to the nuclear industry. He said that the 'basic publicity objective' of the Society was 'to let the public know that due to a frankly acknowledged need, we have a new technology, health physics, which will permit them to enjoy the benefits of nuclear energy safely.' Facts about the harmful effects of radiation were harmful to the Society, but 'while we try to avoid publicizing papers that do not contribute to our basic objective, there is no way to prevent such publicity absolutely.'" The Society did not consider this an admission. They printed it.

For Peat, the relevance was that the same researchers who had been paid for decades to deny harm at low doses were now, in the 2010s, repackaging the same data as proof of benefit. The hormesis literature he encountered was disproportionately authored by, funded by, or descended intellectually from this lineage. He treated peer review, in this corner of the field, as a captured signal.

## The no-threshold counter

If the industry argument is that there is a dose below which radiation is harmless or beneficial, the counter is that any dose causes proportional damage. Peat sided with the no-threshold biologists — Sternglass, John Gofman, Alice Stewart — and cited the experimental evidence that even single quantal exposures produce measurable genetic damage. From the [[radiation-and-growth|*Radiation and Growth*]] newsletter (2011), citing the 1997 PNAS paper *Mutagenic effects of a single and an exact number of alpha particles in mammalian cells* by Hei et al.: "The nucleus of each cell was irradiated with either 1, 2, 4, or 8 alpha particles at a linear energy transfer of 90 keV/μm consistent with the energy spectrum of domestic radon exposure. [...] These data provide direct evidence that a single alpha particle traversing a nucleus will have a high probability of resulting in a mutation and highlight the need for radiation protection at low doses."

The same newsletter cited the Li et al. paper (*Cancer Research*, 2001) showing "persistent genetic instability was induced by nongenotoxic stress exposures such as heat treatment, serum starvation, or the tumor microenvironment, as well as genotoxic stress such as ionizing radiation and exposure to hydrogen peroxide." This is the experimental finding that does most of the work in Peat's framework: heat, starvation, and "stress" produce the same persistent genetic instability as ionizing radiation. The wellness-culture practices that hormesis advocates have repackaged as therapeutic — heat exposure (sauna), starvation (intermittent fasting), oxidative challenge (cold plunge) — generate the same molecular signature as the radiation exposures the same researchers spent decades denying were harmful.

There is also a sub-threshold mechanism that does not depend on DNA damage at all. From the [[2009-04-27-politics-and-science-politics-science-ionizing-radiation-in-context-part-2|April 27, 2009 *Politics and Science* on ionizing radiation, part 2]]: "These little sub-threshold doses actually modify the water structure, which directs the way the tissue develops and affects things like the immune system and the way nerves pattern themselves. In a young individual, that deviation can produce drastic and fatal effects very quickly. In mature people, it accelerates aging and degeneration." The structured water of the cell, in [[gilbert-ling|Gilbert Ling's]] framework, is the actual substrate of cellular organization, and disrupting it at any dose has consequences. The "no observable effect level" measurement is a measurement artifact: the relevant effects are not the ones the standard endpoints track.

## Hormesis as template — the rhetorical structure travels

The argument Peat made about radiation he then applied, methodically, to every other industrial pollutant whose toxicity had been challenged. The structure was always the same: identify the dose-response curve, claim a biphasic shape, locate a "beneficial" zone at low dose, neutralize regulation.

**Estrogen.** From [[the-dire-effects-of-estrogen-pollution|*The Dire Effects of Estrogen Pollution*]] and [[estriol-des-ddt-etc|*Estriol, DES, DDT, Etc.*]]: "How is it possible that the idea of 'protection' from a weak estrogen seems convincing to so many? Isn't this the same process that we saw when the nuclear industry promoted Luckey's doctrine of 'radiation hormesis,' literally the claim that 'a little radiation is positively good for us'?" The argument that estriol, DES, or environmental phytoestrogens "protect" against the strong estrogens is, by Peat's reading, structurally identical to the radiation-hormesis argument and produced by the same kind of money. See [[xenoestrogens]].

**Carbon monoxide and city smog.** CO is currently repositioned in cell-biology textbooks as an "anti-inflammatory signaling molecule," and inhaled low-dose CO has been studied as an experimental therapy. Peat's response, from the same 2017 [[2017-12-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diagnosis|*Ask the Herb Doctor*]]: "It is interesting that [carbon monoxide and nitric oxide] have now become medically and biologically 'desirable.' It makes the fear of smog go away if you think they are a defense. [...] When you look at how [CO] is produced in the body, it breaks down the heme group, which is involved in all our essential oxidative metabolic processes inside the cell. As it breaks down heme, it releases a molecule of carbon monoxide and an atom of iron. The iron acts randomly as an oxidant." A sign that heme is being destroyed and free iron released has been re-presented as beneficial signaling.

**Nitric oxide.** Same logic. NO is sold as a desirable signaling molecule because it dilates blood vessels; the framing ignores that it is a free radical, that it inactivates [[cytochrome-c-oxidase|cytochrome c oxidase]] (terminal step of oxidative phosphorylation), and that chronic elevation marks the failed-metabolism state. See [[oxidative-metabolism]].

**"Adaptogens."** The polyphenol class (resveratrol, curcumin, sulforaphane) is now marketed as longevity compounds on the hormesis premise that low-dose toxins activate protective stress pathways. The newsletter [[caffeine-a-vitamin-like-nutrient-or-adaptogen-questions-about-tea-and-coffee-cancer-and-other-degenerative-diseases-and-the-hormones|*Caffeine: A Vitamin-Like Nutrient or Adaptogen?*]] addresses it with the driest possible aside: "But poisons can stimulate growth ('hormesis'), and food restriction can extend longevity. We still have to determine basic things such as the optimal rate of growth, and the optimal size." The growth signal a poison provokes is not the same as growth from feeding the system. Caffeine, by Peat's account, is an actual nutrient and is misclassified as an "adaptogen" precisely because the category was built around the hormesis premise.

The pattern is always the same: find a substance whose harm at high doses is established; propose a low-dose biphasic curve; identify a downstream biochemical effect (heme degradation, vasodilation, gene silencing); reframe the effect as adaptive; argue that regulation at low doses harms public health. The wellness market and the regulatory market converge.

## Heat shock proteins: survival, not improvement

The single hardest case for Peat's framework is the heat shock response, because heat shock proteins (HSPs) are real, their function in stabilizing damaged proteins is real, and their induction by mild heat does demonstrably improve survival under subsequent severe heat — the classic dose-response curve of "preconditioning." This is the molecular backbone of the sauna and contrast-bath wellness literature. Peat addressed it on Danny Roddy's [[51-heat-shock-proteins-antibiotic-resistance-dht-safety-energy-and-aging-with-ray-peat-phd|*Generative Energy* episode 51 on heat shock proteins]] with a metaphor that does the entire conceptual work of this article in one sentence.

> **Danny Roddy:** People in the health world say you should activate heat shock proteins via sauna to resist stress. How do you see that?
>
> **Dr. Raymond Peat:** It is true they help a cell survive partial cooking. That is where the idea of them being beneficial comes from — they help survival in terrible conditions. However, drug companies have noticed that this process de-energizes cells, turning them toward a lactic acid, cancer-like metabolism. As the life of the cell is saved by the heat shock proteins, its energy reverts to that simple metabolism.

The clarification, when Roddy pressed him on the persistence of the effect, is the line that defines Peat's whole position on hormesis:

> **Dr. Raymond Peat:** That is the general line of aging. It is like a ratchet that keeps you alive for the moment, but each time it is called on, it reduces your flexibility. It plugs a hole but limits function.

The cell that survived partial cooking is not the cell that existed before. It is a cell that has shifted toward [[lactic-acid|lactic-acid]]-producing glycolysis (the [[warburg-effect|Warburg]] direction, the cancer direction), in which 20% of total protein content can now be heat shock proteins themselves (a figure Peat cites in the [[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]] newsletter, noting that "in themselves, the shock proteins are immunosuppressive"). The local repair has come at the cost of permanent capacity. The next stress finds a cell already partially de-energized.

Georgi Dinkov, joining the same conversation, generalized the point: "Any event that the organism is exposed to that demands production of energy to deal with, which is really any event in life, if the energy production is not sufficient to meet the demand, then the heat shock proteins will get activated. So in other words, aging occurs every time there is a demand placed by the environment that we cannot meet up energetically." Peat: "Yep." Aging is the cumulative ratchet of insufficient-energy adaptations. The hormesis literature reads the ratchet click as a sign of strengthening.

This is the deepest layer of the inversion. The molecular markers that hormesis advocates point to — HSP upregulation, sirtuin activation, autophagy induction, AMPK signaling, mitochondrial biogenesis under stress — are, in Peat's reading, the molecular evidence that the cell has been forced into survival metabolism. They are not signs that the cell has been improved. They are signs that the cell has been damaged and has paid a structural price to survive. See [[oxidative-metabolism]] for what the alternative — a high-energy cell that does not have to activate these pathways — actually looks like.

## Stress vs. challenge: what Peat means by "perversion of the idea of stress"

The reason Peat called hormesis "a perversion of the idea of stress" rather than simply "wrong" is that there is a real distinction the hormesis literature collapses. He drew it cleanly in the [[2019-04-27-jodellefit-jodellefit-stress-and-your-health|April 2019 *Jodellefit*]] episode:

> A teenager meeting a challenge — like a tennis game or building something — builds the body and skills. That is not stress. Stress is a demand put on you that you didn't ask for and don't have the ability to do easily and with pleasure. Doing a boring, stupid job that you don't love is poisoning your tissues constantly because you aren't getting pleasure out of the activity.

The teenager building muscle through tennis is not in [[stress|stress]]. The teenager is doing work the body has the energetic resources to perform, with pleasure (which is itself a marker of adequate energy), and the work builds structure. The same teenager subjected to inescapable shock, prolonged cold, fasted training, or a job they hate is in stress — the demand exceeds the resources, and the only adaptation available is a metabolic downshift. The two situations produce different molecular signatures and different long-term outcomes. The hormesis literature treats them as equivalent because it measures only the input (a "dose" of demand) and not the state of the cell receiving it.

The same distinction shows up in [[learned-helplessness]]. The rat that swims for two days after watching another rat escape is not in stress; its sympathetic apparatus is intact and resourced. The rat that drowns in five minutes after inescapable shock is in stress; its parasympathetic apparatus has taken over and the heart stops in a relaxed position. Same physical situation. Different metabolic state. The variable is not the demand. It is whether the cell can meet the demand without falling out of oxidative respiration.

This is also why Peat treated the [[hans-selye|Selye]] tradition with admiration despite Selye's "general adaptation syndrome" having been one of the conceptual ancestors of hormesis. Selye distinguished the *alarm* and *resistance* phases (where the organism is mobilizing real adaptive resources) from the *exhaustion* phase (where the organism collapses). The hormesis literature, in Peat's reading, collapses alarm-and-resistance with exhaustion: any signal of mobilization is taken as evidence of strengthening, even when the same signal in another context would be read as the early phase of collapse. From [[immunodeficiency-dioxins-stress-and-the-hormones|*Immunodeficiency, Dioxins, Stress and the Hormones*]]: "F. Z. Meerson's work showed that cortisol, and the free fatty acids mobilized by stress, have a toxic influence on the mitochondrial energy production system. Both cortisol and the free fatty acids block the efficient use of glucose for producing energy, creating a diabetes-like condition. The exhaustion problem caused by excessive stress is generalized, not just a matter of adrenal insufficiency."

## Application: the wellness cults

Peat's framework on hormesis is most operational when applied to the specific practices built on the doctrine.

**Cold plunge.** Brief cold exposure drives [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]], mobilizes [[free-fatty-acids]] (overwhelmingly [[pufa]] in modern adipose, which then poison mitochondrial respiration), and produces the same thymic atrophy any chronic stressor produces. The brown-fat and norepinephrine signals the hormesis literature cites as adaptive are, in Peat's reading, the catabolic loop ([[cortisol]]) running.

**Intermittent fasting.** The full counter is in [[intermittent-fasting]]: fasting is a stress state; the autophagy is desperation cellular cleanup, not improved housekeeping; the sirtuin activation silences protective genes including [[p53]] and [[klotho]]; the caloric-restriction longevity effect operates by reducing metabolic rate, which Peat considered reduction of life itself. The hormesis case for fasting is one of the strongest exhibits *for* Peat's critique: every cited "benefit" is a metabolic stress signature.

**Sauna and heat shock.** See the HSP section above. Brief warming of cold hands and feet is a different intervention Peat did not oppose; the wellness stack (45–60 minutes at 180°F, daily) is a heat dose well into the range where the HSP-as-scar mechanism applies.

**Fasted training.** Combines fasting and exercise. From [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]]: "Strenuous exercise combined with fasting (e.g., running or working before eating breakfast) not only directly trigger the production of lactate and ammonia, they also are likely to increase the absorption of bacterial [[endotoxin]] from the intestine." The combined stress drives a stronger collapse of oxidative metabolism with measurable endotoxin translocation.

**Hormetic supplements.** The polyphenol class (resveratrol, curcumin, EGCG, sulforaphane, quercetin) is sold on the premise that plant defense toxins confer benefit through stress-response activation. Several are estrogenic; several directly inhibit mitochondrial respiration. The proposed benefit *is* the stress pathway — which Peat read as molecular evidence of damage, not improvement.

The common thread is methodological. The hormesis literature measures intermediate signals (HSP upregulation, AMPK, sirtuin activity, norepinephrine, autophagy markers) and treats their activation as evidence of benefit. Peat measured terminal outcomes (metabolic rate, body temperature, pulse, freedom from chronic illness, longevity on adequate diet) and asked whether the intervention produced more of those. The two methods disagree on cold plunges and fasting because they disagree on what counts as evidence.

## What replaces hormesis: the high-energy frame

The frame Peat counterposed was not "no stress, no challenge." The variable to optimize is not the dose of stressor but the energetic state of the cell. A high-thyroid, high-CO2, well-fed, well-rested cell can absorb a wide range of insults without leaving a scar. A low-thyroid, glycolytic, PUFA-loaded, sleep-deprived cell scars from minor insults. The same dose of cold, heat, or exercise that strengthens the first damages the second. The biphasic curve in the hormesis data is, by Peat's reading, a curve over baseline states, not a property of the stressor.

The operational implication: the protective interventions are not "controlled damage" but adequate substrate. [[Thyroid]], sugar, salt, fruit, milk, cooked greens, gelatin, daylight, warmth, sleep, [[carbon-dioxide]], [[progesterone]], [[pregnenolone]], [[aspirin]], niacinamide, coffee — the things that raise the energetic state so challenge can be met without falling into survival metabolism. The challenges themselves (skilled work, social demands met with pleasure, walking, the brief concentric muscular efforts in [[exercise]]) are not stressors in the hormesis sense; they are activities the well-fed cell performs and is built by. The difference between a challenge and a stressor is not a property of the activity. It is a property of the cell.

The hormesis frame treats benefit as a property of the dose. Peat treated benefit as a property of the state. Once that distinction is grasped, the wellness-culture stack built on hormesis (cold plunges, fasted training, sauna stacks, polyphenol stacks, contrast baths) is legible as a category error — and as the downstream cultural form of an argument that began as nuclear-industry public relations.

## Counter-arguments

The strongest forms of the case for hormesis have to be stated honestly. Peat's framework does not dismiss them all equally.

**Exercise adaptation is real, and operates through stress signaling.** This is the strongest counter. Exercise demonstrably increases mitochondrial biogenesis, AMPK activation, and lactate-tolerance pathways; the adaptations are durable. Peat did not deny the adaptation but reinterpreted the mechanism: brief, concentric, sub-aerobic muscle work (what he recommended) is performed within energetic budget and does not trigger the cortisol-and-PUFA cascade endurance training does. Endurance athletes have measurably higher cortisol, lower thyroid, and elevated rates of cardiac fibrosis. The argument is not "no exercise" but "exercise that is challenge, not stress." See [[exercise]].

**Preconditioning effects are robust in clinical medicine.** Ischemic preconditioning before cardiac surgery does reduce infarct size. Hormetic dose-response is a real pharmacological phenomenon. Peat would grant the local effect while pressing the question of cumulative cost — the cardioprotection is purchased with some loss of cellular function the clinical setting may not measure.

**The Calabrese et al. literature is large.** Edward Calabrese (the Boston-area professor Peat appears to have been referencing) has published hundreds of papers documenting biphasic dose-response curves across pharmacology and toxicology. Peat's argument is not that the curves are fabricated but that they have been over-interpreted — biphasic dose-response is a generic property of regulated biological systems and does not imply the low-dose effect is desirable.

**Mainstream consensus increasingly disputes the Linear No-Threshold model.** Peat's position aligns with Sternglass, Gofman, and Alice Stewart; that position is currently the minority view in radiation health physics, though it remains the working assumption in regulation. The PNAS 1997 single-alpha-particle paper and the Li 2001 nongenotoxic-stress paper are real and cited, but the field has not converged on Peat's interpretation.

**Caloric restriction does extend lifespan in lab animals.** This is the strongest hormesis-adjacent evidence and Peat granted it. His counter (in [[intermittent-fasting]]): the mechanism is reduced metabolic rate and reduced peroxidation of stored PUFA in mitochondrial membranes — achievable without caloric restriction by reducing PUFA intake. Selective amino-acid restriction (tryptophan, cysteine) extends lifespan more than total CR without the metabolic depression. The data does not require the hormesis interpretation.

**Autophagy, sirtuin, AMPK biology is not an industry fraud.** The mechanisms are reproducible cell biology. Peat's argument is that the *interpretation* — "always beneficial" — is the industry artifact, not the mechanisms themselves. Autophagy in a well-fed cell is constitutive housekeeping; autophagy under starvation is desperation. Same pathway, different meaning at different baselines.

## Data gaps

- **Post-2015 sauna and cold-exposure literature.** The Finnish sauna mortality studies, Norwegian endurance-cardiac data, and cold-exposure brown-fat literature are largely post-Peat. His framework predicts a U-curve with the inflection at much lower doses than wellness practice operates at, but he did not engage the specific dose-response data.

- **Mediterranean diet / polyphenol epidemiology.** Peat treated polyphenols skeptically but did not systematically engage Mediterranean-diet cardiovascular data. The Peat-framework counter — that cardioprotection comes from low-PUFA olive oil and adequate calories, not polyphenol "hormesis" — is plausible but undeveloped in the corpus.

- **Plant defense chemistry at culinary doses.** Whether small habitual exposures to plant defense compounds (curcumin, capsaicin, eugenol) confer net benefit through detoxification training is not directly addressed. Peat's preference for fruit over leaves/roots is consistent with the standard view but the culinary-dose question is open.

- **Pediatric and developmental contexts.** Peat noted that sub-threshold doses are more damaging in young organisms: "In a young individual, that deviation can produce drastic and fatal effects very quickly" ([[2009-04-27-politics-and-science-politics-science-ionizing-radiation-in-context-part-2|*Politics and Science*, April 27, 2009]]). But he did not develop a framework for kids' cold tubs, fasting protocols, or altitude training specifically.

- **Long-term outcomes of contemporary wellness practices.** The cold-plunge, intermittent-fasting, and sauna-stacking cohorts have not been followed for decades. Both sides are operating on short-term-marker extrapolation.

- **Mitohormesis (Ristow, Schulz).** The ROS-as-signal literature argues mitochondrial ROS under exercise/CR is the active mechanism, blocked by antioxidant supplementation. Peat would recategorize the ROS spike as damage and the "antioxidant blockade" as evidence of poorly chosen supplements (alpha-tocopherol megadose, NAC). The dispute is unresolved on either side's terms.

- **Contemporary funding of hormesis researchers.** Peat's $40M Boston-professor claim is testable in principle but rests on his reading. The T.D. Luckey / AEC historical claim has substantial documentary support (Sternglass, Gofman, Stewart literature; Project Sunshine documents); the contemporary funding claim is harder to verify directly.
