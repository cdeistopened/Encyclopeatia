# Genetic Determinism

For half a century the phrase "it's in your genes" has functioned as a sentence of fate. The dominant story in twentieth-century biology — refined in textbooks, ratified by Nobel Prizes, reproduced in pharmaceutical advertising, and absorbed into ordinary speech — holds that an individual's traits, diseases, intelligence, temperament, and life expectancy are spelled out in a sequence of nucleic acids established at conception. The environment can permit or suppress the expression of what is written there, but it cannot rewrite the text. The text itself was finalized when the egg was fertilized, and the egg, in turn, was a sealed copy of a germ line that descends in unbroken purity from the first ancestor. This is the doctrine Ray Peat called genetic determinism, and he treated it not as a scientific finding but as a four-generation ideological project that began with August Weismann in the 1880s, hardened into the "central dogma" of molecular biology in the 1950s, and continues to discipline medicine, education, and public policy in a way that no laboratory result has ever justified.

His inversion was as direct as it was unfashionable. Genes are not the executive layer of biology. They are inventories. They record what the cell has needed to make. They are read out at speeds and in combinations governed by the cell's hormonal, electrical, and energetic state. The cell is not a passive readout machine; it is, in Peat's framework, an intelligent system that calls up the genetic material it needs the way a librarian calls up books, modifies it, sometimes writes new entries, and reorganizes the whole library under stress. "Each cell is adapting and adjusting itself to the other parts. When you look at the fact that a skin cell contains all the genes needed to make a brain cell, the idea of cloning an organism from a single cell means that every cell is full of potential," Peat told Sarah Johanneson Murray in the [[2017-04-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-2|April 2017 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on endocrinology]]. "The way the organism comes into being is by each cell confronting its environment. When it divides, the new cell becomes part of its environment. The whole development of the organism is a process of perceiving its environment and adapting to what it perceives."

If the cell is intelligent and the environment is what shapes what gets transcribed, then the diseases the genetic story attributes to defective genes look different. Diabetes is not a thrifty-gene legacy; it is the metabolic state of a population that has switched diets. Cancer is not a sequence of driver mutations that hijack a previously normal cell; it is a respiratory defect that produces somatic mutations downstream, as the disorganized cytoplasm fails to maintain its protein-water phase (see [[oxidative-metabolism]] and [[gilbert-ling]]). Birth defects are not random transcription errors; they are imprints of the intrauterine environment. The intelligence problem the eugenicists thought they had identified — why poor people remained poor across generations — turned out to be a maternal-nutrition problem, transmissible by gestational environment for four generations in animal studies, fully reversible by adequate diet in humans. Each of these reframings depends on the same physiological mechanism: the cell adapts, the genome is one of the substrates the adaptation modifies, and what the next generation inherits is in part the metabolic state the previous generation was in.

This is not a small correction. It collapses an entire structure of medical practice that depends on locating disease in a fixed text rather than in a modifiable physiology. It explains why the genetic theory of cancer has produced sixty years of expensive sequencing and almost no useful therapy. It reconnects medicine to the environmental, nutritional, and metabolic interventions that the genetic frame had quietly demoted. And it puts the cell, not the gene, back at the center of biology — the move that connects Peat's work on [[learned-helplessness]], [[oxidative-metabolism]], hormonal imprinting, and cancer into a single framework. The current article traces that inversion across its components: Weismann's invention of the doctrine, the central dogma's serial collapse, the epigenetic vindication, the Lysenko revision, McClintock's mobile genetic elements, Dawkins's "selfish gene" misreading, and the somatic-mutation theory of cancer that the framework dissolves.

## The Central Dogma and Its Origin

The conceptual core of modern genetic determinism is what Francis Crick named the "central dogma" — information flows from DNA to RNA to protein, and never in the reverse direction. The deliberate use of the religious word was not accidental. Peat treated the doctrine's intellectual history as essential to understanding its persistence. "The Central Dogma of the molecular geneticists, in their own words, was that information flows only from DNA to RNA, and from RNA to protein, never in the other direction," he wrote in his 2012 newsletter [[genes-carbon-dioxide-and-adaptation|*Genes, Carbon Dioxide, and Adaptation*]]. "The Central Dogma was formulated to suppress forever the Lamarckian idea of the inheritance of acquired characters, that Weismann's amputation of the tails of a multitude of mice had attempted to deal with earlier in the history of genetics."

The hyphenated continuity matters. The central dogma did not appear in 1958 as a freestanding empirical claim. It was the molecular-biology restatement of August Weismann's late-nineteenth-century doctrine of an isolated, immortal germ line — a metaphysical apparatus designed to refute Lamarck and Darwin's joint conviction that organisms adapt and pass adaptations on. In the 2009 newsletter [[estrogen-memory-and-heredity-imprinting-and-the-stress-response|*Estrogen, Memory and Heredity*]], Peat narrated the lineage. "Around the end of the 19th century, August Weismann began a systematic attack on the ideas of Darwin. As part of his campaign, he invented the doctrine that the reproductive cells are absolutely isolated from the rest of the organism, and that they are immortal. The rest of the organism is built up by the deletion of genetic information. This doctrine was very convenient for those who maintained that all organisms had been created in a single moment, and that the appearance of evolution resulted from the extinction of some species, but not the new appearance of some species."

The theological undertone is explicit in Peat's reading. "The changes produced by subtraction were compatible with the notion of fallen man in a corrupt world, while the addition of heritable traits through experience would connote a sharing in the process of creation. The hereditary particles making up Weismann's 'immortal isolated germ line' connoted a single original creation." Weismann's experimental program — cutting the tails off more than fifteen hundred mice over five generations and observing that the offspring continued to be born with tails — proved nothing about Lamarckism (which never claimed amputation produced heritable change) but provided the rhetorical cover for an a-priori doctrine of complete germ-line isolation. The doctrine then formed the substrate for the eugenics movement, the IQ-as-fixed-trait literature, the Mendelian-inheritance-of-disease project, and ultimately the central dogma.

By the time Peat began his graduate training in the 1960s, the doctrine was institutional reality. "Just about the time I was starting to read biology, the government was starting to get involved in helping the dogmatists control biological teaching," he told Andrew Murray in the [[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-genetics-x-environment|May 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on genetics and environment]]. "Carl Lindegren wrote a book called *The Cold War in Biology* that explained how the change took place in the 1940s. People who didn't conform to the dogma weren't allowed to teach, even in high schools. It was a very totalitarian institution, with the government as a funding agency and the science journals being influenced by the pharmaceutical industry." The morphogenetic-field theory that had organized embryology in the first half of the century — the picture of development as a self-regulating physical-chemical field in which cells became what their position in the field required them to become — was driven out of the curriculum by 1960. The substrate this left was a passive readout machine reading off a fixed program. The cell as adaptive organ was replaced by the cell as gene-execution device.

What is striking is how quickly the empirical case against the central dogma accumulated. By 1970, Howard Temin and David Baltimore had identified reverse transcriptase, the enzyme that copies RNA back into DNA — the very direction the dogma had ruled out. Retroviruses, including HIV, became impossible to deny. Stanley Prusiner's prion work in the 1980s showed proteins themselves could carry heritable information, with no nucleic acid intermediary at all. John Cairns's directed-mutation experiments in *E. coli* demonstrated that bacteria can produce specifically the mutations they need to survive a particular stressor, in proportions vastly exceeding what random mutation rates predict. "The discovery of enzymes that produce DNA modeled on RNA, 'reverse transcriptases,' began undermining traditional genetics, because it showed that new information can enter our genome," Peat wrote in [[estrogen-memory-and-heredity-imprinting-and-the-stress-response|*Estrogen, Memory and Heredity*]]. "The discovery that bacteria can pass 'genes' from one individual to another, conferring antibiotic resistance upon previously sensitive strains, was a major nuisance to people working in infectious disease, since it complicates the treating of disease, but it indicated that 'evolution,' or genetic change, was capable of happening in non-random ways."

By 2012 the dogma's component claims had each been falsified. "The ability to make organisms produce foreign proteins confirms that information can flow from DNA to protein, but as that technology was being developed, the discovery of retroviruses showed that the Central Dogma of molecular genetics was wrong, RNA is a very significant template for the production of DNA. And the scrapie prion shows that proteins can be infectious, passing along information without nucleic acids as the agent of transmission. The directed mutations demonstrated by John Cairns and others have thoroughly destroyed the Central Dogma of molecular genetics, even as it applied to the simplest organisms, but molecular genetics survives as an industrial and forensic technology" ([[genes-carbon-dioxide-and-adaptation|*Genes, Carbon Dioxide, and Adaptation*]]). The dogma persists not because it has empirical support but because the industrial and ideological infrastructure built on top of it does not survive its removal.

## Genes as Records, Not Causes

The substantive Peat claim is not that genes do not exist or that DNA sequence is irrelevant. It is that the conventional causal arrow runs the wrong way. The cell's energetic and hormonal state determines what is transcribed; the proteins produced are downstream of the cellular environment; the genome that gets passed on is itself modified by the lifetime of cellular adaptation the organism has gone through. Genes record. They do not direct.

This reframing transforms the diagnostic significance of genetic findings. A gene "for" diabetes is, in Peat's reading, almost certainly a gene whose expression pattern reflects a metabolic state. Treat the metabolic state and the gene's effect attenuates. "Although evidence suggests that about 2% of human diseases involve the inheritance of an abnormal protein, the exact way the disease develops is never as clear as the geneticists would imply," he wrote in [[genes-carbon-dioxide-and-adaptation|*Genes, Carbon Dioxide, and Adaptation*]]. "And the major diseases, cancer, diabetes, heart disease, Alzheimer's, epilepsy, depression, etc., that are so often blamed on 'genes,' are so poorly understood that it is arbitrary and crazy to talk about the way genes 'cause' them."

The Israel immigration data is the cleanest natural experiment in the corpus. "People who had never had a problem with diabetes in their culture, very soon suffered from the same rate of diabetes as their neighbors when they immigrated into Israel and began eating the European style diet. The interesting thing about the genetic explanation for disease is how its proponents can believe what they are saying." In the May 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode, Peat developed the same data point in dialogue with a caller worried about his diabetic grandfathers. "30 or 40 years ago, doctors talked about the 'thrifty gene' that caused some families to get fat on very little food. That is undoubtedly another example of inheritance by gestational influence. If you are starving, the fetus adjusts to a poor diet and becomes thrifty. They've never identified a thrifty gene or a gene for diabetes." Two generations of European-style eating produced the European disease rate in a population that arrived diabetes-free. The "genetic predisposition" was a metabolic adaptation to scarcity that became a liability under abundance — and the adaptation was inherited not through DNA sequence but through the gestational programming of the next generation.

The mechanism by which this happens is now described under the heading of [[hormonal-imprinting|hormonal imprinting]], and Peat tracked the literature on it from the 1970s onward. The term covers the long-term shifts in tissue sensitivity to hormones that follow exposure during critical developmental windows. "'Hormonal imprinting' refers to the great changes in sensitivity to hormones (and related substances) that persist after exposure to that substance early in life," Peat wrote in [[estrogen-memory-and-heredity-imprinting-and-the-stress-response|*Estrogen, Memory and Heredity*]]. "When the mother's hormones are imbalanced during pregnancy or nursing, the baby is 'imprinted' with an altered sensitivity to hormones. Leonell Strong showed that these effects could be exaggerated generation after generation. But — strangely, considering that he was a student of T. H. Morgan, who is considered to be the founder of classical genetics — Strong found that a single treatment, or a series of treatments, with an extract of liver, or with certain nucleosides (the units for constructing DNA), could reverse the course of generations of breeding, and eliminate the susceptibility to cancer."

The closing observation in that passage is decisive. Susceptibility to cancer, accumulated across generations of selective breeding, could be erased in a single generation by feeding the mother liver or DNA precursors. This is not a tweak to the genetic story; it is its dissolution. Whatever "cancer susceptibility" turned out to be, it was not a fixed sequence in the germ line. It was a hormonal-metabolic imprint that nutritional intervention reversed.

## Four-Generation Gestational Inheritance

The most consequential finding for everyday medicine is the four-generation transmissibility of nutritional and toxic effects through the gestational environment. The natural experiments are striking. In the [[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-genetics-x-environment|May 2012 transcript]], Peat brought up the Dutch and Russian famine data. "Animal studies show this effect can be passed on for four generations. Studies of people starved during pregnancy in Holland and Russia showed that not only were their babies impaired mentally, but even their grandchildren showed impairment. It is inherited, but it is a physiological, environmental thing. If they had a super-supportive environment, that could be corrected and wouldn't be passed on."

This is not Lamarckism in the loose sense of "use it or lose it." It is something more specific: the fetal endocrine and metabolic environment programs the developing oocytes of the next generation, which carry that programming into their own pregnancies, which carry it into the next, and so on for four generations before the imprint washes out. The mechanism is the protein, RNA, and small-molecule loading of the oocyte. "The developing egg cell is constructed and modified in many ways during its growth. The nurse cells which surround it in the ovarian follicle inject massive quantities of material, especially RNA, into the expanding egg cell. Regulatory substances and energy production modify enzyme activities and structural proteins, which will influence the way it develops after fertilization. During the entire lifetime of the individual person, the developing egg cells are open to influences from the organism as a whole" ([[estrogen-memory-and-heredity-imprinting-and-the-stress-response|*Estrogen, Memory and Heredity*]]).

The oocyte, in this picture, is not an inert capsule of DNA waiting to be fertilized. It is a metabolically active cell that has been receiving regulatory inputs from the mother's tissues across her entire life. Her exposures to estrogen, [[pufa|PUFA]], stress hormones, and environmental toxins all leave traces in the cytoplasm of those eggs, which contribute to the developmental trajectory of any child she eventually bears. The cells of that child's gonads are then, in turn, exposed to her intrauterine environment, and their own oocytes (in a female fetus) or spermatogonia (in a male) carry that exposure forward. The four-generation horizon is the time it takes for accumulated cellular memory to dissipate under a stable new environment.

The clinical implication is direct. "Environmental pollution with estrogens and immunosuppressive substances, when it persists throughout the developmental period, and across generations, will be dangerous at levels much below those that show an immediate hormonal or immunosuppressive effect. Tests that determine the 'mutagenicity' or 'carcinogenicity' of a substance are performed within a context of a theoretical genetics which is demonstrably false; until the complexities of imprinting and transgenerational effects are taken into account, it would be wrong to accept the claim that there are 'safe levels,' or 'thresholds of harmful effects.'" Toxicology that tests for acute mutagenicity in a single generation will systematically underestimate the harm of chronic low-dose exposure to substances that imprint developing tissues across generations. The "safe levels" set by mainstream regulatory bodies are an artifact of the false framework.

The intrauterine environment is also the most under-acknowledged source of the variance that twin studies attribute to genes. In the [[2017-04-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-2|April 2017 transcript]], Peat returned to it: "The ratio of the person's brain to body, their longevity, life expectancy, and the amount of energy they have at any stage of life is pretty much set by the intrauterine condition." Twins, of course, share an intrauterine environment. Separated-at-birth twin studies have been the genetic-determinism camp's strongest empirical leg, but the shared nine months prior to separation account for a great deal of what those studies measure. "I pointed out the evidence of the intrauterine environment and nutrition. The evidence from animal studies is clear: twins have a very similar environment and get the same nutrition. If the mother's health changes from pregnancy to pregnancy, the intelligence of the baby is influenced by what she was eating" ([[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-genetics-x-environment|May 2012]]). The portion of twin similarity that the genetic story claims as evidence of shared DNA is, in Peat's reading, mostly evidence of shared gestation.

Franz Boas's century-old skull-measurement studies remain the clearest non-twin demonstration. "Margaret Mead's approach was that cultures don't have to be static; people make the culture, so they can change the culture. Her professor, Franz Boas at Columbia, thought of himself as a Darwinist, but when he actually studied the facts, he showed that the environment rather than the genes governed even the person's biology. He measured the heads of Europeans who had moved to New York or Puerto Rico and found that the first-generation offspring had heads shaped more like New Yorkers or Puerto Ricans than like the European parents. It showed the powerful importance of the material and social culture shaping the organism" ([[2015-03-04-politics-and-science-politics-science-evolution|*Politics & Science*, March 2015]]). The head shapes that the racial-typology literature treated as the most fixed genetic signature turned out to be plastic across a single generation of changed environment.

## Epigenetics as Vindication

What mainstream biology now calls "epigenetics" — the study of heritable changes in gene expression that do not involve changes in DNA sequence — is, in Peat's framework, the official rediscovery of the very mechanisms the central dogma was designed to deny. DNA methylation, histone modification, chromatin remodeling, non-coding RNA regulation, the small-RNA traffic through exosomes — all describe ways in which the cellular environment modifies the genome's readout and, in many cases, transmits that modification across generations. The framework Peat had been articulating since the 1970s, drawing on suppressed mid-century work, has become the consensus position in molecular biology under a new name, with the political framing carefully stripped out.

Peat himself was direct about what the epigenetic literature had vindicated. "A stressful environment would cause changes in the chromosomes... Layer after layer will be laid down in the stuff around your genes, attaching carbon atoms to the DNA itself and attaching a great variety of molecules to the proteins that handle the genes (the histones)... But if you put them into a super environment — like the enriched environment — you can repair the previous generation's damage very quickly. Some nutrients and drugs can do that: remove methyl groups from the DNA" (from the *Aging and Energy Reversal* KMUD interview cited in [[ch02-energy|Chapter 2 of *The Ray Peat Diet*]]). What the literature was now calling DNA methylation he had been describing as an environmental modulation of cellular memory for thirty years.

The exosome story is particularly striking because it amounts to the rediscovery of Darwin's own theory of inheritance — the one Weismann and Mendel had together driven from the field. In the [[2015-03-04-politics-and-science-politics-science-evolution|March 2015 *Politics & Science* episode]], Peat told the story. "Darwin proposed 'gemmules' or 'pangenesis' — the idea that something the organism acquired through experience was shed from the organs and reached the gonads to be taken up in the germline. That idea was too Lamarckian for anyone up until Lysenko's time. But in the last 10 or 20 years, it has been revisited. It can be traced back to a North Korean, Bonghan Kim, who in the 60s showed there were particles carrying nucleic acids through a lymphatic-like system. Only recently have people looked at particles in the plasma called exosomes or microvesicles. These are now known to carry RNA, proteins, and even DNA, and it has been demonstrated that the DNA carried in these particles can be incorporated into the germ cells and transmitted. Basically, Darwin's gemmules are now called exosomes."

Darwin's pangenesis was a specific empirical proposal: cells throughout the body shed small particles that travel to the gonads and contribute to the germ cells. Weismann's invention of an isolated germ line was designed precisely to refute this proposal. A century and a half later, with the molecular techniques to identify what Darwin could only postulate, the proposal turns out to be substantially correct. Exosomes are membrane-bound particles released by virtually every tissue, carrying RNA, DNA, and protein cargo, traveling through blood and lymph, and — as more recent work has shown — capable of delivering their cargo to germ cells. The mechanism Weismann insisted could not exist exists. The framework Peat had described as "intelligent cells" calling up the genetic material they needed turns out to be, in part, the work of an entire intercellular logistics system shipping nucleic acids and proteins between organs.

The 2017 newsletter pendant to this story is the *Generative Energy* podcast discussion of Albrecht-Buehler's "Cell Intelligence." "I think it's regulated by a very ultra-organized system — that antenna we were talking about. There is a website called 'Cell Intelligence' by Albrecht-Buehler. He gives his argument that the cell knows how to call up exactly the genes it wants by such things as the antigen it's exposed to. The cell has a reading system like a super librarian that knows where to find everything specifically. The normal use of the system that reads out retroviruses is likely the same system that produces exosomes. These are part of a repair and maintenance system. They form particles that are almost indistinguishable from the coronavirus under a microscope. For example, if you have injured lung tissue that needs repair, it composes exosomes by drawing out just the RNA, DNA, and proteins needed for repair. It puts them into the bloodstream, and they travel to the bone marrow" (*Generative Energy* #32). The picture is of cells running a sophisticated information economy, not of cells running a fixed program.

The clinical extension Peat raised in the same conversation was the foreign-RNA case — what happens when an introduced RNA enters this system. "This will be the first RNA vaccine. I think the people who are making it are probably totally unaware that our cells have lots of reverse transcriptases. We're just waiting for a bit of foreign RNA to come in to make copies and send it into our genome as permanent DNA. Then our great-great-grand-descendants will be able to make the most toxic spike protein part of the virus in uncontrolled quantities." Whether or not the prediction holds in detail, the framework that makes the prediction available is the same one that the central dogma was constructed to rule out. Once reverse transcriptase is a fact of cellular biology, introduced RNA can become heritable DNA, and the "isolation of the germ line" is not a feature of biology but a fiction of mid-century biology textbooks.

## Lysenko Reconsidered

The single most heretical move in Peat's framework is his sympathetic re-reading of Trofim Lysenko, the Soviet agronomist whose name has served Western biology as the cautionary parable about what happens when ideology overrides science. The conventional story has Lysenko, an unschooled crackpot favored by Stalin, suppressing Mendelian genetics in the USSR, sending real geneticists to the gulag, and setting Soviet biology back by decades. Peat did not deny that the cultural-revolutionary apparatus around Lysenko was brutal. He did, however, deny that the science was as one-sided as the Western telling claims.

"Lysenko never was the 'Czar of Genetics,'" he said in the [[2015-03-04-politics-and-science-politics-science-evolution|March 2015 *Politics & Science* episode]]. "Even at the height of his power under Stalin, he was always criticizing the university genetics people. He ran the state crop improvement stations. It was Lewontin, an American Marxist biologist, who examined the grain production records under Lysenko's influence and found they were actually increasing grain production steadily. The university genetics departments weren't producing anything of value." The reframe is sharp. The man cast in the Western literature as the scientific charlatan was, in this telling, running the field stations whose grain output rose during his tenure, while the academic Mendelians whose suppression supplied the field's moral story were producing little of practical value during the same period. Richard Lewontin, the Harvard population geneticist, is not a Lysenko apologist; the grain-production data is what it is.

What Peat valued in the Lysenko program was the cytoplasmic emphasis. "We've been given a false history. Lysenko emphasized the predominance of the cytoplasm. The adaptation of the organism involved changes in the organization of the cytoplasm." This is the same emphasis Peat himself drew from [[gilbert-ling|Gilbert Ling]] — the cell as a structured cytoplasm whose state determines what the nucleus does, rather than as a nucleus issuing orders to a passive cytoplasm. Lysenko's "vernalization" experiments — treating winter-wheat seeds with cold and moisture to make them behave as spring wheat — were attempts to demonstrate that environmental conditioning could shift the developmental program in ways that persisted into the next generation. Whether Lysenko's specific empirical claims held up under rigorous replication is one question. Whether the underlying picture — cytoplasm-driven adaptive development with intergenerational effects — has been vindicated by subsequent biology is another, and the answer to the second question is largely yes.

The political reading Peat offered is that the anti-Lysenko campaign in the West was not primarily about defending experimental rigor. It was about ratifying the Weismann-Mendel-neo-Darwinist consensus at a moment when the Cold War made any Soviet scientific position automatically suspect. "We've been given a false history" is not a defense of Stalin's gulag, in which actual geneticists like Nikolai Vavilov died. It is a refusal to accept that the propaganda value of the Lysenko story should retire the cytoplasmic-inheritance hypothesis from serious scientific consideration. The hypothesis, in fact, has not retired. It has come back under other names — hormonal imprinting, epigenetic inheritance, exosomal RNA transfer, mitochondrial inheritance. The historical irony is that the framework Lysenko was denounced for advancing is closer to the current biological consensus than the rigid Mendelism that denounced him.

Peat extended the political reading to the broader history of anti-Lamarckism. "The anti-Lamarckians were actually anti-evolutionists who didn't like the idea that organisms could have a purpose or respond reasonably to the environment in a way that could be passed on. Anti-Lamarckism was a creation of late 19th-century anti-evolutionists who became the basis for the neo-Darwinist movement, which suppressed the idea of a purposeful developmental field" ([[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-genetics-x-environment|May 2012]]). The standard story has Darwin defeating Lamarck and Weismann then defeating Lamarckian-Darwinian residue in favor of the modern synthesis. The actual textual record — including Darwin's own introduction to *The Descent of Man*, where he explicitly says natural selection is one of several mechanisms — looks different. "Charles Darwin was actually not an antagonist of Lamarck. Darwin's grandfather was a Lamarckian, and Darwin got his basic ideas from him. In the introduction to one of the editions of *The Descent of Man*, he made several points that he was not saying it's just a matter of random mutations. He pointed out several other mechanisms of inheritance." Weismann's anti-Lamarckism was anti-Darwin in substance, even as it claimed Darwinian descent.

## Barbara McClintock and Jumping Genes

The clearest experimental refutation of the "DNA as fixed text" picture came from Barbara McClintock's work on maize in the 1940s and 50s. McClintock identified what she called "controlling elements" — sequences of DNA that move from one chromosomal location to another, especially under stress, altering the expression of nearby genes as they go. The genome, in her demonstration, was not a stable program. It was a dynamic system that rearranged itself in response to environmental demands. She received the Nobel Prize for the discovery in 1983, more than thirty years after publishing it, during which time her work was largely ignored by the field that had committed itself to the central dogma.

Peat treated McClintock as a key figure in the dissolution of genetic determinism. "Evidence has been accumulating in universities among well-known researchers demonstrating that stress can produce directional changes in microorganisms. This uses mechanisms similar to what Barbara McClintock recognized in corn: mobile genetic elements that move around under stress and accelerate the ability to adapt" ([[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-genetics-x-environment|May 2012]]). The point is not merely that genes can move. It is that the movement is correlated with stress — the cell rearranges its own genome in response to environmental conditions, in directions that improve survival under those conditions. This is exactly the kind of directed, environment-responsive genetic change the central dogma was designed to deny.

The convergence with Lysenko's cytoplasmic emphasis was not lost on Peat. "Barbara McClintock was finding similar things in her work with corn — that stress increased the variability and adaptability of the organism, changing the chromosomes. She came so close to Lysenko and Lamarck in showing that stress changes heredity, and she was hostilely ignored by the profession for 40 years" ([[2015-03-04-politics-and-science-politics-science-evolution|March 2015]]). McClintock could not be safely dismissed as a Marxist ideologue — she was an American working at Cold Spring Harbor — but the substance of her work pointed in the same direction Lysenko's had, and the field's response was to ignore her for four decades. Only after the rediscovery of transposable elements in bacteria and yeast in the 1960s and 70s did the maize work become unavoidable.

The mobile-genetic-element story dovetails with Peat's broader picture of stress-induced genomic variability. "Barbara McClintock wrote about the effects of stress causing genetic rearrangement, and traced the movements of the mobile genetic elements. At the same time, without knowing about her work, Leonell Strong was working with mice, exploring the role of 'genetic instability' in causing cancer, and identifying estrogen and 'milk particle,' or 'milk factor,' a virus-like particle that interacted with estrogen, as causes of breast cancer" ([[estrogen-memory-and-heredity-imprinting-and-the-stress-response|*Estrogen, Memory and Heredity*]]). Strong's work on transmissible cancer susceptibility and McClintock's on transposable elements were converging on the same picture: the genome is a dynamic system that responds to hormonal and environmental signals, and the response can be heritable.

James Shapiro's "natural genetic engineering" framework is the contemporary extension. "James Shapiro was one of the people who discovered that bacteria can adapt to resist antibiotics, and they can pass on that acquired resistance to their descendants or to other bacteria they interact with. It can be transmitted both genetically and horizontally as an acquired trait. Shapiro calls this 'natural genetic engineering,' in which the organism is adjusting its own inheritance to improve its survival" ([[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-genetics-x-environment|May 2012]]). The picture is of cells running their own genetic modification programs — cutting, splicing, transposing — under the direction of cellular regulatory signals that respond to environmental demands. This is not Lamarckism with a new vocabulary. It is something more interesting: it is the recognition that the distinction between "inheritance" and "adaptation" that the modern synthesis drew so sharply was an artifact of mid-century ideology, not a fact of cellular biology.

## The "Selfish Gene" Rejection

The popular sociobiology of Richard Dawkins is, in Peat's reading, the late twentieth-century cultural ripening of Weismannian doctrine. The famous claim that organisms are "lumbering robots" built by genes to propagate themselves — the genes being the only "real" units of selection, the body a temporary vehicle — is a colorful restatement of the immortal-germ-line doctrine. Peat did not need to engage Dawkins's specific arguments at length; the doctrinal lineage made the disagreement structural.

His more direct target was the use of genetic determinism to underwrite political conclusions. "There's almost no difference between Chomsky's idealistic genetic idea of language and Konrad Lorenz's genetic explanation for all behavior, which Lorenz designed specifically for Hitler to justify racial extermination. Chomsky wouldn't like that comparison, but in fact, they're both committed to ignoring the actual evidence and believing that genes explain everything" ([[2010-11-10-politics-and-science-politics-science-a-self-ordering-world|*Politics and Science*, November 2010]]). The point is not that Chomsky is a Nazi. The point is that the genetic-determinist mode of argument has a structure that survives transplantation into any political register, because it locates the cause of human variation in a substance that is (allegedly) beyond political reach. Once a behavior is "in the genes," the policy implications follow automatically: change is impossible, intervention is futile, the existing distribution of social outcomes is natural rather than political.

The case of Konrad Lorenz is instructive because it makes the political genealogy uncomfortable for Peat's audience. "Konrad Lorenz, who won the Nobel Prize, was a hero of practically all the professors I knew even into the 70s. He was a Nazi who designed his idea that genes control behavior specifically for Hitler's Institute of Racial Hygiene. It was an excuse for killing people who didn't have the behavioral traits they thought were appropriate" ([[2012-05-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-genetics-x-environment|May 2012]]). Lorenz's ethological framework — the idea of fixed action patterns, innate releasing mechanisms, genetically determined behaviors — was developed under and for the Nazi state. After the war it was rehabilitated as scientifically neutral and absorbed into mainstream ethology. The same doctrinal content, with the political origins quietly forgotten, then provided the foundation for sociobiology.

The intellectual common denominator is the refusal of the cell as agent. "The whole point of genetics is to say the environment can't change the reality of the organism," Peat said in the same November 2010 episode. Once the cell is reduced to a gene-execution device, the body is reduced to a phenotype, the phenotype is reduced to a population, and the population is reduced to a gene pool whose distribution determines social outcomes. Reversing the picture — restoring the cell as adaptive agent, the body as energetically structured phase, the phenotype as the cumulative record of cellular adaptation — undermines the entire structure of selfish-gene reasoning. There is no "lumbering robot." There is an organism, and the genes are one of the substrates of its activity, not the program that runs it.

## Cancer: Somatic Mutation as Effect, Not Cause

The application of this entire framework that mattered most to Peat clinically was cancer. The dominant theory of carcinogenesis since the 1970s has been the somatic mutation theory: random mutations accumulate in a cell's DNA, eventually hitting a set of "driver genes" that confer the cancer phenotype, and the resulting mutant clone proliferates. The therapeutic implication is to find and block the products of the driver mutations. Two generations of cancer drugs have been developed on this model, with results that the field itself has had increasing difficulty defending — a small minority of cancers have responded durably, and the overall age-adjusted mortality from solid tumors has barely moved.

Peat treated this entire framework as a category error. The metabolic shift that defines a cancer cell — the Warburg-pattern reversion to glycolytic fermentation of glucose even in the presence of oxygen — precedes the genetic mutations rather than following them. The mutations are the downstream consequence of a destabilized cellular state, not the upstream cause. "Harry Rubin's studies of cells in culture have expanded Warburg's picture of the process of cancerization, showing that genetic changes occur only after the cells have been transformed into cancer," Peat wrote in an email exchange on cancer (cited in [[oxidative-metabolism|the oxidative-metabolism article]]). The cell adopts the metabolic shift; in the disorganized redox state and altered protein-water phase that follows, the genome accumulates damage at an elevated rate, and the conventional sequencing readout picks up the mutations and treats them as cause.

This reframing is supported by the empirical convergence of the McClintock, Strong, and Shapiro programs. Stress causes genomic rearrangement (McClintock). Hormonal imprinting causes genomic instability and cancer susceptibility (Strong). The cell's own regulatory systems can direct mutations (Shapiro). The picture that emerges is that the cancer genome is a record of the cell's prior physiological trauma — its exposure to estrogen, PUFA, ionizing radiation, viral infection, sustained inflammation — not a primary cause of the cancer phenotype. Treating the mutations is treating the smoke, not the fire. Treating the metabolic state — restoring respiration via [[thyroid|thyroid]], reducing PUFA, providing glucose, supporting [[carbon-dioxide|CO2]] production, lowering [[serotonin]] and stress hormones — is treating the cause.

The theological and ideological undertow of the somatic mutation theory deserves explicit naming. The theory locates cancer in a particle (the mutated gene) that pharmaceutical chemistry can target. The metabolic theory locates cancer in a state (the respiratory defect) that diet, environmental adjustment, and hormonal correction can reverse. The two theories are not just different empirical claims. They authorize different industries. Sixty years of capital investment have flowed into the first; the second has been carried on the margins by figures like Otto Warburg, Frederick Koch, Albert Szent-Györgyi, Joseph Gold, and Peat himself. The framework is treated in detail at [[oxidative-metabolism]] and [[warburg-effect]]; the cancer-specific protocol is at [[cancer]].

## Why "Diagnosis" Is Iatrogenic

A final clinical extension brings the genetic-determinism critique back to ordinary medical practice. The diagnostic gesture in mainstream medicine treats a patient's symptoms as expressions of an underlying entity — a disease — that the patient "has." The entity has a name (Hashimoto's thyroiditis, type 2 diabetes, fibromyalgia, ulcerative colitis), a code in the ICD, a typical course, a set of recommended pharmacological interventions. The patient's job, once diagnosed, is to manage the disease the diagnosis has revealed.

Peat treated this entire structure as iatrogenic — that is, harm caused by medicine itself. In the [[2019-10-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-brain-barriers|October 2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]] (also cited in [[learned-helplessness]]), Andrew Murray noted that learned helplessness in animal models is mediated by elevated serotonin. Peat extended the framework to medicine itself. "Yes, and it can be brought on by anything that overwhelms your self-regulation. I think a diagnosis often creates a learned helplessness situation, and the person lets their serotonin get out of control." A diagnosis, in this reading, communicates to the patient that the cause of their symptoms lies in a fixed entity (a disease, often coded as having a genetic basis) over which they have no causal control. The cellular consequence of this communication is the same as the cellular consequence of inescapable shock: elevated serotonin, suppressed thyroid, elevated cortisol, shift toward parasympathetic shutdown. The diagnosis produces the metabolic state that worsens the underlying physiology. "A diagnosis is almost certain to be wrong in some of its explanations, and it makes you think of yourself as a machine that needs fixing." This is the link from genetic determinism to [[learned-helplessness]]: both are forms of causal fatalism, both lock the cell into a stress state, and both make the body harder to repair than it actually is.

The corrective is not naive optimism. It is the restoration of agency at the cellular level — the recognition that the cell is intelligent, that the body is adaptive, that the genome is a record rather than a destiny, and that the metabolic state which the diagnostic vocabulary obscures is precisely the state that intervention can change. "If bacteria can do it, why can't a human brain cell do something at least partly as competent as that?" Peat asked in [[58-bioenergetic-nutrition-continued-authoritarianism-intention-and-learning-with-ray-peat-phd|*Generative Energy* #58]]. "I think the culture, partly to instill a helpless attitude in the public, has created this doctrine that our brain cells are among the stupidest of all parts of creation." The doctrine of cellular stupidity and the doctrine of genetic determinism are the same doctrine. Both deny the cell what every careful experiment shows it has — the capacity to perceive, adapt, modify its own substrate, and pass on the modification.

## Counter-arguments

**Modern genomics and the genome-wide association studies (GWAS).** The mainstream position takes its strongest empirical leg from the GWAS literature, which has identified thousands of single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with disease risk, height, intelligence, and other traits. Polygenic risk scores derived from GWAS data have demonstrated predictive validity for some outcomes. The Peatian counter is that GWAS-identified loci typically explain a small fraction of heritability (the "missing heritability" problem that has plagued the field), that the associations are statistical and population-level rather than causal at the individual level, and that the same loci can shift expression patterns dramatically under different cellular environments — so what GWAS detects is largely the genome's record of how the body has responded to a particular environment, not a prediction of how it will respond to a different one. The polygenic risk scores work best in populations whose environments resemble the discovery sample and degrade rapidly outside it. None of this falsifies the GWAS literature; it does relocate what the literature is actually measuring.

**Twin studies.** The classical behavioral-genetics finding that monozygotic twins reared apart are more similar to each other than to dizygotic twins reared together has been a foundation of the heritability literature for half a century. Peat's response — that twins share an intrauterine environment, that adoption rarely produces truly independent environments, that the heritability statistic is a population-level not individual-level measure, and that "heritable" does not mean "genetic" — does not refute the data but reframes its interpretation. The animal-study finding that gestational nutrition effects propagate across four generations creates a genuine empirical puzzle for the heritability framework: it generates apparent "heritable" variance that is not transmitted through DNA sequence. How much of measured human heritability is actually intergenerational gestational programming remains an open question that the standard literature does not address.

**Single-gene disorders.** The straightforward cases — sickle cell anemia, cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease, phenylketonuria, certain forms of dwarfism — are real, well-mapped, and not contested by Peat's framework. He explicitly conceded in the May 2012 transcript that "there are several conditions that are distinctly controlled by particular genes, such as the type of dwarfism in which the bones don't develop. There is a definite gene mutation involved." His point was that these account for a small fraction of the disease burden and that even in these cases the developmental expression is modifiable. PKU is a clean illustration — a single-gene disorder whose worst effects are entirely prevented by dietary management of phenylalanine. The gene is necessary for the disease in the sense that without the mutation it does not occur, but the gene is not sufficient — the food environment is the proximate determinant of outcome.

**The success of molecular biology as a technology.** Peat acknowledged this directly. "Molecular genetics survives as an industrial and forensic technology" — recombinant insulin, polymerase chain reaction, gene sequencing for forensic identification, CRISPR-based editing. These are real successes. None of them validates the central dogma's causal claim about disease. The biotechnology toolkit works because it manipulates the substrate the cell uses, not because the substrate causes the phenomena the dogma attributed to it.

**The "epigenetics" framing as already-incorporated mainstream.** A defender of the current consensus would argue that all of Peat's points about environmental modulation, hormonal imprinting, intergenerational inheritance, and mobile genetic elements have been quietly absorbed into the mainstream under the heading of epigenetics, and that the dogma critique is therefore beating a dead horse. The Peatian counter is that the absorption has been one-way and incomplete. The political and clinical implications — that intelligence is malleable, that "racial" differences in disease rates are largely environmental, that twin studies overstate the genetic contribution, that the somatic-mutation theory of cancer is misdirected, that pharmaceutical interventions targeting gene products are mostly the wrong therapeutic approach — have not propagated. The textbook epigenetics chapter sits next to the textbook chapter on the central dogma without disturbing the latter's causal architecture.

**The Lysenko historical record.** The mainstream historiography on Lysenko emphasizes the gulag deaths of geneticists like Nikolai Vavilov, the falsification of vernalization results, and the long-term damage to Soviet agricultural science. Peat's re-reading depends on Lewontin's grain-production analysis and on the cytoplasmic-inheritance hypothesis being substantively closer to current biology than rigid Mendelism. Whether the rehabilitation should extend to Lysenko personally — given the cultural-revolutionary apparatus around him — is a question the framework does not require an answer to. The narrower point — that the substantive scientific position attacked under Lysenko's name has substantially returned — does not require defending Stalin's biology policy.

**The strongest mainstream argument is institutional.** Genetic determinism is the operating framework of every major medical school, every pharmaceutical pipeline, every regulatory agency, and most of the science press. A graduate student who finds Peat's framework persuasive cannot easily build a career on it. The frame is not maintained by ongoing experimental confirmation; it is maintained by the institutional infrastructure of which it is a constitutive part. This is not a refutation, but it is the practical reality the framework operates against.

## Data gaps

**Quantification of the gestational-inheritance horizon.** The "four generations" figure for transmission of nutritional and toxic effects through the gestational environment is cited from animal studies and the Dutch and Russian famine epidemiology, but the underlying transmission mechanism is not fully worked out. How much of the inheritance is exosomal RNA, how much methylation of oocyte DNA, how much altered cytoplasmic loading, how much in utero programming of fetal endocrine axes — these are open questions. The horizon may be shorter or longer for different exposures.

**The relationship between epigenetic modifications and exosomal traffic.** Both have been documented; whether they operate as parts of a single coordinated information system or as parallel mechanisms is unclear. Peat described "cell intelligence" as if a single regulatory system orchestrates both, but the underlying coupling has not been worked out.

**The molecular details of cardinal-adsorbant-mediated transcription.** The Ling-Peat framework predicts that the cytoplasmic state, governed by cardinal adsorbants like CO2 and ATP, determines which genes get transcribed. Concrete molecular pathways linking cytoplasmic redox state and CO2 concentration to specific transcription-factor activity remain underdeveloped in the literature.

**Reconciling the somatic-mutation data with the metabolic-priority claim.** Modern tumor sequencing has documented extensive somatic mutation in cancer cells, including mutations that occur very early in tumorigenesis. The Peat-Rubin position — that the metabolic shift precedes the mutation — needs to be reconciled with the observation that some "driver" mutations appear before the full Warburg metabolic signature is established. Possible reconciliations exist (e.g., the metabolic shift is gradual and partial, with mutation accumulating as it deepens), but the quantitative timeline has not been worked out.

**The "intelligent cell" claim and the limits of its application.** Peat's framework attributes considerable computational sophistication to ordinary cells — selecting which genes to express, manufacturing exosomes to transport repair components, modifying their own DNA under stress. The boundary between this and mystification is not always clearly drawn. The Albrecht-Buehler "cell intelligence" framework Peat cited remains controversial within cell biology, and the question of whether "intelligence" is the right description for the regulatory sophistication observed in cells is open.

**The behavioral genetics of intelligence and personality.** Peat dismissed the genetic-determinist position in this area with considerable force, but the framework's positive account — what proportion of measured intelligence variance is gestational, what proportion is childhood environment, what proportion is current metabolic state, what role for actual genetic differences — remains qualitative. A serious developmental-bioenergetic account of cognitive variation has not been written.

**The CO2 / carbamino-group mechanism for gene-environment interaction.** Peat speculated in *Genes, Carbon Dioxide, and Adaptation* that CO2's reaction with protein amine groups (carbamino formation) and its competition with glycation (carbohydrate-protein reactions) might be the universal substrate of gene-environment interaction at the protein level. The hypothesis is intriguing but has not been systematically tested. Direct measurement of carbamino loading on specific transcription factors and chromatin proteins under different metabolic conditions is not part of any current research program.

**The toxicology framework for trans-generational exposure.** Peat's claim that "safe levels" set by single-generation acute-toxicity testing systematically underestimate harm from chronic low-dose multigenerational exposure is empirically defensible in principle but quantitatively undeveloped. What would a regulatory framework that accounted for four-generation gestational effects actually look like? No mainstream agency has attempted to construct one.

**The therapeutic reversibility of accumulated imprints.** Leonell Strong's finding that liver extract or nucleoside supplementation could erase generations of accumulated cancer susceptibility in a single treatment generation is striking, but the work has not been replicated in modern protocols. Whether contemporary nutritional, hormonal, or pharmacological interventions can produce comparable reversals in humans — and what the dosing, timing, and biomarkers should be — remains an open clinical question.
