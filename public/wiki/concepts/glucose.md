# Glucose

In Ray Peat's framework, glucose is not merely a fuel -- it is the preferred fuel, the molecule that cells are designed to oxidize completely into [[carbon-dioxide]] and water when conditions are right. The modern war on sugar has obscured a fundamental truth that Peat spent decades articulating: the problem is never glucose itself, but the failure to oxidize it. When cells cannot burn glucose to completion, they convert it into [[lactic-acid-production|lactic acid]] -- and that metabolic shift, not sugar consumption, is the signature of disease.

## The Preferred Fuel

The human body runs on glucose the way an engine runs on its designed fuel. Every cell possesses the machinery to take a glucose molecule and, through a series of oxidative steps, extract the maximum energy from it as [[atp-production|ATP]]. Each step produces [[carbon-dioxide]], which Peat considered one of the body's most protective substances. As he explained in a [[2015-03-11-politics-and-science-politics-science-biochemical-health|2015 Politics and Science interview]], "We are sandwiched between the sugar energy we get from plants and the carbon dioxide we make as the final product. Each oxidation of the sugar molecule adds chemical energy to the cell. When you are unable to oxidize the sugar all the way down to carbon dioxide and produce lactic acid instead, you lose the greatest part of the energy stored in the glucose molecule."

This distinction -- between complete oxidation and incomplete fermentation -- is the metabolic fault line that runs through Peat's entire body of work. Complete oxidation of one glucose molecule yields approximately 36 ATP. Fermentation to lactic acid yields only 2. The cell that cannot oxidize glucose is an energy-starved cell, regardless of how much sugar surrounds it.

## Glucose vs. Starch: Not the Same Thing

One of Peat's most practical dietary insights concerned the difference between glucose from fruit sugars and glucose from starch. Sucrose -- the sugar in fruit and cane sugar -- is half glucose and half fructose. This composition matters enormously for metabolic stability.

As Peat explained in a [[2022-11-18-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-november-2022-kmud|2022 KMUD episode]], starch delivers a "sudden surge of insulin" that "lowers your blood sugar, which can cause stress reactions." Sucrose, by contrast, "is only 50% glucose. It doesn't have the glycemic reaction that starch has; it is only half as able to stimulate insulin. The fructose component actually has a mild insulin-inhibiting effect. So, it provides sugar energy much more gradually than starch and tends to avoid the glucose-insulin-hypoglycemia stress reaction."

This is why Peat recommended fruit, orange juice, and even white sugar over whole grains and starchy vegetables as carbohydrate sources. The fructose in natural sugar moderates the insulin response, preventing the blood sugar crash that mobilizes stress hormones. Starch, despite its reputation as a "complex carbohydrate," actually produces a more destabilizing glycemic spike because it is pure glucose polymer.

In his email correspondence, Peat noted that "sugar helps the liver to make cholesterol, switching from starch" to sugar, and that "the fructose component of ordinary sugar helps to increase the metabolic rate." He recommended at least 180 to 250 grams of carbohydrate daily for a person of average size, preferring sources that combine glucose with fructose.

## The Brain's Non-Negotiable Requirement

The brain consumes roughly 20% of the body's glucose despite representing only 2% of body weight. This disproportionate demand reflects the brain's absolute dependence on oxidative metabolism for conscious function.

Peat described the consequences of glucose deprivation in stark terms. In a [[2008-10-31-other-hope-for-health-hope-for-health-thyroid|2008 Hope for Health interview]], he explained that when blood sugar falls, "anything becomes a serious allergen. With animal experiments, they found that with the weakest allergen, if they lowered the blood sugar with insulin, the animals would die in shock. But if they gave them extra sugar, even the allergic animals would get nothing but mild sniffles from a deadly, super-strong allergen."

Glucose deprivation forces the brain to rely on alternative fuels -- primarily fatty acids and amino acids -- that generate toxic byproducts. As Peat described in his newsletter on metabolic energy, "Glucose deprivation, by causing glutamine to be used as fuel, increases the formation of ammonia, and ammonia promotes the glycolytic use of glucose, producing lactic acid even in the presence of oxygen and perpetuating the scarcity of glucose."

This creates a vicious cycle: insufficient glucose forces emergency fuel use, which produces waste products that further impair glucose oxidation. The brain in this state becomes excitable, anxious, and vulnerable to degeneration -- a pattern Peat connected to [[alzheimers|Alzheimer's disease]], seizure disorders, and depression.

## Hypoglycemia: The Hidden Epidemic

Peat considered chronic low blood sugar one of the most underdiagnosed conditions in modern medicine. The standard medical approach -- measuring fasting glucose -- misses the functional hypoglycemia that occurs between meals and during sleep.

When blood sugar drops, the body mounts a stress response to generate glucose internally. [[Cortisol]] breaks down muscle and organ tissue to convert amino acids into glucose. [[Adrenaline]] mobilizes free fatty acids from fat stores. [[Serotonin]] rises as free fatty acids displace tryptophan from albumin. The person may not register low blood sugar consciously, but their body is experiencing a full stress cascade.

This is why Peat consistently recommended frequent meals containing sugar and protein. The goal is never to let blood sugar fall low enough to trigger the stress-hormone rescue response. As he explained in his newsletter on sugar and stress, "If stimulation is too intense or too widespread, and if too much fat is mobilized relative to glucose, self-defeating processes can occur."

The mobilized fat itself becomes part of the problem. [[PUFA|Polyunsaturated fats]] released during lipolysis suppress [[thyroid]] function, block glucose oxidation through the [[Randle cycle]], and generate inflammatory breakdown products. A person who skips breakfast does not simply miss a meal -- they initiate hours of tissue catabolism and PUFA mobilization that compounds the metabolic damage.

## Glucose and Cancer

The relationship between glucose and [[cancer]] is widely misunderstood, partly because of how [[Otto Warburg]]'s research has been interpreted. Warburg discovered that cancer cells ferment glucose to lactic acid even in the presence of oxygen -- the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]]. The popular conclusion was that sugar "feeds" cancer and should be restricted.

Peat's reading of Warburg was precisely the opposite. The problem is not that cancer cells use glucose; the problem is that they cannot oxidize it properly. As he stated in a [[2011-03-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-estrogen-vs-progesterone|2011 East West Healing interview]], "Warburg emphasized that sugar is being converted to lactic acid. Lactic acid turns on stress reactions. The 'sugar-free diet' people blame on Warburg doesn't fit what he said."

Restricting glucose from a cancer patient forces exactly the stress response that promotes tumor growth: cortisol rises, tissue breaks down, fatty acids flood the blood, and the immune system is suppressed. The cancer continues fermenting whatever glucose the stress hormones generate from tissue breakdown, while the rest of the body suffers from the deprivation.

Peat noted in his newsletter on cancer metabolism that "insulin resistance produces an inability to oxidize glucose, and is associated with chronic hyperglycemia, or 'type 2 diabetes.' Diabetes of that type is associated with a high risk of cancer." The problem was never excess glucose but the inability to use it properly -- a failure driven by [[PUFA]], [[estrogen]], and other factors that impair mitochondrial respiration.

## The Placenta's Lesson

Pregnancy provides a revealing window into biology's priorities around glucose. As Peat described in a [[2016-09-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-antioxidant-theory-and-the-continued-war-on-cancer|2016 KMUD episode]], "The placenta preferentially absorbs glucose and fructose. But if the mother has low blood glucose, she liberates free fatty acids into the bloodstream. When the mother has hypoglycemia, the placenta loses the ability to screen out the fats. The baby should make its own brain fats purely from glucose."

This reveals a fundamental principle: a developing brain builds itself from glucose, not from dietary fats. The baby converts sugar into the saturated fats and cholesterol its brain needs. When this process is disrupted by maternal hypoglycemia -- allowing [[PUFA]] to cross the placenta -- brain development is compromised. The placenta's selective filtering demonstrates that biology prefers glucose as the raw material for brain construction.

## Glucose and the Hippocampus

Beyond fuel, glucose participates directly in brain growth and repair. Peat cited research showing that the hormonal response to sugar stimulates neurogenesis. In a [[2015-01-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-digestion-and-emotion|2015 episode on digestion and emotion]], he described how "the Gastric Inhibitory Polypeptide responding to glucose has receptors that stimulate the birth of new cells" in the hippocampus -- "the part of the brain that's expanded by opportunity and learning and shrunken by stress." Eating sugar literally supports the growth of the brain region responsible for memory and learning.

## Practical Implications

Peat's dietary recommendations follow directly from his understanding of glucose metabolism:

- **Eat frequently** to prevent blood sugar drops that trigger stress hormones
- **Prefer fruit sugar and sucrose** over starch for more stable blood sugar
- **Combine sugar with protein** (as in milk) for sustained energy
- **Drink [[orange-juice]]** between meals as a reliable glucose-fructose source
- **Avoid fasting** and prolonged exercise that depletes glycogen and mobilizes PUFA
- **Support [[thyroid]] function** to maintain the oxidative machinery that uses glucose efficiently

The goal is to keep the cell supplied with glucose and capable of oxidizing it completely to carbon dioxide. When this metabolic state is maintained, stress hormones remain low, the brain functions optimally, and the conditions that promote [[cancer]], [[diabetes]], and [[aging]] are held in check.

## Glucose vs other fuels

The case for glucose as the "preferred fuel" is not a sentimental one. It is a thermodynamic and chemical claim about what comes out the other end of the burn. Among the substrates a cell can run on — glucose, fatty acids, ketone bodies, amino acids — glucose is the only one that produces nothing but [[carbon-dioxide]] and water when fully oxidized through the [[mitochondria]]. The other fuels each leave a residue.

Fat oxidation produces less CO2 per molecule of oxygen consumed than sugar oxidation does. In the [[2014-02-21-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-diabetes-i|*Ask the Herb Doctor* Diabetes I episode]] and the [SUGAR-RANDLE-ENCYCLOPEDIA synthesis](../../projects/the-ray-peat-diet/research/encyclopedias/ch04-sugar.md), the difference is concrete: "sugar oxidizing to carbon dioxide produces about 50% more carbon dioxide per quantity of oxygen consumed compared to fat." That extra CO2 is not waste in Peat's model — it is the molecule that protects against the calcification of soft tissue, that keeps calcium soluble in the blood, that inhibits lactic-acid formation through the Bohr effect, and that activates the Krebs cycle. Glucose oxidation is therefore *cleaner upstream* and *more protective downstream* than fat oxidation, even when both are run to completion.

Fatty acid oxidation also requires more steps to enter the energy pathway and depends on a high availability of carnitine and cofactors that decline with age. More importantly, when fat is mobilized from storage tissue under stress, what comes out is a mixture — and in modern bodies stored fat is increasingly polyunsaturated. [[PUFA]] released during lipolysis don't just compete with glucose at the [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase]] enzyme; they actively inhibit it, generate aldehydic breakdown products like 4-hydroxynonenal, and form glycation end-products on tissue proteins that are usually misattributed to sugar. Peat noted in the [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context* article]] that "spontaneously oxidized polyunsaturated fats stick to the proteins," doing what HbA1c is blamed for. A cell forced to burn fat — especially the modern fat — is producing free radicals while it produces ATP.

Ketones occupy a more ambiguous position. They are a fallback fuel the liver produces from fatty acid β-oxidation when glucose is unavailable, and the brain can adapt to use them. The popular framing is that this adaptive flexibility is itself a virtue — the "metabolic flexibility" argument behind ketogenic diets. Peat read the same data differently: ketosis is not a graceful adaptation to a more efficient fuel, it is the body throwing emergency switches because its preferred fuel is missing. The signal accompanying ketone production is high cortisol, high free fatty acids, high glucagon, suppressed T3, and elevated lactic acid in muscle tissue under load. Whether the small protective effects of mild ketosis (the [[hormesis|hormesis]] case) outweigh the chronic stress signature is, in Peat's framework, almost always a question with an obvious answer: feed the system its preferred fuel and the stress switches turn off.

The deeper point is that the choice of fuel is not symmetric. Glucose oxidation produces CO2, which is itself a regulator that biases the system toward more glucose oxidation. Fat oxidation produces conditions — high free fatty acids, low CO2, lactic-acid accumulation — that bias the system toward more fat oxidation and more stress. The [[Randle cycle]] is the local mechanism; the systemic loop runs through cortisol, thyroid, and the deiodinases discussed below.

## Gluconeogenesis as a stress signal

Glucose can come from three places: the food just eaten, the liver's stored glycogen, or the protein and fat of the body itself. The third path — gluconeogenesis — is the one mainstream culture mostly ignores and Peat treated as the central explanation for why fasting and ketogenic diets feel "clean" for a while and then produce muscle wasting, [[thyroid]] suppression, anxiety, and insomnia.

A healthy liver, in Peat's framework, stores enough glycogen to carry a person eight to twelve hours between meals — across the night, if [[thyroid]] function is good and estrogen is not interfering. As [Peat said](../../projects/the-ray-peat-diet/research/encyclopedias/ch04-sugar.md) in the *Ask the Herb Doctor* Sugar I episode (KMUD, 2011): "If your thyroid and other hormones are in the right concentration, your liver should be able to store at least eight hours of glycogen. Everyone should be able to get through the night on the amount of glycogen in their liver without resorting to other sources of energy."

When the glycogen is gone — and it can be gone in three to four hours under stress, in an hour during hard exercise, and chronically depleted in hypothyroid people who can't store it at all — the body has to manufacture glucose from somewhere. Adrenaline is the first responder, mobilizing what little glycogen remains and pulling free fatty acids from fat cells. When adrenaline is not enough, cortisol takes over. Cortisol's primary job in this state is gluconeogenic: it breaks down skeletal muscle, [[thymus]] tissue, and connective protein into amino acids, then shuttles those amino acids to the liver to be converted into glucose.

This is the missing piece in most discussions of fasting and ketogenic dieting. The reason a long fast or a sustained low-carb diet "works" — produces weight loss, lowers fasting insulin, reduces inflammation in the short run — is that it forces the body into a sustained gluconeogenic state. The body is, quite literally, eating itself to keep its blood sugar within the narrow band the brain requires. Peat tracked this back to nineteenth-century observations of terminal diabetics described in the [SUGAR-RANDLE-ENCYCLOPEDIA](../../projects/the-ray-peat-diet/research/encyclopedias/ch04-sugar.md): "I have articles on my website about two doctors in France and England in late 19th century who recognized that terminal diabetes patients were dying of starvation because their bodies were cannibalizing their own protein... And what they did was let them eat all the sugar they wanted... approximately 11 or 12 ounces, or about 350 grams a day... and instead of dying over a period of several days, the conversion of protein to sugar decreased and finally stopped and they recovered."

The same mechanism operates on the modern keto-dieter in slower motion. The [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context* article]] describes the sequence with anatomic precision: "If the diet doesn't contain enough protein to maintain the essential organs, especially the heart, lungs, and brain, they are supplied with protein from the skeletal muscles. Because of the amino acid composition of the muscle proteins, their destruction stimulates the formation of additional cortisol, to accelerate the movement of amino acids from the less important tissues to the essential ones."

Three downstream consequences follow. First, the chronic cortisol elevation produces what looks like Cushing's syndrome in milder form — thin skin, easy bruising, capillary fragility, poor wound healing, abdominal weight gain even on a caloric deficit. The [SUGAR-RANDLE-ENCYCLOPEDIA](../../projects/the-ray-peat-diet/research/encyclopedias/ch04-sugar.md) quotes Greenblatt and Schwartz: "Cushing's syndrome is characterized by protein wasting secondary to hypergluconeogenesis, which produces thin skin, poor muscle tone, osteoporosis and capillary fragility."

Second, the breakdown of muscle protein releases tryptophan, which the brain converts to [[serotonin]], which in turn intensifies cortisol secretion in a feedback loop that suppresses [[thyroid]] function. The dieter feels both wired (high adrenaline) and depressed (high serotonin) at the same time.

Third — and this is the link to the next section — the gluconeogenic state actively impairs the conversion of inactive T4 to active T3, which is the metabolic event that would have allowed the body to burn the available fuel efficiently in the first place. Gluconeogenesis is a survival mechanism, not a lifestyle. Peat treated chronic activation of it as one of the most underrecognized causes of degeneration in middle age.

## Glucose and T3 conversion

The orthodox picture of thyroid function stops at the gland: the thyroid produces hormone, the blood carries it, the cells receive it. Peat's framework, drawing on the iodothyronine deiodinase literature of the 1970s and 80s, treated the liver as the actual organ of thyroid function. The thyroid gland makes mostly T4, the inactive precursor. The active hormone, T3, is produced primarily in the liver by the deiodinase enzymes (mainly DIO1 and DIO2) that strip an iodine atom from T4. This conversion is the bottleneck — and it requires glucose.

In the [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context* article]], Peat states the dependency directly: "Triiodothyronine (T3) is mostly produced in the liver, depending on the availability of glucose to the liver, allowing a sensitive adjustment of metabolism to nutrition." Translated: when the liver has glycogen, it produces T3. When the liver is depleted, T3 production falls toward zero — even if the thyroid gland is functioning normally and T4 levels in the blood are adequate.

The mechanism is partly direct and partly mediated by NADPH availability and selenium-dependent enzymes. The [[thyroid]] emails (raypeatemails.com) state the practical requirements: "The liver has to convert T4 to T3 for it to be effective. It needs glucose and selenium to make the conversion. Adequate protein, at least 80 grams per day, is necessary." Selenium is required because the deiodinase enzymes contain a selenocysteine residue. Glucose is required because the conversion is reductively driven and consumes NADPH that the liver generates primarily from carbohydrate metabolism.

The downstream syndrome — sometimes called "non-thyroidal illness," "low-T3 syndrome," or "euthyroid sick syndrome" in mainstream literature — is a near-universal feature of chronic dieting, prolonged fasting, severe illness, and surgery. Lab work shows normal TSH, normal or low-normal T4, and conspicuously low free T3, with elevated reverse T3 (rT3), the inactive metabolite. The [[thyroid]] emails describe the mechanism: "Yes, it's probably induced by stress, with cortisol inducing the type of deiodinase that makes the inactive rT3. A low sugar diet can cause chronically high cortisol."

This is the loop that makes prolonged low-carb diets self-perpetuating. Glucose restriction → cortisol elevation → cortisol shunts T4 into rT3 instead of T3 → low T3 lowers metabolic rate → low metabolic rate makes the dieter feel cold, fatigued, and unable to lose more weight without further restriction → further restriction deepens the cortisol elevation. The system gets locked. Peat's clinical answer was unambiguous: the way out is glucose, not more restriction. In the [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context* article]] he writes that "sugar and thyroid hormone (T3, triiodothyronine) correct many parts of the problem. The conversion of T4 into the active T3 requires glucose, and in diabetes, cells are deprived of glucose. Logically, all diabetics would be functionally hypothyroid."

The same logic explains a clinical paradox: hypothyroid patients on T4-only therapy often report no improvement until they either add T3 directly or stabilize their blood sugar through frequent feeding with sugar-containing meals. The drug provides the precursor; the diet enables the conversion.

## Brain glucose dependence

The brain is roughly 2% of body weight and consumes roughly 20% of the body's resting glucose — a tenfold concentration of metabolic activity above the average tissue. Under conditions of stress, illness, or cognitive load, the proportion rises higher. The [SUGAR-RANDLE-ENCYCLOPEDIA](../../projects/the-ray-peat-diet/research/encyclopedias/ch04-sugar.md) records Peat's framing: "Our body — our brain in particular, and some tissues such as the red blood cells — strongly prefer sugars over fats and function better. When we get enough sugar, we inhibit the release of fats from storage and allow these tissues to have all the sugar they need."

The "preference" is more than a metabolic quirk. The brain is essentially incapable of running on fatty acids directly. The blood-brain barrier excludes long-chain free fatty acids; the brain can derive partial energy from ketone bodies during prolonged glucose deprivation, but ketones are themselves a sign that the body is breaking down its own tissue to produce them. Galactose — what Peat in the [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context* article]] notes is "also called cerebrose, brain sugar" — is the brain's own preferred sugar at certain developmental stages, and it is derived from glucose in dairy. The baseline rule is: when blood glucose falls, the brain is the organ that registers it first and pays the cost first.

The cognitive cost of hypoglycemia is registered as anxiety, irritability, difficulty concentrating, light and sound sensitivity, and in extreme cases shock and seizure. Peat connected the chronic forms — what dieters call "brain fog" and what neurologists call "cognitive impairment of metabolic origin" — to the brain's reliance on amino acids and ammonia when glucose is short. The [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] mobilized by adrenaline displace [[tryptophan]] from albumin, raising brain [[serotonin]] and intensifying the felt-experience of fatigue and depression. The brain in this state is not just under-fueled; it is being chemically pushed into a withdrawn, inflammation-tolerant, "sickness behavior" mode.

The three-a.m. wake is the most familiar form. The liver's glycogen runs out somewhere in the middle of the night — earlier in hypothyroid people, earlier in dieters, earlier under chronic stress. As blood sugar drops, adrenaline and cortisol surge to maintain it. The surge wakes the brain. The [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|*TSH, Temperature, Pulse Rate* article]] describes the pattern: "During the night, at the time adrenaline and free fatty acids are at their highest, TSH usually reaches its peak. TSH itself can produce lipolysis, raising the level of circulating free fatty acids." The person lies awake at 3 or 4 a.m., heart pounding faintly, mind racing, sometimes hungry, often not — because adrenaline suppresses appetite. The orthodox interpretation is "stress" or "anxiety." Peat's interpretation is mechanical: the liver ran out of fuel hours ago, the body's emergency response woke the brain, and the brain is now trying to compose a narrative for why it is awake.

This is why a small carbohydrate-and-protein snack at bedtime — a glass of milk with sugar, a piece of cheese with juice, a small bowl of ice cream — reliably ends the pattern for people who keep it up. The bedtime feeding replenishes liver glycogen enough to last through the night without triggering the rescue response. Glucose, in this model, is what "wakes you up" only in the sense that having enough of it is what *prevents* the body from waking you up.

## Daily dosing strategy

Peat's dietary recommendations cluster around a single operational principle: never let blood sugar fall far enough to trigger the cortisol-and-adrenaline rescue response. The implementation looks unremarkable from the outside — eat regularly, include some sugar with each meal, drink orange juice between meals — but the rationale is precise.

**Morning anchor.** The waking state is the hardest. The liver has been releasing stored glucose all night; for most people the glycogen is exhausted or close to it by sunrise, and cortisol is at its diurnal peak whether or not the person woke up. The first feeding of the day has to break the cortisol curve. Peat's standard recommendation — orange juice, often with salt, often with milk, sometimes with eggs and a small amount of fruit — is engineered to do exactly that: rapid available fructose-glucose to refill liver glycogen, calcium and salt to suppress parathyroid and adrenaline, protein in sufficient amount to support the day's [[T3]] production but not so much that it triggers an insulin-cortisol cascade. Coffee with sugar (and milk) extends the protocol: caffeine mobilizes free fatty acids, but the sugar suppresses the cortisol that caffeine would otherwise trigger, leaving the metabolic activation without the stress signature.

**Three- to four-hour intervals.** Between meals, the liver depletes glycogen at a rate that depends on metabolic state. In a hypothyroid person under stress, glycogen can be gone in two hours; in a well-thyroid person at rest, six to eight hours is possible. Peat's general recommendation — frequent small meals, three to four hours apart, never more than five — splits the difference and provides a margin against the worst case. The principle is not constant grazing; it is *interval feeding*, with each meal large enough to refill liver glycogen and small enough not to provoke an insulin-driven crash an hour later. Orange juice between meals is the standard between-meal feeding because it is glucose-and-fructose in roughly equal proportions, refilling glycogen without the insulin spike of pure starch.

**Carbohydrate paired with protein.** Protein alone causes a drop in blood sugar because amino acids stimulate insulin secretion. The [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|*Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context* article]] makes the point explicitly: "Insulin release is also stimulated by amino acids such as leucine... Since insulin lowers blood sugar as it disposes of amino acids, eating a large amount of protein without carbohydrate can cause a sharp decrease in blood sugar. This leads to the release of adrenalin and cortisol." Peat's practical workaround was to precede a protein meal with fruit ("I think it's good to precede a big protein meal by maybe 15 or 20 minutes with fruits so that you get your liver stocked with some glycogen") or to combine the protein with sugar in the meal itself — milk being the archetypal Peat food precisely because lactose and casein arrive together.

**Bedtime feeding.** The night feeding closes the loop. Peat's recommendation — typically a glass of warm milk with sugar dissolved in it, or ice cream, or a small piece of cheese with juice — is engineered to top up liver glycogen at the latest possible moment before sleep so that the curve from bedtime to breakfast doesn't drop far enough to trigger the rescue cascade. The [SUGAR-RANDLE-ENCYCLOPEDIA](../../projects/the-ray-peat-diet/research/encyclopedias/ch04-sugar.md) cites his summary: "Having a large part of the day's carbohydrate intake late in the day, or even during the night, can help to restore the brain's glycogen with less need for cortisol, and helps to reduce the nocturnal rise of free fatty acids, and their excitatory inflammatory effects." For chronic insomniacs the nighttime sugar is often the single most effective intervention available.

The four-meal day Peat described — breakfast, midday, late afternoon, bedtime — is not a folk pattern. It is calibrated to liver glycogen kinetics under realistic thyroid function. People with worse thyroid function need more frequent feedings; people with better thyroid function can stretch the intervals. The principle is the same in either direction.

## The post-glucose sugar substrates

"Glucose" in Peat's framework is sometimes used loosely to mean any blood sugar, but the practical distinctions among glucose, fructose, sucrose, lactose, and the various honey and fruit sugars are central to the dietary recommendations. The full physiology lives at [[blood-sugar]] and [[fructose]]; the short version here is that *pure glucose* — what comes from starch digestion, dextrose tablets, or maltodextrin — is the most disruptive carbohydrate to blood sugar stability because it stimulates the strongest insulin release with no fructose to buffer it. Sucrose is half glucose and half [[fructose]], and the fructose half has insulin-inhibiting and metabolic-rate-accelerating effects that turn what would be a glycemic spike into a smoother and more sustained energy delivery.

This is why Peat's recommended sugar sources — orange juice, fruit, honey, white sugar, even (carefully) maple syrup — are all glucose-plus-fructose combinations or near-pure fructose, not pure glucose. Honey is the limit case: about half fructose, half glucose, with trace minerals and antimicrobial compounds. The [SUGAR-RANDLE-ENCYCLOPEDIA](../../projects/the-ray-peat-diet/research/encyclopedias/ch04-sugar.md) cites Peat's own framing: "Honey is generally better than white sugar because it has some minerals, though not as much as fruits. It's probably the best refined sugar — bees refined it rather than a factory."

Galactose, the brain sugar, comes from lactose digestion and is one of the reasons milk holds a particular place in Peat's framework that white sugar cannot occupy. Fruit and sucrose feed the liver and muscles; milk feeds the brain in a more specific sense.

For the full metabolic logic — Selye's 300 mg% rule, the cortisol cascade, the liver glycogen buffer, the niacinamide/aspirin anti-stress duo, the dawn cortisol peak — see [[blood-sugar]]. The point of this page is to anchor the *substrate*: what glucose is, what it competes with, what depends on it. The mechanism page lives downstream.
