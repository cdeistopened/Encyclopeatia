# Retine

Retine is the name [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Albert Szent-Györgyi]] gave, in the late 1950s, to a hypothesized class of small electron-withdrawing molecules — most likely dicarbonyls — that he believed held differentiated cells in their non-dividing state. The name comes from the Latin *retinere*, "to hold back": retine was the brake. Its proposed counterpart, [[promine]], was the accelerator — electron-donor molecules that pushed cells toward division and dedifferentiation. In Szent-Györgyi's framework, the ordinary functioning of a multicellular organism required the retine signal to dominate in tissues that should remain differentiated, while promine took the lead briefly during regeneration, wound healing, embryonic growth, and the controlled cell turnover of bone marrow and gut. Cancer, in this reading, was not a genetic accident at all but the chronic failure of the retine/promine balance: either retine depletion, promine excess, or both. The framework predicted that the molecules involved would be small, ubiquitous, present at concentrations far below those of ordinary metabolites, and so closely woven into normal oxidative metabolism that the mainstream cancer literature would have a hard time even seeing them as candidates. Peat read the proposal as essentially correct, and the retine/promine framing — more than the specific molecules — shaped his entire approach to cancer, aging, and the question of why a cell divides at all.

The framework inverts the mainstream picture in exactly the way Peat's framework usually inverts mainstream pictures. Cell division, on the modern textbook account, is the active process: a cell divides because oncogenes turn on, growth factors signal, the cell-cycle machinery is engaged, and quiescence is the special restrained state that requires explicit braking. Szent-Györgyi proposed the opposite — division is the cell's primordial default, the state of the single-celled ancestor, and *not dividing* is the achievement of complex multicellular life. The differentiated, working, organ-specific cell is the unicellular default *restrained* by a molecular brake. Cancer is not the cell *acquiring* a new ability to divide. It is the cell *losing* the brake. "Warburg and Szent-Gyorgyi, in thinking about cancer, emphasized that growth is the primordial function of all cells, and that the differentiated functions of complex organisms involve restraints of that primitive function, imposed by a system that has developed through time," Peat wrote in [[adaptive-substance-creative-regeneration|*Adaptive Substance, Creative Regeneration, Mainstream Science, Repression and Creativity*]]. Retine was the proposed molecular embodiment of that restraint. The whole framework collapses if one looks for what makes a cell start dividing; it organizes immediately when one asks what holds a cell still.

## Szent-Györgyi's experimental basis: the chalone tradition and the electron-acceptor signature

The retine hypothesis sits at the intersection of two earlier experimental traditions that Szent-Györgyi spent his Woods Hole decades synthesizing. The first was the chalone tradition. From the 1930s through the 1960s, several groups — most prominently the embryologist John C. Bullough's lab in London and the regeneration physiologists working on liver — had shown that homogenates of healthy differentiated tissue contained substances that, when added back to regenerating or proliferating tissue, suppressed growth. Cut off half a rat's liver and the remaining half regrows to the right size and stops; surgically join two rats together in shared circulation and one of them regrows enough liver to handle both bloodstreams. Something in the blood of the intact animal was telling the regenerating liver when to stop. The intact tissue carried the inhibitory signal. The signals were given the generic name "chalones," from the Greek for "loosening" or "to relax" — molecules that loosened the proliferative drive. They were tissue-specific, dialyzable, heat-sensitive, and never definitively isolated in chemically pure form.

Andrew Ivy's "loss of a restraining process" framing for cancer (relayed in the [[2001-01-02-politics-and-science-politics-science-suppression-of-cancer-tru-eatments|2001 *Politics and Science* episode on suppressed cancer treatments]]) sat in this tradition. So did Dr. Stevan Durovic's Krebiozen, which Durovic claimed to have extracted from horses that had been inoculated with the tumor-producing organism *Actinomyces bovis*. The premise of Krebiozen was that the inoculated horse's body produced a chalone-like restraint against the induced tumor, and the restraint could be extracted, concentrated, and given to human cancer patients. The political destruction of Andrew Ivy that followed his Krebiozen testing is the load-bearing illustration in Peat's reading of how mid-century American medicine treated the chalone tradition: as a heresy to be crushed rather than a hypothesis to be tested. "And he was intelligent and had been thinking that cancer might be the loss of a restraining process," Peat told John Barkhausen in 2001 ([[2001-01-02-politics-and-science-politics-science-suppression-of-cancer-tru-eatments|*Politics and Science*]]). "This line of thinking has been demonstrated over and over... These chemicals of restraint were called 'chalones.'"

The second experimental tradition was Szent-Györgyi's own work on the electron-donor / electron-acceptor regulation of muscle contraction. In a series of experiments at Woods Hole in the 1950s, he showed that adding electron-donor and electron-acceptor small molecules to living muscle could put the muscle into either the contracted or the relaxed state, depending on the redox match between donor and acceptor. "One of Szent-Györgyi's experiments involved adding electron donor chemicals and electron acceptor chemicals to a living muscle. If the donor and acceptor were related to each other in terms of their oxidation potential, the muscle would contract. If they were not tuned to each other, the muscle wouldn't react. This verified what Koch was saying: donating an electron to a muscle system causes it to go into the active state, and the high-energy oxidant causes it to stabilize and go into a resting state," Peat said in the [[2001-01-02-politics-and-science-politics-science-suppression-of-cancer-tru-eatments|2001 *Politics and Science* interview]]. The active state — contraction in a muscle cell, division in a generic cell — corresponded to an excess of electrons in the protein lattice. The stable resting state — relaxation, or differentiation — corresponded to having those excess electrons pulled out by a high-energy oxidant. Szent-Györgyi generalized this finding from muscle to cells in general. "For Warburg, Koch, and Szent-Györgyi, cancer, allergy, muscle spasms, and seizures were all examples of the activated state in which there are excess electrons. Oxidation was the basic answer to restoring the cell to its stable resting state. This puts the cancer problem in a different evolutionary light. They saw cell division as the basic state of any cell. At a point in evolution, oxidative respiration came in and made it possible for cells to stop dividing long enough to form a multicellular organism. The reversion to cancer became simply interference with the ability to respire on the cellular level."

Retine, then, was the synthesis. The chalone tradition gave Szent-Györgyi the empirical observation that healthy tissue makes molecules that suppress division. The electron-donor-acceptor work gave him the mechanism: the molecule's job was to withdraw electrons from the cell's protein-water lattice, holding it in the oxidized, differentiated state the way the right electron-acceptor held a muscle in the relaxed state. The carbonyl group — C=O, two oxygens bonded by a double bond to a carbon, with the oxygens pulling electron density off the carbon — was the structural signature of a strong electron-withdrawing molecule, and dicarbonyls (two such groups in one small molecule) would withdraw electrons twice as forcefully. The retine candidate would be a small dicarbonyl, present at low concentration in healthy differentiated tissue, depleted in cancer, depleted in aged tissue, and acting catalytically rather than stoichiometrically — the same catalytic vision Szent-Györgyi had brought to the Krebs cycle dicarboxylic acids twenty years earlier (see [[albert-szent-gyorgyi]] for the full Szeged-to-Woods-Hole arc). "The carbonyl group was the essence of Koch's treatment. Whether the carbonyl was in his polymer or part of the benzoquinone molecule, Koch explained it as drawing electrons to itself, away from the electron donors of the ammonia compounds," Peat said in the same 2001 interview, locating the lineage explicitly. The retine framework is the chalone idea, mechanized by carbonyl chemistry, embedded in the electron-flow picture of life.

## The candidate-molecule problem: methylglyoxal and the dicarbonyls

Szent-Györgyi never definitively identified the molecule. The strongest candidate, and the one Peat treated as load-bearing, was [[methylglyoxal]] — pyruvaldehyde, CH₃–CO–CHO, a small α-dicarbonyl produced as a side-stream of glycolysis from the triose-phosphate intermediates. "In the 1950s and 1960s, Szent-Gyorgyi began talking openly about life processes in terms of a very precise balance between oxidation and reduction which kept systems of electrons in cells in a special free-radical-like state, oscillating between 'donor' and 'acceptor' molecules. He believed that a deficiency of the naturally occurring oxidative dicarbonyl molecule methylglyoxal might be a cause of cancer," Peat wrote in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism* (July 2016)]]. The structural fit was clean: two C=O groups separated by a single carbon, with strong combined electron-withdrawing pull, low intracellular concentration, produced continuously by ordinary metabolism, and detoxified by an enzyme system (glyoxalase I and II using glutathione) so conserved that it suggested the cell had been making and regulating the molecule for a very long time.

Methylglyoxal was not the only candidate Szent-Györgyi considered. Glyoxal itself (CHO–CHO, the simpler dicarbonyl) had been one of W.F. Koch's therapeutic reagents in his 1916-onward oxidation-restoration cancer treatments. Benzoquinone — Koch's other major reagent — was an aromatic dicarbonyl whose use Koch had explained in terms of imitating an essential cellular quinone, and which Szent-Györgyi's 1950s work effectively confirmed: ubiquinone, the cell's actual essential oxidizing quinone, was identified in mitochondria in 1950. Other α-ketoaldehydes and dicarbonyls in the family — kethoxal, kethoxol — were investigated by Szent-Györgyi and his Woods Hole collaborators with varying degrees of success. None resolved into the single molecule that the chalone literature wanted.

The reason no single retine molecule could be isolated is, in Peat's reading, a feature rather than a bug of the framework. The retine effect is not the action of one molecule on one receptor. It is the redox state of the whole protein-water lattice of the cytoplasm, maintained by a constellation of small electron-withdrawing molecules in equilibrium with their reduced counterparts. Methylglyoxal is one such molecule. Dehydroascorbate (oxidized vitamin C, a tricarbonyl) is another — and Peat noted that "the oxidized form greatly predominates in healthy cells, but in cancer cells the reduced form is increased. This corresponds to the generally less oxidized state of cancer cells" ([[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]]). ATP itself, in Gilbert Ling's reading, acts as an electron-withdrawer when bound to cellular proteins; ATP depletion releases the electron pressure and shifts the lattice toward the reduced, contracted, division-competent state. Carbon dioxide acts as a Lewis acid on lysine ε-amines, pulling electrons in a way that overlaps the dicarbonyl effect. Several molecules, redundantly, do the retine job. The chalone literature went looking for a single molecule and the wrong question yielded the wrong answer.

The Pal et al. (2016) confirmation that Peat highlighted is therefore important not because it identifies methylglyoxal as *the* retine but because it demonstrates the framework's core empirical prediction: a concentration window exists in which a candidate retine molecule is selectively toxic to undifferentiated dividing cells while tolerated by differentiated tissue. "There is still interest in his theory, for example 'The anti-cancer effect of methylglyoxal is now well established in the literature' (Pal, et al., 2016), but that molecule can also be toxic, depending on the condition of the cell. When the cell is healthy, methylglyoxal will be just one of many factors being created in the right place at the right time" ([[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]]). The "right place at the right time" is the framework: retine is a *state* the cell maintains, not a *therapy* the doctor delivers. The molecular details — and the toxicity of methylglyoxal at the high concentrations that diabetic [[lipid-peroxidation|lipoxidation]] generates (see [[methylglyoxal]] for the Janus dose-response) — are exactly what the framework predicts must be context-dependent.

## How Peat operationalized the retine framework clinically

The framework gave Peat a way of reading every clinical intervention he recommended in his cancer writing through one lens. The interventions are not addressed to the tumor at all — they are addressed to the organism-wide redox state, with the aim of restoring the conditions under which the cell's own retine-equivalents can function. The list is the standard Peat cancer protocol, but the retine framework explains *why* it is the standard protocol.

[[Thyroid|Thyroid hormone]] drives oxidative metabolism, increases CO₂ production, and shifts the cytoplasmic redox state in the oxidized direction — the direction of retine dominance. "When CO2 is increased, the redox balance of the cell is shifted in the direction of oxidation," Peat wrote in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]]. Thyroid is, in this reading, the most upstream lever for the retine state.

[[Carbon-dioxide|Carbon dioxide]] is the cardinal protective Lewis acid of the framework. By occupying amino groups as carbamate it both shields proteins from methylglyoxal-driven [[lipid-peroxidation|glycation damage]] *and* maintains the electron-withdrawing pressure on the lattice that retine-class molecules would impose if present at their healthy concentrations. The bag-breathing intervention that runs through Peat's cancer writing is, on this reading, the retine framework executed at the level of breathing physiology.

[[Cyproheptadine]], the serotonin and histamine antagonist Peat used heavily, opposes the lactate-flooding and inflammation-promoting cascades that drive cells from oxidative metabolism (retine-permissive) toward glycolytic metabolism (retine-depleted, promine-favorable). The drug does not act on retine directly. It removes the upstream excitatory pressure that drives the cell into the reduced state.

[[Vitamin K]] is one of Peat's quieter favorites in the cancer context. Its quinone structure — a 1,4-naphthoquinone for K1 and a related menaquinone for K2 — is a direct electron-acceptor of exactly the type Szent-Györgyi's framework predicted should function as a retine-class molecule. Peat returned often to vitamin K's anti-cancer effects in his transcripts. The retine framework supplies the mechanism: vitamin K *is* one of the dicarbonyl-equivalent electron acceptors that, present in healthy cells, holds the protein lattice in the differentiated state.

[[Aspirin]] and salicylates, in the retine reading, function as pro-oxidants in the cytoplasm — "increasing mitochondrial respiration and reducing inflammation," as Peat put it in [[100-years-of-cancer-metabolism|*100 Years of Cancer Metabolism*]], and shifting the cell toward the oxidized retine-permissive state. The mainstream framing of aspirin as a hydroxyl-radical scavenger that "neutralizes free radicals" is, on Peat's reading, exactly the inversion that the antioxidant-promoting industry has performed on Szent-Györgyi's framework: the cell's healthy state is the oxidized state, not the over-reduced "antioxidant-rich" state that current popular nutrition advice promotes.

[[Progesterone]] supports oxidative metabolism, opposes estrogen's electron-donating, proliferation-favoring effects, and stabilizes mitochondria — all moves that favor the retine state. Peat treated progesterone as having an inherent anti-cancer effect across most tissues, and the retine framework makes the prediction explicit: progesterone shifts the redox balance toward the oxidized lattice in which the body's own retine-class molecules can function.

The pattern is consistent: the Peat cancer protocol is a list of interventions that, by different mechanisms, support the oxidized cytoplasmic state in which the body's own dicarbonyl-class electron acceptors can hold cells in the differentiated, non-dividing state. The protocol is not a tumor-killing protocol. It is a retine-restoring protocol. The tumor — under the right conditions, with the field repaired — is allowed to regress, differentiate, or be silently cleared by the normal phagocytic surveillance that Peat (following Hardin Jones and others) believed handled most malignancies invisibly across a person's life.

## The retine/promine pair as a unified model

Promine is the electron-donor counterpart, and the full retine/promine framework is more than the sum of its parts. Szent-Györgyi proposed that growth in a healthy organism is governed by the *ratio* of retine to promine, not by either molecule's absolute concentration. A wound, a regenerating liver, an embryo, a healing bone — these need a transient shift toward promine to allow the brief proliferative burst that closes the wound or rebuilds the tissue. The shift is reversed when the regeneration is complete, and the retine signal reasserts dominance. Cancer is what happens when the shift fails to reverse. The promine signal is locked on; the retine signal is depleted, blocked, or both. The cell does what cells without a brake do.

This pair-framework gives the model its predictive power. Aging is the *progressive* failure of the retine/promine balance — a gradual drift toward the promine-dominant state in which more and more tissues sit closer to the proliferative threshold. The age-related rise in cancer incidence is not the slow accumulation of mutations until one cell goes rogue; it is the slow loss of retine state until the organism's tissues are all sitting closer to the dividing-cell default. Wound healing in the elderly degrades because the proliferative shift toward promine is harder to achieve and harder to terminate. Cellular senescence — the modern term for cells stuck in a non-dividing, inflammatory, half-functional state — is, on the retine reading, the cell's failed attempt at re-establishing the retine signal in a tissue whose surrounding redox state will no longer support the differentiated state. The framework reads modern senescence biology as a vocabulary problem more than a discovery: cells go senescent because the cytoplasmic redox state can no longer hold them in differentiated function, and the cell does the best it can with what's left.

The same pair-framework reframes regeneration. Peat returned often to the question of why human tissues regenerate so poorly compared to amphibian or invertebrate tissues. The retine framework supplies a candidate answer: amphibian tissues retain the capacity to swing transiently toward the promine-dominant state, do the regenerative work, and swing back. Mammalian tissues — chronically inflamed, chronically PUFA-loaded, chronically estrogen-saturated — have lost the capacity to make the swing cleanly. The regenerative response that an amputated newt limb performs is, in this reading, a retine/promine oscillation that mammalian tissue can no longer execute. Peat developed this thread in [[adaptive-substance-creative-regeneration|*Adaptive Substance, Creative Regeneration*]] and in his comments on Carrel's tissue-culture work: the same cells, in a richer redox environment, can do things they cannot do in the patient.

## Counter-arguments

- **The retine/promine framework has not been confirmed at the level of a specific identified molecule.** Szent-Györgyi's specific candidate molecules — methylglyoxal as retine, growth factors and electron donors as promine — have not been adopted into mainstream cancer biology. The retine/promine vocabulary itself is absent from current oncology textbooks. The framework competes with the dominant somatic-mutation theory, the cancer-stem-cell theory, and the immune-surveillance-failure theory, and has not displaced any of them.

- **Selective methylglyoxal anti-cancer activity is at small-experimental-model scale.** The Pal et al. (2016) work in *Amino Acids* — and the broader Ray-lab program in Calcutta — has shown methylglyoxal at carefully calibrated doses can selectively kill tumor cells in mice while sparing healthy tissue, but no clinical translation to human cancer therapy has occurred. The dose-response window, route of administration, and tumor-type specificity remain unsettled. The framework predicts that the window must be context-dependent on the cell's redox state, but no clinical biomarker for that state has been developed.

- **The chalone tradition was substantially abandoned in the 1970s for reasons that may not all be political.** Tissue-specific growth inhibitors of the kind the chalone literature proposed were never isolated in chemically pure form despite three decades of well-funded effort. Some chalone candidates turned out to be impurities or artifacts of the extraction procedure. Modern cell biology accounts for tissue homeostasis through contact inhibition, growth-factor signaling, and TGF-β-family cytokines rather than circulating small-molecule chalones. The retine framework rescues the chalone idea by relocating its action from a specific tissue-extracted molecule to a generic intracellular redox state, but in doing so it concedes that the original chalone program failed on its own terms.

- **The "cell division as primordial default" framing is contestable.** While it is true that single-celled ancestors divided continuously, modern cell biology has shown that most differentiated cells in a multicellular organism actively maintain quiescence through specific molecular programs (the Rb-pathway, p53, cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors), not through a generic redox brake. The retine framework can argue that these molecular programs are themselves downstream consequences of the redox state, but the causal direction is not established. Modern oncology treats the molecular programs as primary; the retine framework treats them as secondary.

- **The same molecule (methylglyoxal) is the principal driver of diabetic glycation damage.** Mainstream diabetology has spent thirty years documenting methylglyoxal's role in the AGE formation that erodes diabetic kidneys, eyes, and nerves. The retine framework's claim that the same molecule is anti-cancer at homeostatic concentration and damaging at pathological concentration is internally consistent but is special pleading against the diabetic literature unless the concentration window is mapped clinically — which it has not been. The mainstream diabetic view of methylglyoxal is glycating poison; the retine view is essential regulator. The two views are not yet experimentally reconciled. See [[methylglyoxal]] for the full Janus dose-response argument.

- **Pediatric and developmental cancers are awkward for the framework.** A retinoblastoma in a two-year-old child arises in tissue with no plausible accumulated metabolic insult and no decades-long retine depletion. The framework's natural home is adult solid tumors developing on a long background of PUFA exposure, estrogen excess, stress, and oxidative-metabolism decay. Childhood leukemias and embryonal tumors fit the framework less cleanly. Szent-Györgyi did not develop a pediatric-cancer extension.

- **The framework offers a description, not a falsifiable mechanism.** Critics — including sympathetic ones — have noted that "the cell's protein-water lattice is in the wrong redox state" is hard to test directly. The framework is most compelling as an organizing narrative that brings together Warburg's respiratory defect, the chalone tradition, the electron-flow picture of life, and the dicarbonyl candidate molecules. As an organizing narrative it is rich; as a falsifiable theory at the level of contemporary molecular biology it has not been operationalized.

- **The methylguanidine result is contested.** Szent-Györgyi reported in the 1950s that methylguanidine (an electron-donor amino compound) caused muscle contraction by donating electrons to a system that should have its electrons withdrawn by the oxidative catalyst. The replication record is thin, and the experiment's interpretation depends on assumptions about the redox state of the muscle preparation that competing labs disputed.

## Data gaps

- **No clinical concentration window for methylglyoxal or any other candidate retine molecule has been mapped in humans.** The framework predicts the window is real and context-dependent on the tissue's redox state. Without the map, "homeostatic vs. pathological" remains a verbal distinction.

- **The other candidate retine molecules Szent-Györgyi considered — glyoxal, kethoxal, benzoquinone, ubiquinone, dehydroascorbate, vitamin K quinones, possibly some carotenoids — have not been compared head-to-head as candidates.** The framework is consistent with a constellation rather than a single molecule, but the constellation has not been characterized.

- **The relationship between retine signaling and the modern molecular-biology vocabulary of cell-cycle control (Rb, p53, p21, cyclins, CDKs) has not been worked out.** The framework could in principle be tested by showing that manipulating cytoplasmic redox state shifts the activity of the cell-cycle apparatus in predictable ways. The cross-translation has not happened.

- **The promine side of the pair is even less developed than retine.** Szent-Györgyi proposed promine extracts could be obtained from rapidly dividing tissues, but the molecular identity of the promine class is even less specified than retine's. The framework predicts that estrogen, [[serotonin]], lactate, ammonia, and the electron-donor amino compounds Koch was trying to suppress all act as promine-equivalents. None of these has been integrated into a clinical promine assay.

- **Pediatric, developmental, and germline cancers have not been brought into the framework.** The retine reading is most persuasive for adult tumors on backgrounds of accumulated stress.

- **The historical primary literature on the retine experiments is partly in Hungarian, partly in mid-century English journals, and substantially uncited in the modern cancer-metabolism revival.** Investigators reviving the Warburg framework (the 2010s cancer-metabolism wave that produced the Sam Apple *NYT Magazine* piece Peat referenced) have generally not picked up the Szent-Györgyi thread. Why is partly historical and partly because the framework is harder to operationalize as a drug-discovery program than the Warburg framework is.

- **The framework has not been integrated with the chalone literature it descends from.** Bullough's chalone work, Iversen's epidermal chalone studies, Houck and Hennings's bone-marrow chalone studies — these are largely uncited in modern oncology and uncited in Peat's writing. The historical connection is documented in Peat's 2001 *Politics and Science* interview but the molecular bridge to retine has not been written.

- **The cellular senescence literature has not been read through the retine framework.** Modern senescence biology has accumulated a large body of work since the 2000s. Whether the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), the p16 senescence marker, and the transient senescence of wound healing fit the retine/promine pair-framework cleanly is unworked.
