# Aging

The reigning gerontology of the late twentieth century treated aging as a problem of information. Telomeres shorten with each cell division; somatic mutations accumulate; senescent cells secrete inflammatory signals; eventually a programmed clock — whether it ticks in chromosome ends, in epigenetic methylation marks, or in pituitary "death hormones" — runs down. Ray Peat spent fifty years arguing that the picture has the polarity reversed. Aging is not a clock running down. It is a fire going out. The information-level changes mainstream gerontology catalogues — shorter telomeres, more methylation drift, more senescent cells, more inflammaging — are downstream measurements of a cell that has lost its capacity to oxidize glucose all the way to carbon dioxide and water, and the lost capacity has a definite cause: the cumulative damage to the respiratory enzymes themselves, inflicted by a stereotyped sequence of stresses that compounds across decades. "Aging is largely the substitution of the wrong fat into the wrong enzyme over a lifetime of dietary exposure," is the one-sentence form of the claim, and the rest of this article is the working-out of how that substitution is set up, accelerated, measured, and reversed. The conceptual frame, the substrate biochemistry, and the protective-hormone framework are at [[oxidative-metabolism]], [[mitochondria]], and [[stress]] respectively. This page is the synthesis at the top level: what aging *is* in Peat's framework, the cascade by which it unfolds, the levers that slow or reverse it, the markers that say it is happening, and where the framework still owes the literature a quantitative model.

## What aging is in this framework

Peat's central inversion is to take the standard list of "aging biomarkers" and reread them as signs of declining oxidative capacity rather than as the disease itself. Pulse falls. Body temperature falls. Total CO2 in the blood falls. Sleep fragments and shifts earlier. The respiratory quotient drifts toward fat oxidation. Tissue concentration of polyunsaturated fats rises. Mitochondrial cardiolipin loses its palmitate and gains linoleic acid. Cytochrome oxidase loses copper. Iron accumulates in liver, brain, and heart. Estrogen rises in tissue (even when serum estrogen falls, because progesterone has fallen further and stops sequestering it). Cortisol's diurnal trough rises. Serotonin's tonic level rises. Adrenaline's baseline rises. Free fatty acids circulate at a higher resting concentration. Lactate accumulates at lower workloads. Lipofuscin granules become visible in long-lived cells. Collagen cross-links and stiffens. Capillary permeability rises. Each of these changes can be measured. Each is real. Each is, in Peat's framework, downstream of one upstream variable: the rate at which the cell can move electrons from glucose to oxygen and produce CO2 at the far end.

The frame he inherited from Hans Selye was that "a great variety of harmful causes all lead to a few forms of biological harm" ([[stress]]). Aging is the most general expression of that convergence. Cold, surgery, infection, malnutrition, dietary PUFA, ionizing radiation, excess estrogen, social subordination, sleep deprivation, prolonged darkness, and ordinary chronological time all produce, at the cellular level, the same stereotyped response: impaired respiration, lactate accumulation, calcium dysregulation, lipid peroxidation, protein cross-linking, and the slow substitution of degraded enzymes for working ones. "When I first suggested that 'aging is estrogenic,' or that estrogen promotes aging changes, it was because of a large number of biochemical similarities in aging and in the state of estrogen dominance, and the absence of any detectable biochemical differences between the states, except their history," Peat wrote in [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|*Solving Some of the Problems of Aging* (1991)]]. The biochemical state of an aged cell is the biochemical state of a chronically stressed cell that has had decades to accumulate the residue of every previous episode. Strip out the time variable, and you cannot tell them apart at the level of the chemistry.

The implication is that "aging" is not a separate disease category and not a separate research program. It is the same physiology as chronic disease, only longer. Cancer is the terminal Warburg state (see [[oxidative-metabolism]]). Heart disease is the Warburg state expressed in heart muscle. Dementia is the Warburg state expressed in neurons. Diabetes is the Warburg state expressed at the level of substrate selection. Aging is the Warburg state expressed in every tissue simultaneously and gradually. The mainstream framing — that there is some unique gerontological mechanism, distinct from the mechanisms of the chronic diseases of old age, which produces aging *and then* aging produces the diseases — is, in Peat's view, an artifact of medical specialization rather than a finding of biology. "The changes of aging are also the result of complex interactions between organisms and their environment, rather than some genetic program that operates like a clock running down," he wrote in [[aging-estrogen-and-progesterone|*Aging, Estrogen, and Progesterone* (2006)]]. There is no aging program. There is a metabolism in slow collapse.

Peat's inversion vs. mainstream gerontology can be stated explicitly. Telomere shortening: real, but downstream — cells whose redox state has slipped toward the Warburg metabolism divide more often (because differentiation has failed) and therefore wear their telomeres faster. The shortening is the record of the redox failure, not the cause of it. Senescent cells: real, but downstream — a cell whose mitochondria can no longer support differentiated function exits the cycle and starts secreting inflammatory factors, but the upstream event was the energy failure, not the senescence program. Inflammaging: real, but downstream — chronic low-grade inflammation is what cells with damaged respiration do; it is the body's way of trying to clear the lipid peroxidation products and the leaked enzymes that Warburg-state tissues constantly produce ([[leakiness-aging-and-cancer|*Leakiness, Aging, and Cancer* (2006)]]). Genetic-determinist gerontology (the SIR2, FOXO, IGF-1, mTOR program-genes): real signals but, in Peat's reading, signals that respond to the cell's redox state rather than dictate it. The lifespan-extending effects of methionine restriction, tryptophan restriction, and caloric restriction in laboratory animals are real effects, but they work, in his account, by reducing the substrate flow into the serotonin pathway, the homocysteine pathway, and the free-fatty-acid mobilization that drive the stress metabolism — not by activating a longevity gene program. The full case against the genetic-determinist reading is at [[genetic-determinism]]; this article assumes that frame and builds the metabolic alternative on top of it.

## The aging cascade

Peat treated the aging process as a stereotyped sequence in which each step suppresses the energy-producing capacity that would otherwise have prevented the next step. The sequence is not a list of independent risk factors. It is a feedback chain, and the chain is the unique conceptual signal of this page — the individual steps are documented in detail elsewhere in the vault, and what follows is the integration.

**Step 1. The stress events.** Some are unavoidable (cold, infection, injury, ordinary metabolic activity, the daily fall of cortisol's protection through the long dark hours). Some are imposed (PUFA-rich diets adopted in the second half of the twentieth century, fortified iron in flour, hormonal contraceptives, antibiotics that derange the gut flora and increase endotoxin absorption, chronic psychosocial stress, light deprivation in modern indoor life). The signature is the same regardless of source: adrenaline rises, free fatty acids mobilize, glucose oxidation falls through the [[randle-cycle|Randle effect]], lactate rises, cortisol follows to break down protein for gluconeogenesis. The full physiology of the cascade is at [[stress]].

**Step 2. Impaired thyroid response.** Each stress episode pulls active thyroid hormone (T3) out of circulation — the rT3 shunt rises, deiodinases are inhibited, the liver's ability to convert T4 to T3 falls. With aging, the gut also begins to harbor a flora that produces anti-thyroid substances and endotoxin, which further suppresses respiration ([[bowel-toxins-accelerate-aging-process|*Bowel Toxins Accelerate Aging Process* (1991)]]). Hypothyroidism is therefore not a primary endocrine disease but the chronic readout of accumulated stress. "Thyroid is the essence of oxidation for living cells," Peat told Danny Roddy in *Generative Energy* episode 35. As thyroid signal falls, the production of CO2 falls, the protection against lactate falls, the production of new mitochondria falls, and the system loses the single most important defense against the rest of the cascade. The mechanism is at [[thyroid]].

**Step 3. PUFA accumulation in tissue lipids.** This is the slowest, most cumulative, and most consequential step. Polyunsaturated fatty acids — linoleic acid from seed oils, the long-chain PUFA from fish oil — incorporate into cellular phospholipids, and the body's half-life for clearing them out, once they are deposited in tissue stores, is on the order of two to four years. A person eating a Western diet from infancy carries an adipose-tissue linoleic acid content that has roughly doubled over the past century. The mitochondrial inner membrane phospholipid cardiolipin, which holds [[cytochrome-c-oxidase]] in its functional configuration, is the most diagnostic example: in a healthy young mitochondrion, cardiolipin is rich in palmitate; in an aged or PUFA-loaded mitochondrion, palmitate is displaced by PUFA, the enzyme conformation is disrupted, and respiratory capacity falls. "Palmitic acid, which is a major component of the cardiolipin which regulates the main respiratory enzyme, becomes displaced by polyunsaturated fats as aging progresses. Copper tends to be lost from this same enzyme system, and the state of the water is altered as the energetic processes change," Peat wrote in [[mitochondria-and-mortality-diet-exercise-and-medicine-damaging-or-repairing-respiratory-metabolism|*Mitochondria and Mortality* (2016)]]. The full mechanism is at [[pufa]] and [[cardiolipin]].

**Step 4. Lipid peroxidation.** Once PUFA is in the phospholipid, the next stress event — any spike in adrenaline that mobilizes free fatty acids, any episode of hypoxia, any iron-catalyzed Fenton reaction — initiates a chain of peroxidation that produces malondialdehyde, 4-hydroxynonenal, and other aldehydes that attack mitochondrial proteins, cross-link collagen, and damage DNA. The products accumulate as the brown granules of [[age-pigment|lipofuscin]] in long-lived cells (neurons, cardiomyocytes, retinal pigment epithelium). "Rather than using the vague idea of 'free radical damage,' I think I can show a simple mechanism by which the terminal toxin, lipofuscin, produces the essential features of aging," Peat wrote in [[age-pigment-cause-and-effect-of-aging-and-stress|*Age Pigment* (2013)]]. Lipofuscin is not inert. It consumes oxygen and NADH without producing ATP, competes with mitochondria for the cell's respiratory resources, and itself generates more of the free radicals that produce more of itself. The full mechanism is at [[age-pigment]] and [[lipid-peroxidation]].

**Step 5. Iron accumulation.** Iron, once absorbed, leaves the body extremely slowly (menstruation is the major route; men and post-menopausal women lack any meaningful excretion mechanism). Across decades, ferritin rises, and iron concentrates in liver, brain, heart, and joints. In hypoxic or stressed tissue, heme is synthesized spontaneously and iron deposits as part of the age pigment itself. "The high mortality of old age is associated with a high concentration of iron in the tissues, just as the high mortality of young infants is associated with a high concentration of iron. As the infant's iron is diluted by growth, mortality decreases," Peat wrote in [[mortality-again|*Mortality Again* (1994)]]. The bimodal mortality curve — infant + elderly — tracks tissue iron rather than calendar age. Iron amplifies the peroxidation of step 4 (the Fenton reaction generates hydroxyl radicals from iron and hydrogen peroxide), and the peroxidation products help keep iron locked into tissue. The two steps reinforce one another. The full mechanism is at [[iron]].

**Step 6. Estrogen and cortisol dominance.** With falling thyroid function, the liver fails to glucuronidate estrogen efficiently. Aromatase activity rises in fat, skin, and brain — even after the ovaries have stopped producing estrogen, tissue aromatase keeps producing it locally, and beta-glucuronidase in inflamed tissue keeps liberating it from the inactive conjugates. "In aging, the sharply decreased progesterone production creates a situation resembling the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle, allowing tissues to concentrate estrogen even when the serum estrogen may be low," Peat wrote in [[tissue-bound-estrogen-in-aging|*Tissue-Bound Estrogen in Aging* (2008)]]. The tissue/plasma ratio rises sharply. Progesterone, DHEA, and pregnenolone — the protective steroids made in healthy mitochondria — all fall, because their synthesis requires the very respiratory capacity that the rest of the cascade has been suppressing. "When the mitochondria are damaged, the protective steroid pregnenolone (which is made in the mitochondria from cholesterol) can't be produced," Peat wrote in [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|*Solving Some of the Problems of Aging* (1991)]]. "In young people, the brain contains a very high concentration of pregnenolone and its derivatives, DHEA and progesterone, all of which stabilize cells and protect against the effects of cortisol, but in old age these fall to about 5% of their normal concentration, leaving the brain exposed to the destructive action of cortisol." Cortisol's diurnal range compresses, its trough rises, and its catabolic effects on muscle, bone, and brain run unopposed. The full mechanism is at [[estrogen]], [[progesterone]], [[cortisol]], and [[pregnenolone]].

**Step 7. Calcium dysregulation.** With falling cellular energy, the calcium-pumping ATPases of the plasma membrane and the sarcoplasmic reticulum lose efficiency. Intracellular calcium rises. Mitochondria, attempting to buffer the cytoplasm, accumulate calcium until they themselves become dysfunctional — F.Z. Meerson's "calcium triad," the convergence of stress, calcium accumulation, and mitochondrial damage that Peat treated as the central cellular event of both aging and chronic disease. "On the cellular level, in aging and estrogen-dominance, Magnesium-ATP tends to be lost as calcium is retained in excess. The sodium-potassium-ATPase activity declines. Proteolysis increasingly dominates relative to protein synthesis," Peat wrote in [[energy-entropy-and-estrogens-in-aging|*Energy, Entropy and Estrogens in Aging* (1985)]]. Calcified arteries, calcified joints, calcified mitochondria, calcified pineal — the visible deposits of late-stage aging — are the readout of cellular energy failure, not the cause of it. The full mechanism is in the calcium-and-disease line of newsletters and at [[mitochondria]].

**Step 8. Mitochondrial damage and tissue fibrosis.** The terminal step. The cell that can no longer make ATP through complete oxidation cannot maintain its proteins, cannot synthesize the protective steroids, cannot rebuild its damaged enzymes, and falls back on glycolysis — the primitive metabolism that produced lactate even before oxygen-using life existed. The serotonin-driven activation of TGF-beta, the estrogen-driven activation of aromatase and angiogenesis, and the cortisol-driven proteolysis all converge on the same outcome: connective tissue replaces parenchymal tissue. The liver fibroses. The heart fibroses. The kidneys fibrose. The skin thins, the collagen cross-links, and the elastic recoil that distinguished young tissue from old is permanently lost. "Old animals contain a higher proportion of collagen. Old tendons (or tendons that have been exposed to excessive estrogen, which stimulates the formation of collagen) are more rigid, and behave almost as if they have been partly cooked," Peat wrote in [[tryptophan-serotonin-and-aging|*Tryptophan, Serotonin, and Aging* (2006)]]. The full mechanism is at [[fibrosis]] and [[mitochondria]].

These eight steps are not separable. Each one suppresses respiratory capacity, and the suppression makes every other step easier. The cascade is what aging *is*, and the metabolic state at age seventy is the integral of every previous episode that ran a stretch of this chain to completion without being interrupted. This is the unique conceptual signal of this page: aging is the *path-dependent residue of every prior stress episode*, not the readout of a calendar.

## Compounds and practices that slow or reverse aging

If the cascade above is the disease, the protective list is the inverse — substances and practices that interrupt the chain at one or more steps. The catalog below is a survey, not a re-derivation; each item links to its canonical page.

**[[thyroid|Thyroid hormone]] replacement** (T3, T4, or the natural-desiccated combinations Peat recommended) is the first lever, because thyroid restores the oxidative capacity on which the rest depends. "Thyroid supplementation produces increased activity of cytochrome oxidase," Peat wrote in [[chronic-fatigue|*Chronic Fatigue*]]; combined with PUFA depletion, it can begin to reverse the cardiolipin substitution of step 3.

**[[progesterone|Progesterone]]** is the second lever, particularly for women. It opposes estrogen at the tissue level, supports the synthesis of pregnenolone and DHEA, blocks cortisol's catabolic effects on the brain, and (in Peat's clinical experience) reversed many of the symptoms — insomnia, hot flushes, joint pain, brain fog — that conventional medicine attributes to "estrogen deficiency." Men can use it too, in much smaller doses, for its anti-cortisol and anti-aromatase effects.

**[[pregnenolone|Pregnenolone]]** and **[[dhea|DHEA]]** are the precursor steroids whose synthesis the aged mitochondrion can no longer support. Exogenous supplementation in low doses (Peat's typical recommendation for pregnenolone was 30-100 mg daily) can restore the brain's protective steroid pool without the side effects of higher-potency hormones downstream.

**[[aspirin]]** acts at multiple steps: it uncouples mitochondria (raising CO2 production, lowering free-radical leak), it inhibits aromatase, it suppresses prostaglandin formation from arachidonic acid, it stimulates the use of lactate as fuel rather than accumulating it. Peat used 300-1,000 mg per day as a routine anti-aging measure in older adults.

**[[vitamin-e|Vitamin E]]** (mixed tocopherols, with the alpha-tocopherol fraction not isolated and excessive) is the principal interruption of lipid peroxidation (step 4). Vitamin E was the first antioxidant Peat learned about in the Shute-brothers tradition, and he treated it as essentially mandatory for anyone consuming any PUFA at all.

**[[vitamin-k|Vitamin K]]** (especially K2 as MK-4) opposes the calcium dysregulation of step 7 by directing calcium to bone rather than soft tissue, and supports mitochondrial respiration through its role in electron transport.

**[[niacinamide]]** acts at the level of NAD+/NADH balance, supporting the high-NAD+ redox state that prevents lactate accumulation, and (in Peat's late writing) directly improves protein solubility in ways that compensate for the loss of ATP's "cardinal adsorbent" function. It is also a strong anti-lipolytic, suppressing the free-fatty-acid mobilization of step 1.

**[[methylene-blue]]** restores electron flow through the respiratory chain when complex I is damaged, and (in Peat's late writing on the [[51-heat-shock-proteins-antibiotic-resistance-dht-safety-energy-and-aging-with-ray-peat-phd|*Generative Energy* episode 51 on aging]]) "plugs the leak" in cells whose energy economy has begun to fail.

**[[gelatin]]** (or bone broth) provides glycine and proline, the amino acids that oppose the tryptophan-serotonin axis driving fibrosis (step 8), and protects the liver from the cortisol-driven proteolysis of step 6. Eaten in the ratio of roughly half the muscle-meat intake, it counteracts the tryptophan excess of a steak-heavy diet.

**Calcium balance** through dairy (which provides calcium with magnesium, phosphorus, and potassium in approximately physiological proportions) supports parathyroid suppression and bone mineralization without driving the soft-tissue calcification of step 7.

**Salt** (well in excess of mainstream recommendations — Peat suggested salting to taste with no upper limit for healthy people) suppresses serotonin and aldosterone, lowers adrenaline, and improves the blood volume and oncotic pressure that step 8's vascular leakiness has compromised.

**Sugar** — sucrose, ripe fruit, fruit juice, honey — restores glucose availability, suppresses the free-fatty-acid mobilization that drives the Randle effect, and provides the substrate for CO2 production that fat oxidation cannot match. Peat reversed the late-twentieth-century anti-sugar consensus on the grounds that sugar deficiency, not sugar excess, is what drives the cortisol-and-adrenaline baseline of aging.

**[[morning-sunlight|Morning sunlight]]**, **[[red-light|red light]]**, and **[[walking|walking]]** are the practices most directly tied to the cascade: red and infrared light support cytochrome oxidase activity directly; morning light suppresses the cortisol-melatonin pattern of step 6; walking (low-intensity, sustained, oxidative, non-PUFA-mobilizing) maintains mitochondrial mass without triggering the catabolic stress response of intense exercise. Cold-water swimming, ice baths, and other hormetic-stress practices Peat treated with skepticism — see [[cold-plunge-dont-do-it]].

The list above is descriptive, not prescriptive. The clinical synthesis — how to sequence these levers, how to monitor response, what to do when each one is insufficient — is at [[core-diet-protocol]] and the condition-specific protocols ([[anxiety-depression-protocol]], [[hair-loss-protocol]], [[dementia-protocol]], [[heart-disease-protocol]], [[menopause-protocol]]).

## Markers that aging IS happening

Peat treated aging as something the patient and clinician could measure directly, without recourse to telomere-length assays or methylation clocks. The signals are simple and biological, and they track the cascade above step by step.

**Pulse and temperature.** A young, healthy person has a resting pulse in the upper 70s to upper 80s, a basal oral temperature near 98.6°F that rises to 99.0-99.6°F after breakfast, and an axillary temperature that climbs with the day. An aged or chronically stressed person runs cooler and slower. "Temperature reflects the heat that complete oxidation produces. Pulse reflects the rate at which the system is running," is the one-line form of Peat's clinical method. The signal predates any blood test. The methodology is at [[broda-barnes]] and [[temperature-pulse-tracking]].

**Sleep architecture.** Young sleep is consolidated, deep, and unbroken until shortly before waking. Aging sleep fragments, shifts earlier, and is punctuated by 2-4 AM wakings that correspond to the dawn rise of cortisol catching the patient before blood sugar has been replenished. "I knew that many old people like to get up and start their day a little before dawn, but after this experience I began to notice that many younger people have — and are disturbed by — a similar alteration of their sleep rhythm," Peat wrote in [[solving-some-of-the-problems-of-aging|*Solving Some of the Problems of Aging* (1991)]], describing his own pesticide-exposure-induced premature aging. The 2 AM waking, especially if accompanied by sweating, is the early signature of cortisol dominance. Salt, sugar, and gelatin at bedtime — or in the middle of the night if necessary — are the simple intervention.

**Mood compression.** The young brain runs a wide dynamic range — exuberance, grief, focus, drift, sleep, wakefulness — without sticking. The aged brain compresses: less peak, less trough, a flat affective baseline that conventional medicine treats with SSRIs (which Peat regarded as drugs that compound the problem by raising serotonin further). The signal is the loss of the *high* end, more than the increase of the *low* end. Pregnenolone, progesterone, thyroid, and sugar are the interventions that restore the range.

**Hair greying and skin thinning.** Greying is partly driven by the tryptophan-serotonin axis (white hair is the highest in tryptophan content of any hair color) and partly by copper loss from the same enzyme systems that have lost copper at cytochrome oxidase. Skin thinning is the visible readout of falling progesterone (which is anabolic for skin) and rising cortisol (which is catabolic). Both signals are downstream — they are records of the upstream metabolic shift, not the disease itself.

**Libido and reproductive function.** Falling libido in either sex tracks falling oxidative capacity in the gonads more reliably than it tracks any single hormone. Testosterone falls because the steroidogenic mitochondria of the Leydig cells are losing function. Progesterone falls because the corpus luteum (in pre-menopausal women) is making less of it, and the brain (in both sexes) is making less of its neurosteroid pool. The signal is bidirectional: restoring oxidative metabolism restores libido, often within weeks.

**Wound healing and recovery time.** A young animal heals fast; an aged animal heals slow. The signal is mitochondrial: regeneration requires the energy that complete oxidation produces, and the loss of regenerative capacity tracks the loss of respiratory capacity. The clinical signal is the time it takes to recover from a workout, an illness, or a minor injury — and the trajectory of that signal across years is more informative than any single time point.

**Total CO2 in venous blood.** The single most underused signal in conventional medicine. Healthy young adults run venous CO2 around 25-28 mEq/L; aged or chronically stressed adults drift toward 22-24. The signal is cheap, available on any basic metabolic panel, and tracks oxidative capacity directly. Peat treated it as more diagnostic than TSH, which he considered (see [[tsh]]) a downstream and frequently misleading measurement.

**Lactate, if measurable.** Resting lactate above 2 mmol/L in a person who has not just exercised is a signal that the cell is producing lactate when it should be producing CO2. Most chronic disease tracks rising lactate.

Together these signals form Peat's "biological age" — a state the patient can monitor on a thermometer, a pulse meter, a sleep log, and an ordinary metabolic panel, without recourse to commercial aging-clock assays whose underlying biology is at best correlational with the variables that actually matter.

## Counter-arguments

Peat's framework is not the consensus, and the counter-positions deserve a fair hearing.

**The Sinclair / sirtuin / NAD+ camp.** David Sinclair and the resveratrol-NMN-NR line of research argue that aging is, at root, an information-decay problem — epigenetic methylation patterns drift away from their developmental program, and reversing the drift can restore youthful function. The supporting evidence (Yamanaka-factor partial reprogramming, NAD+ restoration in aged tissues) is real. The Peat-frame critique is that NAD+ falls with age because oxidative metabolism has failed — NADH accumulates because mitochondria are not consuming it at the rate at which glycolysis produces it — and that restoring NAD+ supplementally addresses the readout rather than the cause. Niacinamide does some of what NMN does, at a fraction of the cost, and through a more clearly characterized mechanism. The methylation-clock signal Peat would accept as a downstream marker but not as a treatment target.

**The senolytics camp.** Dasatinib, quercetin, fisetin, and other compounds that selectively kill senescent cells have produced impressive results in mouse studies and (in early human trials) for specific diseases of senescent-cell accumulation like idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. The Peat-frame critique is that senescent cells are produced by the cascade above (a cell whose mitochondria are damaged exits the cycle), and killing the senescent cells without addressing the upstream metabolic failure leaves the underlying disease untouched. Peat would predict — and the long-term data is not yet in — that senolytic treatment produces transient improvement followed by recurrence as the same metabolic state produces new senescent cells.

**The calorie-restriction / mTOR camp.** Caloric restriction extends lifespan in many species, and the mechanism is partly mTOR-pathway suppression. The Peat-frame critique is two-fold. First, the rat studies that established the canonical CR effect were comparing CR animals to ad-libitum animals on chow that was approximately 20% PUFA — the CR animals lived longer partly because they ate less of the toxic substrate, not because of any unique effect of energy restriction itself. Second, in human terms, CR produces hypothyroidism, lower body temperature, lower libido, lower muscle mass, and a stress-hormone profile that resembles starvation — the very state Peat treated as the upstream cause of aging. The mTOR signal is real (mTOR responds to amino acid and energy availability), but the Peat-frame reading is that mTOR is the cell's normal response to substrate availability, and suppressing mTOR mimics the metabolic state of malnutrition rather than the state of robust health.

**The telomere-extension camp.** TA-65 and similar telomerase activators, the various gene-therapy approaches that introduce telomerase into somatic cells, and the broader claim that telomere shortening drives senescence — these all rest on the observation that critically short telomeres trigger cell-cycle exit. The Peat-frame critique is that telomere shortening tracks cell division, and cells whose redox state has slipped toward the Warburg metabolism divide more often (because differentiation has failed). Lengthening telomeres in a metabolically broken cell may keep the cell dividing, but dividing is itself the dedifferentiated state Peat treated as the precursor to cancer. The proximate cause of senescence may be telomere shortening, but the distal cause is energy failure.

**The genetic-determinism camp.** The position that aging is fundamentally a problem of DNA damage accumulation, somatic mutation, and read-error rates over time has its purest expression in the work of Aubrey de Grey and the SENS Foundation, and a softer version in most academic gerontology. The full Peat-frame critique is at [[genetic-determinism]], but the relevant compression: DNA damage is real, but mutations accumulate because the cellular machinery that repairs them runs on energy that the failing mitochondria are no longer producing. Reversing the energy failure restores repair capacity (Peat cited niacinamide's effect on poly-ADP-ribose polymerase as one example); attacking the mutations without restoring the energy fails because new mutations replace the old ones at the same rate.

**The longevity-medicine clinic.** The Peter Attia / Bryan Johnson / Andrew Huberman tradition that has emerged in the 2020s combines elements of the above with continuous monitoring, hormetic exercise, time-restricted feeding, and aggressive lipid-lowering. The Peat-frame critique runs differently for each component. Hormetic exercise: Peat accepted that some exercise is protective and that intense exercise is catabolic — see [[exercise]] for the dose-response. Time-restricted feeding: Peat regarded any prolonged fast as a stress that mobilizes free fatty acids and raises cortisol; the apparent benefits are mostly the avoidance of late-night PUFA loading rather than any unique effect of fasting itself. Lipid-lowering with statins: Peat regarded LDL cholesterol as a substrate for steroid synthesis and a marker of liver glucuronidation capacity rather than a cause of disease; statin trials show small all-cause mortality benefits at best, and the side effects on muscle, brain, and steroid synthesis track the mechanism Peat would predict.

Each of these camps has produced real signals. The Peat-frame disagreement is not that any of them is fabricated; it is that they have mistaken downstream measurements for upstream causes, and that the upstream cause — declining mitochondrial respiratory capacity — is itself addressable by interventions that none of these camps has incorporated.

## Data gaps

Peat's framework has not been formalized into a quantitative aging-rate model, and the honest catalog of what it cannot yet do is the following.

**No quantitative aging-rate model.** Peat never wrote down, and the Peat tradition has not produced, a formal equation relating PUFA-intake-history to expected respiratory-capacity loss to expected mortality rate. The components are present (the PUFA half-life is known, the cardiolipin-substitution rate has been measured, cytochrome-oxidase activity loss with age is documented), but the integration into a single model that would let one calculate, from a patient's history, an expected biological-age trajectory and an expected effect-size for each intervention does not exist. This is a real gap. The mainstream gerontology camps have models (methylation clocks, GrimAge, PhenoAge); Peat's framework has the mechanism but not the calculus.

**No population-level mortality validation.** The Peat-frame interventions — high sugar, high salt, low PUFA, thyroid replacement, progesterone supplementation — have not been tested in randomized controlled trials against mortality endpoints in large populations. The supporting evidence is mechanistic (the biochemistry is rigorous), clinical (Peat's correspondents over forty years report improvements), and inferential (the epidemiology of populations with low PUFA intake and high reproductive lifespans is consistent with the framework), but the gold-standard evidence — a 30-year RCT in 50,000 people — does not exist and is unlikely ever to be funded.

**No formal trials of the protective-steroid stack.** Pregnenolone, progesterone, DHEA, thyroid, and aspirin have each been studied individually in trials targeting specific outcomes (cognition, bone, mood), but the combined Peat-frame stack — and the dose ranges Peat recommended, which differ from typical clinical doses — has not been tested as a coherent intervention against aging endpoints.

**The PUFA-half-life estimate is approximate.** The 2-4 year tissue half-life of linoleic acid is supported by tracer studies, but the dependence of that half-life on metabolic state, on dietary fat composition during the depletion phase, and on individual variation has not been precisely characterized. The clinical advice — "expect 2-4 years to deplete tissue PUFA, plan accordingly" — is approximately right but the error bars are wide.

**The cellular-water and dielectric-state mechanisms remain controversial.** Peat's framework relies heavily on Gilbert Ling's association-induction hypothesis — the claim that cell water is structured by association with intracellular proteins rather than partitioned by membrane pumps. The supporting evidence (NMR of cell water, the volume changes during nerve conduction, the impossibility of running the calculated membrane pumps on the available ATP) is real, but the AIH has not been incorporated into mainstream cell biology, and a Peat-frame mechanism for aging that relies on cell-water dielectric state remains, for most readers, an additional assumption rather than a confirmed step.

**The relative weighting of the cascade steps is uncertain.** Peat's writing emphasizes different steps at different times — PUFA in one essay, estrogen in another, iron in a third, thyroid in a fourth. The relative quantitative contribution of each step to a given patient's aging rate is not known. Two patients with the same chronological age can have arrived there by very different paths through the cascade, and the framework has not been formalized to the point of predicting which intervention will yield the largest effect for a given history.

**The framework's interaction with infectious aging.** Recent work on persistent latent infections (CMV, EBV, herpes simplex, gut endotoxin) and their contribution to chronic inflammation has not been formally integrated into the Peat cascade. The framework has the conceptual hook (endotoxin and bowel toxins are step 1 stressors), but a clean account of how infectious exposure-history compounds with PUFA-and-iron exposure-history is missing.

Honest acknowledgment of these gaps does not refute the framework — every framework has gaps — but it places Peat's account where it belongs: as a mechanistically detailed, clinically tested, but formally incomplete alternative to the mainstream gerontology that has produced its own incomplete models.
