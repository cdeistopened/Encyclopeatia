# Metabolism

"Metabolism" is the most overloaded word in popular health writing — used to mean a person's body weight, their digestion, their energy level, their resilience to alcohol, the rate at which they burn calories on a treadmill. In the technical literature it is no less promiscuous: it covers the entire web of chemical transformations through which a cell builds large molecules from small ones (anabolism), breaks large molecules down into small ones (catabolism), and harvests usable energy from the difference. Ray Peat used the bare term constantly, but he used it with a specific resolution: when Peat said "metabolism," he meant the rate and the *quality* with which a tissue oxidizes glucose to carbon dioxide. The umbrella resolves, in his framework, to [[oxidative-metabolism]] when it is working and to [[glycolysis]] when it is failing. Everything else — the [[metabolic-rate|rate]] at which calories are burned, the temperature at which the body holds itself, the felt energy of being a person — is downstream of which of those two modes the cells are running.

This article is the umbrella entry that disambiguates the cluster. It does not re-derive the mechanism of [[oxidative-metabolism]] (the 6,700-word canonical lives one folder over), and it does not re-derive the failure mode at [[glycolysis]] or the diagnostic at [[metabolic-rate]]. It maps the territory: what "metabolism" means before it specializes, how Peat used the term distinctively, what raises it, what lowers it, and how the broader literature pushes back. The detail compresses into [[hub-link|hub-links]] on purpose. Almost every claim here is developed at length somewhere else in the vault, and the right way to read this page is as the table of contents to that argument.

## What "metabolism" means in this framework

In the textbook sense metabolism is the sum of three activities a cell carries out. **Anabolism** is the building work — assembling proteins from amino acids, packaging glucose into glycogen, esterifying fatty acids into triglycerides, condensing nucleotides into DNA. **Catabolism** is the tearing-down work — breaking glycogen back to glucose, hydrolyzing proteins to amino acids, oxidizing fats to acetyl-CoA, degrading nucleic acids. **Energy production** ([[energy-production]]) is the transformation that powers both directions: cells extract chemical free energy from food substrates and store it as ATP, the universal energy currency that drives biosynthesis, contraction, secretion, transport, and signaling. Without ATP, the building and the tearing-down both stop, and the cell dies within minutes.

Peat accepted this triadic structure but inverted the emphasis it usually carries. Mainstream physiology treats metabolism as a roughly neutral accounting exercise — calories in, calories out, ATP produced, ATP consumed. Peat treated the *quality* of energy production as the load-bearing variable. Two people burning the same number of calories per day can be in radically different metabolic states, because one is producing ATP through the full oxidation of glucose to CO2 (~36 ATP per glucose, exhaust: water and CO2) and the other is producing ATP through fermentation (~2 ATP per glucose, exhaust: [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]]). "Glycolysis is very inefficient for producing usable energy compared to the respiratory metabolism of the [[mitochondria]]," Peat wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|*Lactate vs. CO2*]], "and when lactate is carried to the liver, its conversion to glucose adds to the energy drain on the organism." A glycolytic cell is not merely producing less energy — it is forcing the rest of the body to clean up after it.

The Peat-specific framing is that "metabolism" is measured by **how efficiently glucose ends as CO2 versus how often it gets shunted to lactate, fat, or oxidative damage**. This is the conceptual move that lets the vault treat fatigue, [[aging]], [[cancer]], [[hypothyroidism]], [[diabetes]], [[dementia]], depression, and infertility as expressions of the same underlying defect — cells that have stopped completing the oxidation of glucose. The full mechanism, with its experimental lineage from [[otto-warburg|Warburg]], [[albert-szent-gyorgyi|Szent-Györgyi]], and Frederick Koch, is at [[oxidative-metabolism]].

The hormonal axis that activates oxidation is [[thyroid]]. The product that stabilizes it is [[carbon-dioxide]]. The substance class that suppresses it is [[pufa]]. The organelle that runs it is [[mitochondria]]. The marker of its failure is [[lactic-acid]]. These five vault entries are the operational vocabulary of "metabolism" in the Peat sense. Together they constitute the framework Peat called [[bioenergetics]] — life as the controlled flow of electrons from sugar to oxygen, with structure, repair, and consciousness as the residue of that flow.

## The two dominant modes

Within the umbrella, two modes do almost all the metabolic work that matters to Peat's framework.

**Oxidative metabolism** is the mode the body is built around. Glucose breaks down through [[glycolysis]] to pyruvate; pyruvate enters the [[mitochondria]] via [[pyruvate-dehydrogenase|pyruvate dehydrogenase]]; it is oxidized through the Krebs cycle, releasing CO2 at three decarboxylation steps; the electrons flow down the respiratory chain to oxygen, generating ~36 ATP per glucose and leaving water and CO2 as the only products. This is the metabolism of a young, well-fed, thyroid-adequate, [[pufa|PUFA]]-light animal in a warm room with good light. It produces heat, it produces CO2 (which stabilizes the proteins and reinforces the conditions under which the chain keeps running), and it produces structure. The full account is at [[oxidative-metabolism]].

**Glycolytic metabolism** is the failure mode — fermentation without the mitochondrial completion step. Pyruvate is reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase rather than entering the mitochondrion; the yield drops to ~2 ATP, the exhaust is [[lactic-acid]], CO2 production falls to near zero. This is the metabolism of a tumor cell, an exhausted muscle, a hypothyroid tissue, a cell awash in free fatty acids that block glucose oxidation through the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]. The tumor-cell version is the [[warburg-effect|Warburg effect]] — fermentation in the presence of oxygen — which Peat treated as the most dramatic case of a defect that, in milder forms, produces most chronic disease. The mechanism is at [[glycolysis]].

The pair is not Manichaean. A healthy cell does some glycolysis all the time, and during brief bursts of activity lactate production is the appropriate response. The distinction Peat cared about was *chronic* glycolysis in the presence of oxygen — fermentation as the cell's standing mode rather than its emergency mode. The transition is reversible, and most of what the vault recommends (sugar, salt, thyroid, [[carbon-dioxide]], light, warmth) is aimed at restoring the conditions under which a cell preferentially completes oxidation rather than terminating at lactate.

## High metabolism vs low metabolism in Peat's clinic

Peat did not, in his clinical writing, use TSH or laboratory thyroid panels as primary diagnostic instruments. He used the **externally visible signature of how the metabolism is actually running**. The full protocol lives at [[temperature-tracking]]; the clinical lineage runs through [[broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]] and through Peat's own decades of advising people who had been told by mainstream endocrinologists that their thyroid tests were normal. The visible signs of a well-functioning metabolism, in Peat's writing, were these:

- **Pulse** in the mid-80s at rest, rising appropriately to about 100 after a meal, returning to baseline within an hour. A pulse stuck in the 60s in an adult, especially one who is cold or tired, is a sign of suppressed oxidation. A pulse spiked into the 90s without exertion and unresponsive to food is a sign of adrenaline compensating for low thyroid (the "stress-driven pseudo-high metabolism" that hypothyroid patients often present with).
- **Temperature** at or near 98.6°F by mid-morning, having risen from its sleeping low (~97.8°F) after breakfast. A morning temperature that *falls* after breakfast — rather than rising — is the diagnostic Peat treated as nearly pathognomonic of low thyroid being held up overnight by [[cortisol]] and [[adrenaline]]. Food removes the need for stress hormones; when they drop, the true low metabolism is revealed.
- **Mood** stable, oriented, capable of sustained attention. The brain is the most oxidatively demanding organ in the body, and depressed metabolism shows up in cognition before it shows up almost anywhere else: brain fog, anxiety attacks, depression that lifts with sugar and worsens with caffeine on an empty stomach, sleep that does not restore.
- **Libido**, hair condition, skin quality, nail growth, healing speed, cold tolerance, and reflex relaxation time. All of these are downstream of the same variable. Hair falls when ATP is insufficient to drive the anagen phase; skin yellows on the palms because the carotene-to-vitamin-A conversion is thyroid-dependent and stalls when thyroid is low; the Achilles reflex relaxes slowly because muscle ATP is insufficient to clear calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

Peat's frame for this constellation was **youthful tissue**. A high-metabolism adult is one whose tissues behave the way a child's tissues do: warm extremities, fast healing, full hair, clear skin, robust libido, stable mood, sound sleep. A low-metabolism adult is running on lactate and stress hormones — cold, dry, slow, anxious. The metabolic substrate of feeling well as an adult is the same substrate that ran the child, and when it is restored (adequate thyroid, sugar, protein, calcium, light, warmth), adult tissue recovers much of what looks like youthful function. Diagnostic detail at [[metabolic-rate]] and [[temperature-tracking]].

The pseudo-high-metabolism trap is the common confusion. A person with high pulse, high temperature, anxiety, insomnia, and weight loss is not in a Peat-sense high-metabolism state — they are in a stress-driven catabolic state where cortisol and adrenaline are holding the system up at the expense of muscle and bone. Peat addressed this in his emails on [[high-metabolism|high metabolism]]: "About your high metabolic rate and high temperature: In my teens and twenties, I needed about 8000 calories per day when I was physically active…but after I took thyroid, I needed only about half as many calories." Thyroid replacement *lowers* the apparent metabolic rate when the rate was stress-driven, because it restores efficiency. True high metabolism is calm; stress-driven high metabolism is frantic. The split lives at [[metabolic-rate]].

## What raises metabolism

The substances and conditions Peat treated as raising oxidative metabolism share an operational signature: they activate the chain that converts glucose to CO2, suppress the chain that diverts glucose to lactate, or remove obstacles that are blocking the first chain. The full mechanism of each lives in its own vault entry; the one-line versions, with their direction of action, are:

- **[[thyroid|Thyroid hormone]] (T3, NDT, occasionally synthetic combinations).** The master regulator. T3 increases cytochrome oxidase activity, drives mitochondrial biogenesis, raises the NAD+/NADH ratio that keeps the cytoplasm in an oxidized state, and stimulates uncoupling. Of every intervention in the vault, this is the one Peat treated as load-bearing.
- **Adequate sugar.** Glucose is the preferred fuel of the oxidative pathway. Restricting it forces the body into the stress metabolism: [[cortisol]] rises to make glucose from protein, free fatty acids rise to substitute for the glucose, the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]] kicks in, and the metabolism slips toward lactate. Fruit, juice, honey, milk, and ripe fruit are the Peat-canonical sugar sources. Detail at [[glucose]].
- **Adequate protein.** Roughly 80–100g per day in an adult, ideally including gelatin (for glycine, which is anti-stress) and dairy (for calcium, tryptophan-balanced amino acids, and the thyroid-supportive ratio). Protein deficiency stalls thyroid hormone synthesis and forces the body to catabolize its own tissue.
- **Sun and light.** Red and near-infrared light penetrate tissue and activate cytochrome c oxidase directly. UV exposure raises vitamin D, lowers cortisol, and improves dopamine. Peat treated chronic light deficiency as a metabolism-suppressing condition in its own right.
- **Warmth.** Cold drives stress hormones, suppresses thyroid, and shifts the metabolism toward fat oxidation and lactate production. A warm room, warm clothing, warm food, warm baths, and avoiding chronic cold exposure are the Peat-canonical thermal interventions. (Cold plunges, in his framework, are stressors masquerading as adaptations.)
- **Calcium and magnesium.** Calcium (from dairy and bone broth) suppresses [[parathyroid-hormone|PTH]], which would otherwise activate lipolysis and intracellular fatty acid release. Magnesium stabilizes ATP and is depleted by chronic stress and by the early phase of thyroid restoration.
- **[[niacinamide]] (vitamin B3 amide form).** Lowers free fatty acids, supports the NAD+/NADH ratio, partially substitutes for ATP under some conditions, and helps the liver detoxify estrogens and PUFA. One of the most reliable metabolism-supporting supplements in the Peat protocol.
- **Caffeine** (with food, not on an empty stomach). Increases respiratory rate, supports thyroid hormone conversion in the liver, and antagonizes adenosine — but only when the person is also eating enough sugar and protein to prevent the cortisol spike that caffeine on an empty stomach produces.
- **Saturated fats** (coconut oil, butter, beef tallow). Replace PUFA in the cardiolipin pool, do not generate the peroxidation products that suppress respiration, and slightly uncouple the mitochondria when they are oxidized.
- **Aspirin.** Mild uncoupler. Suppresses inflammatory prostaglandins, lowers serotonin, and protects mitochondrial respiration. The full mechanism is at [[aspirin]].
- **CO2 itself.** Bag-breathing, altitude exposure, mineral water with bicarbonate. Raises tissue CO2, which inhibits lactate formation and stabilizes the proteins. Detail at [[carbon-dioxide]].

Peat's synthesis: "The 'treatment' for intracellular fatigue consists of normalizing thyroid and steroid metabolism, and eating a diet including fruit juice, milk, some eggs or liver, and gelatin, assuring adequate calcium, potassium sodium, and magnesium, and using supplements of niacin-amide, aspirin, and carbon dioxide when necessary." Not a list of boosters — the conditions under which a cell preferentially completes oxidation. Full protocols at [[anti-stress-diet]] and [[energy-restoration]].

## What lowers metabolism

The mirror list is more important than the boost list, because in modern environments most of the metabolism-lowering factors are running by default and most of the boosters require active intervention.

- **Chronic [[stress]].** The full cascade — adrenaline → free fatty acids → Randle block → lactate → cortisol → catabolism → estrogen → serotonin → endotoxin — is the operational meaning of "low metabolism" in most adults. The cascade is detailed at [[stress]] and at [[cortisol]]. The intervention is restoring substrate, not adding more stressors (cold plunges, fasting, exercise) on top of an already-failing system.
- **[[pufa|PUFA accumulation]].** The slow, decade-long substitution of polyunsaturated fatty acids into the [[cardiolipin]] pool that holds cytochrome oxidase in functional configuration. Mainstream nutrition treats linoleic acid as essential; Peat treated modern intakes as universally toxic. Tissue PUFA takes 2–4 years to clear after dietary reduction. Detail at [[pufa]].
- **Undereating.** Caloric restriction lowers T3 within days, raises cortisol, mobilizes fatty acids, blocks glucose oxidation, and slips the system into the stress metabolism. The popular [[intermittent-fasting|intermittent fasting]] literature is, in Peat's reading, a sales pitch for the same metabolic state that defines starvation.
- **Cold.** Direct stressor. Drives [[adrenaline]], shifts fuel selection toward fat, suppresses thyroid, raises lactate. The "cold adaptation" literature is partial — yes, the body adapts to cold; no, the adaptation is not a positive intervention on a system already in a low-metabolism state.
- **Estrogen excess.** Estrogen suppresses oxidative metabolism by multiple routes — blocking T4-to-T3 conversion, raising serotonin, mobilizing fatty acids, raising lactate, suppressing progesterone. Detail at [[estrogen]] and at the conditions ([[pms]], [[menopause]], [[pcos]]) where the estrogen-metabolism axis is most visible clinically.
- **Iron overload.** Iron catalyzes the Fenton reaction that generates hydroxyl radicals from hydrogen peroxide, accelerating [[lipid-peroxidation]] and damaging mitochondrial enzymes. The accumulation is slow, the damage is cumulative, and most adult men and post-menopausal women are loading iron whether they intend to or not. Detail at [[iron]].
- **[[endotoxin|Endotoxin]].** Bacterial lipopolysaccharide from intestinal sources. Suppresses mitochondrial respiration, raises cortisol, activates inflammation, and is one of the most underweighted factors in mainstream metabolic medicine. The Peat-canonical interventions — raw carrot salad, charcoal, well-cooked food, low fiber — are aimed directly at it.
- **Exercise done badly.** Eccentric loading, endurance training in a glycogen-depleted state, exercise on top of an already-stressed system. Peat distinguished "concentric" from "eccentric" work and was unimpressed with the modern endurance paradigm. Detail at [[exercise]].
- **Cortisol** and [[adrenaline]] themselves, when chronic. The acute stress response is appropriate; the chronic stress state is the operational meaning of "low metabolism." Each step compounds the next.

Operational rule from Peat's clinic: most adults presenting with low-temperature, low-pulse, low-mood, low-libido, cold-hands-feet, brittle-hair, dry-skin, anxious-sleep are not deficient in willpower. They are running on stress hormones because the substrate is insufficient to meet demand, and almost every popular health intervention — cold plunges, fasting, low-carb, exercise on top, meditation alone — adds another stressor to a system that needs less stress and more substrate.

## Counter-arguments

- **The calorie-restriction-extends-lifespan literature.** Multiple lines of evidence — from McCay's 1934 rat experiments through CALERIE in humans — show that calorie restriction extends lifespan in most laboratory species and improves some health markers in humans. The Peatian position treats most of this benefit as artifactual (cooler housing temperatures in CR rodent studies, removal of inflammatory food sources rather than caloric reduction per se, selection effects) but does not engage the literature in detail. The mainstream interpretation — that lower metabolic rate is protective — is directly opposite to Peat's claim that higher oxidative throughput is protective.
- **The "metabolism doesn't matter, calories in = calories out" school.** Mainstream obesity medicine and most exercise-physiology programs treat metabolic rate as a relatively fixed quantity, largely determined by lean body mass, with only small modifiable variance. The Peatian claim that metabolism is highly modifiable by diet, hormones, and environment — and that the modifiable variance is the whole story of weight regulation — is heterodox and partly contradicted by Hall et al.'s "Biggest Loser" studies and similar work on metabolic adaptation.
- **The "metabolism is fixed by genetics" position.** A common framing in popular health writing: some people have a "fast metabolism" and some have a "slow metabolism," and these are essentially genetic endowments. The Peatian position rejects this almost entirely — the genetic variance is small, the environmental variance is large, and the clinical signature of "fixed slow metabolism" is almost always an environmentally driven thyroid suppression that is reversible. (See [[genetic-determinism]] for Peat's broader position on this.)
- **The lactate-as-physiological-fuel literature.** Brooks and colleagues have rehabilitated lactate as a normal inter-organ fuel, shuttled between glycolytic tissues (muscle, RBCs) and oxidative tissues (heart, brain, liver). Peat's framing of lactate as the marker of metabolic failure was developed before this literature matured. Whether the lactate-shuttle data falsifies Peat's framework or merely refines it (i.e., chronic systemic elevation is pathological even if acute inter-organ shuttling is physiological) is unresolved in Peat's writing.
- **The "fat-adapted" / ketogenic metabolism literature.** A sizable community holds that fat oxidation is metabolically equivalent or superior to glucose oxidation, citing improved insulin sensitivity, weight loss, seizure control, and energy stability on ketogenic diets. Peat's claim that ketosis is a stress state and a slide toward Warburg metabolism contradicts this directly. The mainstream counter-evidence (epileptic remission on ketogenic diets, glioblastoma case reports, mitochondrial-disease applications) is real and not addressed in detail by Peat.
- **The BMR-doesn't-predict-anything school.** Some clinical literature suggests basal metabolic rate, measured by indirect calorimetry, does not strongly predict longevity or disease incidence within a given species. Peat's claim that metabolic rate (especially oxidative throughput) is the master variable is contested by this literature, though the Peatian rejoinder would be that conventional BMR measurement does not distinguish oxidative from stress-driven high metabolism.
- **The hormetic-stress school.** Cold exposure, fasting, exercise stress, and other "hormetic" interventions are widely advocated as metabolism-supporting. The Peatian position treats these as either net-negative on a system already stressed (the usual case in modern adults) or context-dependent in ways the popular literature elides. The full critique is at [[hormesis]].

## Data gaps

- **Operationalizing "metabolic rate" without a calorimetry chamber.** Peat's preferred proxies — temperature, pulse, reflex speed, mood — are valuable but qualitative. The relationship between, say, a 98.0°F mid-morning temperature and the actual mitochondrial CO2 production rate of the liver has not been worked out. Whether the proxies are tracking the same variable that indirect calorimetry would measure, or a different variable that correlates with it under most conditions, is unresolved.
- **Individual variation in optimal metabolic rate.** Some healthy adults run cooler than others without apparent ill effect. Some run hotter. Whether the population-mean target (~98.6°F, pulse ~85) is the right target for every individual, or whether there is meaningful inter-individual variation in the "right" set point, is not addressed in Peat's writing.
- **The obesity-metabolism paradox.** Obese adults often have *higher* absolute metabolic rates than lean adults (more tissue to feed), but lower metabolic rates per unit lean body mass. The Peatian framing of obesity as a low-metabolism state has to be reconciled with this data, and the reconciliation is not explicit in his work.
- **Pediatric metabolism.** Children run substantially higher metabolic rates than adults in proportion to body mass. The Peat-canonical signs of "good metabolism" in adults (pulse ~85, temperature ~98.6°F) are floor levels for healthy children. The thresholds and signs that distinguish a healthy high metabolism from an over-stressed one in a child are not laid out.
- **Aging and metabolic decline.** Whether the decline in metabolic rate with age is intrinsic (mitochondrial wear, [[cardiolipin]] substitution, accumulated [[lipid-peroxidation]]) or extrinsic (PUFA loading, environmental stress, hormonal shifts) is partially addressed by Peat but not fully resolved. The Peatian position leans heavily on the extrinsic, but the intrinsic contribution is non-zero.
- **The hyperthyroid endpoint.** At very high thyroid doses, the apparent benefits of higher metabolism reverse: muscle wasting, bone loss, atrial fibrillation, anxiety. Where the inverted-U turns is not specified quantitatively, and the relationship between iatrogenic hyperthyroidism (Peat's caution: rare in his clinical experience, usually a result of insufficient food rather than too much thyroid) and the Graves-disease endogenous hyperthyroidism (a distinct autoimmune phenomenon) is not fully developed.
- **The chronic-low-grade-inflammation literature.** Modern metabolic medicine increasingly frames "metabolic syndrome" as a chronic low-grade inflammatory state with adipose tissue as the principal source. The relationship between this framing and Peat's stress-cascade framing is partly overlapping (both invoke cortisol, endotoxin, free fatty acids) and partly distinct (Peat does not center adipocyte dysfunction as the originating site). The integration is not worked out.
- **Quantitative thresholds.** The framework is qualitative throughout. How much thyroid is "enough," how much sugar is "adequate," how low temperature has to fall before action is required, how high pulse can rise before the reading is stress rather than health — none are quantified beyond rough rules of thumb.
