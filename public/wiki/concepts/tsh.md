# TSH (Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone)

TSH is the most relied-upon blood test in modern thyroid medicine and, in Ray Peat's view, one of the most thoroughly misunderstood. Mainstream endocrinology treats a TSH reading within the laboratory "normal range" as proof that thyroid function is adequate. Peat treated it as proof of almost nothing. The hormone can be suppressed by [[cortisol]], [[adrenaline]], aging, infection, and other stress-related factors that have nothing to do with thyroid sufficiency, which means a person can be profoundly [[hypothyroidism|hypothyroid]] while displaying a perfectly normal TSH. The range that defines normality was never based on healthy people to begin with. "Over a period of several years, I never saw a person whose TSH was over 2 microunits per milliliter who was comfortably healthy," Peat wrote in his [[tsh-temperature-pulse-rate-and-other-indicators-in-hypothyroidism|"TSH, Temperature, Pulse Rate, and Other Indicators in Hypothyroidism" (2007)]] newsletter, "and I formed the impression that the normal, or healthy, quantity was probably something less than 1.0." Beyond its diagnostic inadequacy, TSH is itself a pathogenic substance, circulating through the body stimulating inflammation, calcifying blood vessels, and promoting tumor growth in tissues far removed from the thyroid gland.

## The "Normal Range" Scandal

The story of how dangerously high TSH levels came to be defined as normal begins not with the TSH test itself but with the blood test it replaced. Until the Second World War, [[hypothyroidism]] was diagnosed on the basis of basal metabolic rate and a large constellation of clinical signs and symptoms. In the late 1940s, the protein-bound iodine (PBI) test was promoted in the United States, and it redrew the entire map of thyroid disease. As Peat recounted in his [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud|"Thyroid" (2007)]] newsletter: "In the late 1940s, promotion of the (biologically inappropriate) PBI blood test in the U.S. led to the concept that only 5% of the population were hypothyroid, and that the 40% identified by 'obsolete' methods were either normal, or suffered from other problems such as sloth and gluttony, or 'genetic susceptibility' to disease."

The PBI test was eventually proven irrelevant to thyroid diagnosis, but the doctrine it created survived. When the TSH assay arrived as a replacement, the new measurements were fitted to the old 5% figure rather than to biological reality. Peat observed that "to make the new TSH measurements fit the medical doctrine, an 8- or 10-fold variation in the hormone was defined as 'normal.' With any other biological measurement, such as erythrocyte count, blood pressure, body weight, or serum sodium, calcium, chloride, or glucose, a variation of 10 to 20 percent from the mean is considered to be meaningful." An eightfold range was accepted because questioning it would have meant acknowledging that a third or more of the population might need thyroid treatment.

In 2003, the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists narrowed the normal range to 0.3 to 3.0 microunits per milliliter, but even the revised standard, Peat wrote, "still isn't based on an understanding of the physiological meaning of TSH." The patients who fell between old and new estimates were never told their diagnosis had been revoked on administrative grounds. "They were taught to inform their patients that the traditional symptoms that were identified as hypothyroidism before 1950 were the result of the patients' own behavior," Peat wrote, "or that the problems were imaginary (women's hormonal and neurological problems, especially), or that they were simply mysterious diseases and defects (recurring infections, arthritis, and cancer, for example)."

## TSH Is Itself Pathogenic

The argument for suppressing TSH does not rest solely on what it fails to tell you about [[thyroid]] function. It rests on what TSH itself does to the body when it remains elevated. In a [[2011-02-22-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-the-thyroid|2011 *EastWest Healing* episode on the thyroid]], Peat stated that "TSH creates all sorts of inflammatory processes. When you have enough actual thyroid hormone to completely shut down your pituitary, you turn off practically all of these toxic inflammatory processes." In a [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on Hashimoto's]], he confirmed that "it has been well established in the last several years that TSH itself causes many of the symptoms blamed on hypothyroidism. It acts on the bone marrow and many other tissues, including the liver, increasing inflammatory cytokine chemicals."

The cardiovascular dimension is particularly alarming. In a [[2019-08-20-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-vitamin-d-thyroid-evolving-consciously|2019 *One Radio Network* episode]], Peat described TSH as working in the same direction as [[parathyroid-hormone|parathyroid hormone]], "calcifying the blood vessels and increasing blood pressure." The 2007 newsletter extended the picture: "TSH has direct actions on many cell types other than the thyroid, and probably contributes directly to edema, fibrosis, and mastocytosis." Fibroblasts, fat cells, pigment cells, mast cells, and bone marrow cells all respond to TSH. In email exchanges on blood tests, Peat noted that "since TSH increases IL-18, finding it elevated would be another argument for keeping your TSH very low." The cancer implications follow directly: "Since TSH produces reactions in cells as different as fibroblasts and fat cells, pigment cells in the skin, mast cells and bone marrow cells, it won't be surprising if it turns out to have a role in the development of a variety of cancers, including melanoma." If a pathologically high TSH is defined as normal, its contribution to breast cancer, mastalgia, multiple sclerosis, fibrotic diseases, and epilepsy will simply be ignored.

## A Stress Indicator, Not a Thyroid Indicator

Peat argued that TSH is at least as much an indicator of stress as it is of thyroid deficiency, and that the two cannot be distinguished from the test result alone. "Stress creates more adrenaline and cortisol. These hormones will suppress the TSH," he explained in the 2019 *One Radio Network* episode. "This makes TSH useless as an indicator of your thyroid status because it's just as much an indicator of your stress status." The catalogue of non-thyroid suppressors is extensive: "Aging, infection, trauma, prolonged cortisol excess, [[somatostatin]], [[dopamine]] or L-dopa, adrenaline, amphetamine, [[caffeine]] and fever can lower TSH, apart from the effect of feedback by the thyroid hormones, creating a situation in which TSH can appear normal or low, at the same time that there is a real hypothyroidism."

The nighttime pattern illustrates how TSH participates in stress physiology rather than merely reflecting it. "During the night, at the time adrenaline and [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] are at their highest, TSH usually reaches its peak," Peat observed. "TSH itself can produce lipolysis, raising the level of circulating free fatty acids." The stress-induced TSH rise mobilizes fats that themselves suppress thyroid function at the cellular level, creating a self-reinforcing loop in which the pituitary signal designed to increase thyroid output actually worsens the metabolic situation. Diseases can produce similar masking: Peat cited Parkinson's as a case where neurological illness conceals hypothyroidism, so a doctor seeing a "normal" TSH would never suspect the thyroid deficiency that, if corrected, might alter the course of the illness.

## What TSH Should Be

What TSH level did Peat consider acceptable? His position grew increasingly firm: as close to zero as possible. In email exchanges on [[blood-tests|blood tests]], he stated that "people with TSH below 0.4 were the freest from thyroid cancer." In a [[2019-09-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fascinating-insights-into-mr-thyroid|2019 *One Radio Network* episode]], he went further: "I think it's good to have close to zero on TSH, as long as everything else is going smoothly, stable blood sugar, feeling good, sound sleep."

The objection that suppressing TSH causes osteoporosis was an argument Peat considered backwards. In the 2011 *EastWest Healing* episode, he addressed it: "Doctors often argue that suppressing TSH causes osteoporosis, but the evidence is the opposite. Thyrotropin (TSH), like [[estrogen]], increases osteoprotegerin, which increases the movement of calcium into your arteries and heart and out of the bones." TSH participates in the same vascular calcification and bone demineralization pattern that characterizes aging. The longevity evidence confirms it: "If people are concerned about the effects of a TSH 'deficiency,'" Peat wrote, "then I think they have to explain the remarkable longevity of the animals lacking pituitaries in W.D. Denckla's experiments, or of the naturally pituitary deficient dwarf mice that lack TSH, [[prolactin]], and [[growth-hormone|growth hormone]], but live about a year longer than normal mice. Until there is evidence that very low TSH is somehow harmful, there is no basis for setting a lower limit to the normal range."

George Washington Crile understood the principle decades earlier. As Peat recounted: "He understood that TSH was driving towards thyroiditis and cancer. He would give usually three or four grains of Armour thyroid, sufficient to lower the TSH to zero."

## TSH as Tumor Promoter

Peat considered TSH analogous to the way excessive [[FSH]] (follicle-stimulating hormone) promotes ovarian cancer. "Just as excessive FSH has been shown to have a role in ovarian cancer, excessive stimulation by TSH produces disorganization in the thyroid gland," he wrote in the [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud|"Thyroid" (2007)]] newsletter. For thyroid cancer specifically, TSH suppression is already accepted medical practice in certain contexts: "Some types of thyroid cancer can usually be controlled by keeping TSH completely suppressed."

The breast cancer connection runs through hypothyroidism. In email exchanges, Peat identified the chain: "Hypothyroidism is a major cause of breast cancer, because of the chronic excess of estrogen and deficiency of [[progesterone]]." US doctors compound the problem because "they don't prescribe the active hormone T3, only the precursor T4, which fails to be converted because hypothyroid women's livers aren't efficient." Elevated TSH signals ongoing hypothyroidism, which means ongoing estrogen excess, which means ongoing cancer risk.

## The Hashimoto's Feedback Loop

The interaction between TSH and [[hashimotos|Hashimoto's thyroiditis]] illustrates how an elevated TSH perpetuates the very damage it is supposed to correct. TSH drives the thyroid gland harder when thyroid output is insufficient, but the harder driving produces inflammation and tissue damage. "Exactly the same TSH excess is produced by a deficiency of iodine," Peat explained in the 2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode. "So, what you typically see is a rising TSH driving the gland to work harder as the organism shows signs of decreasing thyroid function." [[Estrogen]] compounds the problem by causing the pituitary to produce more TSH while simultaneously blocking the thyroid gland's ability to secrete its hormones, resulting in goiter.

The autoimmune component follows from the tissue damage. In the [[2019-09-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fascinating-insights-into-mr-thyroid|2019 *One Radio Network* episode]], Peat described the sequence: "The autoimmune condition is an excited, inefficient state. If TSH keeps rising, it drives the gland harder until it causes thyroiditis. The immune system comes in to clean up the damaged cells. If you supplement enough thyroid to lower the TSH, you stop producing the antibodies. The condition corrects itself after several months." The antibodies are not the cause of the disease; they are the cleanup crew responding to TSH-driven tissue destruction. Remove the stimulus by suppressing TSH with adequate thyroid supplementation, and the immune attack resolves on its own.

## The T4-Only Problem

Many patients whose doctors "treat the TSH" are treated with thyroxine (T4) alone. Peat considered this fundamentally flawed. T4 is the precursor hormone; the active form is triiodothyronine ([[t3|T3]]). The liver converts T4 to T3 under normal conditions, but [[estrogen]] impairs this conversion, and hypothyroid women's livers are already compromised. As Peat explained in the 2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode: "If the liver isn't producing T3, and you give that person big doses of thyroxine, you suppress the TSH. This turns off the little bit of active hormone coming out of their thyroid gland, and they go into an increasingly hypothyroid state."

The result is a clinical absurdity. "I have spoken to several people who told me that their doctors had diagnosed them as 'both hypothyroid and hyperthyroid,'" Peat wrote. "I think biology should occupy a world in which things are not simultaneously themselves and their opposites." High-dose T4 suppresses TSH (making the test look hyperthyroid) while failing to provide adequate T3 (leaving the patient functionally hypothyroid). Peat acknowledged that treating the TSH, even with T4 alone, is "not entirely useless" because suppressing TSH removes an inflammatory stimulus. The problem is that the patient remains undertreated while appearing, by the numbers, to be adequately treated.

## Temperature and Pulse: The Real Standard

Peat's alternative was the same standard that prevailed before 1950: direct measurement of metabolic activity. "The best single test would be the Basal Metabolic Rate from the 1930s, measuring how much oxygen you breathe in and how much carbon dioxide you exhale," he said in the 2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode. "Temperature is just a rough approximation of how much fuel you're burning." [[broda-barnes|Broda Barnes]] argued that waking body temperature was the best practical substitute. Peat refined the method: the combination of pulse rate and temperature, measured at multiple points in the day, is far more informative than either alone.

The critical insight is that low-thyroid people often run on high [[adrenaline]], which masks their deficit. "Low thyroid people often run on high adrenaline, which rises during the night," Peat explained in the [[2019-09-17-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fascinating-insights-into-mr-thyroid|2019 *One Radio Network* episode]]. "Adrenaline speeds your pulse at dawn; cortisol raises the temperature. So you might wake up with a normal 75 pulse and 98-degree temperature. But food lowers your adrenaline and cortisol. Often, these people will drop to a 55 or 60 pulse rate and 96 or 97-degree temperature at 11:00 AM. That tells you they are super low thyroid." A drop in temperature or pulse after a meal reveals the true metabolic rate that stress hormones were concealing. Peat considered a midday pulse of 80 to 85 and a temperature of 98 to 98.6 degrees to be indicators of adequate function.

The Achilles tendon reflex, a functional sign from the pre-blood-test era, remains valid. As Peat described in his [[thyroid-misconceptions|"Thyroid: Misconceptions" (1993)]] newsletter: "Most physicians know how to test the Achilles tendon reflex, but believe it is a poor indicator of thyroid function. Often, this is because they have observed the reflex contraction itself, rather than the relaxation rate, which gives a clear indication of the rate at which energy is regenerated in the muscle." A hypothyroid muscle contracts normally but relaxes slowly. In a [[2009-04-01-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-thyroid-polyunsaturated-fats-and-oils|2009 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode]], Peat noted that this test was abandoned because "the labs and pharmaceutical companies couldn't sell anything."

## Beyond the Pituitary Model

The model that places TSH at the center of thyroid regulation rests on the assumption that the pituitary is the "master gland." Peat rejected this framing. "Many things, including the liver and the senses, regulate the function of the thyroid system, and the pituitary is just one of the factors affecting the synthesis and secretion of the thyroid hormones," he wrote in the 2007 newsletter. In the 2011 *EastWest Healing* episode, he described what happens when you remove the pituitary entirely: animals given exogenous thyroid "live ten times as long as normal, usually about twice as long as normal for the lack of the pituitary gland, as long as they had adequate thyroid." Without TSH, growth hormone, and prolactin, animals age dramatically more slowly. Some patients whose pituitaries appeared functionally absent recovered full function "within a few days of eating more protein and fruit," suggesting the apparent endocrine failure was nutritional suppression, not permanent damage.

If blood tests are used at all, Peat considered [[cholesterol]], [[albumin]], [[glucose]], [[sodium]], lactate, and [[transthyretin]] more physiologically revealing than TSH alone. Cholesterol rises in hypothyroidism because [[t3|T3]] is required for its rapid conversion into steroid hormones. [[Lactic-acid|Lactic acid]] accumulates when cells rely on glycolysis rather than oxidative metabolism. Transthyretin, which carries both vitamin A and thyroid hormones, "is sharply decreased by stress, and should probably be regularly measured as part of the thyroid examination." Even the "free hormone" measurements that dominate modern endocrinology have problems. Peat considered the concept "purely a laboratory construction," noting that "when thyroid hormone is stuck to albumin protein in the blood, it has no trouble getting into cells." For the brain and some other organs, the protein-bound hormone is the active form. If forced to choose a minimal panel, Peat recommended free T3 and TSH, but qualified: "Judging by symptoms, temperature, and pulse rate is really the basic thing."

## The Therapeutic Trial

Peat's position was that pre-1950 clinical methods remain the most valid standard for thyroid diagnosis. "Unless someone can demonstrate the scientific invalidity of the methods used to diagnose hypothyroidism up to 1945," he wrote in the [[thyroid-therapies-confusion-and-fraud|"Thyroid" (2007)]] newsletter, "then they constitute the best present evidence for evaluating hypothyroidism, because all of the blood tests that have been used since 1950 have been shown to be, at best, very crude and conceptually inappropriate methods."

The definitive diagnostic tool was the therapeutic trial. "If the patient's symptoms disappeared as his temperature and pulse rate and food intake were normalized, the diagnostic hypothesis was confirmed. It was common to begin therapy with one or two grains of thyroid, and to adjust the dose according to the patient's response." Peat emphasized that supplementation should be taken in small divided amounts with meals, and that temperature and pulse should be measured at waking, midday, and afternoon.

A common source of confusion is the pattern in which the patient initially improves and then appears to plateau. "When the body has accumulated a steady level of the hormones, and begun to function more fully, the factors such as adrenaline that have been chronically distorted to compensate for hypothyroidism will have begun to normalize, and the early effects of the supplementary thyroid will in many cases seem to disappear, with heart rate and temperature declining." The dose often has to be increased several times as the stress hormones withdraw and reveal the true depth of the deficit. The need for increasing doses is a sign of progress, not overtreatment. Every patient whose hypothyroidism goes uncorrected because their TSH falls within an artificially inflated range is a patient whose infections, arthritis, cancer, or heart disease will be treated as separate problems rather than consequences of a single correctable deficiency.
