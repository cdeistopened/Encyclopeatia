# Image and Perception

The textbook nervous system runs outward. A stimulus arrives at a sensory receptor, climbs through ascending pathways to a primary cortex, is processed, and triggers a descending motor program that produces a behavior. The organism is, in this picture, a reflex arc with a long detour through the brain — input, computation, output. The interesting variables sit on the output side: which motor patterns are wired in, which can be conditioned, which can be triggered by pharmacological or electrical manipulation of the right node. The image of the world the organism builds along the way is treated as epiphenomenal, a residue of the processing rather than its purpose.

Ray Peat rejected this framing root and branch. His first book, *Mind and Tissue: Russian Research Perspectives on the Human Brain* (1976), opened by arguing for the inverse picture inherited from the post-Pavlovian Russian tradition. The nervous system is not primarily a motor-program generator. It is an *image-builder*. The afferent system — the assembling of perceptual gestalts from sensory flow, memory, and the organism's current metabolic state — is the primary nervous-system function, and behavior is what falls out when an image is held against the organism's current possibilities. The motor side is the easy part, the part that follows once the perception is settled. The hard part, and the part the orthodox picture systematically obscures, is the perception.

The Russian word for this organizing unit is *obraz* — closer to "gestalt" or "figured whole" than to "image" in the narrow visual sense. An *obraz* is a perceptual unity that integrates across modalities, across memory, across the body's current state, and across the field of possibilities the environment offers. Peat absorbed the concept from Pyotr Anokhin, Nikolai Bernstein, Lev Vygotsky, and the broader tradition that built on Pavlov but went well past him, and he made it the central unit of his own framework. To understand what Peat believed the brain was doing, you have to understand that he believed it was building *obrazy* — and that he believed everything else, including the orthodoxies of mid-twentieth-century neuroscience and pharmacology, followed from getting the unit of analysis right.

## The Soviet *obraz* concept

The English-language nervous-system literature inherited from Pavlov a simplified picture — conditioned reflexes, stimulus and response, the dog and the bell. What the Russian tradition actually developed in the two generations after Pavlov was something considerably more sophisticated, and it is this developed tradition that *Mind and Tissue* reconstructs for an English audience. Peat's recurring complaint about Western psychology was that the Pavlov it inherited was a caricature — a behaviorist Pavlov — and that the post-Pavlovian Russians had been doing work that mainstream American behaviorism had no apparatus to recognize.

Anokhin's *theory of functional systems* is the load-bearing piece. In Anokhin's account a behavior is not a chain of stimulus-response links; it is the resolution of a functional system that organizes itself around an *anticipated result* — a future *obraz* the organism is working to bring about. The system assembles its afferent inputs (current sensory state), its memory (previous *obrazy* of similar situations), and its current metabolic-emotional state into a *forecast* of what the situation requires, and only then does it select a motor pattern. The motor pattern is downstream. What organizes the behavior is the anticipated perceptual completion — the *obraz* the organism is reaching for. Behavior is the closing of a perceptual loop, not the opening of one.

Bernstein's work on movement made the same point from the motor side. Studying skilled action — blacksmiths swinging hammers, surgeons tying knots — Bernstein showed that the actual motor commands varied wildly across repetitions of "the same" skilled act. What stayed constant was not a stored motor program but the perceptual *target* — the *obraz* of the completed action — and the system improvised whatever motor commands were needed to bring the current state into match with the target. The reflex-arc picture, which treated motor commands as stored sequences triggered by stimuli, simply could not account for the data. The *obraz* was prior; the motor commands were assembled in real time to satisfy it.

Vygotsky's developmental psychology added the dimension of *internalization*. The child first acts on a perceived external situation; later the situation is reconstructed internally as an *obraz*, and the child acts on the internal reconstruction. The mature cognitive system runs on internalized *obrazy* that have been built up from a lifetime of perceptual interactions. Thinking, in this picture, is not symbol-manipulation; it is the construction and manipulation of perceptual gestalts that incorporate the organism's full history.

What Peat took from all of this — and what *Mind and Tissue* presents as the framework's central thesis — is that the brain is best understood as a system whose primary job is to build, hold, and update *obrazy*. Memory is integrated into perception (see [[memory|*Memory*]]) because every perception draws on the residue of past perceptions to make sense of the current input. Consciousness is integrated into perception (see [[consciousness|*Consciousness*]]) because the *obraz* the organism is currently holding *is* what consciousness is at that moment. Action follows from the *obraz*. The motor side is the implementation; the perception is the work.

## Afferent primacy versus motor-program primacy

The orthodox twentieth-century neuroscience that Peat opposed had a different central commitment. Whether in the Sherringtonian reflex-arc tradition, the Hebbian connectionist tradition, the behaviorist stimulus-response tradition, or the contemporary computational-neuroscience tradition with its emphasis on motor cortex and basal ganglia "circuits," the load-bearing claim has been that the brain is fundamentally a *motor-program generator*. The interesting variables are on the output side. Sensory input is the trigger; cognition is the routing; the behavior is what the system produces.

Peat read this commitment as a category error inherited from the engineering metaphors of the 1940s and 1950s — the cybernetic and information-theoretic frames that took feedback loops and signal channels as the basic units. From [[a-biophysical-approach-to-altered-consciousness|*A Biophysical Approach to Altered Consciousness*]] (1975): "Shannon's digitized computational and communication approach to information was formally similar not only to the telegraphic theory of consciousness, but to a reductionist approach to being itself, in which the units of matter, atoms, have no individual history." A theory of brain that treated neurons as switching elements and behavior as the output of the switching circuit could not even pose the question of what the organism was perceiving — perception was not a category the framework supplied. The *obraz* dropped out before the analysis began.

The afferent-primacy alternative is straightforward to state and difficult to overstate the implications of. The brain's primary work is the assembly of *obrazy*; the motor system is the implementer; what we call "behavior" is the trace of *obrazy* being satisfied. The orienting reflex — Pavlov's *what-is-it* response, treated as central by Sokolov and the post-Pavlovian Russian tradition — is the canonical operation of the afferent system. The organism encounters novelty; it assembles an *obraz* of the novelty; the *obraz* organizes the organism's posture, attention, and subsequent action. The orienting reflex is not a behavior to be conditioned. It is the operation by which the perceptual system constitutes the field within which conditioning could happen. See [[orienting-reflex]].

The implications for what counts as evidence about brain function are substantial. The orthodox tradition takes behavioral output as the dependent variable and varies experimental input until the output changes; the *obraz*-primary tradition takes perception as the dependent variable and asks what the organism is now perceiving. The first paradigm generates the rat-in-Skinner-box literature; the second generates the literature on perceptual illusions, on phantom limb, on the conditions under which an organism *fails to perceive* what is in front of it. Peat's complaint with American behaviorism was that it took a single methodologically convenient slice — the output side — and built a theory of the whole organism around what that slice would let it measure. The *obraz* was invisible to the apparatus, so the apparatus declared the *obraz* not to exist.

This is also where the integration with the rest of Peat's bioenergetic framework happens. An *obraz* is not a free-floating mental object; it is a state of the whole organism, requiring the structured cytoplasmic water, the cooperative protein-lipid lattice, the CO2-buffered proteins, and the patterned electron flow that the metabolic apparatus makes possible. A hypothyroid brain does not build *obrazy* well — its proteins are wrong-shaped, its electron flow is sluggish, its cooperative behavior is degraded. The narrowed perception, the foreclosed sense of possibility, the inability to take in the field that depressed patients describe is, in Peat's reading, exactly what a deficient *obraz*-building apparatus produces. See [[consciousness]] for the full energetic substrate; the point here is that *obraz* and metabolism are not separable.

## The rooster aggression electrode experiment reinterpreted

The cleanest empirical handle on the framework's distinctive prediction is also one of Peat's favorite reinterpretations. In the 1960s and 1970s electrode-stimulation experiments on a variety of animals — most famously José Delgado's work on bulls, but also extensive work on roosters, cats, and rats — appeared to localize specific behaviors to specific brain nuclei. Stimulate the right spot in the hypothalamus and a rooster attacks. Stimulate the wrong spot and it freezes. The orthodox interpretation, embraced by Delgado himself and broadcast widely in the popular press, was that the brain contained "aggression circuits," "fear circuits," "pleasure centers," and so on, each implementing a specific motor program that electrical stimulation could trigger.

Peat read the same data through the *obraz* framework and got a different answer. The electrode was not triggering a stored behavior. It was producing a perceptual disturbance — a pain, a memory fragment, a hallucinated stimulus — and the rooster was then *responding* to the disturbance in the way roosters respond to such things, which is to attack a nearby target. The rooster was behaving normally with respect to its altered perception. It was not the case that an aggression program had been activated; it was the case that an aggressive *situation* had been hallucinated, and the rooster was doing what a rooster does in an aggressive situation.

The diagnostic feature of the reinterpretation is its prediction about what changes when the experimental conditions change. If the orthodox circuit theory is right, the rooster should attack regardless of what is in front of it — the program is in the electrode-stimulated nucleus. If the *obraz* theory is right, the rooster should attack whatever target makes sense in the current perceptual field — the response should depend on what is available to attack. The experimental record favors the latter. Stimulated roosters attack the most plausible nearby target; they do not attack inanimate objects in preference to other roosters; they will redirect their attack as the experimenter moves potential targets around. The behavior is intelligent with respect to the situation, not blindly programmed. What the electrode did was alter the perceptual input the brain was working with; the brain's response was the normal response to the altered perception.

The same reinterpretation applies to Penfield's classical cortical-stimulation findings (treated in [[memory|*Memory*]] from a different angle). Stimulating the temporal cortex of awake epileptic patients produced vivid, often repeatable recall of specific scenes — and the orthodox reading took this as evidence that the scene was *stored* in the stimulated cortex. Peat's reading was that the stimulation was producing a perceptual disturbance — a hallucinated re-experience — and the patient was reporting what they were now experiencing. The cortex was not playing back a recorded file; it was being induced, by the electrical disturbance, to assemble an *obraz* of a remembered scene. Penfield himself, late in life, came to a similar conclusion: he became an interactionist, denied that memory was localized in the cortex, and suggested that memory might exist as a field with which the brain interacts. The data fit the *obraz* framework cleanly. They fit the storage framework only with substantial auxiliary assumptions about indexing, addressing, and retrieval that the storage framework has never managed to specify mechanistically.

The pain-memory-hallucination triangulation is the unifying read. Electrical stimulation of cortex does one of three things — produces pain, produces a memory fragment, or produces a hallucinated percept — and the organism then responds to whatever it now perceives. There is no separate library of stored behaviors to be activated; there is only the perceptual field, which the stimulation perturbs, and the organism's normal response to the perturbed field. The "aggression circuit" reading is a reification of the experimenter's behavioral expectation. The *obraz* reading puts the organism's perceptual process back at the center.

## Why "behavior" requires "image"

The deeper claim is that the concept of behavior, taken in isolation from perception, does not actually pick out a coherent unit of analysis. Behaviorism's foundational move — to study the organism without reference to its inner states — fails not on philosophical grounds but on methodological ones. The same motor output means different things in different perceptual contexts; the "same" perception produces different motor outputs depending on what the organism takes the situation to require. The unit that holds these together is the *obraz* — the organism's working perceptual gestalt of the situation, which organizes both the inputs that count as relevant and the outputs that count as appropriate.

Peat treated this as a clinically important point, not just a philosophical one. From [[thought-and-energy-mood-and-metabolism|*Thought and Energy, Mood and Metabolism*]] (October 2001): "The ability to perceive one's environment and adapt to it depends on having one's metabolic requirements met, and these chemical and physical issues are usually overlooked by psychiatrists, just as the issues of 'meaning' may be overlooked by the physiologists. The ability to perceive possibilities, and to act on those perceptions, requires biological resources, especially a high level of energy, but there must also be real possibilities in the environment." The patient's *behavior* is downstream of the patient's *perception of possibility*, and the perception of possibility is itself downstream of the metabolic apparatus that builds *obrazy*. Treating the behavior without addressing the perception is treating the symptom; treating the perception without addressing the metabolism is treating the surface.

This is what gives [[learned-helplessness]] its philosophical shape in the Peat framework. The helpless rat is not a rat with a damaged motor system; it is a rat whose *obraz*-building apparatus has been routed into a perceptual gestalt of futility. The single experience of escape — Richter's vaccination effect, the rat that watched another rat escape and could then swim for days — does not install a new motor program. It installs a new *obraz* of the situation, in which escape is now perceived as possible, and the motor system follows. The cognitive-behavioral therapy literature gets at the same point from the opposite side: changing what the patient *perceives* about the situation produces changes in behavior that no amount of motor retraining can achieve. The *obraz* is the lever.

## Implications for pharmacology

If the brain is fundamentally an *obraz*-building organ, then drugs that target specific neurotransmitter receptors are, in Peat's framework, acting on a fundamentally wrong unit of analysis. The receptor-targeted-drug paradigm assumes that a specific behavior or mood follows from the activity of a specific neurotransmitter at a specific receptor — block or activate the receptor and you change the behavior. The framework that gives this paradigm its plausibility is the motor-program-primary framework: behaviors are stored as circuits, circuits run on specific neurotransmitter systems, and pharmacology hacks the circuit at the synapse.

The *obraz* framework predicts that this should not work cleanly, and the half-century clinical record bears the prediction out. SSRIs do not reliably treat depression in the way the receptor model predicts; antipsychotics do not abolish the underlying perceptual disturbance, only blunt the patient's responsiveness to it; anxiolytics produce dependence without resolving the perceptual situation that produced the anxiety. The drugs work, when they work, by perturbing the organism's general state — sedating, blunting, exciting, suppressing — and the patient's perceptual system then assembles a different *obraz* of the situation given the altered state. The improvement is real but mediated, and the route is through the *obraz*, not through the circuit.

Peat's specific complaint with the SSRI literature is treated in detail at [[serotonin]] and [[learned-helplessness]]. The point here is the more general one. The receptor-targeted-drug paradigm rests on a substrate-content distinction (the drug acts on the substrate, the behavior is the content) that the *obraz* framework rejects. If the perception and the metabolism are not separable — if the *obraz* and the cellular state are the same thing under different descriptions — then drugs that perturb the metabolic state are perturbing the *obraz*-building apparatus, and the clinical effects are mediated by changes in perception rather than by triggering stored behaviors. The "neurochemical imbalance" picture of mental illness is a particular case of the broader category error.

The metabolic alternative follows directly. If the *obraz*-building apparatus depends on adequate thyroid, sugar, CO2, and structured cytoplasmic water, then the route to better perception is metabolic restoration rather than receptor manipulation. The cognitive symptoms — narrowed perception, foreclosed possibility, the inability to take in the field — are not symptoms of a circuit defect to be corrected pharmacologically; they are symptoms of an *obraz*-builder running on inadequate substrate, and the substrate is what needs the intervention. See [[thyroid]], [[progesterone]], [[carbon-dioxide]].

## Implications for psychology

The mirror-image implication runs through psychology. If behavior is downstream of *obraz*, then psychological theories that take behavior as the dependent variable — behaviorism, behavior-modification therapies, the broad applied-behavior-analysis tradition — are working on the wrong level. The interesting variable is what the patient is *perceiving*, and the route to change is through the perception. Cognitive-behavioral therapy gets at this partially by addressing the patient's explicit beliefs about the situation, but the framework is incomplete: an *obraz* is more than a belief, it is a whole-organism perceptual gestalt with metabolic, emotional, memorial, and somatic components, and treating it as a corrigible cognition misses most of what it is.

Peat's positive proposal — never developed into a treatment manual but legible across the corpus — was that psychology should be a *perceptual* discipline. The therapist's job is to widen the patient's *obraz* of the situation; the patient's job is to build perceptual capacity through metabolic restoration and through deliberate exposure to situations that require new perceptual integrations. The orienting reflex is the natural mechanism of this — the *what-is-it* response that interrupts a settled *obraz* and forces the assembly of a new one. Environments rich in novelty (literal novelty, not the manufactured novelty of consumer media) feed the orienting reflex; environments stripped of novelty starve it. The familiar Peat recommendations — meaningful work, unfamiliar food, time outdoors, conversation across difference, attention to one's own body's signals — are not lifestyle advice but operations on the *obraz*-building apparatus.

The connection to [[learned-helplessness]] makes this concrete. The helpless patient has settled into an *obraz* of the situation in which no action matters. The recovery levers (T3, sugar, salt, light, social contact, environmental enrichment) all act on the *obraz* — they restore the metabolic substrate, expose the patient to perceptual situations that demand new integrations, and let the assembly of a different *obraz* of the situation become possible. The cognitive-behavioral framing captures part of this but locates the change in beliefs about the situation rather than in the perceptual gestalt that the beliefs are part of. The *obraz* framing has more room. It can hold the metabolic, the perceptual, the memorial, and the somatic in one unit.

## Protocol attachment

The framework's practical recommendations follow directly. To build a healthy *obraz*-building apparatus you need:

**Metabolic substrate.** [[Thyroid]], sugar, salt, light, [[progesterone]], adequate CO2, suppression of [[pufa|PUFA]] and [[estrogen]]. The apparatus that builds *obrazy* runs on the same oxidative metabolism that runs everything else; the cognitive consequences of metabolic deficit are deficit *obrazy* — narrowed, futility-prone, foreclosed.

**Perceptual training.** Deliberate practice in attending to fields wider than the habitual one. The classical mnemonic disciplines (method of loci, deliberate sensory observation), the slow looking of art-history training, the close listening of musical training, the close attention to bodily signal that somatic practices cultivate — all are operations on the perceptual side rather than on the motor side, and all build the capacity to assemble richer *obrazy*. Peat himself was an inveterate close observer: light, color, the body's signals, the texture of conversation, the small physical signs of metabolic state in himself and others. The discipline is trainable.

**Environmental enrichment.** Variety, novelty, and complexity in the environment feed the orienting reflex; sameness and impoverishment starve it. The University of California enrichment experiments showed that rats in larger, more varied environments developed thicker cortices, more cholinesterase, and resistance to learned helplessness; the human version is the choice-rich environment of meaningful work, real conversation, useful labor, and exposure to natural complexity. Peat's recurring complaint about modern life — that it has been engineered to eliminate meaningful choices for most people — is a complaint at the level of the perceptual apparatus.

**Deliberate cultivation of unfamiliar stimuli.** The orienting reflex is fed by novelty, and novelty fades into familiarity unless the organism actively seeks out new perceptual situations. Travel, unfamiliar food, conversation across difference, learning a new craft, taking up a new language — these all act as orienting-reflex feedback, keeping the *obraz*-building apparatus exercised. The link to [[orienting-reflex]] is direct: the orienting reflex is the operation by which a settled *obraz* is interrupted and a new one assembled, and a system that gets to exercise this operation regularly remains capable of it under stress.

**Perception-first framing of learned helplessness.** The clinical implication for depressed, helpless, or chronically stressed patients is that the route in is through the perception rather than through the behavior. Restore the metabolic substrate so the *obraz*-building apparatus has what it needs. Expose the patient to perceptual situations that demand new integrations (Richter's vaccination effect, the rat watching another rat escape). Avoid pharmacology that blunts the apparatus further. The behavior will follow when the perception widens. See [[learned-helplessness]] for the full apparatus and [[orienting-reflex]] for the operational lever.

## Counter-arguments

**Motor-cortex orthodoxy.** Decades of motor-cortex mapping (Penfield's homunculus, the Mountcastle columnar tradition, the Georgopoulos population-vector work) have produced detailed maps of motor representation in primary motor cortex and clear evidence that specific motor cortex regions are necessary for specific movements. The *obraz* framework does not deny these findings; it denies that they constitute a complete theory of behavior. Motor cortex is where the *implementation* happens; the *obraz* that organizes the implementation lives in the broader perceptual-cognitive apparatus, and the motor-cortex literature has tended to study the implementation in isolation from the *obraz* it implements. The Bernsteinian observation — that the same skilled act recruits wildly different motor commands across repetitions — remains the framework's clean prediction, and the motor-cortex literature has accommodated it only by pushing the variability into "higher" cognitive regions whose function it has not specified.

**Computational neuroscience and the predictive-coding turn.** Contemporary computational neuroscience has moved partway toward the *obraz* picture without giving it credit. Friston's free-energy principle, the broader predictive-coding literature, and the Bayesian-brain frameworks treat perception as the brain's active construction of a generative model that predicts incoming sensory input. The convergence with the *obraz* framework is partial: both reject the bottom-up reflex-arc picture, both put perception at the center, both treat behavior as downstream of perception. The divergence is on the substrate. The computational literature treats the brain as a computer running predictive algorithms; the *obraz* framework treats the metabolism as the substrate of perception and rejects the algorithm-substrate separation that the computational picture requires. Peat would have said that the computational literature has the structure right and the substrate wrong, and that the substrate matters because the apparatus that builds *obrazy* runs on metabolism rather than on symbol-manipulation.

**Behaviorist holdouts and applied behavior analysis.** Applied behavior analysis remains the dominant framework in autism therapy, certain branches of organizational psychology, and large parts of educational practice. The framework's clinical effectiveness in narrow domains (operant conditioning for specific skill acquisition, token economies in institutional settings) is genuine. The *obraz* framework does not deny that operant principles describe one slice of behavior; it denies that they describe the central organizing principle of nervous-system function. The standard reply to behaviorism — that the organism is doing something between the stimulus and the response, and the something is the interesting part — applies here, and the post-Pavlovian Russian tradition was developing the apparatus to describe that *something* fifty years before American cognitive psychology rediscovered it.

**Neurotransmitter pharmacology as clinical pragmatism.** A defender of receptor-targeted pharmacology might grant that the *obraz* framework is theoretically more complete and still argue that, for practical clinical purposes, drugs that perturb specific neurotransmitter systems produce measurable clinical benefits. The pragmatic defense has force in some cases (acute antipsychotic effects in florid psychosis, benzodiazepine sedation in acute crisis) and limited force in others (the SSRI literature, where the placebo-corrected effects are small and the long-run effects on the underlying *obraz* are mixed). Peat's reply was that the pragmatic defense conceals a long-run cost: the patient on chronic receptor-targeted medication has been routed away from the metabolic interventions that could rebuild the *obraz*-building apparatus, and the chronic suppression of the apparatus produces its own pathology. The pragmatic gain at the level of acute symptom is paid for at the level of long-run perceptual capacity.

**Optogenetic localization of "engrams."** The contemporary engram-mapping literature (Tonegawa, Josselyn) identifies sparse ensembles of hippocampal and amygdala neurons that are necessary and sufficient for the expression of specific learned behaviors in mice — reactivate the ensemble and the mouse expresses the conditioned response; silence it and the response is blocked. This is the strongest empirical case for circuit-level localization of specific behaviors, and the *obraz* framework needs to engage it directly. The Peatian reply, parallel to the reply in [[memory|*Memory*]]: ensemble manipulation works only in conjunction with the brain's intact metabolic state; ensembles may be necessary nodes in the assembly of a specific *obraz* rather than storage sites for a specific behavior; and the framework's prediction that the same ensemble manipulation produces different behaviors in different perceptual contexts has not been systematically tested. The engram-mapping literature has tended to study its preparations in highly stereotyped contexts where the *obraz* variability is suppressed by design.

## Data gaps

**The full Russian tradition has not been adequately translated.** Peat read Anokhin, Bernstein, Vygotsky, Bykov, and Filatov in English translations that were partial and dated. The *obraz* framework as it exists in the Russian primary literature is considerably more developed than the version available to English-language readers, and a serious contemporary engagement would require either the original Russian or substantially better translations than now exist. The *Mind and Tissue* reconstruction is the best English-language synthesis on offer, but it is a synthesis filtered through one reader's idiosyncratic interests.

**The *obraz* framework has not been operationalized.** The orthodox motor-program tradition has succeeded partly because it generates testable predictions at the level of single-neuron recordings and computational models. The *obraz* framework's predictions are at the level of whole-organism perceptual gestalts, which are harder to operationalize. A serious contemporary engagement would require methodological apparatus the framework does not currently have — ways of measuring the *obraz* the organism is currently holding that go beyond inference from behavior.

**Integration with the engram-mapping literature.** As with the memory framework, the contemporary optogenetic and chemogenetic ensemble-manipulation literature is the strongest empirical case against the *obraz* framework's specific predictions, and the engagement has not been done at the level of detail it would require. The framework's most distinctive prediction — that ensemble manipulation produces different behaviors in different perceptual contexts — is testable in principle and has not been systematically tested.

**The substrate of the *obraz*.** The framework asserts that the *obraz*-building apparatus runs on metabolism, but the specific molecular events that constitute "building an *obraz*" are not specified at the level the framework would need to compete head-to-head with synaptic-plasticity and predictive-coding models. The structured-water-electron-flow substrate (see [[gilbert-ling]], [[a-biophysical-approach-to-altered-consciousness]]) is a candidate; the specific operations on the substrate that correspond to perceptual integration are not worked out.

**Cross-cultural and developmental data.** The framework predicts that perceptual capacity varies systematically with metabolic state across development and across cultures; the prediction is broadly consistent with what is known but has not been tested at the quantitative level. The substantial cross-cultural variation in perceptual style (visual-illusion susceptibility, attention to figure-vs-ground, the holistic-vs-analytic distinction in cognitive psychology) is consistent with the *obraz* framework but underdetermines it.
