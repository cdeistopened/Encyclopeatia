# Xenoestrogens

Environmental estrogen pollution ranks among the most consequential and least acknowledged health crises of the modern era. Ray Peat treated the subject not as a niche environmental concern but as a central illustration of his unified theory of biological harm: all estrogens, whether synthesized by the body, manufactured by pharmaceutical companies, leached from plastic bottles, or concentrated in soybeans, share the same fundamental action. They interfere with oxidative energy production. In his [[the-dire-effects-of-estrogen-pollution|"The Dire Effects of Estrogen Pollution" (2006)]] newsletter, Peat wrote that "estrogenic pollution kills birds, panthers, alligators, old men, young women, fish, seals, babies, and ecosystems," a litany whose breadth was the point. The sources are everywhere, the victims are everything alive, and the mechanism is always the same: a shift away from the efficient use of oxygen toward the stressed, fermentative state that characterizes disease and aging.

The conventional distinction between "strong" and "weak" estrogens suggests that weaker compounds provide a buffer against more potent ones. Peat rejected this framing entirely. In the 1940s, Alexander Lipshuts demonstrated that a continuous, weak estrogenic stimulus was immensely effective in producing first fibromas, then cancer, in one organ after another, and the effect was not limited to the reproductive system. The modern environment, saturated with low-level estrogenic chemicals from plastics, pesticides, food additives, and pharmaceutical runoff, provides exactly the kind of continuous weak exposure that Lipshuts showed to be so dangerous. Adding dietary phytoestrogens from soy on top of this background load does not protect against [[estrogen]]; it deepens the exposure.

## The Oxygen Principle

The unifying mechanism behind all estrogenic harm is interference with cellular respiration. Peat returned to this point throughout decades of writing and interviews, because it explains why substances as chemically different as coal soot, bisphenol A, soy isoflavones, and pharmaceutical estradiol can all produce the same biological consequences: [[inflammation]], [[fibrosis]], and [[cancer]].

"Estrogen's effects, ranging from shock to cancer, all seem to relate to an interference with the use of oxygen," Peat wrote in his [[the-dire-effects-of-estrogen-pollution|"The Dire Effects of Estrogen Pollution" (2006)]] newsletter. "Different estrogens have different affinities for various tissues, and a given substance is likely to have effects other than estrogenicity, and the presence of other substances will modify the way a tissue responds, but the stressful shift away from oxidative energy production is the factor that all estrogens have in common." He posed the question that made the principle unavoidable: "Otherwise, how could suffocation and X-irradiation have estrogenic effects?"

The connection between soot and estrogen was not a modern discovery. In a [[2017-06-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-language-and-criticism-estrogen-part-1|2017 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on estrogen]], Sarah Johanneson Murray noted that chimney sweeps were developing testicular cancer from coal soot as early as the eighteenth century. Peat explained that "the identity of estrogen as the carcinogen wasn't clearly chemically identified until about 1935," when a series of papers demonstrated that "the soot carcinogenesis known from the 18th century was essentially estrogenic, creating inflammation, fibrosis, and cancer." The polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in smoke were among the first synthetic estrogens recognized, and the commercial manufacture of estrogen drugs grew directly out of this research. In a [[2013-07-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-heart-iii|2013 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on heart disease]], Peat reiterated that "the real danger of smoke is the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, which are inflammatory and estrogenic and carcinogenic. Just anything you burn is going to produce some of those."

The oxygen principle also means that any injury or toxin, even one with no direct estrogenic chemistry, can produce estrogenic effects through the inflammation it causes. As Peat stated in a [[2019-05-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-pollution|2019 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on pollution]], "anything injurious creates inflammation, and inflammation leads to the production of estrogen."

## Industrial Estrogens

### Bisphenol A and Plasticizers

[[BPA|Bisphenol A]] is one of the most widespread synthetic estrogenic substances used in plastics. Peat noted in the [[2017-06-16-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-language-and-criticism-estrogen-part-1|2017 estrogen episode]] that BPA has "been known for about 120 years," placing its discovery well before the modern plastics industry made it ubiquitous. In that same discussion, he described the man who developed [[DES|diethylstilbestrol]] in the 1930s as having "warned it shouldn't be used in humans, but drug companies immediately used it as a 'female hormone' to prevent miscarriage, even though it was known to cause miscarriage and cancer." The histories of BPA and DES are intertwined: both emerged from the same era of estrogen chemistry, and both illustrate how commercial interests overrode clear scientific warnings.

The plasticizers used to make rigid plastics more flexible are effective estrogens in very small amounts. In an [[2025-11-28-ask-the-herb-doctor-the-hormones-behind-inflammation|*Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on the hormones behind inflammation]], Peat stated plainly that "all of the water supply of the world is contaminated with those." The contamination pathway runs from food packaging through groundwater into municipal water systems. BPA crosses the placental barrier, and its effects are most devastating during early development. In a 2012 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hot flashes, Peat described prenatal BPA exposure as "disastrous early in life," explaining that it "shapes the whole development of the organism."

Research Peat cited in his [[radiation-and-growth|"Radiation and Growth" (2011)]] newsletter demonstrated that prenatal or neonatal exposure to extremely small amounts of nonylphenol and bisphenol A greatly increased adult sensitivity to estrogen. This finding is particularly alarming because it means early exposure does not merely cause immediate harm but permanently alters the organism's susceptibility to estrogenic stimulation for the rest of its life. Peat drew the further implication that "the synergy of estrogen and ionizing radiation suggests that early exposure to either could increase adult sensitivity to both."

Temperature affects leaching. Sarah Murray reported in the 2017 episode that a colleague from Oxford University found that cold or frozen plastic water bottles "become brittle and leach much more plastic compounds into the water" than those kept at room temperature, a finding with direct implications for refrigerated food stored in plastic containers.

BPA's effects extend beyond reproductive harm. Peat cited research showing that environmental estrogens such as BPA can exacerbate ventricular arrhythmia, connecting plastic contamination to cardiac disease through the same estrogenic mechanism that produces cancer and infertility elsewhere in the body.

### DES and the Transgenerational Pattern

DES stands as the most thoroughly documented case study in estrogenic harm. Peat described it in his [[estriol-des-ddt-etc|"Estriol, DES, DDT, etc." (2010)]] newsletter as "one of the most notorious estrogens, because studies in humans revealed that its use during pregnancy not only caused cancer, miscarriages, blood clots, etc., in the women who used it, but also caused cancer, infertility, and deformities in their children, and even in their grandchildren." He was careful to add that "those transgenerational effects are not unique to it," a point that elevates DES from a historical cautionary tale to an illustration of what all estrogenic exposures can do across generations.

### Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons

The combustion of any organic material produces polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, compounds that are simultaneously carcinogenic and estrogenic. Peat noted that burning plant material also produces dioxin-type compounds. Cannabis smoke received specific mention: "Known effects of heavy marijuana smoking are definitely estrogenic, but some of that could just be that any kind of smoke is estrogenic." The estrogenic character of smoke explains why populations exposed to industrial emissions, wood fires, or tobacco products show elevated rates of the same diseases associated with pharmaceutical estrogen exposure.

### DDT and Pesticides

Pesticides, chlorinated hydrocarbons, and herbicides persist in the environment and in body tissue, contributing to the cumulative estrogenic burden. Peat's [[the-dire-effects-of-estrogen-pollution|"The Dire Effects of Estrogen Pollution" (2006)]] newsletter noted that "many estrogenic herbicides, pesticides, and industrial by-products persist in the environment, causing infertility, deformed reproductive organs, tumors, and other biological defects, including immunodeficiency." Some of these chemicals are sprayed on forests by the U.S. Department of Agriculture, entering lakes, aquifers, rivers, and oceans.

On glyphosate, Peat recalled in the [[2019-05-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-pollution|2019 pollution episode]] that professors at Oregon State University had been taught that "Roundup is specifically toxic to plants, so it can't hurt animals," a claim he dismissed as ignoring the deep biochemical commonality between plant and animal cells. He noted dryly that "it's not at all surprising that Roundup is extremely toxic to animals too."

## Phytoestrogens and Soy

Plants produce estrogenic compounds as part of their chemical defense system. These phytoestrogens evolved not as pigments but to regulate interactions with other organisms, including repelling insect predators. Their binding to human estrogen receptors is, in Peat's view, "probably misleading, because of their many other effects, including inhibiting enzyme functions involved in the regulation of steroids and [[prostaglandins]]." The biochemistry of phytoestrogens in animals is far more complicated than that of the body's own estrogens, "which is itself so complicated that we can only guess what the consequences might be when we change the concentration and the ratio of substances in that complex system."

### Clover and the Sheep Problem

The estrogenic properties of legumes were first studied when sheep farmers discovered that their flocks miscarried after grazing on clover. Peat found it significant that "this terribly toxic effect has been neglected in recent decades." The clover estrogens had a clear contraceptive action in sheep, and Peat saw them as forerunners of the estrogenic effects later observed in wild animals exposed to industrial pollutants. All legumes share this property, and all parts of the plant contain some of the active chemicals.

### Soy Isoflavones

Soy occupies a special position in Peat's critique because it is actively marketed as a health food while containing multiple compounds that promote the very conditions it supposedly prevents. The isoflavones in soybeans, "many of which are now being promoted as 'antioxidants' and 'cancer preventives,'" are, as Peat wrote in his [[natural-estrogens|"Natural Estrogens" (2008)]] newsletter, "toxic to many organs" with "clear estrogenic effects." They are active immediately in the mature individual and, when present prenatally, "cause feminization of the male genitalia and behavior, and early maturation of the female offspring, with the tissue changes that are known to be associated with increased incidence of cancer."

The estrogenic harm of soy operates through multiple pathways simultaneously. The soy oils are themselves estrogenic, as are the isoflavones, and the well-known antithyroid actions of legumes produce an indirect estrogen-promoting effect, since [[hypothyroidism]] leads to hyperestrogenism. Estrogens are themselves [[thyroid]]-suppressors, so the problem tends to be self-accelerating.

Peat was particularly sharp on the argument that Asian populations prove soy's safety. "Recently, an advocate of soybeans said, 'if they inhibit the thyroid, why isn't there an epidemic of hypothyroidism in Asia?'" he recounted. "I happened to hear this right after seeing newspaper articles about China's problem with 100,000,000 cretins. Yes, Asia has endemic hypothyroidism, and beans are widely associated with hypothyroidism." He further noted that Japanese women's relative freedom from breast cancer is independent of soy products: traditional soy foods are not the same as those widely used in the United States (soy sauce, for example, does not contain the isoflavones), and tea consumption in Japan provides cancer protection through its [[caffeine]] content rather than through its polyphenolic compounds, which actually raise estrogen levels.

The influential study in *Lancet* that compared phytoestrogen levels in the urine of women with and without breast cancer used, in Peat's analysis, "a method known to produce false results." For over fifty years, it has been known that the liver excretes estrogens and other toxins, and that when liver function is impaired, estrogen is retained in the body. Estrogen itself suppresses the detoxifying systems, causing fewer carcinogens to be excreted. The logical reading of the data is that "the women who have cancer are failing to eliminate estrogens, including phytoestrogens, at a normal rate, and so are retaining a higher percentage of the chemicals consumed in their diets." Lower urinary excretion of phytoestrogens indicates worse liver function, not better health.

The genistein in soy has a specific neurological effect as well. Peat noted in his [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]] newsletter that genistein "antagonizes the inhibitory effect of glycine" at the glycine receptor, where [[progesterone]] and its metabolites normally increase inhibition. This means soy isoflavones directly oppose one of the calming, protective actions of progesterone in the nervous system.

### Other Dietary Sources

Beer contains estrogenic compounds from two sources: the hops, which are directly estrogenic, and the yeast, which produces estradiol as its own reproductive hormone. Peat mentioned in a [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 *Ask the Herb Doctor* episode on hair loss and inflammation]] that yeast-produced estradiol is "the powerful human hormone," making alcoholic beverages a significant and underappreciated source of estrogenic exposure. Cannabis smoke, as noted above, is also estrogenic, as are most plants to some degree. Peat observed that "almost every plant has some estrogenic or anti-estrogenic material, but mostly they're estrogenic because it's a defensive material that, for example, acts like birth control pills for predators."

## Environmental Pollution

The scale of estrogenic contamination reaches every ecosystem on the planet. Peat described a study finding that about 25% of the otters and muskrats in the Columbia River were anatomically deformed. The 1995 international conference on environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals, which he cited extensively, stated that "man-made endocrine-disrupting chemicals range across all continents and oceans. They are found in native populations from the Arctic to the tropics, and, because of their persistence in the body, can be passed from generation to generation."

### Oral Contraceptives in the Water Supply

The pharmaceutical estrogens administered to menopausal women and used in oral contraceptives pass through sewage treatment plants and enter rivers, where they feminize fish and other aquatic organisms. Peat confirmed in the [[2011-07-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hair-loss-inflammation-osteoporosis|2011 episode]] that "you can identify the specific type of estrogen and progestin from the fields going right through the sewer processing plants right into the rivers and causing fish, muskrats, anything that lives in the rivers getting the sewage from cities, they're being feminized." When asked whether any filter could remove these compounds, Peat answered simply: "No, nothing practical that's in use."

Natural estrogens from human urine also enter rivers from sewage, and the synthetic estrogens administered in quantities much larger than the body ever produces metabolically add an enormous pharmaceutical component to the natural load.

### Microplastics and the Food Chain

Andrew Murray raised the question of microplastics in the [[2019-05-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-pollution|2019 pollution episode]], noting that plastic debris had been found at the bottom of the Mariana Trench. Peat's response focused on the concentrating mechanism: "Plastics are generally very hydrophobic. Things like polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, famous carcinogens, concentrate on the surface of the particles. When ingested, they deliver a concentrated dose of these organic carcinogens." The particles accumulate up the food chain, meaning that "the bigger, older organisms are the most polluted and dangerous." Peat had personally stopped eating large, long-lived fish like orange roughy and halibut because of their accumulated toxin burden.

This bioconcentration effect means that even low ambient levels of estrogenic contamination become significant exposures through the diet, particularly through seafood, large fish, and animals fed soy-based feeds. Peat noted that in the United States and Europe, livestock are fed large amounts of soybeans, and "the estrogenic materials in soybeans increase the animals' tendency toward inflammation."

## The "Weak Estrogen" Fallacy

The marketing of phytoestrogens, estriol, and other supposedly weak estrogens as protective against stronger estrogens represents, in Peat's view, a fundamental misunderstanding of estrogenic biology. "The concept of a 'protective estrogen' is very similar to the idea of 'protective mutagens' or 'protective carcinogens,'" he wrote, "though in the case of estrogens, their promoters don't even know what the normal, natural functions of estrogen are."

Lipshuts's experiments in the 1940s demonstrated the core problem: continuous exposure to a weak estrogenic stimulus was more effective at producing disease than intermittent exposure to a strong one. The environmental background of estrogenic pollution already provides a continuous exposure. Adding dietary phytoestrogens or pharmaceutical "weak estrogens" on top of this background does not compete with stronger estrogens for receptor binding in any protective sense; it deepens and extends the continuous stimulus that Lipshuts showed to be so effective at producing tumors.

Synergistic interactions compound the problem. "In the same way that weak estrogens in the environment may become hundreds of times more estrogenic by synergistic interactions," Peat warned, "combinations of natural, medical, dietary, and environmental estrogens are almost certain to have unexpected results." The endocrine-disrupting chemicals conference he cited found that many such contaminants, "even if less potent than the natural products, are present in living tissue at concentrations millions of times higher than the natural hormones." Potency per molecule is irrelevant when the total tissue concentration dwarfs that of the body's own estrogens.

Peat compared the "weak estrogen" doctrine to the nuclear industry's promotion of radiation hormesis: "literally the claim that 'a little radiation is positively good for us.'" Both represent the same rhetorical strategy of reframing a known toxin as a therapeutic agent at low doses, in defiance of the evidence for cumulative harm.

## The Receptor Paradox

The conventional definition of an estrogenic substance is one that binds to the estrogen receptor. Peat considered this definition dangerously incomplete. In the [[2019-05-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-pollution|2019 pollution episode]], he stated: "The official concept of what's estrogenic is what sticks to the 'estrogen receptor.' But even in the total absence of estrogen, if you injure the cell or cut off the oxygen supply, the estrogen receptor acts like estrogen is present."

This observation reframes the entire concept of estrogenicity. The receptor is not simply a lock waiting for a chemical key; it is a sensor of cellular energy status. Oxygen deprivation activates the same downstream processes as estrogen binding. Conversely, "just because a substance binds to the receptor doesn't mean it activates estrogen-like processes. It can inactivate the system depending on what it does to that protein." The critical variable is not receptor occupancy but the metabolic state of the cell. "Estrogen characteristically acts like the loss of oxygen. If you have a so-called phytoestrogen that sticks to the receptor but catalyzes oxidative processes, then it is acting as an anti-estrogen."

This principle explains why the estrogenic activity of a compound cannot be predicted from its receptor binding affinity alone, and why the "weak estrogen" concept is misleading. A substance that binds weakly to the receptor but impairs [[oxidative-metabolism|oxidative metabolism]] is functionally a strong estrogen. A substance that binds to the receptor but supports oxygen consumption may be anti-estrogenic despite its receptor affinity. The measurement that matters is not what happens at the receptor but what happens to the cell's energy production.

## Protection Strategies

Because estrogenic contamination is ubiquitous, Peat's approach to protection focused on strengthening the body's capacity to detoxify and eliminate estrogens rather than on the impossible task of avoiding all exposure.

### Liver Function and Nutrition

The liver is the primary organ for inactivating and excreting estrogens. Peat's mentor in this area was Biskind, whose 1940s experiments showed that [[protein]] deficiency leads to accumulation of estrogen "because the liver normally inactivates all the estrogen in the blood as it passes through the liver. This applies to phytoestrogens and industrial estrogens as well as to the natural estrogens of the body." When asked about foods that leach BPA, Peat redirected the focus to internal defense: "It's important to keep your protein and B-vitamins up. Deficiency in those will allow the liver to let estrogen build up in your body. Protein and [[vitamin-b1|Vitamin B1]] are the main anti-estrogen nutrients."

### The Raw Carrot

Peat discovered through personal experience that eating a [[raw-carrot|raw carrot]] daily prevented his migraines, and he came to suspect that carrot fiber was having both a bowel-protective and antiestrogen effect. Several women who suffered from premenstrual symptoms had their serum estrogen measured before and after adopting a daily raw carrot, and found that "the carrot lowered their estrogen within a few days, as it relieved their symptoms." The mechanism is mechanical: undigestible fiber that is not broken down by bowel bacteria increases fecal bulk and speeds transit through the intestine, accelerating the elimination of estrogen excreted in the bile and preventing its reabsorption. Not all fiber works this way; some fiber materials like lignin are themselves estrogenic, and others promote bacterial growth that can convert harmless substances into toxins.

### Avoiding Sources

Peat was pragmatic about dietary avoidance. Phytoestrogens "are so often associated with other food toxins, antithyroid factors, inhibitors of digestive enzymes, immunosuppressants, etc., that the avoidance of certain foods is desirable." Avoiding [[PUFA|polyunsaturated fatty acids]] is important because the same fatty acids that suppress thyroid function have direct estrogenic effects. Protein deficiency contributes to the harm done by all toxins, and at a certain point the resulting hyperestrogenism causes infertility, but before that point is reached, it causes "a great variety of birth defects, deformities of the male genitals, and later, testicular cancer in the sons and breast cancer in the daughters."

The [[progesterone]] and thyroid support central to Peat's framework also serve as anti-estrogenic interventions, since both hormones directly oppose estrogen's metabolic effects. The EPA itself, Peat noted, had published a review acknowledging that progesterone is not carcinogenic in rats and is instead protective against cancer "because of its antiestrogenic effects."
