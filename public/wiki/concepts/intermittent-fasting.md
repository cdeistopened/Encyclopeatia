# Intermittent Fasting

Intermittent fasting is a stress state, not a cleansing state. Every hour without food drives a predictable hormonal cascade: [[cortisol]] rises to catabolize tissue for fuel, [[adrenaline]] mobilizes [[free-fatty-acids|free fatty acids]] from storage, [[thyroid-function|thyroid function]] declines as T4-to-T3 conversion stalls without glucose, and [[estrogen]] accumulates because the liver loses the energy it needs to detoxify it. The popularity of intermittent fasting rests on a cluster of real observations: caloric restriction does extend lifespan in laboratory animals, autophagy does increase during starvation, and people often feel sharper and more energetic when they skip meals. Ray Peat acknowledged each of these observations while systematically reframing the mechanisms behind them. The lifespan extension comes from a reduced metabolic rate, which Peat considered a reduction in life itself. The autophagy is a desperation response to starvation, not superior to the protein turnover a well-nourished cell already performs. And the mental clarity of a skipped meal is the euphoria of cortisol, the body's alarm hormone, not evidence that the brain has found a better fuel. "Cortisol produces a sort of anesthetic euphoria," Peat explained in a [[2011-12-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-questions-and-answers-ii|2011 discussion on fasting and longevity]]. "But in the long run, it's stressful to turn protein into the carbohydrate your brain needs."

The practical consequence of this framework is a diet philosophy that runs exactly opposite to fasting culture. Where intermittent fasting advocates skip breakfast, Peat considered the morning meal essential for suppressing the cortisol peak that accumulates overnight. Where fasting proponents extend the overnight fast as long as possible, Peat recommended a bedtime snack and even setting an alarm to eat during the night. Where modern protocols frame hunger as a signal that the body is "healing," Peat treated hunger as an alarm indicating that the body has begun to destroy itself.

## Fasting as Stress: The Hormonal Cascade

The central claim in Peat's critique is that fasting activates the full stress response. This is not controversial in endocrinology, but fasting advocates tend to frame these hormonal shifts as beneficial adaptations. Peat framed them as damage.

A healthy person stores roughly 24 hours' worth of glucose as glycogen in the liver and muscles. Once glycogen is depleted, the body must manufacture glucose from other sources, and the hormones that drive this process are the stress hormones. As Peat told Jodelle Fitzgerald in a [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|2019 interview on cortisol]]:

> In one day of fasting, the body stores several ounces of glucose in the form of glycogen. If you're healthy, you can go up to 24 hours before you are short on glycogen. As the body senses stress, lowering the T3 slows the rate of fuel use. When your glycogen is gone, you have to start using your tissue to make glucose out of your muscles. If your thyroid didn't slow down during this prolonged stress, you would eat your whole body up in just a few days. But since the depressive process turns off metabolism, it slows the rate of degeneration.

The sequence is precise. First, thyroid hormone conversion drops: the liver requires [[glucose]] to convert the relatively inactive T4 into the metabolically active T3, so without glucose, the [[metabolic-rate|metabolic rate]] falls. As Peat wrote in *Generative Energy*, "Glucose is essential for the conversion, so during fasting there is a sharp decrease in metabolic rate." Second, [[adrenaline]] rises to mobilize stored fat. Third, [[cortisol]] rises to break down tissue protein into amino acids that the liver can convert to glucose for the brain. The subjective experience of this cascade can be pleasant: the adrenaline produces alertness, the cortisol produces a calm, focused feeling. But the underlying reality is tissue destruction and metabolic suppression.

The insulin-glucose dynamics compound the problem. Large protein meals without adequate carbohydrate trigger [[insulin]] release (since amino acids like leucine stimulate insulin), which drops blood sugar further. This triggers adrenaline to pull fatty acids from storage and cortisol to break down tissue for gluconeogenesis. As Peat wrote in [[glycemia-starch-and-sugar-in-context|"Glycemia, Starch, and Sugar in Context"]], "Unsaturated fatty acids, adrenaline, and cortisol cause insulin resistance." The fasting state, in other words, creates the very metabolic dysfunction that fasting advocates believe they are curing.

## The Tissue Destruction Hierarchy

One of Peat's most striking claims is that fasting destroys the body's tissues in a specific order, and that the most immunologically critical tissues are sacrificed first. In a [[2013-02-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-weight-gain|2013 discussion on weight gain]], he outlined the sequence:

> The first to go is your thymus, which dissolves in just a few hours of intense stress. A long fast will completely devastate your thymus tissues. It turns into sugar very quickly.

The [[thymus]] is the organ responsible for maturing T-cells, the adaptive immune system's primary effectors. Its rapid sacrifice during fasting means that even short fasts carry an immunological cost. After the thymus, Peat identified the skin as the next tissue to be consumed:

> The skin is relatively dispensable too, so your skin atrophies very quickly with a fast or low-calorie diet. Then the muscles go. You can live months on your muscles providing sugar for your brain.

This hierarchy explains a paradox of chronic fasting and extreme dieting. Many women who pursued prolonged caloric restriction came to Peat with muscles "the size of a finger," having lost enormous amounts of lean tissue while retaining body fat. The body preferentially burns protein over fat during stress because the stress hormones driving the process are designed to produce glucose (from protein), not ketones (from fat), for the brain.

The ratio of tissue destruction is dramatic. Peat cited studies showing that on a complete water fast, "80% or more of the weight loss came from the destruction of muscle and skin." But a modest caloric intake changed this ratio entirely: "If they had a 500 or 600 calorie diet with adequate minerals, almost all of the weight loss was in the form of fat loss." The difference between a fast and a reduced-calorie diet with adequate minerals is the difference between destroying your body and losing fat. The stress hormones are the variable. Sufficient calories and minerals keep cortisol low enough to prevent the catabolism of lean tissue.

Peat also cited MRI evidence of brain shrinkage during prolonged fasting. In the same 2019 Jodellefit interview, he described the neurological consequences of anorexia:

> They have done MRI pictures of brains during anorexia and during recovery. During anorexia, the brain, especially the frontal lobes, shrinks. When they repeat it during recovery, it rebuilds itself. So it's actually causing brain damage to fast too much.

The frontal lobes, which handle long-range planning and judgment, are particularly vulnerable to the combination of high estrogen and high cortisol that fasting produces.

## Fasting Impairs Detoxification

Perhaps the most directly counterintuitive claim in Peat's framework is that fasting damages the liver's detoxification capacity rather than enhancing it. The popular concept of a "detox fast" assumes that relieving the liver of digestive work frees it to process stored toxins. Peat argued that the opposite occurs.

The liver detoxifies estrogen and other harmful substances through two primary pathways: sulfation and glucuronidation. Both pathways require energy in the form of glucose. When fasting deprives the liver of sugar and exposes it to rising cortisol, these systems shut down. Peat described the mechanism in the [[2019-06-01-jodellefit-jodellefit-cortisol-low-testosterone|2019 Jodellefit interview]]:

> Some professors advocated fasting for detoxification, but that got me interested in what really happens. The liver is the organ of detoxification. When you deprive the liver of sugar and expose it to cortisol, not only does estrogen rise, but the liver loses its ability to detoxify other things. Estrogen rises because the sulfation and glucuronidation detoxifying systems are shut down by fasting.

Estrogen accumulation during fasting is not a trivial side effect. In Peat's framework, estrogen is one of the primary drivers of inflammation, [[cancer]], and degenerative disease. A fast intended to "cleanse" the body actually allows one of its most dangerous endogenous substances to accumulate unchecked.

Peat illustrated the problem with a vivid personal anecdote. After being exposed to an insecticide on a bus in Mexico, he noticed that the toxin had been stored in his fat tissue and was released during periods of low blood sugar:

> Every time I got hungry, I would smell this insecticide in my exhaled breath. For about two months, every time I had low blood sugar at 3:00 AM, I would wake up smelling this Pine-Sol insecticide odor. As soon as that odor stopped leaving my body when I got hungry, I went into a hyperthyroid state. It had been suppressing my thyroid.

The anecdote captures a broader principle. Fasting mobilizes stored toxins from fat tissue through the action of stress hormones, but because those same stress hormones have simultaneously impaired the liver's detoxification pathways, the toxins circulate freely rather than being processed and eliminated. "A fast is a way to maybe clean up your liver eventually," Peat conceded, "but definitely, it's not good for your brain and immune system."

## The Free Fatty Acid Problem

Fasting liberates stored fats from [[adipose-tissue|adipose tissue]], and in the modern world, those stored fats are overwhelmingly [[PUFA|polyunsaturated]]. The body preferentially stores polyunsaturated fatty acids and burns saturated fats for immediate energy, so the fat reserves of anyone who has eaten a standard diet for decades are concentrated with the linoleic acid, arachidonic acid, and other highly unsaturated fats that Peat considered toxic at the cellular level.

When [[free-fatty-acids]] flood the bloodstream during fasting, they poison [[mitochondria|mitochondrial]] respiration and block glucose oxidation through what Peat, following Philip Randle, called the [[randle-cycle|Randle cycle]]. Cells forced to burn fat instead of glucose produce less [[carbon-dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and more [[lactic-acid|lactic acid]], shifting the body toward the inefficient, inflammation-promoting metabolic state that Peat associated with aging and disease.

Peat described the brain's particular vulnerability in a [[2019-07-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-herbalist-sophie-lamb|2019 discussion with herbalist Sophie Lamb]]:

> Thyroid is essential for storing glucose as glycogen in the liver and muscles. When you run out of glycogen, you mobilize free fatty acids. The free fatty acid condition creates a state of diabetes in the brain, turning off brain metabolism by essentially poisoning the mitochondria and blocking the ability to use glucose.

The fasting brain, in Peat's model, is not a brain running on a cleaner fuel. It is a brain in a diabetic-like state, with its mitochondria impaired by circulating unsaturated fats. The clarity that fasters report comes from the alerting effects of adrenaline, not from improved neural metabolism.

The combination of exercise and fasting amplifies this problem. Peat wrote in [[lactate-vs-co2-in-wounds-sickness-and-aging-the-other-approach-to-cancer|"Lactate vs. CO2 in Wounds, Sickness, and Aging"]] that "strenuous exercise combined with fasting (e.g., running or working before eating breakfast) not only directly trigger the production of lactate and ammonia, they also are likely to increase the absorption of bacterial [[endotoxin]] from the intestine." The fasted morning workout, a staple of many fitness protocols, compounds the stress of low blood sugar with the stress of exercise-induced endotoxin absorption.

## The Caloric Restriction Longevity Paradox

Peat did not deny that caloric restriction extends lifespan in laboratory animals. The original studies by Clive McKay in the 1930s are well established, and Peat cited them directly. What he denied was that the mechanism represented anything desirable.

Caloric restriction extends lifespan by reducing the metabolic rate. A slower metabolism accumulates less damage from the peroxidation of unsaturated fats in mitochondrial membranes. As Peat wrote in [[unsaturated-fatty-acids-nutritionally-essential-or-toxic|"Unsaturated Fatty Acids: Nutritionally Essential, or Toxic?"]], "Caloric restriction slows the accumulation of the highly unsaturated fatty acids in mitochondria, and reduces peroxidation." The organism lives longer because it is doing less living. Lower temperature, lower energy production, lower function across every organ system.

Peat's crucial insight was that the same lifespan extension could be achieved without caloric restriction by eliminating the toxic substances that make a high metabolic rate dangerous. He highlighted the work on specific amino acid restriction in [[gelatin-stress-longevity|"Gelatin, Stress, Longevity"]]:

> Restricting toxic heavy metals, without restricting calories, produces about the same life-extending effect as caloric restriction. Restricting only tryptophan, or only cysteine, produces a greater extension of the life span than achieved in most of the studies of caloric restriction.

[[Tryptophan]] and [[cysteine]] both inhibit thyroid function and mitochondrial energy production. Tryptophan is the precursor to [[serotonin]], which promotes inflammation, immunodepression, and the general changes associated with aging. Restricting these specific amino acids, through a diet supplemented with [[gelatin]] (which lacks tryptophan and is low in cysteine), extends lifespan more than restricting total calories, and without the metabolic depression that caloric restriction imposes.

The implication for intermittent fasting advocates is significant. If the goal is longevity, the data points away from eating less and toward eating differently: reducing PUFA, limiting tryptophan and cysteine through gelatin supplementation, and maintaining a high metabolic rate on foods that support mitochondrial respiration rather than poison it.

## Autophagy: Metabolism, Not Starvation

The 2016 Nobel Prize awarded to Yoshinori Ohsumi for his work on autophagy mechanisms gave intermittent fasting its most powerful scientific narrative. If the body "cleans up" damaged cellular components during fasting, then periodic fasting becomes a kind of cellular housekeeping. Peat's response to this framing was characteristically redirectional: yes, autophagy occurs during starvation, but it occurs as a desperation response, and a well-functioning cell already performs the same cleanup more effectively.

When Josh Rubin asked Peat directly about fasting to upregulate autophagy in a [[2011-12-15-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-questions-and-answers-ii|2011 Q&A session]], Peat's answer pointed toward metabolism rather than deprivation: "Activating your metabolism is going to eat up proteins that shouldn't be there. One of the main things interfering with the breakdown of protein is the PUFA." The problem is not insufficient fasting but insufficient metabolic energy. Cells that are properly nourished and producing adequate energy through oxidative metabolism continuously recycle their damaged components. It is the accumulation of polyunsaturated fatty acids that interferes with this process at the deepest levels of cell function.

The distinction matters because starvation-induced autophagy carries costs that metabolically driven protein turnover does not. During fasting, the stress hormones that trigger autophagy simultaneously cause the tissue destruction, immune suppression, and metabolic depression described above. Raising the metabolic rate through adequate nutrition, thyroid support, and PUFA avoidance achieves the same cellular cleanup without the collateral damage.

## The Sirtuin and Resveratrol Critique

The molecular biology of caloric restriction longevity found its celebrity molecule in the sirtuins, a family of proteins whose name stands for Silent Information Regulators. A group at Harvard argued that sirtuin activation was the mechanism behind caloric restriction's life-extending effects, and that [[resveratrol]], a compound found in grape skins, could activate sirtuins without the need for actual caloric restriction. The supplement industry built a market worth billions of dollars on this premise.

Peat dismantled the argument on multiple grounds in a [[2014-10-17-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-longevity|2014 discussion on longevity]]. First, he pointed out the chemical ancestry of resveratrol: it "is chemically very closely related to DES (diethylstilbestrol, a synthetic estrogen)" and "has estrogenic activity." Promoting a synthetic-estrogen analog as a longevity supplement was, in Peat's framework, promoting one of the primary drivers of aging and cancer.

Second, he identified the consequences of sirtuin activation at the protein level. Sirtuins work by silencing genes, moving cells toward "a spore-like or hibernating state of not producing proteins." Among the specific proteins turned off by sirtuin activation are [[klotho]], "a phosphate-regulating protein where turning it off causes rapid aging," and [[p53]], "a tumor suppressor protein." Turning off p53 to achieve longevity is a contradiction: you are suppressing the protein that prevents cancer in the hope of living longer.

Third, Peat cited the direct experimental refutation. [[Niacinamide]], which suppresses sirtuins, was predicted by the Harvard group to accelerate aging. Instead, "someone put human cells in a dish, gave them a supplement of niacinamide, and they lived 60% longer." The substance that suppresses the supposed longevity pathway actually extended cellular lifespan, while the substance that activates the pathway (resveratrol) carries estrogenic effects associated with aging and disease.

## Growth Hormone and Prolactin: Fasting's Hidden Costs

Intermittent fasting advocates frequently cite the increase in [[growth-hormone|growth hormone]] (GH) during fasting as a benefit. Peat's framework treats this increase as one of fasting's most damaging effects.

Growth hormone is a stress hormone. As Peat wrote in [[growth-hormone-hormone-of-stress-aging-death|"Growth Hormone: Hormone of Stress, Aging, & Death?"]], "Heat, hypoglycemia, running, and some types of shock are known to stimulate growth hormone secretion, sometimes to levels ten or twenty times higher than normal. I consider the growth hormone to be, almost as much as [[prolactin]], a stress-inducible hormone."

The metabolic effects of growth hormone parallel those of adrenaline. "One of the best known metabolic effects of GH is that, like adrenalin, it mobilizes fatty acids from storage," Peat wrote. Growth hormone antagonizes insulin and drives the shift from glucose oxidation to fat oxidation. At puberty, this creates a mild insulin resistance that "tends to increase progressively with age." Fasting amplifies this age-related shift by spiking growth hormone repeatedly.

Peat connected growth hormone to W. Donner Denckla's research on a hypothetical "death hormone" secreted by the pituitary. Denckla found that removing the pituitary "drastically slowed" aging in animals. He was unable to isolate the specific death hormone, but reported that "it was 'in the prolactin fraction.'" Both growth hormone and prolactin rise during fasting, during the night, and during stress. Both mobilize free fatty acids, and growth hormone specifically "creates a bias toward oxidizing of the most unsaturated fatty acids," wasting energy through the oxidation of the most toxic stored fats.

The spiking of growth hormone during fasting is, in Peat's analysis, not a regenerative signal but a stress signal with the same metabolic signature as aging itself.

## Darkness and Fasting: The Metabolic Parallel

Peat drew a striking parallel between fasting and darkness, treating them as two versions of the same metabolic threat. Both deplete glucose, raise cortisol, damage mitochondria, and activate the full catabolic stress response. The parallel illuminates why the overnight fast is so consequential and why Peat placed such emphasis on eating before bed and upon waking.

In the chapter "The Stress of Darkness" from *Generative Energy*, Peat described how darkness progressively damages mitochondria from sunset to dawn:

> Beginning at sunset, darkness progressively damages mitochondria structurally and functionally. Mitochondrial damage, and the functional impairment of organs such as the heart muscle, reach a peak at dawn.

The cortisol response to darkness follows the same curve. "Cortisol begins to rise as soon as there is darkness, regardless of sleep or waking," Peat wrote. "Falling glucose is the signal for cortisol to rise." Sleep reduces but does not eliminate this damage: "Sleep minimizes the increase of cortisol. The inhibitory, sleep inducing brain chemicals, GABA and the sleep peptide, have a quieting, anti-stress effect even during waking stress."

[[Melatonin]], the hormone of darkness, shares properties with the hormones of fasting. Peat warned that it "makes the brain sluggish, causes the sex organs to shrink, and damages immunity by shrinking the thymus gland," in [[using-sunlight-to-sustain-life|"Using Sunlight to Sustain Life"]]. The thymus shrinkage caused by melatonin parallels the thymus destruction caused by fasting, reinforcing Peat's view that darkness and food deprivation represent metabolically equivalent threats.

The nighttime stress cascade explains a phenomenon that diabetics call the "Dawn Phenomenon," where blood sugar spikes upon waking without any food intake. As Peat described in a [[2013-11-15-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-hashimoto-s-antibodies-temperature-and-pulse|2013 discussion on Hashimoto's]], "The stress hormones that raise temperature and pulse around dawn also increase blood sugar. Diabetics call it the 'Dawn Phenomenon,' but it's the result of stress hormones rising during the night." The morning blood sugar spike is not a mystery of glucose metabolism; it is the body's stress hormones converting tissue protein into glucose because glycogen has run out during the night.

The cumulative effects of darkness-plus-fasting are especially pronounced in winter, when "there is cumulative damage to mitochondria, because of too few daylight hours to complete the rebuilding of mitochondria." This seasonal vulnerability suggests that fasting during winter months would compound an already damaging metabolic environment.

## Why Breakfast Matters

If fasting is a stress state and the overnight period represents the body's most prolonged daily exposure to the fasting-plus-darkness combination, then the timing of the first meal takes on particular importance. Peat was unequivocal about the value of eating promptly after waking.

In a [[2019-11-12-jodellefit-jodellefit-listener-q-a-calories-cortisol-cellulite-exercise-ephedra-more|2019 listener Q&A]], he described what a healthy post-breakfast response looks like: "After breakfast, you should pop right up to a working temperature of 37 degrees Celsius (98.6 Fahrenheit) and a pulse rate of more or less 80 or 85 beats per minute." The morning rise in temperature and pulse after eating is the sign that stress hormones are receding and oxidative metabolism is resuming.

The alternative, skipping breakfast to extend the overnight fast, means extending the period of high cortisol, high adrenaline, and high free fatty acids into the morning hours. Peat noted in a [[2019-01-24-one-radio-network-one-radio-network-fats-and-questions|2019 interview on morning stress]] that "thyroid people have extremely high cortisol and adrenaline in the morning, which can make the morning temperature misleadingly warm. If you have orange juice and milk and your temperature drops a couple of hours later, that means you were running on stress hormones." The warmth that an intermittent faster feels in the morning, often interpreted as a sign that fasting is "working," may be the warmth of stress hormone activation rather than healthy metabolic function.

## The Bedtime Snack as Fasting Prevention

Peat's dietary recommendations for the evening hours were as specific as his breakfast recommendations, because the goal was to minimize the metabolic damage of the overnight fast before it began. He recommended carbohydrate-containing foods before bed to maintain liver glycogen through the night: [[orange-juice|orange juice]], [[milk]] with honey, [[ice-cream|ice cream]], salty snacks like pork rinds or tortilla chips with milk.

The rationale connects to glycogen storage and the stress response. As Peat explained in a [[2017-05-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-endocrinology-part-3|2017 discussion on endocrinology]], "It's very important to restore your glycogen level during sleep. I think that's one of the main purposes of sleep, to get the glycogen back into your brain and heart cells so you can expend it to resist stress during the daytime."

For people who wake during the night, Peat recommended eating immediately rather than trying to fall back to sleep on stress hormones. Sophie Lamb, who overcame eight years of severe insomnia using Peat's recommendations, described the logic in a [[2019-07-19-ask-the-herb-doctor-ask-the-herb-doctor-herbalist-sophie-lamb|2019 interview]]:

> If we haven't laid down enough glycogen during the day, we get a rise in stress hormones (cortisol and adrenaline) to catabolize fat and muscle for energy. A side effect of stress hormones is mental alertness, which you don't want at night. Insomnia is actually a daytime disorder presenting itself at night.

Peat himself advised patients with nighttime waking to "set an alarm to wake up shortly before their usual waking time and eat something with carbohydrate, milk, juice, or a cracker. Within a week, they were sleeping through the night." The snack prevents the stress hormone surge that causes both the waking and the tissue catabolism.

Salt at bedtime was another specific recommendation. "Taking some extra salt at bed-time is logical," Peat wrote in *Generative Energy*. "It lowers adrenalin, and tends to increase blood volume." The combination of sugar and salt before bed addresses both halves of the stress response: sugar prevents the cortisol rise triggered by falling glucose, and salt prevents the adrenaline rise triggered by falling blood volume.

## The Fasting Bowel Exception

Despite his thoroughgoing opposition to fasting, Peat acknowledged one context in which not eating has genuine therapeutic value: when the intestine itself is the source of inflammatory disease. In a [[2011-07-12-eastwest-healing-eastwest-healing-questions-and-answers-i|2011 Q&A on fasting and inflammation]], he explained:

> There have been studies in which rheumatoid arthritis and similar conditions are completely relieved during a week-long fast, but they come back as soon as the person eats again. That is partly because cortisol increases tremendously when you aren't eating. The cortisol converts muscle tissue into fuel to live on, and at the same time, it has an anti-inflammatory effect.

The relief from autoimmune conditions during fasting is real, but it works through two mechanisms that Peat considered problematic: the anti-inflammatory effects of elevated cortisol (a stress response, not a healing response) and the reduction of intestinal endotoxin absorption when nothing is being digested. Peat's alternative was not fasting but a carefully chosen diet that achieves the same intestinal quieting without the stress hormone cost: "You must do it in a way that doesn't produce endotoxin, lactic acid, or serotonin excess. This means using a very easily digested diet, avoiding undercooked vegetable matter that feeds bacteria, and having antimicrobial foods like raw carrots or boiled bamboo shoots."

He also noted that sick animals often refuse food spontaneously, and that this natural anorexia can be therapeutic: "I've seen animals with huge tumors that refused to eat, and if their owners let them sit around not eating, they would recover and live for years." The distinction is between an organism that stops eating in response to illness, letting the immune system work without the burden of processing food that feeds intestinal bacteria, and a healthy organism that deliberately induces a stress state through food deprivation.

## Recovering from Intermittent Fasting

When a listener asked Peat directly about transitioning away from caloric restriction and intermittent fasting protocols, his advice centered not on calorie counting but on metabolic indicators. In the [[2019-11-12-jodellefit-jodellefit-listener-q-a-calories-cortisol-cellulite-exercise-ephedra-more|2019 listener Q&A]], he recommended watching temperature and pulse: "The important thing is to watch your temperature and pulse rate as indicators of how energetic your metabolism is."

The recovery process follows a predictable pattern. Women who came to Peat after prolonged dieting often experienced an initial period of water loss as their tissues came out of the stress-induced edematous state, followed by a month or more of weight gain as muscles began rebuilding. The weight gain alarmed many of them, but it represented the restoration of lean tissue that chronic caloric restriction had destroyed.

Peat's general recommendation for weight management was to eat to increase the metabolic rate rather than to restrict calories: "My recommendation is to eat to increase the metabolic rate (usually temperature and heart rate), rather than any particular foods. Usually the increased metabolic rate, with adequate protein, causes some muscle increase, and when that happens the basic calorie requirement will increase." In his email correspondence on weight loss, he identified the stored PUFA as the primary obstacle: "It's the stored PUFA, released by stress or hunger, that slow metabolism." The interventions he recommended for managing the free fatty acid problem during recovery included [[niacinamide]], [[aspirin]], [[coconut-oil|coconut oil]], and adequate [[thyroid]] support.
